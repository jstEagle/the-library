## 2. Ninety-Nine Steps

The summons had come on card stock, cream-colored and crisp as a winter sheet, folded once and delivered by a boy who looked at Ansel as though the paper itself might be soiled by passing through his hands.

*Mister A. Nakamura. The master clock at the Observatory has failed in the night. Your attendance is requested at your earliest convenience. — E. Whitcombe, Keeper.*

Ansel read it four times on the walk up from Harbor Row, turning it over as though the reverse might hold smaller print, some clause retracting the invitation, some joke at his expense he hadn't yet located. Garrick had read it once, sniffed, and said, "Well. Don't embarrass me," which was, from Garrick, nearly a blessing. Then he had gone back to his ledger and his cold tea and his habit of not looking at Ansel directly, as if looking too long might cost him something.

The observatory stood on the hill above the town like a chess piece forgotten mid-game—a white dome and a square stone tower, the whole of it owned by the Admiralty and tended by one keeper and, everyone assumed, by nobody else's interest at all. Ansel had never been inside it. He had seen it every day of the three years since his father died: from the shop window, from the harbor wall, from the churchyard where he knelt on Sundays with his head bowed and his eyes open. It hung above the town the way the master clock's time hung above everything—the trains, the tides, the fishermen's arguments about whose watch was wrong. Nobody thought about it until it stopped.

The path climbed through gorse and blackthorn, and the wind came off the sea in long, deliberate pushes, smelling of salt and wrack and the cold iron scent of early spring. Ansel counted the steps because counting steadied him the way tools steadied him—there was an order to numbers that people lacked. His father had taught him that. *A gear does not dislike you, Ansel. A spring does not grow weary of your face. Learn to live among things that only ask that you be careful.*

He was at step sixty when he realized he was climbing quickly, eagerly even, and made himself slow down. Hope was an unregulated escapement. It ran fast when you wound it too tight.

At the top, the tower door stood open, and a woman was waiting in it.

Not waiting for him specifically—or so he first told himself. She was leaning against the frame with her arms crossed, watching the harbor below with the proprietary squint of someone who had grown up counting boats, and she might have stepped back inside and shut the door and let him knock for an hour. But she turned when his boot scraped the last step, and she looked at him—*at* him, full in the face, the way no one in this town had looked at him since his father's funeral—and she said:

"You're the clockmaker."

"Apprentice," Ansel said. The word came out before he could weigh it, worn smooth by three years of saying it. "Ansel Nakamura. My master sends his regards." He did not know if that was true. Garrick sent nothing anywhere except invoices.

"Maren Whitcombe." She did not offer her hand; she simply pushed off from the doorframe and stood aside, gesturing him in with a sweep of her arm, as though he were expected, as though he had always been expected. "My father wrote the note but I'm the one who's been cursing at the thing all morning, so you'll be dealing mostly with me. Fair warning—I'm not polite about machines that betray me."

It took Ansel a moment to place what was strange about her, and then he understood: she had spoken to him exactly as she would have spoken to anyone. No pause before his name. No careful pronunciation, tested and abandoned. No glance at his skin, his eyes, his half-foreign face, followed by the small tightening around the mouth that meant a person was recalculating their manners and finding the sums inconvenient. She had said *Nakamura* the way she'd say *Whitcombe*—once, cleanly, done with it.

Inside, the tower was colder than the morning and smelled of lamp oil, stone dust, and old brass. A spiral stair wound upward along the inner wall, and light fell through narrow slitted windows in pale bars that lay across the steps like rungs of another ladder. Maren went ahead of him without ceremony, talking over her shoulder.

"It stopped sometime between eleven and midnight. Father was up here until ten winding it and setting the pendulum—he does it himself, always has, won't let anyone else near it—and it was keeping true to within two seconds when he left. This morning I came up at six to light the lamps and it was dead. Not slow. Not stuttering. Dead. Like a man who'd been shot between one breath and the next."

"Shot," Ansel repeated, and felt something small and cold shift in his chest.

"You'll see."

They came up into the clock room, and Ansel stopped in the doorway despite himself.

The master clock filled the far wall like a cathedral organ built by someone who worshipped time instead of God. Its case rose nearly to the vaulted ceiling, dark oak gone amber with a century of oil and handling, and behind glass panels the great brass wheels hung suspended in their hierarchies—the barrel, the great wheel, the escape wheel, the pendulum hanging below like a held breath. Even dead, it dominated the room. Even still, it seemed to be listening.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Maren said, and it wasn't a question, and she wasn't looking at the clock. She was looking at him looking at it.

"Yes," Ansel said simply, because there was no honest alternative.

"Then you'll understand why I've been swearing at it in two languages."

He set his tool roll down on the table she indicated and washed his hands in the basin she'd filled—someone had thought of that, had guessed a workman would want clean hands before this mechanism, and the small courtesy landed in him strangely, like a coin dropped into a dry well. Then he opened the case.

The smell of the interior rose up to meet him: graphite, aged oil, the faint green breath of old brass. He put his ear close to the works and moved the crutch gently with one finger, feeling for the catch, the bind, the place where the machine refused. A clock that dies overnight is rarely mysterious. Weights run out. Suspension springs snap. Pivots seize after damp. There is usually a body.

There was no body.

"Weight's fully wound," Ansel said slowly, straightening. "Barrel's got tension. Cord's sound." He bent again, tracing the train wheel by wheel with his fingertip, downward from the great wheel, and his finger stopped where the train simply—ended. Where motion should have passed from one wheel to the next and found instead a gap, an absence, a hole in the argument of the machine.

He opened the access panel wider and looked, and then looked again, and the cold thing in his chest shifted once more and settled into a shape he recognized.

"The escape wheel," he said. "There's no second wheel. The pivot holes are empty—" He touched them, one, then the other, and they were bright inside, freshly bright, the marks of recent removal plain as handwriting to anyone who knew how to read metal. "Someone has taken the intermediate wheel out. Entirely. And cleaned the holes after."

Maren had come to stand beside him. "Taken it out. You mean it fell?"

"Wheels don't fall out of a closed case, Miss Whitcombe." He heard his own voice and was surprised by how level it was. Inside, his heart had begun a quick, uneven ticking of its own. "And they don't leave the pivot holes polished. That takes a cloth and time and a steady hand. Whoever did this knew clocks. Knew this clock." He paused. "And wanted it to look like simple failure to anyone who didn't look closely."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved across the dome with a low oceanic hush, as though the sky itself were turning over in its sleep.

"But you looked closely," Maren said.

"I looked closely."

She was quiet a moment. When he glanced at her, expecting alarm or suspicion—expecting, in some tired corner of himself, the familiar arithmetic by which trouble near a half-Japanese boy becomes the boy's fault—she was frowning at the clock instead, her jaw set, thinking not *who can we blame* but *who has done this to us.* It was such a small difference. It undid something in him.

"Father will have to report it," she said at last. "The Admiralty keeps its own men for the chronometers, but the master clock—they'll want to know why the tide tables went wrong, why the signal station was late. They'll want to know badly enough to start asking questions of everyone in town." She turned to him. "Can you fix it? Can you make it look like nothing happened, until we know what did?"

"I can cut a replacement wheel. Two days' work, maybe three, if I can find brass of the right thickness." He hesitated. "Miss Whitcombe—your father should know that this wasn't wear, and wasn't accident. If someone stopped this clock deliberately, they'll do it again, or worse, unless they're found. A clockmaker who finds sabotage and says nothing—" *becomes suspect*, he almost said, *becomes the natural culprit, especially one with my face*, and did not say it, because she was looking at him with those clear gray eyes and he found he did not want to teach her the arithmetic.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"This clock was stopped on purpose. By someone patient, and skilled, and close."

"Close," she repeated.

"A stranger couldn't have gotten up here past your father. It wants knowledge, opportunity, and a reason. I can supply the first two from any decent journeyman in the county. The third—" He spread his hands. "That's yours to wonder about, not mine. I only repair what's broken."

"No," Maren said, and there was something new in her voice, something that made him look up. "You don't only repair what's broken. Anyone can see that. You're the first person who's ever actually *looked* at this thing." She rested her hand flat against the oak case, the way one touches the shoulder of an old horse. "Everyone else in this town treats the clock like weather. It works or it doesn't. You looked at it for five minutes and saw a crime."

Ansel felt heat climb his neck. Three years of being looked *through*—by customers who addressed their complaints to Garrick over Ansel's bowed head, by shopkeepers who watched his hands and not his face, by children who pointed and mothers who pulled the pointing fingers down without ever correcting the pointing itself—and now this woman, this stranger, had handed him back to himself in a single sentence.

"It's my trade," he managed.

"It's more than that." She said it without flattery, without softness even—flatly, as fact, the way she might have said *the tide comes twice a day*—and that was precisely why it went through him like a struck bell. "Father will want to speak with you. He's down at the customs house now, filing the report he'll hate filing. Come back tomorrow with your measurements and whatever you need. Come at supper, actually—we eat at seven, there's too much of everything as usual, and Mother likes company she hasn't already heard the opinions of." A corner of her mouth lifted. "You'll be asked about yourself. Be warned. We're rude that way. We're interested in people."

*I'm interested in you,* said the animal part of his brain, treacherously, and he strangled it at birth.

"I'll be here," he said.

He packed his tools slowly, making himself orderly, using the ritual of the roll and the buckles to press down the strangeness rising in him—that flicker of hope he'd tried to regulate on the stairs, now running wild. As he worked, Maren lit the lamps against the thickening afternoon, and the light caught the empty pivot holes, the bright absence where the wheel should turn.

"Miss Whitcombe," he said, before he could stop himself. "When you came up that first morning. Was anything disturbed? Papers, instruments—anything beyond the clock itself?"

She paused, lamp in hand, and he watched her consider the question seriously rather than brushing it aside, and that too was a kind of gift.

"Now you mention it—the logbook was out of place. Father is particular about it, spine to the north edge, always. It was lying flat. He'd have noticed." Her eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because a thief takes what's valuable," Ansel said quietly, looking at the dead pendulum, "and leaves the machine. Someone wanted this clock stopped and wanted something read, or taken, or hidden where only stopping the clock would send searchers elsewhere." He heard himself and stopped, appalled at the shape of his own speculation, at how much of it he suddenly believed.

The wind pressed at the tower windows. Below, faintly, the harbor bell rang the quarter-hour—wrong, now, by eleven minutes, and getting wronger by the day, the whole town's time drifting slowly out of true like a ship losing its bearing in fog.

"Supper at seven," Maren said again, but her voice had changed; the warmth in it had acquired an edge, like sun on a blade. "Come hungry, Mister Nakamura. And come careful. If what you're saying is true, then somebody in this town has very good hands"—she looked at his tool roll, then at his face—"and we ought to find out whose they are before they reach for anything else."

Ansel bowed, an old habit of his father's that no Englishman ever returned, and started down the ninety-nine steps with the wind at his back and the letter of summons in his pocket and a cold, precise unease ticking somewhere beneath his ribs, keeping perfect time.

* * *

Halfway down the hill, he stopped and looked back. The white dome stood against a bruising sunset, and in one high slitted window of the clock room a lamp still burned.

He thought of the polished pivot holes. Of the logbook lying flat. Of a wheel removed by a patient, practiced hand—and of how many hands in this town were patient and practiced, and how few of them belonged to men anyone would ever think to suspect.

His own, he thought, were counted among them. That, presumably, made him a fool to be walking away instead of running.

He walked faster anyway, and did not look back again.
