## 3. What the Case Keeps

The master clock's housing smelled of old oil and older dust, a smell Ansel had known since he was small enough to stand beneath his father's workbench and breathe it in like incense. But here at the top of the observatory tower the smell was different—colder somehow, sharpened by salt air that seeped through the stone seams and settled into every crevice of the great case like a guest who intended to stay the winter. He knelt on the canvas sheet Maren had fetched from the instrument room and worked by lamplight, his tools laid out in their precise rows, each one aligned as his father had taught him: files to the left, pliers to the right, screwdrivers ordered by size so that a blind man could find them.

Above him the pendulum hung dead, its bob still as a drowned thing. It was a strange sensation, working inside a stopped clock of this size. In Garrick's shop the clocks were small enough to hold against one's ear, to feel their hearts ticking through the brass into the palm. This one you entered. You became part of its anatomy. The escapement loomed above him like the jaw of some patient animal, and when he reached up to examine the pallets, his fingertips came away black with grease that had hardened over decades.

Maren had gone down an hour after dark, promising to bring up tea and bread if the housekeeper could be persuaded not to ask questions. Her father, Edmund Whitcombe, had pressed Ansel's shoulder once on his way out—the briefest touch, but weighted—and said, "Take whatever time the work needs, lad. We're grateful." *We.* As though Ansel were included in that plural without effort or arithmetic. He had turned back to his lamp quickly then, because something in his throat had needed swallowing, and he did not want Edmund Whitcombe to see it.

The failure itself continued to trouble him. He had spent the first two hours confirming what he already knew in his bones: nothing here had simply worn out. The escape wheel was sound, its teeth unworn, oiled within the last month by someone who knew what they were doing. The suspension spring was intact. The crutch was true. Only the gear was missing—the intermediate wheel that carried motion from the going train to the strike mechanism, or rather the wheel that *should* have been there and was not. Its arbor remained, polished clean where the wheel had been drawn off. No corrosion. No accident of metal fatigue. Someone had stood exactly where Ansel knelt, with exactly this lamplight or another like it, and had removed that gear with care, with skill, with intent.

He kept returning to the question the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Who? And more urgently, why? A clockmaker might steal a gear for parts—a mean thought, but a common one; Garrick himself had been known to cannibalize a dead movement for a wheel in good condition. But no thief would have left everything else untouched. No thief would have re-seated the hands at precisely ten minutes past eleven, a time with no meaning Ansel could find, and closed the case door so gently that the latch showed no scratching. Whoever it was had wanted the clock to stop quietly, unremarkably, the way clocks sometimes did. Wanted the town to shrug and say *these things happen.*

But the observatory clock was not a clock one shrugged about. Half the fishing fleet set its departure by the time-gun that fired off this very mechanism. The harbormaster consulted it. The customs men consulted it. When it failed, the whole harbor lost its single agreed-upon truth, and every man's watch became a small private kingdom declaring its own hours.

Ansel sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on his apron, staring into the dark throat of the case above the escapement.

That was when he saw it.

It was nothing, at first—or it seemed like nothing. A shadow behind the escapement bridge where no shadow should sit evenly. Dust fell unevenly in a stopped clock; that was natural. But this shadow had edges. Straight edges. He fetched the lamp and held it high, tilting his head until his neck ached, and there, wedged flat into the narrow space between the back plate and the wooden case wall—a space no wider than two fingers—a rectangle of paper. Sealed. Oiled paper over wax over folded linen-paper, the whole of it wrapped tight as a surgical dressing, and pressed so far back that only a man lying on his belly with a lamp raised above his head would ever have found it. A man repairing the clock, perhaps. A man whose work required him to put his arm into places arms were not meant to go.

His first instinct, absurdly, was to leave it. His second was to pretend he had never seen it at all—to finish the repair, fit a new gear from the stock he'd brought, collect his fee, and descend the ninety-nine steps as though the housing had contained nothing but dust and dead oil. He actually drew his hand back. He actually began to turn away.

Then he thought of Maren's face that afternoon, lit sideways by the lamp as she watched him work—not watching the work, he'd realized later, but watching him, with an attention so unguarded it had felt almost indecent, like seeing someone without their clothes. He thought of Edmund Whitcombe's hand on his shoulder. *We're grateful, lad.* He thought of three years of eating at their table every Sunday, of Thomas Whitcombe teaching him to gut mackerel and laughing at his clumsiness without a grain of cruelty in it, of Mrs. Whitcombe—dead now these two years—calling him *our Ansel* in front of the vicar's wife, in front of everyone, as if the word *our* cost her nothing at all.

If something hidden in this clock concerned the Whitcombes—and he could not yet say that it did, he told himself firmly, he could not say anything of the kind—but if it did, then leaving it in the dark would be a kind of theft. Worse than theft. A theft of knowing.

He lay down full-length on the canvas, raised the lamp above his head, and worked two fingers into the gap.

The packet was stiff and cold and lighter than he expected. It came free with a whisper of friction, and for one irrational moment he imagined the whole tower inhaling around him—the building itself noticing, the way a sleeper notices a change in the weight of the bed. He lay still for a long moment, listening. Above him the dome creaked as the temperature fell, and somewhere far below, the sea said what the sea always said. Nothing else.

He sat up cross-legged and turned the packet over in the light.

It was addressed. Not formally—not with the direction of a letter meant for a post office—but inscribed, in a small, upright, careful hand, ink gone brownish with age:

**For E. W., when the clock is opened.**

E. W. Edmund Whitcombe. Or Edward—there had been an Edward Whitcombe in the parish registers, an uncle long drowned. But Edmund first, always Edmund first, because it was Edmund who had taken in a half-Japanese boy with no family and no prospects and fed him roast mutton as though feeding him were the most ordinary act in England.

And below the inscription, smaller, almost an afterthought, in the same hand:

**Whitcombe — and let them hang together.**

Ansel read it twice. Three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into kindness upon rereading. *Let them hang together.* Plural. Them. Hang.

The seal was wax, thick, unbroken, stamped with a device he did not recognize—a bird of some kind, its wings folded, rendered crudely as though by someone working from memory or from a stolen impression. He turned it under the lamp. The wax was old. Not ancient—it had not crumbled, had not gone the color of dried blood—but old. Two years, perhaps three. Around the time Mrs. Whitcombe had died. Around the time the harbor trade had begun its slow strangulation, if the grumbling he overheard at the fish market meant anything.

He should open it. Of course he should open it. Any sensible person would open it; the instruction on the face of it was addressed to Edmund, and Ansel was not Edmund, and the second line was plainly criminal, and a sealed letter bearing a threat against the family that had fed him had no business remaining sealed while he sat alone in a tower with it all night.

But his father's voice spoke in him then, quiet and level as it always was in memory: *A sealed thing belongs to whoever sealed it until it is opened properly. Steam, Ansel. Never tear. If a thing must be read before its time, read it so it can be sealed again.*

His father had been a man of strange ethics, part Japanese rigor and part English stubbornness, and half of his maxims had seemed like superstition to the boy Ansel had been. But this one he had demonstrated a hundred times at the workbench—steaming open a client's sealed bill of lading to check a declared weight, then sealing it again so cleanly the merchant never knew. His father had called it *honest snooping*, and had laughed his dry laugh, and Ansel had learned the trick at twelve years old without once thinking he would need it for anything heavier than dishonest weights.

His kit was in the corner of the instrument room. He went down for it quietly, though there was no one to hear—he had developed the habit of quietness so early in life that it no longer felt like a choice—and returned with the kettle Maren had left, cold water still standing in it, and a spirit burner from the same shelf. The observatory keeper's stores were generous; astronomers, apparently, were great ones for tea at odd hours.

He set the packet on a clean cloth, boiled the water, and held the envelope in the steam, rotating it slowly, counting under his breath the way his father had taught him—not seconds but heartbeats, because a heartbeat was always with you even when clocks were not. Thirty heartbeats. Forty. The wax softened at its edge, loosened its grip on the paper with a reluctance he could feel through his fingertips, a tiny resistance like a sleeping thing being shifted.

Fifty-five. Sixty. He slipped a thin bone folder—the one he used for seating dial feet—under the flap, and the seal lifted whole, intact, perfect. The wax came away like a scab that had never truly healed.

Inside: three pages, closely written, both sides, in that same small upright hand. Ansel smoothed them on his knee beneath the lamp and began to read.

The first page was numbers. Dates, tonnages, marks—a ledger in miniature, entries running back across months and years. He recognized the format immediately; he had spent enough evenings copying out Garrick's books to know a shipping manifest's grammar when he saw it, even abbreviated, even coded. Vessels named only by initial. Cargo named only by weight. And beside certain entries, small ticks in a different ink—a darker ink, added later, he judged—which appeared beside roughly one entry in five.

The second page was worse. It was instructions, or near enough. Times of tide. Names of men—one of them, Ansel felt his stomach tilt, was a name he knew from the customs house, a name spoken with respect in the town. Phrases like *the usual arrangement at the pier* and *he will want his share raised after Michaelmas*. And then, in the margin of the third page, written larger, angrier, the pen having pressed hard enough to emboss the paper beneath:

**W. has grown frightened. If he will not come in willingly, see that he is in it regardless. The papers can say what we need them to say. Let them hang together, or none of us hangs at all.**

Ansel lowered the pages.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The tower was silent except for the small sounds of stone cooling, and the sea, and his own breathing, which had become audible to him in the way breathing does when a man forgets how to do it automatically.

*W.* Edmund Whitcombe. *See that he is in it regardless. The papers can say what we need them to say.*

Not evidence of guilt. Evidence of a frame. Someone—several someones—moving contraband through this harbor under arrangements that reached into the customs house itself, and Edmund Whitcombe had either refused them or frightened them or simply known too much, and so they had decided to make him guilty on paper. To bury him in entries and ticks and initials. *Let them hang together.* All the Whitcombes, presumably. A whole family, weighed and dropped, because one man would not bend.

And the letter had been left here. Hidden here. Behind the escapement of the observatory clock, of all the hiding places in Christendom—hidden by whom, and for whom, and why had the clock been stopped?

He sat with that for a long time, the pages trembling faintly in his grip, and the pieces began to arrange themselves with the terrible click of a mechanism coming together. The gear had been removed to stop the clock. The clock had been stopped to summon a clockmaker. Summon a clockmaker—*any* clockmaker—and the letter would be found. Found by whoever came, read or unread, and carried out of this tower into daylight. Perhaps carried to the authorities by an honest tradesman. Perhaps carried straight into a trap laid for whoever touched it.

Or—his blood went colder—perhaps the summons had been arranged with a particular clockmaker in mind. Garrick had been summoned, first, before Ansel. Garrick had pleaded his knee and sent his apprentice instead, and Ansel had seen, for just a moment, something in his master's face as he gave the commission—something too quick to name, gone before it arrived.

No. He shut the thought. He was tired, that was all, and tired men saw patterns in spilled salt.

But the letter could not stay here, and it could not stay in his head alone, and it could not—this he understood with total certainty—go to the authorities. Not yet. Not first. Because the names on these pages reached into the customs house, and a half-Japanese apprentice walking into a customs house with accusations against a respected family would be laughed out of doors at best and clapped in irons at worst. They already watched him. They already murmured. He had spent three years learning to walk through those murmurs as though he could not hear them, and he knew with a clarity that felt like falling exactly what would happen if he handed this letter to the wrong man.

It goes to Edmund, he thought. First, before anyone. Edmund, tonight, tomorrow, whatever hour allows it. Let him see what is being built against him. Let him decide.

He refolded the pages along their original creases, pressed the wax seal back onto the flap with the warm handle of the bone folder until it took again, seamless, and slid the whole packet deep into the roll of his tools, between the boxwood mallet and the spare mainsprings, where it made no lump, no line, no sign. Then he sat back on the canvas and looked at the escapement, gaping and innocent above him, and understood that he had just become part of the mechanism. A small gear in someone else's train. The difference between him and the missing wheel was that he intended to choose which way he turned.

He worked until nearly four. The new gear went in sweetly—he had brought three candidates and the middle one seated like a word completing a sentence—and when he set the pendulum swinging at last, the great clock gathered itself out of silence beat by beat, tick and tock filling the tower like a heart remembering its work. He regulated it against his own pocket watch, corrected the hands, closed the case door gently, and latched it.

Dawn was still an hour off when Maren climbed up with bread and tea wrapped in a cloth, her hair pinned hastily, her eyes finding his face at once with that unguarded attention of hers.

"You've been at it all night."

"It's done," he said. "She'll keep time now."

"Then why," said Maren Whitcombe, setting down the cloth and looking at him the way she looked at instruments she suspected of lying, "do you look like a man carrying something heavy down a mountain?"

Ansel smiled, and heard how much effort the smile cost, and hated that she would hear it too.

"I'm hungry," he said. "Is that bread for sharing?"

But as they descended the ninety-nine steps together, her footsteps light above his, the letter rode against his ribs in its wrapping of oilcloth and lies of omission, and he was already composing the words he would need—*Mr. Whitcombe, sir, I found something in the clock, and I think you should be the first to see it*—and wondering whether he would manage to say any of them aloud, or whether the whole truth would stick in his throat like a swallowed gear, all the way to Harbor Row.
