## 4. Supper at Harbor Row

Maren had known since the clock stopped that something in Ansel Nakamura had gone wrong, though she could not have said what. It was not the way his hands moved—those were as steady as ever, patient over small mechanisms, sure of themselves in the way that only true skill is sure—but everything else about him had gone quiet in a new way. Ansel was always quiet; she had learned to read him the way one reads a tide chart, by what was absent rather than present. And tonight, walking down from the observatory with him through lanes going blue with dusk, there was an absence in him so large it seemed he might tip into it.

"You'll stay for supper," she said. Not a question.

"I shouldn't."

"You should. Father's been asking after you for a fortnight. He wants to know whether you've grown taller or whether we've all simply shrunk." She glanced at him sidelong. He did not smile. That alone told her more than any confession might have.

The Whitcombe house stood at the crook of Harbor Row where the street bent away from the water, a narrow gray house with a green door and a window box Maren neglected faithfully every summer. Smoke rose from its chimney now, and through the kitchen window she could see her brother Tom already setting the table with his elbows, which meant her mother was out and Tom was doing it wrong. The ordinary sight loosened something in her chest—and then tightened it again when she saw how Ansel paused at the gate, one hand on the latch, gathering himself as if the house were a checkpoint rather than a home.

"Ansel." She said his name plainly, the way her father said it. "You've eaten here a hundred times."

"A hundred and four," he said quietly. "I count."

Of course he counted. He was a clockmaker's apprentice; counting was his native language. She pushed the gate open and let him follow her in.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of onions and bay leaf and the smoked herring Edmund had traded for at the fish market that morning, and it smelled, underneath all of that, of bread and lamp oil and the particular warm dust of a house full of boys. Maren's brothers were Tom, seventeen and loud, and Wren, twelve and louder, and between them they filled the room the way weather fills a valley.

"Ansel!" Tom bellowed it as if announcing a ship coming into harbor. "Sit down before Wren takes your chair. He takes everyone's chair. It's a disease."

"It's strategy," Wren said, already halfway into Ansel's usual place at the end of the bench.

Ansel sat, and the strange tension in his shoulders eased by a degree—not gone, but banked, like a fire raked down for the night. Maren watched him do it, watched him fold himself into the family's noise the way a man folds himself into a coat that almost fits.

Her father came in from the yard with his sleeves rolled and sawdust on his forearms—he'd been re-caulking the boathouse door—and his whole face changed when he saw who sat at his table.

"There he is." Edmund Whitcombe crossed the kitchen and put his hand on Ansel's shoulder, brief and firm, the way he touched things he intended to keep. "Maren said the observatory clock had swallowed you whole. I told her clocks don't eat people, but she wouldn't have it."

"The clock didn't eat me," Ansel said. "It only kept me."

"Kept you late, I'll wager." Edmund dropped onto the bench, and the meal began around them the way meals began in that house: all at once, without ceremony, dishes passed hand to hand, Tom talking with his mouth full, Wren stealing potatoes off Ansel's plate with the impunity of long practice.

"Your master let you off, then?" Edmund asked. "Garrick's not one for generosity, from what I hear."

"He had no choice. The observatory sent for me directly." Ansel turned his spoon in his stew without eating much of it. "The master clock failed. A gear was missing."

Maren saw her father's eyebrows rise. "Missing? Slipped its pin, you mean."

"No." Ansel's voice was level, but Maren heard the effort beneath it, the way one hears the strain in a rope. "Removed. Deliberately. Someone took the escapement gear out of the case and closed it up again."

The table went quiet for exactly two seconds, which in the Whitcombe household was an eternity.

"Someone broke into the observatory?" Tom demanded.

"Took a part out of a clock and left everything else?" Wren added, delighted by the absurdity. "Why not take the silver? There's silver up there. Old silver."

"Why indeed," Edmund said slowly. He was looking at Ansel with the particular attention he gave to charts and weather glass—the attention of a man reading signs. "And what does Mr. Pellow say, the observatory keeper?"

"That it must have been vibration. Or age. Or a careless apprentice." Ansel's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "He was not pleased to be told otherwise."

"And you told him otherwise."

"I told him the truth. The wear marks on the arbor show the gear was pulled after the last oiling. Within days. Perhaps hours before the clock stopped." He looked down at his plate. "But a half-Japanese apprentice's word carries less weight than a keeper's wish for a simple explanation."

The bitterness was so faintly stated that anyone less familiar with him would have missed it entirely. Maren did not miss it. Neither did her father.

"Your word," Edmund said, "has carried weight at this table since you were eleven years old and told me my barometer was lying about the storm. You were right then. I'd lay money you're right now." He reached across and tapped the table once, a gesture that ended discussions. "We'll not speak ill of it further while there's supper to eat. Tom, pass the bread before your brother inhales it."

The noise resumed. But Maren kept watching Ansel.

* * *

After supper the boys went out to the yard to argue over a kite frame Tom was building, their voices carrying through the open window along with the smell of the sea and the first cool of the evening. Edmund lit his pipe and settled into his chair by the fire, content to smoke and listen to the house settle. Maren washed dishes; Ansel dried, because he always dried, because drying required no conversation and he had never yet been able to resist making himself useful.

They worked side by side in a silence that was comfortable on her side and, she suspected, anything but on his.

"You barely touched the herring," she said. "You love herring. You once told Tom you'd trade your left hand for smoked herring."

"I said I'd consider it."

"Mm." She handed him a plate, watched him wipe it dry with those careful hands. "Something happened up at the observatory besides the clock."

His hands did not stop moving. That was the thing about Ansel—his hands were incapable of betraying him. But his breath caught, just slightly, a hitch small enough to hide behind the sound of water in the basin. She would not have heard it from anyone else. From him, it was as loud as a bell.

"Nothing happened."

"You count things," she said. "You told me. So count this: you arrived at our gate looking like a man walking to his own trial. You sat through supper without tasting it. You laughed at Tom's story about the goat—" the goat story was unfailingly funny, even Maren laughed at it, "—one second too late, which means you weren't listening, which means you were somewhere else entirely. And when Father mentioned the missing gear, you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched the way a clock ticks. Small, regular, invisible unless you're watching for it." She set the last dish in the rack and dried her hands, and turned to face him fully. "I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm telling you that I see you. Whatever it is—it's heavy, and you've been carrying it since the observatory, and you are carrying it alone, which is the part I mind most."

For a moment he stood very still, plate in hand, lamplight catching the fine bones of his face. He looked, she thought, like a boy standing at a window deciding whether to open it.

"Maren." His voice came out low. "If I told you something that put your family in danger—"

"Then you'd tell my father, not me, because my father has faced danger before and I have only ever read about it." She held his gaze. "But whatever it is, Ansel—you don't have to decide tonight. Just know the door here opens from the inside. Nobody in this house will ever lock you out of it."

Something moved through his expression then, quick as a shadow under water—gratitude and grief and fear braided together, too fast for her to name any strand of it. He set the plate down with a soft click.

"Thank you," he said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it.

* * *

Edmund walked him to the gate afterward, pipe still lit, the two of them framed against the last gray light over the harbor. Maren stayed at the window and watched them talk—watched her father say something quiet, watched Ansel nod twice, watched Edmund grip the boy's arm above the elbow in the way he gripped rigging in bad weather. Then Ansel went down the lane toward town, toward Garrick's shop and whatever waited for him there, and his figure grew smaller against the darkening street until the corner took him.

Edmund came back inside slowly, shaking out his match.

"Well?" Maren asked.

"Well," he said, sitting down heavily, drawing on his pipe, "that boy knows something. Something with teeth in it." He exhaled smoke toward the rafters. "I asked him plain if he was in trouble. He said no. Then he said—" Edmund's eyes narrowed, recalling the words exactly, because Edmund recalled everything exactly, "—he said, *not yet, sir.*"

"Not yet," Maren repeated.

"Not yet." Her father stared into the fire, and the lines of his face deepened, and Maren understood suddenly that he had seen the same thing she had, and perhaps more. "I've known men carry trouble like that. Sailors mostly. They walk steady and eat little and listen too hard, and then one morning they're gone, or someone else is." He looked up at her. "If he comes to us, Maren—if he comes to us—we stand with him. Whatever it costs. Do you understand?"

"Yes," she said. She found she did not need to think about it at all.

Upstairs, later, she heard Tom and Wren quarreling sleepily over the kite, and the sea working at the harbor wall below, and beneath all of it the great silence of the town, the observatory dark on its hill with its broken heart of a clock. She thought of Ansel walking alone through those streets with whatever he carried pressed against him, invisible as always, unseen as always—and she made herself a promise, quiet and absolute, in the dark of her small room:

She would find out what it was. And whatever it cost him to keep silent, she would make certain he did not have to keep it alone.

She fell asleep before she could know how soon that promise would be tested—or how heavy the thing he carried would prove to bear the name *Whitcombe*.
