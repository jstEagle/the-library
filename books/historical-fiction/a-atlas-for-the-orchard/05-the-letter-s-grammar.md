## 5. The Letter's Grammar

The attic room above the shop was six feet by eight and had been Ansel Nakamura's entire world for three years—narrow bed, washstand, a shelf of borrowed books, and the one window that looked out over the tiled roofs of the town toward a thin grey line of sea. On most nights he was asleep by ten, his body trained to the rhythms of Garrick's shop the way a pendulum is trained to its escapement: swing, catch, release, swing again.

Tonight he sat on the edge of the bed with his boots still on and the letter in his lap, and he could not make himself move.

He had carried it down from the observatory inside his tool roll, wedged between the pin-vice and the oiler, and every step of those ninety-nine stairs he had been aware of it the way one is aware of a splinter driven deep under the skin—not painful, exactly, but *present*, insisting on itself with every movement. The walk home through the dark lanes had been worse. Twice he had heard footsteps behind him and stopped to let them pass, his heart going like a carriage clock wound too tight. Both times it had been nothing. A drunk finding his way home from the Anchor. A cat on a wall.

Nothing. And yet everything in him insisted otherwise.

The letter lay where he had set it on his knee: a rectangle of good paper, folded twice, sealed with plain wax that had gone amber in the lamplight. No crest. No initials pressed into the seal. Whoever had made it had not wanted to be known—and yet they had written the Whitcombe name in their own hand, three times at least, because Ansel had seen it through the paper when he'd held it up against the lamp before leaving the observatory. He had stood there in the dead clock's housing, the brass smell of the mechanism all around him, and read the name *Whitcombe* backwards through the page, mirrored and faint, like something seen underwater.

Edmund Whitcombe, who had given Ansel's father work when no one else would. Edmund Whitcombe, who had paid for the funeral when Ansel could not. Edmund Whitcombe, whose daughter had handed him a mug of tea that evening as though he were any other tradesman, as though his half-blood were a fact of no more interest than the colour of his hair.

*They would hang him for this,* Ansel thought, and did not know whether he meant Edmund or himself.

He looked at the letter for a long time. The lamp hissed softly. Below him, through the floorboards, came the sounds of Garrick closing the shop: the till drawer running shut, the bolt thrown on the back door, the particular scrape of the master's stool being pushed beneath the bench. Then footsteps crossing the shop, pausing—Ansel held his breath—at the foot of the attic stair. A long moment. Then the footsteps retreated, and the house settled into silence.

Garrick always paused at the foot of the stair. Ansel had never understood why until tonight. Tonight it felt like a hand laid flat against a door, testing whether it was locked.

He rose quietly and went to the washstand. The jug of water was nearly full; he poured some into the basin and then set the kettle he kept for tea onto the small spirit burner in the corner. While it heated he turned his tools out of the roll onto the bed and took the letter up last, weighing it in his palm like something that might still be alive.

His father had taught him to read seals. It was one of the small magics of the trade, passed across the dinner table in a low voice while the shop below rang with Garrick's temper: *steam lifts wax if you are patient; heat breaks it if you are not.* His father had learned it in Yokohama, in the counting-house where he'd worked before the world turned and carried him to England, and he had taught it to Ansel not for any purpose but the pleasure of knowing a thing few others knew. *A seal is only wax,* his father used to say, smiling. *Wax remembers what is done to it. You must simply ask politely.*

Ansel had never once used the trick. It had lived in him the way the Japanese words of his childhood lived in him—kept, unused, half-ashamed of themselves. Now he held the letter over the kettle's rising steam, and his hands were steadier than he expected them to be.

The wax warmed slowly. He turned the letter, letting the vapour curl against the amber seal, watching it soften from the edges inward, and he found himself thinking, absurdly, of the clock upstairs—the way a mechanism must be coaxed rather than forced, the way a seized pivot will snap if you bring brute strength to bear but will turn free if you give it oil and patience and time. Everything in his life came back to clocks. Perhaps that was why he could do this. Because he knew, better than most men, how to be gentle with things that would break.

After five minutes the seal lifted at one corner. He worked it with the flat of a watchmaker's knife, easing the wax away from the paper fibre by fibre, and when it came free whole—still bearing its shape, still recognisably a seal—he set it on the washstand like a small sleeping animal and unfolded the letter with fingers that had begun, at last, to tremble.

The handwriting was cramped and slanted, the hand of someone accustomed to writing quickly and in poor light. The ink had faded unevenly, as though the letter had been written some years ago and left waiting. Ansel bent close to the lamp and read.

*To whom it may concern at the Customs & Revenue,*

*Be it known that I write these lines under compulsion of conscience and not for gain.*

It began the way all such letters began, Ansel supposed—there was a grammar to accusations, just as there was a grammar to clocks, and this one followed its pattern faithfully. Self-righteousness first. Reluctance performed at length. Then, at last, the substance:

*I can no longer hold my peace regarding the traffic moved through this harbour under colour of legitimate trade. Edmund Whitcombe of Harbor Row is principal among those engaged therein. It is Whitcombe's boats that carry the goods, Whitcombe's cellar that stores them, Whitcombe's standing in this town that shields the enterprise from scrutiny. I have myself seen coin pass from his hand to men now beyond the reach of honest law.*

Ansel read the paragraph twice. The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about ink—it stayed put, patient as a spider, no matter how many times you asked it to mean something else.

He made himself go on.

*Further, I warn the Board that its own house is not clean. There sits within the customs house at [here the word was blotted, or perhaps deliberately scraped] an informant who carries warning of each inspection to the ring before the officers' boats put out. Until that man—I say man advisedly—is named and taken, no seizure will succeed and no conviction stand.*

There was more. Dates—dates Ansel did not recognise, seven and eight years old. Names, some half-familiar from gravestones and shipping notices: *Pell, Marchmont, the younger Hale.* A passage about a brig called the *Cormorant* that had, so far as Ansel knew, been lost with all hands before he ever came to this town. And at the bottom, unsigned, a final line written in a different pressure of pen—a hand pressing harder, hurrying:

*Deliver this to the Board and let justice have her day, though it cost me everything.*

Ansel sat back on the bed. The room seemed very cold, though the kettle still breathed steam into the corner.

*Though it cost me everything.* Whoever had written this had believed it. Or had wanted whoever found it to believe it. Those were not the same thing, and Ansel had spent three years learning to tell the difference between mechanisms that ran true and mechanisms that had been made to look as though they ran true.

He read it a third time, and this time he read it the way his father had taught him to read a movement—not the whole, not the impression, but the parts. Where the file marks ran. Which screw had been turned most recently. What did not belong.

The dates were wrong.

Not wrong in themselves—they were consistent, ordered, plausible. But they were wrong the way a replaced gear is wrong: correct in every dimension except the one that mattered. Ansel had spent the evening working on the observatory's master clock. He knew its age, and therefore he knew the age of its records, because Edmund Whitcombe had shown him the log-books, proudly, while the tea went cold between them. And one date in this letter—only one, buried in the middle of the list—described a shipment landed on a night when Ansel knew, absolutely, that no boat had come into the harbour at all, because the storm of the fourteenth of November had driven every vessel in the fleet onto the beach at Marsden and kept them there for a week. Everyone in the town knew it. It was the kind of night that became a story, told and retold. The *Providence*, the *Dolly Varden*, the Whitcombes' own *Kestrel*—all ashore, all battered, all accounted for.

No cargo had moved that night. Nothing had.

And yet here it was in black ink, entered in this letter like a gear cut to fit, dropped into the train to make the whole mechanism appear sound.

Ansel sat very still. Outside, the wind had come up off the sea and was moving through the town, setting shutters ticking against their frames like a hundred small clocks all losing time together. Downstairs, faintly, he could hear Garrick snoring.

*Someone wrote this to be found,* he thought. *Someone hid it where it would be found by the right person at the right time, and they built it out of true things and false ones so that no single flaw would show unless you already knew the truth of that November night.*

And who would know? Only people of the town. Only people who had been here in 1905. Only someone close enough to the trade to know which names would carry weight—which name, spoken to a customs officer or a magistrate, would land hardest.

*Whitcombe.*

Not a fisherman's name. Not a name that could be ruined quietly. The Whitcombes owned boats and standing and the goodwill of half the harbor row; Edmund Whitcombe's word was trusted by the very customs men the letter claimed to serve. If you wanted to destroy the smuggling trade—or better, to seize control of it—you could do no finer thing than hang it about Edmund Whitcombe's neck.

And if the letter named an informant inside the customs house—well. What better way to set honest men hunting each other than to bury one true accusation among nine false ones? Perhaps there *was* an informer. Perhaps there wasn't. Either way, every officer in the district would now be looking sideways at his colleagues, and no one would be looking at the men who had commissioned this letter in the first place.

It was not evidence of guilt. Ansel understood that now with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like frost—the slow, silent arrival of cold that turns a wet rope stiff overnight. It was evidence of *intent*. Someone had wanted the Whitcombes blamed, wanted it badly enough to forge a document, age the ink, hide it in the one place guaranteed to draw a careful pair of eyes: the case of the observatory's master clock, which failed—no, which *had been failed*, a gear deliberately drawn—so that a clockmaker would be sent for.

So that *he* would be sent for.

The thought arrived whole and terrible. The removed gear had not been sabotage for its own sake. It had been bait on a hook, and Ansel had swallowed it with both hands. Whoever had done this had known the observatory would summon Garrick's shop. Had known Garrick would send the apprentice, because the apprentice was cheap and the job was beneath the master's dignity. Had known—or guessed—that the boy with the foreign face would find the letter and be afraid, too afraid to go to the authorities, too afraid even to speak of it, and would either destroy it in terror or carry it away quietly where no one could trace how it had travelled.

Either outcome served them. Either the letter vanished and the plot waited for another day, or the letter surfaced from *his* possession—from the half-Japanese apprentice nobody would believe, nobody would defend—and the Whitcombes fell, and the boy fell with them, and the harbor changed hands.

*They chose me because I am invisible,* Ansel thought, *and because I am deniable.*

The lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick with fingers that no longer shook—there was a strange steadiness that comes after fear has gone as deep as it can go, a cold orderliness, the same steadiness he felt when a movement finally yielded its fault to him after hours of searching. He folded the letter along its original creases, fitted the softened wax back over the fold, and pressed it gently with his thumb until it held. Not perfect. Close enough in the dark.

Then he did not hide it in the tool roll again. He considered the loose board under the window, the gap behind the shelf, the false bottom of his father's old chest—and rejected them all. Garrick's eye missed nothing in this house; the master had walked past Ansel's open toolbox a thousand times and could say what lay in it. Whatever place Ansel chose, Garrick would find eventually, because Garrick had found every hiding place Ansel had ever used since he was fourteen years old, and had never once told him so, only smiled a certain smile at breakfast the following morning.

So Ansel did the one thing no searcher believes: he wrapped the letter in a scrap of chamois, tucked it into the roll of cleaning cloths that sat openly on the shelf beside the lathe, and covered it with the oily rags every clockmaker keeps and no one ever disturbs. Hidden in plain sight. His father again: *the best place to keep a thing is where everyone can see it and no one can look.*

He put out the lamp and lay down without undressing, and the cold clarity did not leave him. If anything it sharpened as his eyes adjusted to the dark, the way details emerge from a dial when the light is right.

Because one question remained, and it was worse than all the others.

The letter named an informant inside the customs house. The letter was forged—or mostly forged—by someone with knowledge of the harbor's trade, its boats, its storms, its men. Someone who could enter the observatory unnoticed, who understood clocks well enough to strip a gear from the master and know precisely what would follow. Someone patient, meticulous, familiar with forgery and with fear.

Ansel thought of the ledger he was required to keep as part of his apprenticeship—the daily record of commissions, repairs, collections, deliveries. Thought of entries he had made at Garrick's dictation without ever quite understanding them. Odd errands. Packages collected from the pier and delivered to addresses in the next town. Money received in amounts that never matched the work.

Seven years ago, the dates in the letter said. Seven years ago, Ansel had been fourteen, newly arrived, barely able to speak English in public without drawing stares. But Garrick had been here. Garrick had been here for thirty years.

The snoring below continued, steady, untroubled, and Ansel lay awake above it in the dark, listening to his master breathe, and understood that tomorrow he would have to walk down those stairs and stand across the bench from the man who might have written the letter in his pocket, and say *good morning, sir*, and mean nothing by it.

He would have to look at the ledger.

* * *

Morning came grey and early. Ansel was at the bench before Garrick descended, sleeves rolled, a disassembled carriage clock spread before him with the appearance of industry. The letter rode against his ribs inside his waistcoat, sewn hastily into the lining during the small hours with stitches his father had taught him for securing hair-springs to their cocks—tiny, invisible, strong.

Garrick came down at his usual hour, paused at the foot of the attic stair longer than usual, and emerged into the shop rubbing his hands.

"Cold morning," he said pleasantly. "You were late down from the observatory."

"The master clock wanted a full reassembly, sir. The third wheel's pivot was scored. I'll need to cut a new one."

"Mm." Garrick poured himself tea from the pot on the stove, and his small eyes moved over the bench, over the carriage clock, over Ansel's rolled sleeves, unhurried, thorough, the inspection of a man checking a movement for faults. "And the old case. Cleaned it out properly?"

Ansel kept his gaze on the pivot polish. "Swept it, sir."

"Good." Garrick drank his tea standing, looking out the front window at the street, where the first customers of the day were beginning to pass. Then, mildly, almost kindly: "Mind you sweep everywhere, lad. Old cases collect all sorts of rubbish. Papers. Dust. Things best forgotten."

"Yes, sir," said Ansel, and his voice did not break, and his hands did not stop, and only the new stitches against his ribs knew how hard both cost him.

When Garrick turned to the ledger, opened it, and licked his thumb to turn back through pages Ansel had never been allowed to touch alone, the apprentice reached for his loupe, bent over the carriage clock, and began, with perfect steadiness, to work.

That afternoon, when Garrick went out to call on a customer, Ansel unlocked the ledger with the key kept in the master's coat pocket—copied in wax, months ago, for reasons he had not then understood.
