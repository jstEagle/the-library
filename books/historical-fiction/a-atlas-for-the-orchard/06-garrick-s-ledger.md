## 6. Garrick's Ledger

The shop smelled of oil and brass and the particular dust that gathers in the teeth of old gears—Ansel had known no other smell for three years, not since his father died and Garrick Fenwick took him in as apprentice, half out of charity and half out of the cheapness of a boy who asked for nothing. He knew the smell so well he had stopped noticing it, the way one stops noticing a limp or an ache. Tonight, standing alone behind the counter with the lamp turned low and Garrick gone to the Anchor & Crown for his evening pint, the smell seemed suddenly foreign. Hostile, even. As though the shop itself were holding its breath.

He had told himself he would not do this.

He had told himself it all through supper at Harbor Row, all the way back down the hill, all through the long grey hour of pretending to true up a carriage clock while his hands shook too badly to hold the tweezers steady. The letter sat where he had hidden it—in the false bottom of his tool roll, beneath the broaches and screwdrivers, wrapped in a chamois cloth like something diseased. It had been there for two days now, and for two days it had done what such things do: grown heavier without changing weight at all.

*Who wrote you?* he thought at it. *And why did they hide you inside a clock?*

A letter does not simply appear wedged behind the escapement of the observatory master clock. Someone had opened that case deliberately—someone who understood the mechanism well enough to know which panel would never be disturbed, who could remove a gear and reseal the housing and trust that no one would look inside until the clock failed. And when it finally failed, as clocks do, the repair would fall to whoever kept such knowledge in this town.

It would fall to Garrick. Or to Garrick's boy.

That was the thought that had been sitting on his chest like a stone since supper. Because if someone had wanted the letter found by a clockmaker's hands—and only a clockmaker's hands—then either they trusted the clockmaker, or they intended to use him.

He set down the tweezers. He listened to the street outside: a cart passing, the gulls quarrelling over the fish waste behind the smokehouse, Mrs. Pellowe next door putting out her cat with her usual litany of complaint. Ordinary sounds. A town going about its evening, innocent of him.

Then he went to the ledger.

Garrick kept it under the counter, bound in green calf leather gone soft and bald at the corners, its spine cracked from thirty years of daily use. Every commission, every part ordered, every shilling taken in or paid out—it lived there in Garrick's narrow, upright hand, a hand like fence posts driven into the page. Ansel had seen it ten thousand times. He had copied figures from it. He had been beaten once—he still remembered the sting across his knuckles—for smudging an entry with an oily thumb.

He had never once read it as anything other than a book of accounts.

Now he laid it open under the lamp and began, carefully, to read it as a book of lies.

He started with the dates. That much of the letter was burned into him now; he had read it so many times he could recite it with his eyes shut—the smuggling runs listed by month, contraband moving through the harbor on specific nights, sums paid, names coded but not quite concealed, and the warning about an informant inside the customs house. Edmund Whitcombe's name appeared four times, each occurrence tied to a date. Four nights, over eighteen months, when the letter claimed Whitcombe boats had carried more than herring.

Ansel turned the ledger's pages backward through the years, past commissions for the church clock, past repairs for the harbormaster himself, past the endless small transactions of a small town's small business. He found nothing at first, and felt a foolish relief rise in him like a bubble—and then, in the entries for October of the year before last, he found the first one.

*A. Whitcombe — regulator, overhaul — 14th Oct.*

Edmund's initials. A regulator brought in for overhaul on the fourteenth of October.

There was nothing strange in that. Edmund owned a regulator; every household of any substance did. But Ansel remembered—because he remembered everything about the work that came through this shop—that no regulator had come in that autumn. He had catalogued the incoming repairs himself that week; it had been a thin season, mostly watch glasses and mainsprings. No regulator. No overhaul.

Yet there it was in the ledger, dated, initialled, and—when he checked the facing column—a sum received. Two pounds, ten shillings. Paid and pocketed for work that had never existed.

His mouth had gone dry. He turned the page.

*E. Whitcombe — carriage clock, new escapement — 3rd Dec.*

He remembered December. He remembered it precisely, because it had been the week of the frost fair on the ice pond, and Maren had dragged him and her brothers out to watch the skaters, and Edmund had stayed home with a cold. There had been no carriage clock. Ansel had swept the bench that whole week; he would have swept it into his memory if a carriage clock had sat upon it.

Another sum. Another payment for phantom work.

Four entries. Four. He counted them twice, then a third time with his finger under each line, because his eyes refused at first to accept what his finger insisted upon. Four fictitious commissions from Edmund Whitcombe, spread across eighteen months—and each one dated within a day or two of the smuggling runs named in the letter. Not before them, not after them. *On* them. The ledger didn't merely echo the letter. It corroborated it.

Someone had built a second record. A shadow account. If the letter ever surfaced—if the authorities ever came asking—here, in a respectable tradesman's book, kept in an honest hand over honest years, was the paper proof that Edmund Whitcombe had been paying a local man for services rendered on exactly those nights. The letter accused. The ledger confirmed. Between them, they would hang a rope around the Whitcombe name that no amount of good character could cut.

And the ledger lived here. In *this* shop. Under *this* counter.

In Garrick's hand.

Ansel stood very still. The lamp hissed softly, the way lamps do when the wick wants trimming, and the sound seemed suddenly enormous in the silence. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked as the house cooled, and he flinched from it as though a hand had touched his shoulder.

No. It wasn't proof. He made himself say it inside his own head, slowly, like a man talking himself down off a roof. Entries in a ledger proved nothing by themselves. Ledgers held mistakes, misremembered jobs, jobs recorded in advance and never collected. Garrick was careless about many things—about the boy himself most of all—but he was not careless about money, and money was all these entries were. Perhaps someone had paid Garrick to record them. Perhaps Garrick had accepted the money the way he accepted everything: greedily, incuriously, without ever asking what it was for.

That was the kinder explanation. Ansel reached for it the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood.

But his hands, which had never once disobeyed him at the bench, were already turning pages. Deeper. Further back. Past the false entries into the ordinary country of the ledger, looking—not consciously at first, but with the instinct of a craftsman examining another man's work—for the telltale marks of forgery.

He found them.

The ink was wrong. Subtly, but wrong. The four entries had been written with a slightly different pen—or rather, with the same pen held by a different pressure. Garrick bore down hard when he wrote; the nib bit into the paper, left grooves you could feel with a fingertip, and his ink always feathered a little at the ends of strokes because he pressed too long at each finish. These entries sat on the surface of the page. Light. Practiced. Written by someone imitating Garrick's hand, and imitating it very well indeed—well enough to fool a magistrate, though not well enough, thank God, to fool a boy who had spent three years studying that hand because his master's corrections were the only writing ever addressed to him.

He ran his fingertip along the fourth entry, feeling the smooth absence of groove, and something in his chest went quiet and cold, the way the sea goes calm before it turns.

Then he found the dates themselves—the real ones, underneath.

Because there was one more thing. The entries had been added to pages already written. On the facing columns, the genuine transactions of those weeks continued in their proper order, but the false entries had been squeezed into gaps—small spaces left between legitimate lines, spaces just wide enough for a single line of text. No honest accountant leaves such perfect gaps. Gaps like that are left *deliberately*, in advance, by a man planning what will fill them. Which meant whoever wrote the false entries had known the ledger intimately. Had known its rhythm, its habits, its empty spaces.

Had perhaps written in it before.

Ansel closed the ledger. Then, after a moment, opened it again, because closing it felt like cowardice, and cowardice had already cost him two days.

He worked backward through the years now with a new eye. And once he knew what to look for, he saw it everywhere—or rather, he saw the architecture of it everywhere: the little reserved spaces, the occasional surface-light line in an otherwise grooved page. Not often. Once or twice a year at most, scattered like stones across a riverbed, invisible unless you were wading. He found one from three years ago. One from five. Each attached, when he cross-checked against the shipping notices posted at the harbor office—which he had begun memorizing two nights ago without fully understanding why—to a night when some vessel had come in or gone out at an unusual hour.

This had not begun last year. This had been running, quietly, patiently, for as long as Ansel had been in this town. Possibly longer. A slow machinery, turning in the dark behind the face of an ordinary shop, keeping its own kind of time.

And at the center of it, winding it, the man whose bed lay directly above Ansel's cot.

*He sold us,* Ansel thought, and the word *us* surprised even him—because he meant the Whitcombes, meant Edmund and Thomas and young Peter and Maren, meant the table where he'd eaten supper two nights ago and been handed bread like a brother. But he also, treacherously, meant himself. Because whatever else Garrick Fenwick was, he was the man who had given a grieving half-Japanese boy a cot and a trade when the town's charity extended no further than its pity. Three years of cold porridge and colder silence, yes—but also three years of learning escapements and trains and the deep grammar of mechanisms, which was the only inheritance anyone had offered him since his father died.

If Garrick was the author of the letter—if he had built, gear by gear, the machine that would destroy the only family Ansel had ever been allowed to love—then he had built it using Ansel's hands. Had let him climb the ninety-nine steps. Had sent him, knowingly, to find it.

Or worse: had expected him to find it and say nothing. Had counted on the boy's terror of the authorities, his gratitude, his invisibility, and assumed that a found letter would pass silently through a silent boy into the world, doing its work, implicating everyone except the man who profited.

Ansel became aware that he was gripping the edge of the counter hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and that his breath was coming fast and shallow, and that the lamp flame had guttered low. He trimmed the wick with fingers that trembled only a little, and made himself think.

Think like a clockmaker. When a train refuses to run, you don't smash the movement. You find the wheel that binds. You isolate it. You watch it turn.

What did he actually know? He knew the letter was a frame—he had known that since he read it, since no man guilty of smuggling seals evidence of his own crime inside a town clock. He knew the ledger was forged, and forged with intimate knowledge of this shop. He knew the dates aligned. He knew the hand was not Garrick's, though it wore Garrick's clothes.

Not Garrick's hand. He seized on that, gratefully, desperately. Whoever wrote the entries had *imitated* the master. Could Garrick imitate his own hand badly enough to leave those tells? Unlikely. A man doesn't practice deceiving himself.

Which meant a third person. Someone who knew the shop, knew the ledger, knew the harbor, and knew Garrick well enough to borrow his identity. Someone who had access—recent access, repeated access, over years.

Ansel looked around the shop slowly, seeing it as if for the first time: the bench with its scarred top, the drawers of parts, the wall of clocks ticking their hundred small disagreements about the hour. Who came here? Customers. Suppliers. The harbormaster, occasionally, to complain about the office chronometer. Men from the customs house. Garrick's Thursday-evening visitors, the ones who arrived after closing, when Ansel had been sent upstairs, and whose voices came low through the floorboards—voices Ansel had never been curious enough to put faces to.

Thursday evening. Tomorrow was Thursday.

Above him, unmistakable now, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps crossing the room overhead, unhurried, heavy on the left foot—Garrick's tread, back early from the Anchor & Crown, or never having properly gone. Ansel slid the ledger beneath the counter in one motion, damped the lamp to a coal-red glow, and picked up the carriage clock he had been failing to repair, holding it to his eye like a loupe as the stair door opened below.

Garrick filled the doorway, hat still on, cheeks flushed with more than ale. His small eyes moved across the shop—the lamp, the boy, the clock—with the quick inventory of a man checking whether anything in his house had shifted while he was away.

"Burning oil late," Garrick said. It was not a question, and it was not friendly.

"Fine work," Ansel said, and heard his own voice come out level, and marveled at it. "The train's binding. I want it right by morning."

Something passed across his master's face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. Suspicion, perhaps. Or patience. Garrick hung his hat on its peg, and as he did, his gaze rested for half a heartbeat on the counter. On the place where the ledger slept.

"Mind you don't overwork yourself," Garrick said mildly, climbing toward his bed. "Big day tomorrow."

At the bottom of the stairs he paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at his apprentice with an expression Ansel had never seen him wear before. Almost gentle. Almost sorry.

"You've been up at the observatory a good deal," Garrick said. "Making friends with the keeper's girl."

"Yes," Ansel said.

"Mm." Garrick's thumb rubbed slowly along the banister, back and forth, like a man polishing a memory. "Friends can be expensive, boy. Remember that."

He went up. The door closed. And Ansel sat alone in the reddening lamplight with the dead clock in his hands, listening to his master's footsteps cross the ceiling, and understood at last that the trap was not the letter, and not the ledger, and not the checkpoints between here and the harbor.

The trap was that Garrick Fenwick already knew exactly where his apprentice had been—and was waiting, with a clockmaker's patience, to see which way the boy would run.
