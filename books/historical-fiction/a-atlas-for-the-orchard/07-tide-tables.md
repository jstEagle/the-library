## 7. Tide Tables

Maren found him in the observatory's lower room, hunched over the tide tables like a man reading scripture in a language he half remembered.

He hadn't heard her come down. That alone told her something was wrong. Ansel Nakamura heard everything—the whisper of a gear tooth out of true, the creak of the third stair, the particular sigh the master clock made when the barometer fell. He had ears tuned like his father's instruments, and he moved through rooms the way light moved through water: everywhere at once, touching everything, claiming nothing. For her not to register in that awareness, he would have had to be somewhere else entirely—somewhere inside himself where the world's small sounds couldn't follow.

She stood in the doorway and watched him for a long moment.

The lamplight caught the edge of his jaw, the dark sweep of hair that always looked as though he'd been caught in weather, though it hadn't rained in a week. His fingers rested on the paper—not moving, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a wound. Spread before him on the long table were the tide tables for the fortnight, the shipping notices Edmund had brought home from the customs house, and a chart of the harbor approaches so old its ink had gone the brown of dried seaweed.

Shipping notices. Maren felt something turn over slowly in her chest, the way the tide turned over the shingle at night—unhurried, inevitable, rearranging everything it touched.

Ansel Nakamura did not read shipping notices. Ansel Nakamura cared about escapements and balance wheels, about the difference between a lever and a cylinder movement, about whether the observatory clock could be coaxed to hold its time within two seconds of Greenwich over a month. The movements of vessels meant nothing to him. He had once, with complete sincerity, asked her whether a brig and a barkentine were different ships or the same ship spelled differently.

And here he was, tracing the departure column with one finger, his lips slightly parted, reading names of ships the way other men read names of horses they'd bet against.

"Maren."

He said it without looking up, and she realized he'd known she was there after all—had perhaps known from the moment her foot found the stair. He simply hadn't been ready to be seen doing what he was doing.

"You've gone very quiet," she said, coming into the room. "Quieter than usual, I mean. You've achieved some new depth of silence. Father will want it measured and recorded."

That earned her half a smile—the left corner of his mouth, no more—and it broke her heart a little, how little it took to buy even that much, and how much it seemed to cost him to spend it.

"Your father's notices," he said. "The *Kestrel*. When does she sail?"

Maren came around the table and looked down at the papers. The *Kestrel* was a revenue cutter, black-hulled and quick, moored at the inner pier these three days while her crew took on stores. Everyone in town knew her by sight; her masts rose above the harbor sheds like the fingers of a hand reaching for the sky.

"She sails on the morning tide," Maren said. "Day after tomorrow. Why?"

"Who commands her?"

"Inspector Hale. You've seen him—he comes up for the trustees' meetings sometimes. Grey coat, grey eyes, a face like a door that hasn't decided whether to open." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, deliberately, making herself part of the geometry of the table. "Ansel. Look at me."

He looked at her.

It was, she thought later, the strangest conversation they ever had, because it began without words. He looked at her the way he'd looked at the letter she'd once seen him slip into his tool roll—the letter he believed she hadn't seen, three nights ago, when he'd drawn it from behind the escapement with hands that shook like a drunkard's. She had seen. She had said nothing then, because she'd understood instinctively that whatever he'd found inside the clock's housing was not hers to demand. Some things you waited for. Some things came to you the way the tide came: not called, but certain.

"The letter," she said quietly. "The one from behind the escapement."

His breath went out of him. "You saw."

"I see most things. It's a family affliction." She folded her hands on the table, over the tide tables, over the shipping notices, anchoring them both to the facts. "You've been carrying it since Tuesday night. You haven't slept properly since Tuesday night. You eat like a bird being watched by a cat. And now you're memorizing the sailing schedule of a revenue cutter." She let that sit between them. "Tell me."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't. She watched the whole war happen behind his eyes—she'd learned to read that war in the three years since her family had taken him in, the war between what Ansel felt and what Ansel permitted himself to show. He had been raised, she knew, in the space between his English mother's people and his Japanese father's blood, and both sides had taught him the same lesson by different roads: make yourself small, make yourself quiet, make yourself nothing, and perhaps nothing will strike you.

But then something shifted. It was like watching a mainspring finally allowed to run down after years of being held at full tension by a trembling thumb.

"It's addressed to the customs house," he said. "Sealed. In a hand I don't know. But it names your family, Maren. Your father. Your brothers. It names them as party to a smuggling ring—contraband through the harbor, moved under cover of legitimate cargo." His voice stayed low and level, which somehow made it worse; this was Ansel reciting, Ansel reporting, Ansel holding himself together with grammar. "I steamed the seal. I know I shouldn't have, but I had to know what I was carrying, and—" He stopped. Started again. "It isn't proof of guilt. That's the thing I couldn't stop turning over. It reads like proof, it's written *like* proof, but there's a warning in it too—a warning about an informant inside the customs house—and no guilty man warns anyone about informants. Someone wrote that letter to be *found*, or to be *delivered*, and either way it lands on your father's name like a stone."

The lamp hissed softly. Above them, faint through the floor, the master clock ticked its repaired and patient tick—the sound Ansel had given back to the house, working three nights in its brass belly.

"When?" Maren asked.

"What?"

"When is it dated? Or dated to be delivered? Letters that are meant to be found are usually meant to be found at a particular time."

He blinked at her, and she saw the thought land—that she was not going to weep or rage or ask him why he hadn't told her sooner, but was going to do the thing her father did, the thing the Whitcombes did around their supper table every night: take a hard fact and set it on the table among the bread and the salt and look at it squarely until it gave up its meaning.

"Thursday," he said. "There's a line at the bottom. 'To be delivered before the tide.' Thursday's morning tide."

"Tomorrow," Maren said. "The same tide the *Kestrel* sails on."

"Yes." He swallowed. "I think—I think someone means for that letter to reach Inspector Hale aboard that ship, with your father's name inside it, before the ship sails and the accusation goes wherever accusations go when they're carried in official hulls. To London, maybe. To the Board. Somewhere your father can't stand in front of it and answer it face to face."

Maren sat very still. Fear came into her the way cold water comes into a boot—sudden, total, finding every seam. She thought of her father's face at supper last week, abstracted, turning his fork over and over; of the crumpled paper Thomas had pulled from the garden gate one morning and burned without showing anyone; of her mother saying, lightly, too lightly, that Edmund had been sleeping badly.

"Daddy's been getting letters," she heard herself say. "Anonymous ones. For weeks. He burns them, but I found one he missed—under the mat, rain-soaked. It didn't name anything. It just said, *We see what leaves your house at night.* Nothing else. No signature. Just that, over and over in my head for days." She met Ansel's eyes. "I never told you because I thought it was spite. There are men who resent Father—men who lost the lighthouse contract, men who drink and blame. I thought someone was frightening him for sport."

"And now?"

"Now I think someone has been preparing the ground. Weeks of threats, so that when the real blow came, everyone in town would already believe the Whitcombes were hiding something. You don't accuse a man out of clear sky. You darken the sky first." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Who else knows about the letter?"

"No one. I hid it in my tool roll. I was going to—" He stopped, and she watched him refuse a lie, watched him choose truth the way a man chooses to step off a ledge. "I didn't know what I was going to do. I kept thinking if I just held it, held it still, time would show me what it was for. That's what my father taught me about a mechanism you don't understand. Don't force it. Hold it still and watch."

"And what has time shown you?"

Ansel looked down at the tide table, at the column of figures that measured the sea's great slow breathing, and when he spoke again the recitation was gone from his voice and there was only the boy underneath, tired and frightened and three years grateful.

"It's shown me that whoever wrote that letter knows this harbor. Knows your father's routines, knows the customs house has a soft place in it, knows the *Kestrel*'s schedule. And it's shown me that the only person who benefits is whoever wants the Whitcombes out of the harbor trade entirely. And Maren—" his voice dropped further, almost to the lamplight's own frequency, "—I think the person who removed the gear from the master clock wanted me to find that letter. Wanted *my hands* on it. Because if it's found in the possession of the Nakamura boy, the half-breed apprentice nobody trusts, then it isn't evidence anymore. It's a story. A foreign boy steals a letter accusing an honest customs officer—except it wouldn't even need to make sense. People would believe what they already believe. They always do."

The word *half-breed* hung in the air like smoke, and Maren flinched at hearing it in his mouth—heard, in it, all the times other mouths had said it within his hearing, casually, the way people say *weather*.

"Stop that," she said.

He looked up, startled.

"That word. Not in this room. Not in this house. Not in my hearing again." She leaned forward. "Listen to me, Ansel. Whoever did this chose you because they thought you were a perfect instrument—you're invisible and you're distrusted and you'd have nowhere to run. They thought you'd carry that letter like a good little clockwork boy, or they thought you'd panic and destroy it, or they thought you'd be caught with it and hanged twice over—once by the law and once by the town. They made a mistake. They didn't count on you having a family."

Something moved through his face then that she would remember for the rest of her life—something breaking open, like ice going out of a river.

"They gave you to us," she went on, softer now, "the winter your father died. Father signed the apprenticeship papers and Mother put a fourth plate on the table and nobody made a speech about it because that's not how this family does things. So you don't get to decide alone what happens to a threat against this family. That decision belongs at this table. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Ansel said. And then, wonderingly, as if the word were new to him: "Yes."

* * *

They worked by lamplight for two hours, and by the end of it the fear had changed its shape entirely.

That was the thing Maren understood about fear, watching it happen: fear kept in one chest grew heavy as ballast and dragged you under, but fear divided became something else—it became weight enough to steady a keel. She talked and Ansel drew, and the plan assembled itself out of the tide tables the way a watch assembled itself out of parts, each piece necessary, each piece fitted to the next.

"The letter has to reach Hale," Ansel said, tapping the *Kestrel*'s entry. "Not the customs house—the customs house may have a rotten plank in it, the letter says so itself. Hale aboard his own ship, with the letter in his own hand, before the tide turns. Once she's at sea with that letter unreported, we've lost. Once she's at sea with Hale holding it and knowing what it is, the conspiracy loses its vessel and its messenger in one stroke."

"So we deliver it tonight. Tomorrow is too late—too many chances for whoever wrote it to learn it's missing and send it themselves, or worse, to move against Daddy first."

"Not tonight." Ansel turned the shipping notice toward her. "Hale dines ashore tomorrow evening—the harbormaster hosts the officers before a sailing; it's posted. He'll be back aboard by eleven. The pier guard changes at midnight, and the new watch won't have settled into its habits yet. If we go at half past twelve, the tide will be turning at four. We'll have hours."

"*We*," Maren repeated.

Ansel set down the pencil. She watched him gather himself for the argument she'd already decided he was going to lose.

"Maren. If I'm caught with that letter, I'm a thief and worse. If you're caught with me, you're—the keeper's daughter, mixed up in it, and your family's name gets dragged through it anyway. One of us should carry it. It should be me. I'm the one it was meant for. Let the trap close on the trap."

"No."

"Maren—"

"You misunderstand the design of the thing," she said, and heard her grandmother's crispness come into her own voice and did not mind at all. "You cannot walk onto that pier alone. Your face is known at the customs gate—you repair the regulator clock there, you told me yourself the clerk makes you sign the book every time. A young man approaching a revenue cutter at one in the morning is stopped on principle. But a young man *and* the keeper's daughter, walking openly, laughing, carrying a lantern and a basket—no guard in England stops that pair without a reason he'd have to write down. We go together or the plan fails. Those aren't two options, Ansel. That's one option wearing different clothes."

He stared at her. Then, slowly, the corner of his mouth lifted—that same half smile, but different now, less like something rationed and more like something offered.

"You've thought about this," he said.

"I've lived on this harbor my whole life. I know how guards think, how tides run, which constable drinks and which one doesn't. You know clocks. Neither of us is sufficient alone." She reached across the table and, for the first time in three years of careful friendship, took his hand—his right hand, the one scarred faintly with the small nicks of his trade. "Together, then."

"Together," he said.

They bent over the table again, heads nearly touching, and finished the plan: the route along the cliff path rather than the high street, past the churchyard where the yews stood black as spilled ink; the lantern dimmed to a coal; the letter sewn into the lining of Maren's basket beneath a layer of apples, because no guard in history had ever demanded a lady open her fruit basket at midnight. Ansel would carry his tool roll openly—if questioned, they were answering a call: the *Kestrel*'s chronometer had been running slow, the harbormaster had sent word, and the keeper's daughter had insisted on walking her friend down because the fog was coming in. A plausible errand, wrapped around an impossible one, like a lie wrapped around a truth wrapped around a family's whole future.

"One more thing," Maren said, as the candle guttered low. "If it goes wrong—if we're taken before we reach the gangway—then you give them the letter and you tell them everything, and you tell them I forced you. Swear it."

"I won't—"

"Swear it. You are the person in this town with the least protection and the most to lose, and you will not spend yourself protecting people who have spent three years failing to protect you. That ends tonight. Whatever happens at that pier, you did not act alone. Say it."

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, far below the cliff, the sea worked at the rocks with its old patience, and somewhere in the walls above them the master clock ticked on, keeping its faithful count of every second they spent planning and every second they had left.

"I didn't act alone," Ansel said at last. "I never did, did I. I just couldn't see it."

"No," Maren said. "Now you can."

She blew out the candle. In the sudden dark, the harbor lights swam at the window like a scatter of drowned stars, and down at the inner pier, invisible but perfectly known to them both, the *Kestrel* rode at her moorings—black hull, grey commander, and thirty hours until the morning tide.

Somewhere in the town, she thought, someone who had written a letter and removed a gear and sent anonymous threats to a decent man was sleeping soundly, believing their machine would run exactly as built.

Maren smiled in the darkness, and it was not a gentle expression.

They had forgotten one thing about machines. She had grown up in a house full of them, and Ansel could build one blindfolded, and they both knew the oldest truth of the trade:

Every machine can be broken by someone who understands it well enough—and loves the people it was built to hurt.
