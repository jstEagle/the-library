## 8. Three Checkpoints

The tide table lay between them on the workbench like a confession neither of them had meant to make.

Ansel had spread it out with the same care he brought to a disassembled movement—every wheel laid in its proper place, every tooth accounted for. Beside it he had drawn, on the back of an old repair ticket, a rough map of the harbor: the customs house at the head of the quay with its iron gate and its clerk who peered at everyone as though they owed him money; the harbormaster's office halfway down the row, its door always half-open, its master always half-listening; and then the pier itself, where the revenue cutter *Vigilant* would lie until the morning tide, and where the pier guard walked his beat with a lantern and a rifle and the particular boredom that made men dangerous.

Three checkpoints. Three pairs of eyes between him and Inspector Hale's cabin.

"You've drawn this before," Maren said. It wasn't a question. She was leaning over the bench, her braid slipping forward off her shoulder, and she did not seem to notice how close her cheek came to the lamp, or how the light turned her face to bronze at the edge and shadow at the center. "The way you've spaced everything. The times written beside each place."

"I draw movements," Ansel said. "Gears, springs, escapements. A route is only a movement you walk through. Every part has to do its work at exactly the right moment or the whole thing stops." He touched the little penciled cross he'd made at the customs house gate. "This is the mainspring. Everything winds from here. If they stop me at the gate, none of the rest matters."

"And if they don't stop you there?"

"Then the harbormaster is the escape wheel." He moved his finger down the map. "He lets you pass one tooth at a time. Slow. Deliberate. And he looks at you the whole while, because that's what an escape wheel does—it watches the train it's feeding."

Maren laughed, a short breath of a sound, and then pressed her hand over her mouth as though the hour were too serious for it. But Ansel found he was glad of the laugh. Fear, he had learned in three years of Garrick's shop, was like a mainspring wound too tight: left alone it would either freeze the works or snap something vital. It needed a release valve. Her laugh was the release valve.

"The pier guard," she said, when she had mastered herself. "What's he?"

"The balance," Ansel said quietly. "The last thing the power passes through before the whole clock strikes. One impulse, one swing, and then—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They both understood what happened when the balance caught.

Outside the workshop above Harbor Row, the wind had come around to the northeast, the kind of wind that pushed the tide in early and hard. It rattled the loose pane in the gable and set the sign-hanging bracket creaking, and beneath those sounds Ansel could hear the town settling into night—the last cart on the cobbles, a dog somewhere by the fish cellars, a woman calling a child in. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who could walk from one end of the quay to the other without calculating, at every step, what their faces were doing.

He had spent the day doing that calculation and hated how good at it he was becoming.

* * *

They worked through the plan twice more before either of them spoke of anything but the plan.

"Customs house first," Maren said, tapping the gate. "You'll have your commission papers—the observatory work. That's real, that's signed by my father, so lean on it. You're the clockmaker's boy come about the harbor clock, which is even true, because the harbor clock *is* stopped and everyone knows it. Truth is better than invention. Invention has edges; truth doesn't."

"Mr. Petch at the gate will still ask why I'm going toward the water and not up to the harbor clock."

"Then let him ask. Answer slowly. Men like Petch grow impatient, and impatience waves people through faster than permission does." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen there before—not pity, exactly, and not admiration either, but a fierce attention, the look of someone studying a problem she intended to solve. "You've been watched all your life, Ansel. You know how to be boring. Tonight, being boring is a skill."

It should have stung. Somehow it didn't. There was a strange comfort in being understood down to the mechanisms.

"And you?" he asked. "Petch won't only watch me. He watches the whole gate."

"Half past four," she said, "there's a fishmonger named Tredwell who sets out his morning catch on the quay side of the customs wall, and half past four there's also a boy—a different boy every week, but always a boy—who knocks over Tredwell's tray running for the early boat. It's practically liturgical. Tomorrow the boy will be late and clumsy and very sorry, and Tredwell will be loud about it, and every guard within forty feet of that gate will look at the fish instead of the clockmaker's apprentice walking through it." She said it flatly, without pride, and somehow that made it worse and better at once. "My brothers taught me. They used to run contraband apples past our mother's kitchen window when we were small. The principle scales."

"Maren." He heard his own voice change, and she heard it too, because she went still. "If any of this goes wrong—if anyone asks why the keeper's daughter is staging accidents at the customs gate the same morning a letter goes missing—you tell them you were buying mackerel. You tell them I acted alone. You never saw the letter. You don't know what's in it. Your family has enough to answer for that isn't true. Don't add something that is."

"No."

"Maren—"

"I said no." She didn't raise her voice. She said it the way her father Edmund said things, the way that ended discussions rather than beginning them. "You keep treating me like someone standing on the shore waving a handkerchief. I'm not. My father's name is in that letter, and yours is the only pair of hands carrying it, and that makes us the same movement, Ansel—one train of wheels. You don't cut a gear out of a going train and expect the clock to run kinder for it. You just expect it not to run at all."

He stared at her. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the pilings, patient as a file. He thought of all the silences he had ever kept—the ones his father had taught him, careful and protective, the silence of a man making himself small enough to be overlooked—and he understood suddenly that Maren Whitcombe had never once in her life been silent out of fear. Only out of judgment. Silence, for her, was a room she could enter and leave as she pleased.

He envied her that the way he envied other men their fathers' names.

"All right," he said at last. "One train of wheels."

* * *

They rehearsed the harbormaster next, and it went harder, because the harbormaster could not be distracted—only delayed—and delay was a currency Ansel did not have.

"Mr. Quillan likes apprentices," Maren admitted, pulling a face. "He'll want to talk about the trade. He'll ask after Garrick. He'll ask what you're doing on the quay at five in the morning, and 'walking briskly' is not an answer he'll wear."

"Then I give him one he can wear. I'm delivering a repaired chronometer to the *Vigilant*." Ansel held up the tool roll, already packed, the letter sewn flat inside the canvas lining beneath the loop that held his smallest screwdriver. "Which is nearly true. I'm delivering something to the *Vigilant*. Quillan respects deliveries. Deliveries are business, and business is the one language every man at that harbor speaks fluently."

"And if he asks to see the chronometer?"

"Then he sees a tool roll, and I tell him the piece is wrapped so it won't take a knock, and I look offended. Offense is useful. No one searches a man who might complain about them to the chamber of commerce."

She studied him for a long moment. "You're frightening me a little," she said. "Not because you're frightened. Because you're not, or you've put it somewhere I can't see it."

That was precisely the moment, Ansel reflected later, when the fear chose to find him again. It came up through the floorboards of himself, quiet and total, the way cold water finds the seams of a boot. Because the truth—which he would not say aloud, which he would carry with him through all three checkpoints along with the letter—was that he *was* frightened, had been frightened since the moment he'd steamed the seal and read Edmund Whitcombe's name in a stranger's hand, and the only thing keeping the fear from stopping his heart outright was the work. The planning. The drawing of routes like movements, wheels like alibis. Give a frightened man a mechanism and he will survive almost anything; it was the men with nothing to adjust who froze.

"I'm frightened," he told her, because she deserved the truth and because saying it took some of the weight off. "I've been frightened since I was nine years old, when my father told me that in this country a Nakamura's fear is assumed and his innocence must be demonstrated. I'm simply going to be frightened in the correct order. Gate first, then office, then pier. One fear at a time, properly seated in its jewel. That's all courage is, I think. I don't actually know. I haven't done this before."

"No," Maren said softly. "But you've done everything else alone, and you're not doing this alone, and perhaps that's the part that matters." She reached across the bench and set her hand briefly over his—briefly, deliberately, the way one taps a crystal to see if the movement is alive. "Third checkpoint. The pier guard."

"The pier guard is the problem." Ansel pulled the map toward him and looked at the last cross, at the end of the pier where the lantern swung and the sentry stamped against the cold. "The first two, I can talk past, because talking is their trade and they'll trade with anyone. But the pier guard has orders and a description—*if* Garrick has already sent word ahead, and I have to assume he has. A half-caste apprentice, they'll say. Answers to the clockmaker. Carries a canvas roll." His mouth twisted. "I'm not difficult to describe, Maren. I've spent my whole life being described."

The words came out with more edge than he'd intended, and he saw her hear the edge and choose not to flinch from it.

"Then you don't go down the pier," she said. "Not the way everyone goes down the pier."

"There's no other way."

"There's the oyster smack *Dorcas*." She leaned over the map, and her finger came down not on the pier but on the mud flats west of it, where the small boats lay over on their bilges waiting for the returning tide. "Old Hewett leaves her moored aground until the flood lifts her, and he rows out an hour before high water. The *Vigilant*'s gangway comes down on the seaward side. From the smack's deck, at high water, you could step across to the cutter's chains with a running jump and a decent excuse. No gate. No sentry. No description answered."

"It's trespass."

"It's *ambiguity*," Maren said. "Trespass they can prove. Ambiguity they can only argue about. And arguments take time, and time is what Hale needs to hold the ship." She straightened, and for a moment the lamplight caught her eyes and he saw in them the same expression he'd seen on her father's face over supper—the Whitcombe steadiness, the look of people who had decided long ago that the world was survivable and meant to hold the world to it.

Ansel looked down at the map. Three crosses. Three chances to be stopped, searched, questioned, ruined—and behind all three, faint as a watermark, the shape of Garrick Voss, who had sold whatever he knew to whoever was paying, and who would discover soon, if he had not already, that his apprentice had been absent in ways that mattered.

"He'll know," Ansel said quietly. "Garrick. Even if everything works—even if the letter reaches Hale and the tide carries the truth in like ballast—he'll know it was me. And I have to go back to that shop tomorrow, and the day after, until whatever happens happens. I'll be working across a bench from a man who framed the only family I ever had, and he'll be watching my hands."

"Then don't go back," Maren said simply. "Come to the observatory. My father would take you on tonight if you asked."

"My father took me on once," Ansel said, "and died anyway. Apprenticeship is a contract, Maren. It binds both ways, and I've three years of it unexpired, and a man who walks out of a contract in this town becomes exactly the sort of man the customs house stops at gates." He shook his head slowly. "I go back. I wind the regulators and sweep the floor and say 'yes, sir,' and I watch him watching me, and I do it until the tide turns. That's the last wheel in the movement. The ugliest one."

Maren was quiet for a while. Then she began gathering the candle stubs and the scrap of thread she'd used to demonstrate the fishmonger distraction, putting the evidence of their planning away piece by piece, and her voice, when it came, was gentler than he had ever heard it.

"One train of wheels," she said. "But Ansel—when the clock strikes, you strike with it. Don't stand in front of that man one hour longer than the mechanism requires."

* * *

He walked her home through streets gone soft with fog, the gas lamps haloed and uncertain, and at the corner of Harbor Row they parted without ceremony, because ceremony was a thing they had somehow already agreed to skip. He watched her until the fog took her, and then he stood a moment longer in the wet dark, feeling the letter press flat against his ribs inside the tool roll, patient as a loaded spring.

Back in his narrow room above the shop, he did not sleep. He sat on the edge of the cot with the lamp turned low and ran the route in his mind until it wore smooth: the gate at half past four, the accident with the fish, the slow bored wave of Mr. Petch's hand; the harbormaster's half-open door and his own invented chronometer, delivered with offense; the mud flats and the grounded *Dorcas* and the running jump onto the cutter's chains. Three checkpoints. Three teeth of the escape wheel, each one letting the train advance by exactly one step.

Below him, he heard the shop door open.

It was past eleven. Garrick kept hours as regular as one of his own regulators—had done for thirty years, the neighbors said, with something like awe. Ansel rose without sound and crossed to the top of the stairs, and through the gap beneath the door he watched lamplight move along the corridor boards, slow, deliberate, pausing outside his room for the length of two breaths before continuing on to the workshop.

A drawer opened. Closed. Another. Then the small, precise sounds of a man examining the ledger he kept locked in the bottom cabinet—the ledger whose dates matched the letter's dates, whose columns Ansel had copied into his memory and nowhere else.

The lamp passed back along the corridor. The stair treads took the descending weight one at a time. And then, faintly, from the workshop below, came a sound Ansel knew better than his own heartbeat: the whir of the lathe being tested, the click of a chuck tightening.

At midnight, Garrick Voss was preparing to work.

Ansel stood in the dark with his hand pressed flat against the door, and understood that the movement had already begun—that somewhere below him the first wheel had turned, and every wheel after it, including his own, would now turn in consequence, faster and faster, until the clock struck or the mainspring snapped.
