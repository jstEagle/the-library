## 9. A Broken Escapement

Horace Garrick had learned long ago that the best lies were made the way a good escapement was made: with tolerances so fine that no one could feel the mechanism turning. A clock did not announce its gears. It simply kept moving, and men trusted it because it moved without complaint.

He was thinking about this on Thursday morning while polishing the glass of a regulator that would never run again—a dead thing he kept in the window for exactly this purpose, to occupy his hands when his mind wanted to pace. Outside, the light came up grey over Harbor Row, and inside, the shop smelled of oil and brass shavings and the faint vinegar tang of the pickling solution he used on old movements. Familiar smells. Safe ones. He had built this shop out of nothing twenty-two years ago, tooth by tooth, wheel by wheel, and he would not see it pulled down for anyone—not for the Whitcombes, not for the harbor men who paid him, and certainly not for a boy with the wrong face and too much curiosity.

Ansel had been absent three evenings running.

Garrick noted it the way he noted everything: silently, precisely, in the ledger of his attention where no one else could read. Tuesday, the boy had claimed errands for Mrs. Pellow—yet Mrs. Pellow's mantel clock sat unrepaired on the shelf by the door, untouched since Saturday. Wednesday, Ansel had said he was at evening prayers. Garrick had never once seen the boy pray, and God, if He existed, would have found that claim as funny as Garrick did. And last night—the boy had come back after dark with observatory grease on his cuffs and something behind his eyes that Garrick recognized because he saw it every day in his own mirror: a man doing sums he didn't want anyone else to check.

The bell over the door chimed. Speak of the devil's apprentice.

Ansel came in with the cold on him, stamping his boots, his tool roll under his arm. Seventeen now, nearly eighteen, tall in a stooped way, always seeming to apologize for the space he took up. Black hair, black eyes, that unfortunate face that drew stares down every street from the church to the fish market. Garrick had taken him in at fourteen because his father had died owing money and the parish had nowhere better to put him, and in three years the boy had become—Garrick admitted it grudgingly—the finest pair of hands he had ever employed. Better than Garrick's own hands had been at that age. Better than Garrick's hands were now, if it came to it. That was precisely the problem with fine hands. They reached for things.

"You're late," Garrick said, without looking up from the dead regulator.

"Half past seven, sir." Ansel hung his coat on the peg. "You said eight."

"I said half seven yesterday. Weren't you listening?" It was a lie; he'd said eight. The point was not the time. The point was to watch the boy accept the fault, which he did, as always, with a small dip of the head and no argument. Obedient. That had been useful once. Now obedience that concealed other activities was merely camouflage, and camouflage, in Garrick's experience, was worn by things that intended to move.

He set down the rag. "Come here. Look at this."

Ansel approached the workbench, all deference. Garrick turned the regulator's movement toward him under the lamp.

"Tell me what you see."

Ansel bent over it. His fingers hovered but did not touch—good training, that—and his eyes went through the mechanism the way a doctor's eyes go through a chest. "Third wheel's stripped," he said. "And someone's forced the mainspring barrel. There are burrs on the arbor here—see? They tried to wind it against a seized train."

"And?"

"And it's not worth saving." The boy straightened. "The plate is cracked through the pillar hole. You'd spend more than the clock is worth."

"Wrong," Garrick said pleasantly. "It can be saved. Everything can be saved, if you're willing to replace enough of what's rotten." He let that sit a moment, watching Ansel's face for flickers. Nothing. The boy was getting harder to read, which was itself information. "I've noticed you've been keeping hours of your own lately."

Ansel's shoulders stiffened—one degree of arc, no more. "The observatory commission, sir. The master clock. It's taken longer than—"

"The master clock." Garrick picked up his burnisher and worked it along the edge of a pallet stone that needed no working at all. "How is our grand lady of the tower? Still dead?"

"Yes, sir. I've got the train apart. I think I can have her running by Sunday."

"Sunday." Garrick smiled at the movement, not at the boy. "Funny thing about that clock. Thirty years she ran without so much as a cough, and then the week the town's whole gentry happens to be watching, she dies of a missing part." He set the burnisher down with a soft click. "A gear, wasn't it? Removed clean?"

Ansel was very still. "I assumed it came loose. Fell somewhere into the case."

"Did you." Not a question. Garrick rose and crossed to the stove, lifted the kettle, poured two cups of chicory though he only ever drank one—he had learned in forty years of trade that a shared cup opened more doors than a shared confidence—and carried them back to the bench. He pushed one across the wood toward Ansel, an offering, a small kindness performed with the precision of a jeweler setting a stone.

"Sit," he said. "Talk to me. You've been like a cat on hot bricks all week, boy, and I'll not have it said I worked an apprentice into the ground. Your father—" he paused, delicately, the way a man steps over a grave he has dug himself, "—your father was the same. Carried everything alone until it crushed him. I'd hoped better for you."

It was beautiful, he thought, how easily grief could be used as a handle. He watched Ansel take the cup. Watched the boy's knuckles whiten around it, just slightly. Watched him choose, visibly, between truth and some invented thing.

"I've been staying late at the tower," Ansel said finally. "The light's better there. For the fine work."

"The light." Garrick laughed, warm as mulled wine. "The light's better. Well, that's honest at least." He sipped his chicory. *Liar,* he thought, with something close to professional admiration. *Where did you learn to lie so quietly? Not from me. I never taught you that.* Though perhaps he had. Perhaps everything he had ever done in front of the boy had been a lesson, the way a spider's whole body is a lesson in patience.

* * *

The letter had been four weeks in the making.

Garrick thought about it now the way he thought about any mechanism he had built: with pride he couldn't show and couldn't entirely suppress. The men from up-country—Corven, the smooth one, and the heavy silent one whose name nobody used—had come to the shop in September with a proposition laid out like a surgeon's instruments. The harbor trade was to be rerouted north, to their quays, their warehouses, their railways; certain interests had arranged it with certain other interests, and the only obstruction was the Whitcombe name, which stood over the harbor customs like a coat of arms. Ruin the name, they said, and the rerouting becomes a rescue. Give us a scandal, Mr. Garrick, and we'll give you your debts cleared and a new lease and fifty pounds besides.

His debts. That had been the knife's edge of it. Eleven years of slow drowning—bad leases, worse luck, two fires, a wife buried under a stone that cost more than her funeral—and the shop held together with string and prayer and the labor of a half-Japanese orphan whom the town tolerated only because Garrick vouchsafed for him. Fifty pounds. A cleared ledger. A future.

So he had written the letter. Three drafts, burned. The fourth he had aged with tea and smoke until it looked thirty years old, and written into it a smuggling conspiracy with Edmund Whitcombe's name stitched through it like a red thread, and dates—carefully chosen dates, drawn from old tide tables and harbor notices, plausible to any inspector who didn't look too hard—and, in the margin, in a hand he had practiced until it betrayed nothing, a reference to the informant inside the customs house. That touch had been Corven's suggestion. Corven understood that fear works best when it points inward.

Then he had placed it where only chance would find it: wedged behind the escapement platform of the observatory master clock, in the one place in the town where a search would be conducted slowly, carefully, by someone with the skill to open the case without tearing anything. He had removed the count wheel gear himself, three nights before, climbing the ninety-nine steps with the gear wrapped in flannel in his pocket like a stolen heart, so that the clock's failure would summon exactly the right pair of hands.

It had been elegant. It had been, he still believed, necessary. The Whitcombes would survive a scandal—they were gentry, and gentry floated—but Garrick had been sinking for eleven years, and no one had ever once thrown him a rope.

And then the letter had vanished.

Not immediately. That was what gnawed at him. For six days the plan had run like a well-oiled train—Ansel up the tower, Ansel elbow-deep in the case, Ansel finding (he assumed) nothing, because the letter was tucked where no repair required looking. Then on the seventh day Corven had sent word: the letter must have surfaced by now; the customs man was asking questions that meant someone had shown him something. Except no one had shown anyone. The letter was gone, and the only person who had been alone with the clock was the boy.

Garrick had gone up to the observatory himself on Sunday, hat in hand, playing the concerned tradesman come to admire the great clock's restoration. He had brought a lantern and gotten his hands inside the housing on the pretext of admiring Ansel's work. The compartment behind the escapement was empty. Empty, and—the detail that had kept him awake since—one of the felt dust seals he had disturbed when placing the letter had been pressed back down again. Pressed, not brushed. Someone had found the space, looked into it, and closed it gently, like a drawer.

Only one person had hands gentle enough to notice a felt seal.

* * *

"You're doing that thing," Ansel said.

Garrick blinked. The boy was watching him over the rim of his cup with an expression of mild concern, and Garrick realized he had been staring into the dead regulator's movement for the better part of a minute, seeing nothing.

"What thing?" he said, chuckling. "Old men stare, boy. It's all we have left."

"You were staring like you were counting something." Ansel set his cup down. "Counting teeth."

"Occupational disease." Garrick waved it off, easy as winding a watch, and changed the subject the way he had changed ten thousand subjects in this shop, with a question shaped like generosity. "Tell me about the tower. Maren Whitcombe helping you, is she? The keeper's girl?"

Something crossed the boy's face then—fast, but not fast enough. Not guilt. Something warmer and more dangerous than guilt.

"She brings me tea," Ansel said. "Her father's been kind to my family since before—" He stopped, adjusted. "Since I came to town."

"Kind." Garrick nodded slowly, turning the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. Kindness. Yes. That was the whole cursed architecture of it, wasn't it. The Whitcombes had been kind to the orphan, folded him into their suppers on Harbor Row, called him near-family in front of people who then repeated it in front of other people, and kindness, unlike debt, could not be discharged or denied. If the boy had found the letter—if he understood what it threatened—he would not take it to the authorities. He would take it *for* them. Somewhere safe. Somewhere far from the case of a broken clock.

Or he would take it to the pier. To Inspector Hale's revenue ship, if gossip served, and gossip on Harbor Row served everything within a day.

"Edmund Whitcombe," Garrick said musingly, testing the name in the open air the way a man tests ice before stepping onto a pond. "Good family. Old family. You know, when I was young, before your time, there were stories about the harbor. Contraband, excise men, all that romance." He shook his head fondly. "Rubbish, of course. Men like Whitcombe don't dirty their hands. They only sign the papers that let dirtier men work. Funny, isn't it—how a signature can hang a man quicker than a pistol."

Ansel's eyes came up. Held his. Black eyes, unreadable as deep water. "Is that so, sir."

"It is so." Garrick smiled and gathered the dead regulator's parts into their box, piece by piece, unhurried. "Mind yourself at that tower. Big clocks keep big secrets. Best to fix what you're asked to fix and leave the rest where it fell." He snapped the lid shut—a small sharp sound, like a lock taking. "Speaking of which. When you're finished with the master clock, I'll want the case interior swept. All that felt packing sheds. Dust gets everywhere."

He watched the boy hear it. Watched the pupils contract, fractionally, the way they do in sudden light. *There,* Garrick thought, with the cold satisfaction of a man feeling a mechanism engage at last, tooth meeting tooth. *There you are.*

"Swept," Ansel repeated. "Yes, sir."

"And Ansel." Garrick kept his voice light, almost tender, and laid his hand briefly on the boy's shoulder—the first time in months he had touched him, and he felt the boy go rigid as a suspended pendulum beneath his palm. "If you should find anything up there that doesn't belong to the clock—a tool of mine, a paper, some trifle dropped by a previous workman—you'd bring it to me, wouldn't you? Rather than trouble anyone else with it. We look after our own here. That's what this shop is *for*."

The silence lasted two seconds. Garrick counted them, as he counted everything.

"Of course, sir," Ansel said. "If I find anything."

*If.* Such a small, well-made word. It slipped past a lie detector smoother than polished steel, and it told Garrick everything, because honest boys don't reach for *if*. Honest boys say *when*, or *yes*, or *what sort of thing?*

"That's all," Garrick said warmly. "Get to the bench. Mrs. Pellow's mantel clock won't fix itself, and neither will your reputation, sitting idle as it has."

Ansel went. Unrolled his tools. Bent his dark head over the little movement, and began, with those infuriatingly perfect hands, to work.

Garrick returned to the window and his dead regulator and his polishing cloth, and for a long while neither of them spoke, and the shop ticked around them with all its small honest voices, and Garrick thought about escapements. About how the entire secret of a clock lay in the fact that its most important part did nothing but *release*—catching the train, letting it slip one tooth at a time, controlling ruin by rationing it. Power, properly managed, was just destruction arriving slowly enough to be useful.

The boy had his letter. Almost certainly. And Garrick could not take it back by force—not without questions, not without the boy screaming, not without the whole town remembering the strange foreign apprentice had a grievance. No. Force was for amateurs. What was needed was timing: to know where the boy would run with it, and to be there first—or to make certain that wherever he arrived, he arrived carrying blame instead of evidence.

After supper, when Ansel had gone, Garrick unlocked the ledger drawer and did not open the ledger. He opened instead the loose board beneath it, and took out the flannel-wrapped shape that had lain there four weeks: the count wheel gear from the observatory clock, clean and bright and damning, proof positive that the failure had been engineered. He turned it over in his hands. If the boy ever spoke, this gear was the thread that unraveled everything—which meant it could not stay here, and the boy could not be allowed to speak.

He wrapped it again, carefully, and set it aside, and then took pen and paper and wrote in a small disguised hand:

*To the gentleman at the pier guard, Thursday night. A lad will come, likely after dark or before dawn. Sixteen, seventeen years. Dark-eyed, foreign-looking, answers to Nakamura or to nothing. He carries a letter that is not his and never was. Stop him gently. Search him thoroughly. Send word to me before you send word to anyone.*

He sealed it with plain wax, no crest. Then he put on his coat, turned the shop sign, and stood a moment in the doorway breathing the cold harbor air, listening to the gulls argue over the water. Somewhere down the row, the Whitcombe house would be lighting its lamps, setting its table, laying another place for the boy who belonged to them more than he knew. Garrick smiled at the dark, patiently, the way a clock smiles with its hands.

Everything could be saved, if you were willing to replace enough of what was rotten.

He locked the door behind him and walked toward the pier.
