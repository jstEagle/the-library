## 10. The Customs House Gate

The letter lay against Ansel's ribs like a second heart.

He had sewn it there himself the night before, working by candlelight in the back room of Garrick's shop with the door barred and his breath held at every creak of the settling timber. The tool roll's oilcloth had seemed too obvious—a parcel, a package, a thing a man might be asked to surrender—and so he had cut open the lining of his waistcoat instead, the one his father had worn to church and then passed to him along with everything else that remained of their life in Yokohama, and stitched the letter flat against the left panel, where a pocket watch would ordinarily ride. It fit there as though it belonged. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the master clock entire.

Now, in the grey half-light of a Tuesday morning in March, he walked down Fore Street with his collar up and his commission papers folded in his breast pocket—the legitimate ones, signed by the observatory trustees, authorizing *Ansel Nakamura, apprentice horologist, to attend upon instruments of precision within the harbour district as required for the maintenance of said observatory's timepiece*—and he tried to breathe the way Maren had taught him on the harbor wall the evening before.

*You're not doing anything wrong,* she had said, her hands jammed in the pockets of her father's borrowed coat, the wind pulling strands of hair loose across her face. *That's the whole trick of it, Ansel. You don't have to act innocent. You only have to remember that you are.*

He had wanted to believe her. He was trying still. But innocence, he was discovering, was like a watch spring: easy to describe, maddening to keep under tension without letting it snap.

The customs house stood at the landward end of the quay, a squat Georgian pile of grey stone that had been built, Ansel suspected, by an architect who believed that commerce should feel like penance. Two arched gates opened onto the cobbled yard where carts queued for inspection, and between them ran a wicket gate of black iron, attended by a wooden booth in which sat the gate clerk—a man named Prosser, if Maren's intelligence could be trusted, who had held the post for eleven years and considered every person passing through his gate to be, in some essential and unprovable way, attempting to steal from the Crown.

Prosser was at the booth this morning. Of course he was. Maren had confirmed it twice, once through her brother Tom, who delivered fish to the customs house kitchens, and once through simple observation from the top of Coal Exchange Lane, where she had stood yesterday afternoon with a basket of nothing in particular while Prosser took his supper of cold pie at the booth window and glared at the tide.

The plan was simple. That was what frightened Ansel most about it—that it depended on simplicity, on ordinary things happening in their ordinary order, when his whole nervous system was screaming that the world had tilted and everyone must surely see it in his face.

Maren would make her disturbance at the fishmonger's stall thirty yards up the quay, drawing the two gate guards—Dunmore and the young one, Ketch—who drifted toward any quarrel the way gulls drift toward gutting tables. Ansel would approach the wicket alone, present his papers, name his business: the regulator clock in the harbormaster's outer office had been losing four minutes a day, and the harbormaster himself had complained, and here were the papers, sir, and here was the work ticket, and no, he wouldn't be long, an hour perhaps, maybe two if the escapement needed cleaning.

Every word true. Every word prepared. And beneath the letter, warm against his skin, the lie that gave them all their shape.

He turned onto the quay.

The morning smelled of tar and herring and the green iron tang of low tide. The revenue ship rode at her mooring beyond the inner wall, her masts swaying gently against a sky the colour of dishwater, and Ansel made himself look at her only once, briefly, the way a man looks at a ship because ships are there to be looked at. Inspector Hale was somewhere aboard her—or would be by tomorrow's tide, which is why today mattered, which is why his pulse was drumming in his throat, which is why he counted his steps the way he counted gear teeth, one and two and three, because numbers did not tremble.

At the fishmonger's stall, Maren was already arguing.

She was magnificent. That was the first coherent thought he had. She stood before Mrs. Tregale's slab of iced mackerel with her arms crossed and her chin lifted, and even at forty yards Ansel could see the performance in the set of her shoulders—not a quarrel but a *production*, pitched precisely between indignation and grievance. Her voice carried over the cobbles in fragments: *"—sold me Thursday's catch called fresh, my mother's been ill and I've a mind to—"*

Mrs. Tregale, who had sold bad fish to half the town without ever once apologizing for it, rose to the bait like a pike after a fly. Her voice came back sharper. A small crowd of dockhands began to gather the way crowds do, not because they cared about mackerel but because someone else's trouble was the cheapest entertainment going.

One of the gate guards—Dunmore, broad and bored—turned his head. Turned his shoulders. Took two steps up the quay for a better view.

Ketch lingered. The young one lingered, hands in his pockets, watching Maren but also, damnably, watching the street.

*Don't look at him,* Ansel told himself. *Look at your papers. Look at the gate. Walk like a man with an appointment, because you are a man with an appointment.* He adjusted the strap of his tool bag on his shoulder—his real tools, his honest weight of screwdrivers and pliers and pin vises, and how strange that these should be his shield when for three years they had only been his chains—and he walked toward the wicket gate at the pace of a man thinking about escapements rather than ruin.

"Oi." Prosser's voice stopped him six feet short of the booth, as Ansel had known it would; the clerk liked to stop people just short, to make them come the last distance under his eye. "State your business."

Ansel looked up. Prosser had a face like a closed ledger, grey and ruled and ruled again, with small suspicious eyes set close above a nose that had been broken sometime in some previous decade of disapproving of things. His pen was in his hand. His book was open. Everything about him said: *I have written down better men than you.*

"The harbormaster's regulator, sir." Ansel heard his own voice come out level, quiet, faintly bored—which surprised him, since inside he felt like a kettle at full boil. He drew out the papers and the work ticket together, holding them the way Garrick had taught him to hold documents: thumb and forefinger, presentation-side forward, the gesture of a tradesman and not a supplicant. "Four minutes slow a day and gaining, Mr. Penhale says—he wrote it on the ticket himself. I'm to clean the movement and true the escapement. Shouldn't take above two hours."

Prosser took the papers without hurrying. He read them the way he presumably read everything, as though expecting to find contraband between the lines. His eyes went down the commission, across the trustee's signature, down to the work ticket, and Ansel watched him and thought, absurdly, of the master clock's dead escapement, of a wheel with its teeth deliberately filed away, of all the many ways a mechanism can be made to fail while looking perfectly sound.

"Nakamura." Prosser pronounced it carefully wrong, flattening it into something he could dismiss. His gaze lifted from the page to Ansel's face, and stayed there. "You're Garrick's boy."

"I'm apprenticed to Mr. Garrick, yes, sir."

"Half-foreign, aren't you."

It was not a question, and it was not polite, and Ansel felt the old flinch begin in his chest—the flinch that had lived in him since his first week in this country, since schoolyard stones and shop-door whispers, the reflex of a body that had learned that attention always preceded harm. He let it pass through him and out, the way Maren had shown him to let the wind pass through the harbor wall without moving it.

"Yes, sir," he said. "My father was Japanese. My mother was from Falmouth. Both gone now." Let Prosser chew on that. Grief was the one coin that even suspicious men hesitated to counterfeit.

Prosser grunted. Up the quay, Maren's voice climbed to a register of pure wounded virtue—*"—and I'll thank you to weigh it again in front of witnesses, madam, or I'll fetch the bailiff myself"*—and Dunmore had abandoned all pretense of duty, leaning on the bollard with his arms folded, grinning. Even Ketch's head had turned, pulled by the gravitational field of public spectacle.

But Prosser didn't turn. That was the terrible thing about Prosser: he didn't turn. He kept his small eyes on Ansel's face, and then—slowly, deliberately—they travelled downward, over the tool bag, over the coat, over the waistcoat, and Ansel felt that gaze like a hand pressed flat against his chest, directly over the letter, as though the man could read through wool and linen and oilcloth the way he read commission papers.

"You'll sign in," Prosser said. "Name, purpose, time of entry." He spun the ledger around and pushed it through the booth's slot, along with a pen whose nib was crusted brown. "And you'll sign out. If you're not out by noon, I come looking. We've had irregularities down this quarter lately." His mouth thinned into something that was not quite a smile. "Thieving, mostly. Papers going missing. You'd know something about that, being handy with small mechanisms."

The words landed harder than they should have. Harder than they were meant to, perhaps—unless they were meant exactly so, unless word had already traveled down from somewhere, unless a certain shopkeeper had already begun laying his traps. Ansel's hands wanted to shake and he would not permit it, so he took the pen and wrote his name in the ledger in his careful draftsman's hand, each letter placed like a jewel in a setting, and he thought: *this ink will outlive us both, Prosser, and someday it will say that I walked through your gate in daylight and told the truth on paper while the lie stayed sewn against my ribs.*

"Thank you, sir," he said aloud. "Noon."

Prosser pulled the papers back, glanced at them one final time, and thrust them at him. For a moment—one long, ringing moment—the clerk simply held them, Ansel tugging lightly at one corner, Prosser's grip firm on the other, the two of them connected by a foot of paper and the whole difference in their worlds.

"The harbormaster's clock," Prosser said slowly. "Funny thing. He never mentioned it to me."

"He mentioned it to the observatory, sir. I couldn't say what he mentions to whom." Ansel let the smallest, safest smile touch his mouth—the smile of a young man mildly bewildered by officialdom, the smile he had practiced in the dark glass of the shop window until it stopped feeling like a stranger's. "Clocks don't keep regular hours when they're failing. Neither do I, most weeks."

Something shifted in Prosser's face—not warmth, nothing like warmth, but the grudging recalibration of a man who has braced for evasion and found instead the tedious truth of trade. He released the papers. "Go on, then. Through the yard, up the outside stair, third door. Don't touch anything you weren't sent for."

"I never do, sir."

The wicket gate's lock was old and well-oiled and turned without a sound, and Ansel stepped through it, and the yard swallowed him.

* * *

He did not run. Running was for guilty men, and besides, there was nowhere to run to—the harbormaster's office was his destination, and the outside stair was exactly where Prosser said it would be, and so he walked the length of the cobbled yard past two carts of inspected barrels and one sleepy carter and the great grey flank of the customs house itself, and he climbed the iron stair with his hand steady on the rail, and he let himself in through the third door into a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paperwork.

The regulator clock stood in its glazed case against the far wall, a fine eight-day movement by Frodsham of London, and it was indeed running four minutes slow, because Ansel had made it so—two days ago, with a quarter-turn of the rating nut and a whisper of thickened oil, a sabotage so gentle the clock itself probably didn't know it had been wronged. It had bought him his reason to stand in this room. Now it bought him his hour.

He opened the case. He laid out his tools on the baize cloth with the ritual calm of his craft: loupe, tweezers, oiler, peg wood. He put the magnifier to his eye. And only then, with the movement half-disassembled before him and his hands deep in honest work, did his body finally file its report.

His hands began to shake.

Not at the gate—at nothing so dramatic as the gate—but now, here, in the safety of the third door, they shook like leaves in a gutter wind, and he had to set down the tweezers and press his palms flat against the workbench and breathe, in and out, counting the way Maren counted, until the trembling drained out through his fingers into the wood. His heart was going like a carriage at speed. His waistcoat clung to his back with sweat that had waited, all this time, for permission.

*I did it,* he thought, and the thought arrived strangely, without triumph, almost with grief. *I lied to a man's face and he believed me.* He searched himself for shame and found something more complicated: a cold, clear understanding that he would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, if Edmund Whitcombe's name required it of him. His father had taught him that silence kept people safe. His father had never taught him that sometimes keeping safe meant walking through a locked gate with a stolen truth against your ribs and a smile you'd rehearsed in a dark window.

Below the window, the commotion on the quay was dissolving. He could hear Maren's voice descending through its scales of outrage toward resolution—Mrs. Tregale, it appeared, had agreed to reweigh the mackerel before witnesses—and then Dunmore's laugh, and then the crowd dispersing like a dropped rope. In a minute or two she would stroll past the gate with her empty basket, and she would not look at the customs house, because looking was for amateurs, and she would walk home by Coal Exchange Lane, and Ansel would finish the regulator, and sign out, and the first checkpoint would be behind them.

Two more. The harbormaster's office tomorrow, with whatever Garrick's messengers were carrying. And then the pier.

He picked up the tweezers. He removed the balance cock. The little wheel sprang free and spun in its jeweled bearings, quick and bright and obedient, and Ansel bent over it with his loupe and let the work hold him the way it had always held him, since the first clock his father ever put in his hands—because gears did not care what blood ran in your veins, because a well-made movement asked only that you be patient, and precise, and present.

Outside, the tide was turning. He could hear it below the harbor wall, that vast slow exhale, and he thought of Inspector Hale aboard the revenue ship with seven years of grief folded in his coat, waiting—though he did not yet know it—for a letter that was, at this very moment, learning to beat in time with Ansel Nakamura's heart.

He had an hour of honest work ahead of him. He intended to spend every minute of it being exactly what his papers claimed.

It was the last hour, he understood, in which that would be entirely true.
