## 12. The Harbormaster's Office

The harbormaster's office sat at the elbow of the harbor wall like an old man leaning on a rail to watch the world go by. It was a squat stone building with a green door that had been repainted so many times the hinges could barely close, and above it a sign whose gilt letters had flaked down to ghosts: *HARBORMASTER — ALL VESSELS MUST REPORT*. Through the salt-fogged window Ansel could see a lamp burning inside, and behind the lamp, unmistakable even at this distance, the broad silhouette of Mr. Petch.

Ansel stopped in the lee of the fish-curing shed across the way and made himself breathe. The letter lay flat against his ribs, bound there by a linen bandage he had wound himself in the churchyard vestry with fingers that would not stop trembling until he pretended they belonged to someone else—a trick his father had taught him for delicate work: *give the hands a fiction and they will perform it*. The fiction had held through the winding. It held now, mostly.

He had rehearsed this. He and Maren had rehearsed it twice over the tide tables, her finger tracing the route while he spoke his cover aloud until the words wore smooth as river stones: he had been commissioned to repair the observatory's master clock; the clock required a replacement mainspring of a particular weight; the only merchant who stocked such springs received his crates off the morning boats; therefore Ansel Nakamura must speak with the harbormaster regarding which vessels had berthed and which were expected before the tide turned. Every word true except the one that mattered. A lie built from truths, Maren had said, is a bridge no eye can catch the joints of.

*Give the hands a fiction.* Give the whole self a fiction, then, and walk through the green door.

He crossed the lane. The morning was gray and soft, gulls wheeling over the masts, the harbor breathing its cold smell of tar and weed and herring. A boy with a barrow of kelp rattled past without looking at him—one small mercy, to be unlooked-at—and then the door was before him, and it stuck on its swollen frame exactly as it always had, and he shouldered it open as if he had done it a hundred times, because he had done it a hundred times, delivering Garrick's invoices, collecting parts, standing invisible in corners while men talked over his head. That was the strangest thing about deception, he thought as the warmth of the stove hit him: how much of it was simply doing what you had always done.

"Ah." Mr. Petch looked up from his ledger. He was a heavy man gone soft at the edges, with a face like risen dough and small quick eyes that were not soft at all, eyes that had counted every hull and hauled every man too clever by half out of these waters. "The clock boy."

"The clock boy," Ansel agreed, and heard his own voice come out level, faintly wry, as though the name amused him rather than filed him down. "Ansel Nakamura. Garrick's shop, on Chapel Lane."

"I know where the shop is." Petch set down his pen with deliberation. "Been up the observatory, I hear. Fixing the old girl."

"That's the commission." Ansel moved toward the counter, unhurried, letting his tool roll show at his shoulder where any eye could see it—the honest burden of a workingman. "I need a mainspring. Particular weight, particular temper. The merchant who stocks them ships them in with the coastal trade, and I've lost three days already to the fault. I wanted to ask which boats have berthed since Thursday, and whether the *Cormorant* is expected on the morning tide."

"*Cormorant* runs out of Falmouth." Petch said it idly, but Ansel felt the small quick eyes pass over him like a hand searching pockets. "You'll want the manifest shelf, third from the window. Help yourself. Paperwork's public enough to a lad with a commission."

Relief rose in him like water up a well shaft, and he throttled it down, because relief was the moment men got careless. He went to the shelf. He drew out the ledger for the week and began turning pages he did not need to read, and behind him Petch returned to his own writing, pen scratching, stove ticking, gulls crying somewhere beyond the fogged glass. For four or five minutes it was almost ordinary. Ansel let his shoulders drop a fraction. He noted, in passing, that the *Cormorant* was indeed listed—expected Friday morning, cargo general—and that Inspector Hale's revenue ship rode at anchor in the roads, its name entered with the dry notation *detained, customs business*. Detained. Maren's word had been right. Hale had held the ship, and a held ship could be held a little longer, if the letter reached it.

Then the door banged open, and the cold came in wearing a boy.

He was perhaps sixteen, red-cheeked from running, one of the errand lads who haunted the lanes with notes and parcels for anyone with a penny. He clutched a folded paper in his fist and looked around the office with the blind urgency of the employed.

"Mr. Petch? Message from Mr. Garrick, the clockmaker. Urgent-like. He said it couldn't wait on ceremony."

Ansel did not move. He understood, in the space of a single heartbeat, with a clarity that arrived like cold water finding a crack, exactly what that paper contained. Garrick had searched—or had not needed to search, had merely watched Ansel leave with his collar buttoned high on a cold morning—and had done the arithmetic. *Stop anyone answering the apprentice's description.* And what description traveled faster than any constable's memory? Paper. A note passed to every checkpoint between Chapel Lane and the pier, naming the thief and the theft.

His body wanted certain things. It wanted to turn and look at the boy, which it must not do; wanted to set down the ledger, which it must not do yet; wanted to run, which it absolutely must not do. Instead Ansel gave his hands their fiction. He kept turning pages. Turn, read nothing, turn.

Petch took the note, unfolded it, and read.

The silence that followed lasted three seconds and cost Ansel a year. He kept his eyes on the manifests—the *Doris Ellen*, ballast out, timber in; the *Marguerite*, coasting coal—and listened to his own heart with the detached interest of a man timing an escapement. Tick. Tick. Even a broken clock keeps some kind of time.

"Well, now," Petch said.

"Sir?" The boy hovered at the door.

"You can go. Tell your master the message is received." Petch folded the paper once, slowly, along its original crease, and did not put it away. He held it in one broad hand and looked across the office at Ansel's back, and Ansel felt the look land between his shoulder blades like a laid palm. "Nakamura."

He turned. There was no use pretending otherwise—Petch had called him, and a man who failed to answer his own name confirmed everything. So he turned, and arranged his face into mild inquiry, and discovered that the arrangement held. That was the terrible discovery, the one he would carry out of this room and live with afterward: the face held. Somewhere in him a mechanism engaged, smooth and quiet as brass on oil, and produced an expression of polite attention belonging to an innocent man.

"You'd say," Petch said, settling back in his chair, which groaned under the shift of him, "that you came in for a mainspring."

"I did come in for a mainspring." True. Let the first word be true; it steadied all the rest.

"And you'd know nothing of any letter gone missing from your master's premises."

The stove ticked. The gulls cried. The boy's footsteps faded up the lane, taking the ordinary world with them.

"A letter?" Ansel said.

It was not denial—not quite, not crudely. It was the tone of a man offered information too incomplete to respond to, and it bought him the half-second he needed to watch Petch's face and learn what the note actually said. Because Petch was not reaching for the bell, not calling for the guard at the door, not doing any of the things a harbormaster does when handed a thief. He was watching. Weighing. His small eyes moved over Ansel the way they must have moved over strange hulls in fog, estimating tonnage, draft, whether the vessel would bear her load or founder.

"Gone missing," Petch repeated. "A sealed letter. My colleague writes—" he glanced at the paper, though he plainly knew it by heart, "—that a letter containing matters touching upon harbor business was taken from the shop sometime yesterday. Taken by persons unnamed. But described, near enough." His gaze flicked to Ansel's shoulders, his dark hair, the tool roll. "Near enough."

"Matters touching upon harbor business." Ansel made himself frown, and the frown too came readily, and he marveled distantly at the facility of it, the way one marvels at a limb moving after frostbite. "Mr. Petch, I've known Mr. Garrick six years and he has never trusted me with a letter in my life. He doesn't trust me with the till. If something of his is gone missing, I'm sorry for it, but I'd be the last soul in this town he'd trust to steal it—he says so often enough when I'm within earshot." He paused, and let something almost like hurt cross the arrangement of his face, because hurt was true as well, six years deep and true. "What was in this letter, that the harbormaster needs telling of it?"

It was a risk. Questions were risks; questions invited answers, and answers might contain hooks. But he had seen the hesitation in Petch, and he understood its shape now: Garrick's note had been urgent, vague, and slightly beneath the dignity of the office. *Stop a boy on a harbormaster's say-so, on a clockmaker's word alone, without charge or witness*—Petch was not a man who moved fast for other men's conveniences. He was a man who liked to know what he was hauling aboard before he cut the line.

"Nothing to do with you, in any case," Petch said slowly.

"Then I'll get back to my spring." Ansel turned to the shelf—an economy of movement, closing the exchange—then thought better of it, and gave Petch the whole of his face again, candid, faintly bored. "Is the *Cormorant* likely Friday, or shall I send to Falmouth?"

That did it. Nothing disarms suspicion like a grievance about delivery times. Petch grunted, pulled the current manifest toward himself, and ran a blunt finger down the column. "*Cormorant*—Friday, weather permitting. But you're in luck, boy. The *Merrow* came in Tuesday out of Plymouth, and she carries marine fittings regular. Ask for Tregaskis at the chandlery opposite. If he hasn't your spring he'll know who does."

"The *Merrow*," Ansel said gratefully. "Thank you. Truly. Three days lost—I'll be up the hill all night as it is."

"Mind how you go, then." Petch's eyes lingered on him a moment longer, and Ansel bore it the way one bears the probing of a dentist's instrument: still, breath shallow, waiting for it to be done. Then the harbormaster shrugged, an avalanche in miniature, and returned to his ledger. "Tell Garrick he still owes me for the mooring buoy, spring of 'ten. He thinks I've forgotten."

"He never forgets what he owes," Ansel said. "Only what he's owed."

The words were out before he could weigh them—too easy, too familiar, the kind of joke apprentices make about masters and masters pretend not to hear. Petch barked a laugh, short and surprised, and waved him off with the pen, and Ansel gathered the tool roll onto his shoulder and walked to the green door with his pulse hammering so hard against the linen bandage that he fancied the letter itself had learned to beat.

* * *

Outside, the fog was thinning. The masts stood sharper against the gray, and down the length of the harbor wall he could see the revenue ship riding at anchor, dark-hulled, patient, her pennant hanging slack. Too far to reach unseen; too near to fail now.

He made himself walk, not run. Past the chandlery, where he paused—cover demanded it—to inquire after a mainspring of a fat, aproned man who scratched his head and promised nothing useful. Past the net-drying racks, where two fishermen nodded to him, or perhaps to the air beside him. The letter burned against his ribs. Not hot, exactly—it was only paper and ink, sealed again with wax he had melted and pressed in the vestry, imperfect, a forgery of a forgery—but it seemed to radiate, seemed to have weight beyond its ounces, the way a gear removed from a clock still governs the machine it left. Every step he took was a tooth engaging. Every breath, another.

He had lied. That was the thing that kept surfacing, that he kept pressing down and it kept rising, patient as cork. Not a stammered excuse, not a child's obvious fib, but a constructed, sustained, fluent lie, delivered with jokes in it, delivered *well*. Six years of swallowing his tongue in Garrick's shop, six years of being the boy who said nothing because saying anything was dangerous, and it turned out the silence had been practice. All those hours of holding still had taught him exactly how still a liar must sit. His father's voice, somewhere: *a quiet mouth and busy hands, Ansel, and the world will let you pass*. He had meant it as shelter. He had not meant it as armor for this.

But the Whitcombes would hang for it—or worse than hang; be ruined, transported, scattered, Edmund's laugh silenced, the boys' names made filth, Maren—Maren, who had looked at him across the tide tables last night and said, *you are not the letter's keeper, you're ours*—and the lie was smaller than that. The lie was one room wide and three minutes long. Against a family, against a town's whole appetite for blame, it was almost nothing. He told himself this walking the wall, and it was true, and it did not make the taste go away. Deception, it turned out, was not a door you went through once. It was a climate. He would carry the chill of that office for a long time, he suspected, no matter how the matter ended.

At the bend of the wall, where the stone gave onto the lower quay, he stopped and looked back once. The green door stood shut. In its window, Mr. Petch's silhouette bent over his ledger—and beside it, just entering the frame, a second silhouette, tall, narrow-shouldered, familiar in the way a splinter is familiar to the flesh.

Garrick had not waited for the harbormaster to do his work. Garrick had come to see it done.

Ansel stepped back from the line of the window, into the shadow of the capstan house, and stood very still among the coiled ropes and the smell of hemp, and watched through the gap between bollards as his master was shown into the office, as the door closed behind him, as the two silhouettes merged into one conference against the lamplight. How long had Garrick known? Since last night—since the search of his room that Ansel had not witnessed but could reconstruct piece by piece: the lifted mattress, the shaken tool roll, the emptied trunk. Since the note sent ahead at dawn. Since before, perhaps. Predatory patience, Maren had called it, and she had been right; a man who repairs time for a living knows how to spend it.

They would compare accounts now. Petch would describe the boy who asked after the *Cormorant*, who complained about lost days, who joked about his master's debts—and Garrick would hear the shape of it and understand that the boy had walked through the trap and out the other side, smiling. And then the game would change. No more checkpoints, no more descriptions passed on paper. Garrick would come himself. Or he would send someone worse.

The revenue ship lay at anchor in the roads, and between Ansel and that ship stretched the whole exposed length of the lower quay, the pier gate, and the guard posted there—the third and last checkpoint, the one no distraction could be staged for now, because Garrick knew, because they all knew, because his description was no longer paper but pursuit.

Ansel settled the tool roll on his shoulder, checked the linen bandage with one flat hand, and slipped out the seaward side of the capstan house, into the thinning fog, walking fast toward the only place left to go.
