## 14. Garrick Closes the Shop

The bell above the shop door had not rung all morning, and Garrick found the silence intolerable.

He had lived with silence for thirty years in this shop—the tick and tock of a hundred mechanisms filling it, true, but that was a living silence, a working one, the sound of time being kept honestly. This was another kind entirely. This was the silence of a thing already done, waiting only to be discovered.

Ansel had not come back.

Garrick stood at his workbench with a loupe pressed to his eye, pretending to examine the escapement of a carriage clock he had no interest in, and listened to the absence of the boy. Ansel was ordinarily quiet—had been quiet from the first day, three years ago, when Edmund Whitcombe had practically deposited the half-orphaned lad on Garrick's doorstep with a letter of introduction and a look that said *you will take him, and you will be glad of it*. Quiet hands, quiet feet, quiet way of moving through a room as though apologizing for the space he occupied. Garrick had told himself for three years that he barely noticed the boy's presence.

He noticed its absence now the way one notices a stopped clock in an otherwise empty house.

*He has it.* The certainty had been growing in him since dawn, since he'd climbed the stairs to check the boy's cot and found the blankets thrown back, the tool roll gone from under the mattress where Ansel hid everything he cared about. Gone out before light. Gone out without word. And last night—that conversation on the stairs, the boy's face pale as tallow in the lamplight, those dark eyes refusing to meet his own. *You wanted to ask me something, sir?* No. That wasn't what he'd wanted to ask at all. He'd wanted to say something. He'd been trying to decide whether to say it.

And now he was gone, and the letter was gone—or rather, the letter was exactly where Garrick had left it, wedged behind the escapement of the observatory's master clock, but it was gone from *safety*, which amounted to the same thing. Because if the boy had found it—and he must have, must have found it yesterday while his fingers were inside that housing for six hours, clever fingers, the best hands Garrick had ever trained, God rot him—if the boy had found it, then the whole careful architecture of the thing was standing on one leg.

Garrick set down the loupe. His hand was steady. He noted this with a kind of detached professional approval, the way he might note the steady hand of a colleague at a difficult repair. Thirty years of delicate work gave a man that: hands that did not shake when the world did.

He went up the narrow stair to the room above the shop.

It was a mean little room, and he had never once felt anything about that. A cot, a washstand, a shelf of books—the boy read voraciously, in English and in his father's Japanese, filling margins with notes in both hands like some kind of scholar rather than a tradesman. A chair. On the windowsill, a small tin of tea his father had sent before dying, hoarded now past any sensible use, the leaves surely long stale. The room smelled of lamp oil and pencil shavings and, faintly, of the sea that came up over the roofs from the east.

Garrick searched it the way he would search a movement for a fault: systematically, without sentiment.

Mattress first—he had already checked beneath it, but he stripped the ticking now and ran his palms through the straw. Blankets folded into quarters, shaken out. Under the floorboard by the window, loose—he knew it was loose; he had loosened it himself years ago and used it for the ledger pages he could not let a clerk see—and nothing there but dust and a dead moth. The boy's own small cache was elsewhere, under the mattress, and it was empty except for the things that meant nothing: a stub of pencil, a broken mainspring he was too stubborn to discard, a pressed flower that made Garrick's lip curl. Sentiment. The boy was full of it, whatever else he was.

The coat on the peg. The pockets turned out. A fare token for the tram. Half a biscuit wrapped in paper. Nothing.

Garrick stood in the middle of the small room with the coat still hanging from his fist, and admitted it plainly, the way he admitted a diagnosis: the letter was not here because the boy was carrying it. The boy had taken the letter somewhere, or was going to take it somewhere, and there was only one somewhere that mattered. The harbor. The revenue ship. The tide.

He had learned the tide tables by heart two weeks ago, when the arrangement was first made. High water at seven minutes past six in the morning. The revenue vessel would stand off the pier until slack water, no longer. Everything had been timed to it: the clock sabotaged to draw the observatory's attention, the repair commissioned—through the trustees, through channels, so neatly—to put the boy's own hands inside that case, so that when the letter surfaced bearing the Whitcombe name, it would be *the apprentice who found it*, the foreign boy with the suspicious face whom half the town already eyed sideways. Who better to have discovered such a thing? Who less likely to be believed when he denied planting it?

It had been elegant. It had been, Garrick thought bitterly, the finest piece of work he had ever done, and he could not even sign it.

And now the boy had the letter, and instead of running—as Garrick had calculated he might, as any sensible creature would, taking the incriminating thing as far from himself as his legs would carry him—the boy appeared to be walking toward the harbor. Toward the customs men. Toward Hale.

Garrick hung the coat back on its peg, smoothing the shoulders flat. He was surprised at himself, that small gesture of tidiness, and then not surprised at all. One left a workbench clean. Whatever else happened, one left things orderly.

* * *

Downstairs, he did not light the lamps. The shop knew how to conduct its business in half-dark; he had worked by winter light for decades and needed no more than what came through the smoky panes to find what he wanted. From beneath the false bottom of the parts drawer—the same drawer where he kept the gold leaf and the French jewels—he took a sheet of paper that had waited there for a fortnight, folded twice, addressed in a hand that was not his own.

It had been written for him in advance, which he had found presumptuous at the time. *For when you are certain,* the man at the Anchor and Chain had said, sliding it across the table with a pint, *because there is no second version. There is the plan, and there is the gallows, and nothing between.*

Garrick unfolded it now and read it again by the grey light. It named the pier. It named Sergeant Coyle. It described the boy—not hard to describe; the town had been describing him for three years, and always with the same handful of words, *the Nakamura lad*, *the half-breed clockmaker's boy*, as though there were only one of him in the world, as though his face were not precisely the sort of face a frightened port town remembers whether it wishes to or not. *Stop anyone answering this description at the third checkpoint. Detain quietly. Send word to the Anchor and Chain.*

Quietly. That word had done its work on Garrick over the fortnight, settling into him like damp into stone. He understood what it meant. He had chosen, fourteen days ago in the back room of the Anchor and Chain, not to look at what it meant, and he chose not to look at it now. He was fifty-eight years old. He had buried a wife and outlived his trade's best years, watched cheap American movements flood the market and honest men's shops close one by one down the high street, until his own name over the door had come to feel like an inscription on something. They had offered him money enough to leave. Money enough to start again in Southampton, or further, where no one knew the shop had failed or why. All he had to do was write one letter and place it in one clock, and let other men do the rougher business of carrying it the rest of the way.

He had told himself, in the beginning, that no one would be hurt. That was the first lie, and he had known it was a lie even as he told it, because he had sat across from those men and seen their hands and heard the word *quietly* before he ever saw it written. But the lie had been necessary, the way a wedge is necessary to open a case that must be opened. You did not blame the wedge.

The Whitcombes would be ruined, not killed. He clung to that. Ruined commercially, ruined in reputation, their shipping interests seized or starved—and the harbor trade rerouted south to Falmouth where his paymasters' friends held the leases. Edmund Whitcombe would survive it; men like Whitcombe always survived. And it served him right, in a way Garrick had carefully never examined, because it had been Whitcombe's letter of introduction that had saddled him with the boy in the first place, Whitcombe's charity that had made Garrick a keeper of other men's dependents—

No. That was not why, and he knew it was not why, and the knowledge sat in him like a swallowed pin. It had nothing to do with the boy. It had nothing to do with gratitude or grievance. It was money, and fear, and the plain animal desire to not go under with the rest of his trade. He would not dress it up. Dressing things up was for men with more future than he had.

There was one matter to settle, though, before he wrote his addition to the prepared note. Because the printed instruction covered the letter; it did not cover the boy. And the boy was the flaw in the design, the removed gear that stopped the train. If Ansel reached the ship, if Ansel spoke to Hale, then the letter's contents would be read against the truth, and the truth had a way—in Garrick's long experience of human mechanisms—of catching on the teeth.

He took a fresh sheet and wrote, in his own neat craftsman's hand, the lines he had composed sometime in the black hours before dawn, when he had lain awake listening to the shop tick below him like a heart:

*Messrs. Coyle & party, Pier Three — The bearer, my late apprentice, has taken monies and stock from the premises, among them a lady's watch in a gold case entrusted to me for repair, and papers belonging to a client whose name I am not free to give. He is not to be harmed. He is to be held, and I am to be sent for, and the papers returned to me unopened. I will answer for the rest. — T. Garrick, Clockmaker, Fore Street.*

*I am not to be harmed.* He stared at that clause a long moment, quill suspended. It was the single decent line in the document, and he despised himself for it, and he did not strike it out. Let it stand. It changed nothing—it bound no one—but it stood, and somehow it was the price of his continuing, the small coin he paid himself to keep his hand steady.

Unopened. That was the load-bearing word. If the letter came back to him unopened, he could re-wedge it, re-time the whole mechanism, find another pair of hands. If it was opened—if it passed into Hale's hands—then the train was stopped for good, and every wheel between here and Southampton would come crashing down on the man who had cut the gear.

He folded the note, sealed it with a plain wafer, and put on his hat.

* * *

The boy who ran errands along the harbor for a penny was called Tam Osgood, eleven years old, gap-toothed, and utterly without curiosity, which made him the most valuable messenger in the town. Garrick found him mackerel-fishing off the steps by the fish market, handed him the sealed packet and a sixpence, and gave his instructions in the flat tone he used for dictating invoices.

"To the sergeant on Pier Three. Into his hand, no one else's. Don't read it, don't lose it, and if anyone asks who sent you, you'll say a gentleman you don't know."

Tam pocketed the sixpence and the packet with equal indifference and was gone, rod trailing, before Garrick had finished speaking. Good. The less said the better. The message would pass through four hands and arrive denuded of origin, and Sergeant Coyle would know exactly what it meant, because Coyle had been paid to know.

That was done, then. Garrick walked back up Fore Street slowly, past the ironmonger's and the chandler's and the bakery with its breath of warm bread spilling into the cold street, and he looked at the town the way a man looks at a house he is leaving. Not with love—he had never loved the town, and the town had never loved him—but with the particular attention of long tenancy. He knew which flagstone rocked outside the post office. He knew the exact hour the light came down the street's throat in June. He had repaired, over thirty years, a clock or a watch in nearly every house on this hill, and he had listened, in this shop, to half the secrets in the parish, told casually across the counter by people who forgot a clockmaker was a kind of confessor, that men bring their time to you and with it their lives. He had sold none of those secrets. Until now he had sold none of those secrets.

But the smuggling intelligence—that had been different, hadn't it? He had told himself it was different. Names in a ledger, dates in a letter, a conspiracy already seven years old and seven years dead, its ruin already accomplished in the person of a customs officer drowned at Plymouth with his career wrecked ahead of him. Selling the past, he had told himself. Not the present. Not anyone who would bleed.

The brother's name had been in the margins of the letter he wrote. He had included it because his paymasters wanted the bait rich, because grief makes the sharpest hooks. He had thought of Inspector Hale perhaps twice in fourteen days, and both times had thought of him as an instrument, a man positioned conveniently at the end of the plot like a clamp at the end of a repair, holding everything closed until the glue set.

He was thinking of him differently now.

At the shop door, Garrick paused with the key in the lock. Through the glass he could see the dim interior, the tall case clocks standing against the walls like a congregation, pendulums swinging in their slow disordered agreement, no two keeping quite the same hour, all of them certain they were right. He had spent his life among them and had found it restful. Now, for the first time, he heard it as it was: a room full of machines each insisting on its own private time, and no master among them.

Inside, he moved quickly. The ledger went into his valise—all of it, including the pages under the floorboard upstairs; a fire on the Southampton boat would finish what ink began. Cash from the till, but not all of it; he counted out what the shop owed its suppliers through month's end and left it in the drawer under the weight paper, because he was not a thief of his own creditors, because a man must keep some account straight even at the end. His tools he left. They were the shop's tools. He took only the loupe from his waistcoat pocket, and then, after a moment, put that back in the drawer too, and laughed once—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it—at himself for the gesture.

Last of all, he wrote the note that would wait for Ansel on the workbench, propped against the carriage clock, weighted with the brass key to the front door.

*Ansel — Your apprenticeship is ended. The premises are closed and the lease surrendered. For the thefts discovered upon your leaving, I have informed the authorities, and the record stands against your name. Do not return. If you return, the record grows. — G.*

He read it over twice. It was cruel, and it was airtight, and it was the only wall he knew how to build. The boy had no family, no advocate but the Whitcombes—who would shortly be too deep in their own ruin to help him—and no proof of anything except a habit of being where suspicion settled. If he came back, the note would hold him at the door like a trip-bar. If he stayed away, it would follow him to whatever town he fetched up in, because Garrick would make sure it followed him. Either way the boy was caged. Either way he was silent.

Garrick set the note down square against the clock, adjusted it a quarter inch, adjusted it back, and stepped away.

At the door he turned for one last look. The shop lay in its half-light, forty faces of forty clocks giving back the grey of the windows, and the smell of oil and cedar and dust, the smell he had breathed longer than any other in his life. He thought—absurdly, unwelcome—of the boy's first week here, twelve years old and straight-backed as a lance, holding a movement up to the light and saying, in his careful English, *the pallet stones are worn, sir, may I show you?* May I show you. As if Garrick were the one who might learn something. He had laughed at that, privately, for a year.

He pulled the door shut. The bell rang once, bright and brief, and died.

The key turned, and the tumblers fell into place with the small decisive click he knew better than his own heartbeat, and Thomas Garrick stood a moment on his own doorstep in the falling afternoon light, a man who had just finished the worst repair of his career and knew, the way he always knew, that the fault was deeper than the symptom—that somewhere in the train, a tooth was already cracked that he had not cut, that the stoppage would not hold, that time kept by dishonest means does not merely run slow but runs wrong, gaining and gaining until the hour it strikes belongs to no one.

Then he picked up his valise and walked down toward the harbor, to the Anchor and Chain, to wait for word from the pier.

Behind him, in the darkened shop, forty clocks went on telling forty different hours to no one at all—and on the workbench, propped against the carriage clock, the note bore his signature, and the brass key caught the last of the light, and the door that had opened for three years to a quiet boy's careful knock would open next, come morning, to whoever reached it first.
