## 15. Night Watch

The rendezvous point was the old coal shed behind the customs house wall, where the stones had gone black with a century of soot and the door hung on one hinge like a jaw left slack. Maren had chosen it herself, three nights ago, tracing the route on the back of her father's tide tables while Ansel held the lamp. *Here,* she'd said, tapping the map. *No windows facing it. No lamps near it. If anything goes wrong at the harbormaster's, you come here and I'll be waiting.*

He had smiled at that—the small, crooked smile he made when someone trusted him with something—and said, *You make it sound as though things go wrong often.*

*In this town,* she'd answered, *things go wrong on schedule.*

Now she stood in the shed's cold dark with her pocket watch open in her palm, and the schedule had broken.

Half past eleven. The moon was a thin paring of rind over the harbor, too low to throw light, and the wind came off the water smelling of kelp and tar. She'd been there since ten o'clock, first leaning against the shed's warped planking, then pacing its length—eleven steps one way, eleven steps back—then standing at the gap in the wall where she could see the lane that led up from the town toward the harbormaster's office.

The lane stayed empty.

She told herself the things a sensible person tells herself. That checkpoints took time. That Ansel was careful—Ansel was the most careful person she had ever met, a boy who could spend four hours truing an escapement wheel without once breathing hard. That her father's clock at home had chimed midnight twenty minutes ago and perhaps she'd miscounted, perhaps it was not yet so late.

But there was another voice underneath, quieter and colder, and it said: *You watched him walk out of the churchyard at nine. You counted his steps down the hill. He should have been here by half past ten, and it is now half past eleven, and the tide turns at four.*

The letter had to reach Inspector Hale before the *Meridian* cast off. Not by noon, not by breakfast—by the turn of the tide, or the ship sailed and the conspiracy sailed with it, free, and the Whitcombe name went into the customs records as a smuggler's name forever. They had planned for delays at two checkpoints. They had planned for Maren's detour to Hale's lodgings. They had not planned for nothing at all—no footsteps, no whistle signal, no scrap of chalk on the wall where they'd agreed marks would be left.

The wall was blank. She checked it for the ninth time anyway.

Maren Whitcombe was not, by temperament, a girl who waited well. Her father called it impatience; her brothers called it a curse; her mother, when she was alive, had called it *your father in skirts*, which always made Edmund laugh until he coughed. But this was not impatience now. This was something with teeth in it, a feeling that paced inside her chest the way she paced inside the shed, and it would not sit, and it would not be reasoned with.

It said: *Go and find him.*

And underneath it, quieter still, the voice of simple arithmetic: if Ansel had been taken—if Garrick had done something, said something, sent word ahead—then every minute she spent being sensible was a minute off Ansel's ledger, not her own. She could stand in a coal shed being careful until dawn and live with the shape of what that care had cost, or she could go into the town where people knew her face and her name and her father's name, where a Whitcombe abroad after midnight was itself a kind of accusation—and do *something*.

Her mother had died being careful about the wrong doctor. Maren did not put much stock in careful anymore.

She closed her watch, pulled her shawl tight, and slipped out through the gap in the wall.

* * *

The town at midnight was a different country. By day Harbor Row shouted and smelled and jostled; by night it held its breath, the shops shuttered, the lamps burned down to blue nubs, and the only sounds were rigging ticking against masts far below and her own boots on the cobbles—which sounded, no matter how softly she stepped, enormous.

She kept to the darker side of the street, past the fishmonger's where she'd staged her quarrel that morning, past the chandler's window where coils of rope sat like sleeping animals. Twice she heard voices and flattened herself into a doorway: once it was only a cat overturning a bin, once two sailors reeling home singing very badly and very quietly, as though even their drunkenness observed the hour.

Garrick's shop sat at the top of Cutwater Lane, wedged between the ironmonger and a house that had been empty since the winter. From the corner Maren stopped and looked at it the way you look at a dog you suspect of going mad.

Dark. Shutters drawn. And yet—

The shutters were drawn *wrong*. Ansel had told her once, with the shy pride he took in his craft, that a clockmaker's shop closed like a clockmaker's shop: each shutter seated in its groove, the bolts thrown in a particular order, the whole thing done with the same precision Garrick demanded of him in everything else. It was a small vanity. Now she stared at the ground floor and saw that the left shutter stood proud of its frame at the bottom, a finger's width of blackness beneath it, as though someone had slammed it in haste or anger and not cared whether it sat true.

Her heart began a slow, heavy knocking.

The side door was the problem. It faced the alley, and the alley was open to the lane, and anyone at a window above the ironmonger's could look straight down into it. Maren stood in the shadow of the corner for a long moment—long enough that the cold found its way through her shawl and into the marrow of her shoulders—and made herself think like Ansel instead of like herself. What would he do? He would count the windows. He would wait for the wind. He would choose the moment the way he chose a gear: only the one that fit.

The wind came. A shutter somewhere clattered; a sign swung on its bracket up the lane. Maren crossed the alley in six strides and pressed herself flat against the shop's side wall, breath held, counting the windows above her. Nothing moved. Nothing called out.

The side door was locked. Of course it was locked. But the keyhole was old and the door older, and Maren had grown up in an observatory where every lock was a negotiation, and she had a hairpin, and she had eleven minutes of fumbling terror in which she learned precisely how loud her own heartbeat could become. Then the bolt gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and she was inside.

* * *

The dark of the shop was total. She did not dare a match. Instead she stood in the doorway and let her eyes adjust and let the room tell itself to her the way it told itself to Ansel every morning: the smell of oil and brass shavings and old wood, the tick of the regulator clock on the far wall—still running, still faithful, keeping time for a man who did not deserve it—the workbench a long pale shape in the gloom.

And on the workbench, one object that should not have been there.

A folded paper, weighted under the shop's largest loupe, positioned dead center, exactly where a lantern would fall if a lantern were lit. Even in the dark, even without reading it, Maren understood what it was. Some objects announce their purpose by their placement alone. This had been set out the way a trap is baited, squarely, patiently, in the path of whoever came looking.

She lifted the loupe. The paper came with it. She carried both to the window, where the thinnest thread of harbor-light seeped between the shutter slats, and angled the page until the words surfaced out of blackness like drowned things rising.

*To the Magistrate and Trustees of the Borough —*

*It is my duty to report that my apprentice, Ansel Nakamura, has this night absconded with property of mine: to wit, a sealed letter entrusted to my keeping by parties unknown, and certain tools of the trade valued at upwards of two pounds...*

The rest descended into legal boilerplate, but she read the first lines three times, because the mind resists believing some things on the first pass. *Absconded with property.* So neat. So reasonable. A concerned master, reporting a theft—reporting it in advance, in writing, addressed and ready to be delivered, dated tomorrow's date. Garrick had written the accusation before Ansel had even reached the second checkpoint. Perhaps before he'd left the churchyard. The letter was not evidence of a crime committed; it was evidence of a crime *prepared*, waiting like the removed gear had waited, like the sealed letter had waited, patient machinery in a patient machine.

And it meant—

Maren's hands went cold in a way the night hadn't managed. It meant Garrick knew Ansel had the letter. It meant Garrick knew Ansel would come back here—or that someone would—for tools, for money, for the spare coat Ansel kept behind the partition because the shop was always cold. The note wasn't only insurance. It was a snare set on the path home.

Which meant the description of the "stolen" letter had already gone wherever descriptions go. The harbormaster's office—where Ansel had walked out clean at dusk. And beyond it, the last checkpoint, the pier guard, the one they had saved for last because by then Ansel would be nearly safe, nearly at the gangway, nearly done.

Nearly.

*Stop anyone answering the apprentice's description.* She could almost hear the words being written in some office, copied onto some slate, passed to men with lanterns at the foot of the pier. Half-Japanese apprentice, slight build, carries a canvas tool roll. The whole town could describe Ansel Nakamura; describing him was the town's oldest pastime.

The tide turned at four. The pier guard changed at three—her father had mentioned it at supper, complaining about the new sergeant's punctuality—and if Ansel went to the pier at all, he would go close to sailing time, when the crowd of departing crew gave cover to strangers on the quay. Which gave her—she opened her watch, tilted it to the light—just over four hours. Four hours to reach a man who might already have been stopped, questioned, seized, handed over with the forged accusation presented like a receipt.

Or four hours to reach Inspector Hale aboard the *Meridian*, and bring him down the gangway to meet whatever was coming, so that when the trap closed, it closed on the conspirators instead.

Her legs were shaking. She noticed it with a strange detachment, as though observing someone else's trouble, and then she did what she had learned to do in the observatory on storm nights when her father was aloft and the wind was trying to take the roof: she let the shaking be there and did the next thing anyway.

Silence can be a choice. She'd told Ansel that on the ninety-ninth step, the night the great clock lay open between them like a body on a table. She had meant it as comfort then. Tonight it was a weapon, and she turned it on herself: *be silent, be swift, be seen by no one*—and she folded Garrick's note into her bodice (it was evidence now, real evidence, and it would hang him), and let herself out the side door, and ran.

* * *

She ran the way she had never run in daylight, where running was for boys and the unseemly—down Cutwater Lane with its cobbles slicked by sea-fog, along the dark throat of Quay Street, past the Harbormaster's Office where a single lamp still burned and a silhouette moved behind the glass and she flattened against the opposite wall and let the shadows eat her. Her lungs burned. Her shawl tried twice to strangle her and she tore it loose and knotted it around her waist like a fishwife and did not care.

The harbor opened below her at the bottom of the last stair, all silver-dark water and black hulls, and there—the third mast from the quay, taller than its neighbors, riding a hand higher than the rest—the *Meridian*, revenue cutter, His Majesty's ship, with a lantern burning at her bow rail like a single dropped star.

Between her and the gangway: the pier. And on the pier, at its waist, two figures with a brazier, coals glowing dull orange, the pier guard. One of them she recognized even at this distance by the way he stood, weight on one hip—Sergeant Coyle, the punctual one, the new one, the one whose appointment her father had found *sudden*, and who had, three weeks ago, asked Edmund Whitcombe in a voice of idle curiosity whether the observatory ever received goods by night.

Her father had laughed it off. Her father believed, still believed, that sudden questions were only questions.

Maren crouched at the top of the stair, chest heaving, and forced herself to think past the pounding of her own blood. She could not cross the pier openly—a Whitcombe girl on the quay at two in the morning would be remembered, questioned, possibly detained, certainly reported, and any report touching the Whitcombes tonight was a report that served Garrick's masters. She could not go around; the pier was the only road to the ship.

But the *Meridian* was not only reachable by the pier. Boats moved on water.

Forty yards along the quay, in the lee of the breakwater, the fishermen kept their punts, tied and bobbing, none of them locked because no thief alive could sell a punt without the whole harbor knowing by breakfast. Maren had rowed since before she could write. Her father used to say she could handle a boat in weather that made grown men religious.

She looked at the brazier once more—at Sergeant Coyle, warm and vigilant and bought—and felt something in her settle, harden, take on a fine clear edge. Fear was still there; she could feel it humming in her wrists. But fear, she understood suddenly, watching the bow lantern rock on the black water, was only love standing at attention. And hers had work to do.

She went along the quay in the breakwater's long shadow, found the lightest of the punts, and slipped the painter from its cleat.

The water took her silently. Out past the moorings, oars shipped, letting the current carry her the last stretch, she raised one hand and gripped the cold chain of the cutter's anchor buoy, and looked up—all the way up the black flank of the ship to the rail where the lantern burned—and drew breath to call out to the watch.

From shore, faint across the water, came the sound of voices raised—not angry yet, but sharpening, the tone of a question being asked a second time. At the pier head, where the gangway touched stone, a lantern was moving quickly. And in the middle of its small trembling circle of light, thin and slight, carrying a canvas roll, stood a figure the whole town could have named.

They had him at the third checkpoint after all.

Maren hauled herself up the buoy chain, hand over hand, and began to shout.
