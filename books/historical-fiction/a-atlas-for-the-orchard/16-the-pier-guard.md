## 16. The Pier Guard

The harbor at three in the morning was a different country.

Ansel had known it all his life in daylight—gulls screaming over the fish stalls, tar and salt and the shouts of men who never seemed to finish arguing—but he had never walked it in the small hours, when the water lay black and patient against the pilings and the only sounds were the creak of moored boats and his own footsteps ringing off wet stone. The revenue ship *Vigilant* rode at anchor beyond the pier head, a dark shape with one lantern burning at her rail, and that single point of light had become, over the last six hours, the most important thing in Ansel's world. Everything else—the customs house gate behind him, the harbormaster's office where he had lied for the first time in his life and discovered the lie came easily, which frightened him more than any of it—was behind him now. Ahead was only the guard at the foot of the pier, and beyond the guard, Inspector Hale, if Maren's intelligence held true, if Hale had come aboard as she'd learned he would, if the letter pressed flat and warm against Ansel's ribs under his coat could survive one more pair of eyes.

He had rehearsed this on the churchyard path, among the leaning headstones, while the last light died. He was an apprentice clockmaker. He had been sent to fetch a part from a chandler who kept marine chronometers. He had no business aboard the ship; he only wanted to reach the pier head, where the chandler's boy sometimes waited with parcels from the supply boats. It was thin. He knew it was thin. A lie, like a watch, was only as good as its smallest wheel, and his smallest wheels were worn nearly through.

The guard stood beneath the pier lamp with a lantern of his own hanging from a hook beside him, and he was not the bored sentry Ansel had hoped for. He was broad through the shoulders, younger than his sergeant's chevrons suggested, and he was reading something by the light of the lamp—a folded paper he did not put away when Ansel's boots sounded on the planks. He looked up. He looked at Ansel's face. And then, deliberately, he looked down at the paper again, and Ansel felt the whole night tilt.

"Evening," the guard said. Not a question. A door being opened slowly, to see what would try to come through it.

"Good evening," Ansel said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "I'm looking for the chandler's boy. Parsons. I'm told he waits near the pier head."

"Parsons." The guard folded the paper with great care, creasing each fold with his thumbnail. "At three in the morning."

"He keeps odd hours when the supply boats run."

"And you keep odder ones." The guard stepped away from the lamp, and the light fell full across Ansel—the tool roll slung at his hip, the salt-stiffened coat, the face that half this town knew and none of it trusted quite. "You're the Nakamura lad. Garrick's boy. The clockmaker's apprentice."

"I am," Ansel said. There was no use denying what every eye in the town confirmed. "I'm expected—"

"Expected." The guard smiled without warmth. "That's a word for it. Turn out your pockets."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way a room goes after a bell is struck. Ansel heard the water slapping the pilings. He heard his own pulse in his ears, faster than any escapement he'd ever regulated. Against his ribs, under his coat and waistcoat and shirt, the letter lay like a coal that had not yet cooled. If the guard found it—if he read even the direction on it—everything ended here, ten yards from the water, with the tide still two hours from turning.

"Those are my tools," Ansel said, when the man's hand came toward the roll at his hip. "Apprentice's kit. Files, screwdrivers, oil, tweezers. My master will want them back whether you arrest me or not."

"Garrick Fenn can send his bill to the customs house." The guard took the roll anyway, turned it over, weighed it. "You know why I'm asking, don't you? Because I think you do. I think you've known since you saw me reading." He tapped his chest, where the folded paper sat. "Description came down from Mr. Fenn himself, not two hours past. Boy of seventeen, slight build, foreign look about him. Carrying a letter that isn't his. Stolen property, says Mr. Fenn. Says the boy's been acting strange for days. Says—" the guard's eyes lifted from the paper to Ansel's face, and there was something almost apologetic in them, which was somehow worse than cruelty—"says he'll thank whoever stops him. That's not how a man talks about an apprentice he means to see safe home. That's how a man talks about a thief."

Ansel's mind ran the escape routes the way he'd run gear trains: over the rail into black water he couldn't swim well enough to cross; back up the pier past a man twice his weight; left along the quay into streets where Garrick's other messengers might already be walking. Every route ended the same. He thought, absurdly, of the master clock at the observatory, of the gap where the stolen gear had left its clean absence, of how a mechanism could be made to fail by removing one small piece—and understood that he was the piece, and someone above him had reached into the works and taken him out.

"You've got it wrong," he said. "Whatever Mr. Fenn told you—"

"Hold still." The guard set the tool roll down between his boots and took Ansel by the collar, turning him, patting him down with quick professional hands—coat pockets, waistcoat pockets, trouser pockets. Ansel kept his arms slightly out from his body, the way he imagined a man might who had nothing to hide, and prayed to a God he mostly addressed through his father's memory that the letter's pasteboard edge wouldn't shift, wouldn't crackle, wouldn't give him away. The guard's hands were thorough but hasty. They passed over the letter's corner where it pressed against Ansel's side and registered it, perhaps, as nothing more than a folded bill of lading, a scrap of the paper that clutters every workingman's coat.

Then the guard stepped back and picked up his lantern and raised it, and the light went through Ansel's coat like the coat were made of gauze.

"There's something under there," the guard said. "Left side. Against your ribs. Don't make me ask twice."

* * *

Later—he could never say how much later, minutes or hours or the length of a lifetime compressed into lamplight—Ansel would remember that moment as the point at which he stopped being afraid. Not brave. Fear didn't leave; it simply changed state, the way water becomes ice, solid and cold and strangely clear. He stood on the wet planks with a customs man's fist knotted in his collar and the sea breathing below and the letter burning its shape into his skin, and he thought: *Father crossed an ocean so I could stand on English stones and tell the truth, and I have spent three years pretending the stones weren't mine.* Whatever happened next, he was done pretending. If they took the letter from him, they would have to take it knowing whose hands had carried it and why.

"It's a letter," he said. "And before you touch it, you should know that if it leaves my person tonight, it goes to Inspector Hale and no one else. Not to Mr. Fenn. Not to the harbormaster. To Hale, aboard that ship, or it goes into the harbor and the harbor can keep its secrets better than I can."

The guard stared at him. "You're threatening to destroy evidence."

"I'm telling you what evidence is." The words came out of Ansel low and fast and with a certainty that felt borrowed from someone braver. "Mr. Fenn sent you a description because he wants that letter back. Ask yourself why a man sends his apprentice's description to a pier guard at three in the morning instead of simply asking the constable to fetch the boy home. Ask yourself what's in a letter that a respectable clockmaker would rather drown than deliver." He could hear his father's voice under his own now, the quiet cadence he used for explaining why a movement must be disassembled completely before it can be trusted. "You have a sergeant somewhere. Wake him if you like. Search me if you must. But whatever you find, you'll find it in front of witnesses, or you'll find nothing at all."

The guard's grip loosened—not in surrender, but in the particular hesitation of a man who has just realized the ground under him may not be the ground he was told about. Ansel watched the doubt move across his face like cloud shadow moving across a field, and he understood that he had done the only thing left to do: made the lie too complicated to swallow quickly. A simple thief would have gone quietly. Only an innocent man—or a desperate one—would talk about inspectors and witnesses.

It bought seconds. Perhaps a minute. Then boots rang on the planks behind him, running, and the guard's hand went back to his collar, and Ansel's heart dropped through the pier into the black water, because he knew that stride, that urgent uneven rhythm, the stride of a girl who had been taught to walk lighthouse stairs two at a time since she could climb.

Maren came around the bollard at the pier foot with her shawl flying and her breath ragged and her eyes finding Ansel first, always Ansel first, before they fixed on the guard's fist in his collar with an expression that would have curdled milk at forty paces.

"Unhand him," she said. "Please," she added, because Edmund Whitcombe's daughter had manners beaten into her alongside her letters, though both arrived a beat late.

"Maren—" Ansel began.

"Miss Whitcombe." The guard straightened, releasing Ansel by degrees, caught between his orders and his upbringing. "This is customs business, miss. Your father—"

"My father is at home asleep, which is more than I'll say for either of you if you don't hear me out." She planted herself between them, and Ansel saw that her hands were shaking and that she had chosen, visibly, deliberately, to ignore them. "What exactly are you holding him for?"

"Stolen property. A letter. Description came from his own master, signed and sworn."

"His master," Maren repeated, and let the words hang there like something dead. "Garrick Fenn sent a sworn statement about a stolen letter at"—she glanced at the sky, at the stars still wheeling westward—"half two in the morning. And you took it. You read a paper handed to you in the dark by a man nobody in this town trusts further than his own doorstep, and you laid hands on Ansel Nakamura, who has repaired half the watches in Harbor Row without so much as losing a screw." She dug into her reticule with fingers that trembled until she stilled them against her skirt. "Do you want to know what he stole? Here. This is what he stole."

She held it out on her open palm: the pocket watch. The one from the shop, the one Ansel had spent that ordinary morning bringing back to life while Garrick muttered and the townsfolk peered through glass—the brass-cased Waltham with the hairline crack across the enamel dial and the new mainspring he had fitted by lamplight. She had it wrapped in her own handkerchief, and she unwrapped it now with the ceremony of a woman producing a relic, and the case caught the pier lamp and threw a small coin of gold light onto the guard's astonished face.

"A watch," Maren said, "that I asked him to repair. Three weeks ago. It belonged to my grandmother, it stopped keeping time, and I brought it to the shop because everyone knows the apprentice does the work and the master takes the credit—which, incidentally, is the entire trouble with Garrick Fenn in one sentence. Ansel's had it ever since. Parts are slow to come by. And tonight I asked him to bring it to the pier because I was coming down to meet a friend off the supply boat and I wanted it back, and if that's theft, then arrest me too, because I asked for it, and I'd like to see the charge sheet that explains why a girl reclaiming her own grandmother's watch needs two men and a sergeant to manage."

The guard looked at the watch. He looked at Ansel. He looked, with visible longing, at the folded paper in his own breast pocket, as though it might explain itself if he read it hard enough.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's not what Mr. Fenn described."

"No," Maren agreed sweetly. "Because Mr. Fenn described what he was paid to describe. Or should I say—" and here her voice dropped, and the sweetness went out of it like a tide going out, leaving rocks showing, "—should I ask you who pays a clockmaker, at half two in the morning, to swear lies about his own apprentice? Because I intend to ask exactly that, loudly, in daylight, with my father standing next to me. Edmund Whitcombe. Keeper of the observatory. Whose name, I promise you, carries a little more weight in this town than a shopkeeper's midnight scribblings."

It was magnificent. It was reckless. It was, Ansel thought with a kind of dizzy admiration, the most dangerous thing anyone had ever done on his behalf, and she was doing it with her chin up and her grandmother's watch glinting in her palm, spending her family's good name like coin to buy him thirty seconds of doubt in a guard's mind. He loved her. The thought arrived whole and unannounced, in the middle of everything, and he filed it away the way he filed all the things that mattered, deep in the case where nothing rattled loose.

The guard wavered. Ansel could see the wavering happen—the sergeant's chevrons on the man's sleeve fighting with the sergeant's absence, the sworn paper fighting with the girl's steady voice, the fear of being wrong fighting with the worse fear of being used. Half-believing, exactly half, balanced on the knife's edge of it.

"Nobody's going anywhere," the guard said finally, and it was neither surrender nor victory but the sound of a man choosing to postpone his mistake. "You'll both sit on that crate until the ship's officer comes ashore, which should be within the hour, and you'll tell this story to him word for word, and if there's so much as a splinter of truth missing from it, miss, it won't be your father's name that saves you." He pointed the lantern at a stack of fish crates by the net shed. "Sit. Both of you. And you—" he jabbed a finger at Ansel— "keep your hands where I can see them."

They sat. The wood was cold and smelled of mackerel and old ice. The guard retreated to his post by the lamp, where he stood with his back half-turned, reading his paper again and again as though the words might rearrange themselves into sense.

Maren's shoulder came to rest against Ansel's, feather-light, deliberate.

"The letter," she breathed, so quietly the words were barely more than warmth. "Still there?"

"Still there."

"Then we haven't lost. We've only stopped winning for a moment." Her hand found his on the crate between them, hidden from the lamp by the angle of their bodies, and her fingers were cold and certain. "Hale will come ashore soon. Boats don't wait for dawn and neither does he. When he comes, we speak. Nothing else matters until then."

Ansel looked out past the pier head to where the *Vigilant* swung at her anchor, her riding lamp rising and falling with the swell, and he thought of the hour hand on a great clock approaching the mark, unstoppable, indifferent, carrying everything toward whatever came next.

Then, out on the black water, a boat's crew began singing out the officer's return—and the guard by the lamp straightened, tucked his paper away, and reached for the whistle chain around his neck.
