## 18. The Morning Tide

Ansel had thought he would shake until the sun came up. He had been wrong. Somewhere in the small hours, after Inspector Hale's men had marched the sergeant and his two confederates down the gangway with their wrists bound behind them, after Maren had finally stopped gripping his sleeve as though the pier might pitch him into the water, the shaking had simply—stopped. What remained was something stranger: a stillness so complete it frightened him more than the night had.

He sat now on the cold stone of the harbor wall with Maren a hand's width away, both of them wrapped in a blanket someone from the *Vigilant* had fetched without being asked, watching the east go grey and then pearl and then the faintest bruised rose above the headland. The lanterns along the quay had guttered out one by one as the light arrived to replace them, and each one that died felt like something loosening in his chest.

"You're not sleeping," Maren said. It was not an accusation. She said it the way she said most things—as if the observation were simply hers to make and she trusted him to do what he liked with it.

"I don't think I can." He flexed his fingers inside the blanket. They ached dully, the way they always did after a long night at the bench, after hours of fine work under bad light. But there had been no work tonight. Only walking. Only waiting. Only the letter, which had left his possession an hour past midnight, passed into Hale's hands on the deck of the revenue ship while gulls screamed overhead like something being unmade. "My hands think they've been working anyway."

"Your hands have been holding on to things all night," Maren said. "It counts."

He almost laughed. It came out of him sideways, half a breath, and she glanced at him with the corner of her mouth lifting, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because there was a kind of talk that only worked in silence and they had both learned it somewhere between the customs house gate and the pier.

Out at the harbor mouth, the water had begun to run. The tide was turning—the first of the flood, pushing in over the bar in long silver seams that caught the growing light. And against that current, absurdly, stubbornly, a small lugger was trying to leave.

* * *

Ansel saw her before anyone shouted. He had spent three years learning to read mechanisms, and a boat working out of a harbor against a making tide moved with the same doomed, grinding effort as a clock missing a tooth—a motion that wanted to succeed and could not. The lugger came off her mooring awkwardly, sails half-set, sliding toward the gap in the breakwater where the grey water stood up in ridges.

"They're going," he said, standing. "Maren. They're actually going."

She was on her feet before he finished. Down the quay, a whistle blew—one short blast, then another—and figures detached themselves from the shadow of the customs house where, he understood now, men had been waiting all night for exactly this stupidity. Hale had guessed it, Maren told him later. Men who would forge a letter to ruin a family would also believe the sea belonged to them, that the tide was a door built for their use alone.

The revenue cutter came around the mole with her engine barely murmuring, running dark even in full daylight, as though she were ashamed of how easy it was. Ansel watched the whole thing happen with the peculiar clarity of exhaustion: the lugger trying to fill her sails in the wind-shadow of the eastern headland; the cutter sliding across her bow with the unhurried certainty of a hand closing a watch case; the crack of a line thrown across the lugger's rail; men in the lugger's waist scattering like beads from a snapped string. Someone shouted something the wind took away. A sail came down crooked and stayed there.

"It's over," Maren said beside him, but she didn't sound triumphant. She sounded like someone setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its shape. "Ansel. It's actually over."

He found he couldn't answer yet. He kept watching. This, too, was part of the mechanism—he knew that much about himself by now. He needed to see every wheel turn, needed to watch the conspirators brought in across the cutter's gunwale with their hands tied, needed to count them: the sergeant from the pier, already manacled, staring at nothing; a heavyset man he half-recognized from the fish market; a third whose face he never did learn, who wept openly and made no attempt to hide it. Three men who had paid his master to write a lie. Who had wanted Edmund Whitcombe ruined and the harbor trade bent toward their own wharves further down the coast, and who had nearly succeeded, and would have, if a clock had not failed on purpose in an observatory above the town.

If a gear had not been removed by hands that never imagined an apprentice would be sent to find the wound.

The prisoners were rowed ashore just as the sun cleared the headland and laid a path of hammered gold across the harbor. The crowd that had gathered along the quay—fishwives, dockhands, the customs men in their braided hats, old Mr. Penlowe who sold ropes and had never once spoken to Ansel in three years—all of them craned and muttered. And through the middle of that muttering crowd, unnoticed, unwitnessed, the morning tide came in. It filled the harbor basin inch by inch, lifting the moored boats on their lines, straightening them, setting every hull in the town gently right. Ansel watched it do this and thought: *that is what has happened to us.* Something vast and patient and indifferent to deserving had turned, and everything riding on it rose together.

* * *

They brought Garrick in at half past seven.

Ansel heard it before he saw it—a change in the texture of the crowd, a ripple of heads turning up Harbor Row—and then his master came down the quay between two constables, and the world narrowed to the width of a cobbled street.

Garrick looked smaller than Ansel had ever seen him. Not bent—that would have been too simple, too merciful—but diminished in some interior way, as though the man had been a case all these years with nothing in it but weights and springs arranged to look like a person, and now the springs had let down. His collar was open. He had not buttoned his coat. In thirty years of trade, Ansel thought, Garrick Voss had probably never once appeared in public with his coat unbuttoned, and somehow that detail wounded more than anything else: proof that the man had been taken by surprise in his own shop, at his own bench, in the one place he had believed himself untouchable.

Hale walked beside them. The inspector's face was grey with a tiredness that went back years, back to a brother and a drowned name, but when his eyes found Ansel across the crowd there was something in them that nodded—an acknowledgment given and returned in less than a second, the whole long transaction between them completed without a word.

Then Garrick saw him.

Ansel had rehearsed nothing. He had not known he would need to. But in the space between one cobblestone and the next, his body chose for him: he stood straight. He did not lower his eyes. Three years of dropping his gaze to the bench whenever the master crossed the shop, three years of making himself narrow and quiet and convenient, and now his spine simply refused, and he let it.

Garrick stopped. The constable on his left tugged the chain and he ignored it. For a moment, master and apprentice looked at each other across ten feet of morning, and the whole quay seemed to hold its breath along with them.

There was hatred in Garrick's face—Ansel expected that, and found it, and found it curiously painless. But underneath the hatred was something worse and sadder: bewilderment. Genuine, uncomprehending offense, as though Ansel had committed not a betrayal but a category error, as though the ledger of the world had plainly stated *apprentice* and the world had gone and contradicted its own figures. You were supposed to be invisible, that face said. I made you invisible. That was the arrangement.

"Yes," Ansel said quietly, though the words reached no one but himself and the gulls. "I know. That was the arrangement."

Garrick's mouth opened. Closed. Whatever he had meant to say—if anything—he swallowed it, and turned, and walked on between the constables with his coat flapping open, past the fishwives and the rope-seller and the customs men, and no one said a word to him either, and that silence, Ansel thought, watching him go, was the loudest sentence any of them would pronounce.

Maren's hand found his. Her fingers were cold; so were his; the two coldnesses together made something almost warm.

"He never once asked my father's name," she said. "All those years. He used to call him 'your keeper' when he passed us on the street. Like Father wasn't a man, only an occupation."

"He called me 'boy' for the first year," Ansel said. "Then 'Nakamura.' Never my first name. My father gave me that name and he never once—" His throat closed. He breathed through it, the way Maren had taught him without knowing she was teaching him: not suppressing the feeling, just letting it pass through like weather. "It doesn't matter now."

"It matters," Maren said. "It just doesn't get to decide anything anymore."

* * *

By nine the harbor had filled properly, the tide high and green-gold, and the crowd had thinned to stragglers and children chasing the wakes of gulls, and someone—Ansel never learned who—brought out a kettle and began pouring tea onto the quay as if the arrests were merely weather and tea the proper response to weather. The *Vigilant* rode at her berth with orders to stay put, her crew leaning on the rail in the sun, and Inspector Hale had gone aboard to sleep, Maren reported, "for about a week, I should think," before taking his brother's name and the forged letter and the whole rotten ledger of it to London.

Ansel drank his tea and watched the water.

Three years. That was the arithmetic that kept surfacing, that he kept having to set down and pick up again. Three years since his father died and the Whitcombes had folded a half-orphaned apprenticeship into their supper table without ever once making the charity visible. Three years of held breath—not dramatically, not all at once, but the way you hold your breath in a cold room, shallowly, continuously, without noticing until something makes you exhale. Every stare on the high street. Every customer who spoke to Garrick over his head. Every time he had translated himself smaller to fit the space allowed him. All of it had been held breath, and now, sitting on the warm stone with tea in his hands and Maren's shoulder against his and the tide coming home below him, he finally let it go.

It hurt. That surprised him. Relief, he discovered, was not gentle; it came up through him like a gear train engaging after long disuse, clanking and shuddering and finding its mesh, and he had to press his knuckles briefly to his eyes, and Maren pretended very courteously to be watching a fishing smack come in.

"My father used to say the tide doesn't forgive," Ansel managed at last. "It just returns. Whether you've prepared for it or not. Whether you deserve it or not."

"That sounds like something a clockmaker would say about clocks," Maren said.

"He was a clockmaker."

"Then he was describing his own profession and calling it philosophy." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "The tide forgives plenty. It brings boats home that had no business making it back. Look at that smack—half her paint gone, and look how gently the water takes her."

Ansel looked. The battered little boat came in on the flood, and the water did indeed take her—lifted her, steadied her, guided her bumping and scraping to within reach of her mooring, where a boy in a yellow sou'wester caught the line and hauled her in as though catching lost things from the sea were the most ordinary work in the world.

"All right," Ansel said. "Maybe it forgives."

* * *

They stayed on the wall most of the day, or he stayed and she stayed with him, and the hours passed in the unhurried way of hours after a storm. Word traveled and changed shape as word does: by noon the story on the quay involved six smugglers and a chase; by two o'clock, the fishwives had Inspector Hale personally boarding the lugger with pistols in both hands. Ansel let the stories grow. Truth, he decided, could afford a little ornament now. It had survived worse than exaggeration.

What did not change, what grew only truer with each retelling he happened to overhear, was the name at the center of it. *The Whitcombes.* Said differently now. Said the way you say the name of someone who has been wronged and stands among you still. A woman at the tea urn pressed a second cup on Maren and said, "For your father, tell him," and could not finish the sentence, and Maren had thanked her for both of them.

Once, only once, Ansel felt the old reflex stir—the reflex that ducked the head, dropped the eyes, made itself furniture. A gentleman in a good coat stopped near them on the quay, looked directly at Ansel, and opened his mouth. Ansel braced.

"Mr. Nakamura," the gentleman said, and inclined his head. "I'm told the town owes you a considerable debt."

And then he walked on, before Ansel could assemble any reply at all, and Maren laughed at whatever expression crossed his face, and the reflex lay back down and did not stir again.

Toward evening, when the light went long and copper over the water and the tide began its slow withdrawal, Ansel took the removed gear from his coat pocket—the stolen tooth of the observatory clock, which Hale had handed back to him that morning with the words *"Yours, I think, by rights of discovery"*—and turned it over in his palm. Brass, worn bright at the teeth, no bigger than a shilling. The whole conspiracy had hung on this absence: one gear lifted from its pivot by careful criminal hands, so the great clock would stop, so a summons would go out, so a sealed lie could be planted in the case and later "found." One missing piece, and everything downstream of it had run false—his master, the sergeant, three ruined reputations waiting in the wings, and a boy who had spent a night inside a clock's housing and come out carrying the truth against his ribs.

He closed his hand around it. Small gears, he thought. His whole life had turned on small gears: a watch repaired badly in a cramped shop, a summons delivered at the wrong moment, a letter wedged behind an escapement. People spoke of fate as something vast, tidal. But he had opened enough cases to know better. Fate was a small brass wheel, and whether it was present or absent determined whether everything above it told the truth.

"Come on," Maren said, rising, pulling the blanket off both their shoulders. "Father will have heard eleven versions of today and none of them correct. If we don't come put him right, he'll start believing the one where Hale swam out to the lugger."

Ansel stood. His legs were stiff and his eyes burned and somewhere behind his ribs, three years of held breath had emptied out and left a space that ached and felt, unmistakably, like room to live in. He looked once more at the harbor—the water drawing back off the stones, the boats settling on their lines, the whole great mechanism of the sea winding down toward evening, patient and forgiving and certain to return.

Then he followed her up Harbor Row, toward the lit windows of the only house that had ever fed him, where Edmund Whitcombe was waiting—and where, Ansel would understand only when he stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands, the evening had one more thing to say.
