## 20. The Empty Bench

The key turned in Garrick's lock for the last time under Ansel's hand, and he stood a moment on the threshold listening to the way the mechanism gave—smooth, obedient, as though even the lock had decided to stop resisting him. Three days ago a constable had turned this same key while Garrick stood between two officers with his collar crooked and his eyes fixed on the middle distance, refusing, even then, to look at his apprentice. Now the shop belonged to nobody. The trustees had sealed it pending their inquiries, and then, after those inquiries ended in a customs house cellar full of confiscated ledgers, they had unsealed it again and handed Ansel the key with a nod that carried more weight than any speech.

Inside, the morning light came through the window in a long slanted bar and lay across the workbench like something laid out for burial.

He had expected the shop to feel different. It did not. It smelled as it always had—oil and brass shavings and the faint vinegary breath of the pickling solution Garrick used on verdigris—and it sounded as it always had, which was to say it didn't sound at all. A clockmaker's shop was never quiet; it only learned to make its noise so regular that human ears stopped claiming it. Forty-odd cases ticked in the dimness, each one fractionally out of step with its neighbors, so that the whole room breathed in a slow arrhythmic pulse, like a sleeping animal dreaming of many different mornings.

For three years Ansel had worked in that sound. He had learned to hear inside it—the hitch of a mainspring near the end of its coil, the dry click of an escapement running without oil, the particular thin whine a balance wheel made when a hair of dust had found its jewel. His father had taught him first, in a room above a laundry in Yokohama, before the crossing, before the gray English sea, before the laundry became a rented room became a coffin became a grave on the hill above St. Aldric's churchyard. But Garrick had taught him the rest of it here, in this exact light, at this exact bench—or rather, Garrick had taught him everything except the part that mattered, which was that a man who keeps time for other people ought to keep some kind of faith himself.

Ansel hung his coat on the peg by the door, because there was no one left to see him do it and because the work would raise dust, and went to his corner.

His corner was not really a corner. It was the north end of the main bench, the worst-lit stretch, where the window's light failed and the lamp's reach began—a territory of perhaps four feet that he had defended for three years the way small nations defend small borders: quietly, persistently, without ever naming the war. Garrick's tools owned the south end, laid out in ranks on a leather roll worth more than Ansel's annual keep. Ansel's own kit fit inside a single wooden case with a folding lid, and half of what was in it he had made himself.

He began to pack.

It should have been simple. It should have taken ten minutes. But his hands kept stopping—not hesitating, exactly, but pausing over things the way a man pauses over words in a language he is only beginning to read. The burnisher his father had given him, its handle worn to the shape of two generations of thumbs. A jar of shellac he had mixed himself, amber as bottled afternoon. And beneath these, wrapped in a square of oilcloth he had not unwrapped since the night he'd found it behind the escapement of the master clock, the letter.

The letter that had undone everything.

He did not need to open it. He knew every line now the way he knew a gear train—knew the dates, knew the false ledger entries Garrick had written into his own book with a steady hand and a steady heart, knew the name that appeared in the margin in ink slightly fresher than the rest: *Hale, T.* — Thomas Hale's brother, crossed through years before the ink dried, ruined and drowned and waited for. The customs men had taken the original into evidence; what lay in the oilcloth was Maren's careful copy, made by lantern light at her kitchen table while Edmund Whitcombe sat in the next room pretending not to watch the door. She had copied it, she said, so that Ansel wouldn't have to carry the true weight alone. As if paper had weights. As if he could set this one down anywhere.

He wrapped it again and put it in the bottom of his tool case anyway, beneath the burnisher, where a thing you cannot put down goes.

Then he cleared the bench itself.

Garrick's leavings told on him more honestly than the man ever had. A drawer full of watches promised to customers and never repaired, their tickets yellowing, some of the names already dead. A tin of coins sorted into wrong denominations. An invoice book whose arithmetic drifted further from true the deeper the year ran—as if honesty were a spring that lost tension with use. Ansel gathered all of it into a crate for the trustees, item by item, writing each thing on a slip of paper in his small careful hand. Let them find the ledger themselves; he was done reading other men's accounts.

Under the false bottom of the parts cabinet—he had found it in his first month and never told Garrick, a private country the size of a hymnal—he found the things he'd hidden there across three years and forgotten: a photograph of his father taken in front of a shop that no longer existed in a city that no longer spelled itself the same way; a stub of pencil worn to the length of a thumb joint; and the pocket watch.

The old pocket watch. The first commission.

It had come in on a rainy Tuesday from a fisherman's widow who could pay almost nothing, and Garrick had tossed it on the bench and said, *Practice, boy. You can't ruin it worse than the sea already has.* Ansel had spent eleven evenings on it. He had made a new pallet stone by hand, filed from a scrap of synthetic ruby, and re-pivoted the escape wheel, and when it finally ran—when it lay in his palm ticking with the stubborn modest heartbeat of a well-made thing—he had understood, for the first time since his father died, that his hands still knew how to make the world keep its promises. The widow had wept over it. Garrick had charged her triple what the work deserved and given Ansel sixpence.

And later—three days ago, at the pier, with a guard's hand twisting his arm and the letter burning against his ribs—it had been this same watch that Maren had thrust into the argument like a shield. *He stole nothing,* she had said, holding it up so the lantern caught the case, *except the chance to finish a repair I asked him for.* She had produced it from her own pocket, having gone back to the shop alone in the dark to find it, having run the length of Harbor Row with her hair coming loose and her breath tearing, to stand between him and the sergeant's grip and lie for him as fluently as he had lied for her. The watch had passed from her hand to the guard's to the officer's and back to Ansel's, and somewhere in all that passing it had stopped being a sixpenny commission and become something else entirely. A witness. Proof that he existed, that he made things, that his work could be recognized in the dark by people who loved him.

He wound it now. It took the key without complaint, and the second hand walked its circle, patient as tide.

He meant to put it in the case with the rest. His fingers were actually around the lid when he understood that he was not going to.

There was a place this watch needed to go, and it was not into another drawer.

* * *

St. Aldric's yard climbed the hill in terraces, the older stones leaning landward as if craning to watch the harbor, the grass kept short by sheep that came up through a gap in the wall and regarded visitors with the flat insolence of creatures who had outlasted everyone buried there. The path was steep enough that Ansel felt it in his legs, and he was glad of it. Grief sat easier in a body that was working.

His father's grave stood on the third terrace, where the yew's shadow reached by late afternoon and the sea was visible in slices between the houses below—a whole harbor broken into pieces and reassembled by the eye, masts and roofs and the bright scar of the tide channel. They had chosen the plot for that view. Ansel had chosen it, actually, sixteen years old and hollowed out, standing in this yard with the parish priest asking gentle questions he could barely understand through the accent, and he had pointed at the slice of water and said, *He liked to watch ships.* It was the truest sentence he possessed in those days. Perhaps it still was.

The headstone was granite, plain, the lettering already softening at the edges the way English weather softened everything it touched:

**KENJI NAKAMURA**
1858 – 1909
*CLOCKMAKER*
*A KEEPER OF FAITHFUL HOURS*

They had argued about the word *clockmaker*, gently, the priest thinking perhaps *gentleman* or simply nothing at all, and Ansel—who in that first raw week had barely been able to string five English words together—had found the words for this: *He was a clockmaker. Write it large.* So they had written it large. It was the only monument his father needed. A man who spent his life making time honest for strangers deserved the trade on his stone, whatever the parish register said about nationality or legitimacy or any of the other columns in which Ansel's family had always been entered in someone else's hand.

Ansel sat down on the bench beside the grave. It was a proper bench, oak, put there by some Victorian conscience for the comfort of mourners, and its seat was worn pale in two places where generations of grief had sat. He had sat here perhaps a dozen times in three years—more in the first winter, fewer as the town's coldness taught him to do his grieving privately, quickly, between commissions, the way everything else in his life was done. Today he sat in the middle of the bench, taking up the whole of it, the way a man sits in his own house.

"Father." The word came out in Japanese, the first Japanese he had spoken aloud in weeks, and it startled him how the syllables still fit his mouth like a tool made for his hand. He tried again in English, because his life was in English now, and both languages were his, whatever anyone said. "Father. I stayed."

The sheep on the upper terrace watched him. The wind came off the sea carrying salt and tar and the cries of gulls arguing over the day's waste, and far below, tiny as toys, the customs men were leading two more prisoners along the quay from the revenue ship—the last of the ring, taken at the harbor mouth trying to run on the morning tide, as if a tide could be argued with. Ansel watched them for a moment, small figures moving through the bright water-light, and found that he felt almost nothing. Not mercy, not triumph. Just the quiet of a debt being settled at last.

"You remember what you told me. Before we crossed." He turned his father's phrases over slowly, translating them back and forth, wanting them exact. *Silence is a wall, and walls keep what's inside safe. Keep your head down, keep your hands busy, let the work speak.* He had built his whole English life inside that wall. Three years of lowered eyes in the market street. Three years of answering Garrick's contempt with silence because silence couldn't be reported to anyone. Three years of being *the Japanese boy* even to people who had known his name since his father's funeral, because a name is a claim, and he had been afraid of claiming anything.

"And I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought you meant forever. I thought the wall was the whole teaching. I built it high and I lived behind it and I called it safety, and it was safety, of a kind. Walls are safe." He looked down at the watch in his hands. "But some silences are just complicity wearing a coat. I learned that too late to save myself the trouble, and early enough to save the Whitcombes. I think you would have liked them."

He told him, then. All of it, in no particular order, the way you tell the dead things—the removed gear and the sealed letter and the steamed seal over the lodging-house kettle at midnight, his heart hammering like a bad escapement; the tide tables spread on Maren Whitcombe's kitchen table while her brothers argued about football in the doorway and her father pretended to read the shipping notices; the three checkpoints, the gate clerk's suspicious pen, the harbormaster's terrible friendly small talk, the sergeant's grip on his arm at the pier head; Maren arriving like weather, holding up a sixpenny watch and lying for him with her chin up and her voice steady; Inspector Hale coming down the gangway with his brother's ruin folded in his face, reading the letter by lantern light until he reached the margin and his hands began, very slightly, to shake.

"I lied," Ansel said. "Twice. Well. Three times if you count the fishmonger, but that was Maren's lie, I only benefited from it. I lied to the harbormaster's face and it came out smooth and I hated how easy it was and I'd do it again tomorrow." He laughed, briefly, not really at anything. "You'd have boxed my ears. And then you'd have done the same."

A gull landed on the wall below and screamed once and left. The shadow of the yew had crept to the edge of the grave. Ansel realized he had been talking for the better part of an hour, and that it was the longest conversation he had held with anyone—including himself—in days, and that it had not tired him at all.

"The thing I came to tell you," he said, and his voice dropped, because this was the hard part, harder than the pier had been. "I'm not staying because I have nowhere else. I want you to know that. I've thought about leaving—I thought about it on the ship's deck at dawn, watching them take Garrick past me, and I thought, I could go to London, I could go anywhere, my papers are in order now, no one will stop me at a border ever again." He looked at the slice of harbor between the roofs, at the masts swaying their slow agreement with the water. "But this town saw me at my worst and my best inside one week, and it didn't look away. Maren didn't look away. Edmund shook my hand in front of the whole customs house and called me family, and I felt it go through me like a struck bell. And I find that I don't want to be invisible anymore, Father. I want to hang my name on glass where anyone can read it. I want to climb ninety-nine steps on Sunday and keep the master clock. I want—" He stopped, and smiled at the granite, rueful. "I want to stay because I choose it. That's all. That's the whole of it. Belonging isn't granted. I used to wait for someone to grant it. Nobody's coming to grant it. You take it. You cut a key and you walk in."

The tide below had turned; he could see it in the channel, the water's color changing as it began its long pull back toward the sea. Time keeping on. Everything keeping on.

He rose, and knelt, and set the pocket watch on the headstone's ledge, square in the center, beneath the word *CLOCKMAKER*, where the rain would find it and the lichen would eventually accept it and the view of the harbor would hold it the way the ground held everything else here—with patience and without judgment. It was ticking. He had wound it that morning and it would run for thirty hours, through one full turning of the dark and one full turning of the light, and after that it would rest, and that seemed right. His father had kept faithful hours for forty years. Let him have one more, given freely, by a son who had finally learned what they were for.

"It was the first thing I finished properly," Ansel told him. "I want it to be yours."

He touched the stone once, two fingers, the way he had seen sailors touch the masthead for luck, and walked down out of the churchyard without looking back—not because looking back was weakness, but because there was nothing behind him that required it anymore.

At the bottom of the hill, where the lane met Harbor Row, a figure was waiting under the gas lamp that hadn't been lit yet in the morning sun: Edmund Whitcombe, hat in hand, in his good coat. With him stood a thin, dry man Ansel recognized distantly as one of the observatory trustees, and behind them both, unable apparently to wait even the length of a street, came Maren, walking fast, her face doing something between gravity and delight.

"Ansel," Edmund said, when he was near enough. "We've been hoping to catch you. There's a matter of a shop on Candle Street that's going to need a keeper—and a matter of a clock on the hill. The trustees would like a word. If you're willing."

The trustee put out his hand. Maren, a step behind her father, mouthed something that was unmistakably *say yes.*

Ansel looked from the offered hand to the harbor beyond them, bright and turning, keeping its faithful hours.

"Yes," he said. "I'm willing."
