## 21. A Key of His Own

The letter from the observatory trustees arrived on a Tuesday, carried by a boy who ran the whole way up Harbor Row and arrived at the Whitcombe door breathless and important, as though he bore news of war. Maren read it standing in the doorway with the wind pulling at her collar, and by the time she reached the second paragraph she was smiling so widely that the boy took a step backward, uncertain whether he had delivered good tidings or witnessed something alarming.

*The trustees of the Ashcombe Observatory,* it began, in the careful copperplate of Mr. Pellowe, who was treasurer and therefore wrote everything twice, *being desirous of securing the continued service of a competent horologist for the maintenance of the master clock, and being further persuaded that no man in this town is better qualified—*

She stopped there. *No man.* She read it again to be sure, holding the page steady against the gusts. No man. Not *no other man*, not *no Englishman*, not any of the hundred small qualifications a committee might have reached for when they meant something narrower than they were willing to say. Just: no man better qualified.

"Maren?" Her father's voice from the study, where he sat with his tide tables spread out like a general's maps. "Is it the trustees?"

"It's them." She carried the letter through and laid it on his desk, her finger under the line that mattered. Edmund Whitcombe put down his pen, adjusted his spectacles, and read. He was not a quick reader; he was a thorough one, and he read the letter all the way through, then read it again, and when he looked up his eyes were bright in a way Maren had not seen since before the arrests.

"Well," he said. "Well."

"Say it properly, Papa."

He laughed—a short, rough sound, like a boat coming off its moorings. "They're offering him the shop. The shop *and* the clock. Garrick's lease reverts to the town, the trustees hold the option on the clockwork, and they want Ansel Nakamura to have both." He tapped the letter. "Signed by all five of them. Even old Cobbett, and Cobbett wouldn't sign his own name to a compliment."

Maren leaned against the desk and looked out the window toward the harbor, where the morning light lay flat and silver on the water. Three weeks since the pier. Three weeks since Inspector Hale had come down the gangway with the lantern held high and the letter open in his hand, and the world had turned over like a tide chart in a gale. Three weeks of depositions and hearings, of Garrick led away in irons without meeting anyone's eyes, of her father cleared in front of half the customs house while men who had avoided him for years suddenly remembered his name and clasped his hand too hard.

Three weeks of watching Ansel move through it all like a man walking on ice he didn't yet trust—grateful, careful, waiting for the thaw to crack beneath him.

"He'll refuse," she said. "Or rather, he'll say yes and then spend a week finding reasons why he shouldn't."

"Then we'd best go and see him before he does." Her father was already reaching for his coat.

* * *

They found him at the churchyard, which was where Maren had known they would find him. He went every Sunday now, and sometimes midweek besides, up the hill to the row of stones where his father slept under thirty years of weather. She had been there once herself, unasked, and had seen how he stood—not bowed exactly, but inclined, the way a man inclines toward a conversation—and she had waited at the gate until he finished, because some things are not meant for witnesses.

Today she did not wait at the gate. Today her father walked straight up the path and Maren followed, and Ansel heard their footsteps on the gravel and turned, and there was a moment—she saw it clearly, though he would have denied it—when he braced himself, the way one braces for bad news delivered in a pleasant voice.

"Mr. Whitcombe. Miss Whitcombe." He still said it formally, even now. Even after everything, after supper at their table and nights spent planning in the harbor office and the terrible crowded hour on the pier when she had lied to an armed man with a stolen watch in her fist and meant every word. Formality was his armor; she had learned that. But lately he left the visor up.

"Ansel." Her father held out the letter. "This came this morning. I'll not summarize it—you've had enough of people talking about you in rooms you weren't invited to. Read it yourself."

Ansel took the page. Maren watched him read it, watched his eyes move down the trustee's careful sentences, watched him reach the part about the shop and stop, and go back, and read it again, and then look up at her father with an expression she could only call *suspicion*, as though the offer might be a trap sprung late.

"They know what he did," Ansel said quietly. "What Garrick did. They know I worked for him three years."

"They know you found what he buried," Edmund said. "There's a difference, boy, and everyone in this town knows it now, thanks to you. Though I notice—" and here his voice went dry and amused, "—that nobody thanked you quite so loudly until it was safe to do so. That's the way of towns. They apologize in crowds."

"The shop needs work," Ansel said. It was not a refusal. Maren had catalogued his deflections by now, and this one—the immediate listing of obstacles—was nearly a surrender. "The roof over the workroom leaks. The lathe belt is perished. Half his tools will need cleaning, and some of them—" his jaw tightened, "—some of them I don't want to touch."

"Clean them or replace them. It's your shop now, if you'll have it." Edmund glanced at the headstone behind them, at the name HIDEO NAKAMURA worn soft by salt air. "I think your father would understand a man taking honest work where he finds it."

Ansel looked back at the stone. For a long moment he said nothing, and the wind moved the long grass between the graves, and somewhere below them the harbor bell rang the quarter hour, slightly flat, the way it always rang, no one having tended it in years.

"Yes," he said finally. "Yes, I'll have it." And then, quickly, before the word could settle into pride: "If the trustees are certain. If there's no objection raised. If—"

"Ansel." Maren stepped forward and took the letter gently from his hands and folded it and gave it back. "They signed it. All five. That's more certainty than most men get in a lifetime. Take it."

He took it. And he smiled—not the careful, measured smile he wore for strangers, but the other one, the real one, the one that started in his eyes and only afterward remembered to reach his mouth—and Maren thought: *there he is.*

* * *

The shop on Cooper's Lane had stood empty three weeks, and emptiness had gotten into it the way damp gets into plaster. When Ansel unlocked the door with Garrick's key—the old key, the one he would replace within the day—the smell of the place rose up to meet them: oil and brass shavings and dust, and underneath it all something faintly sour, the residue of the man who had kept the place. Maren watched Ansel stand just inside the threshold and breathe it, and she saw him decide, deliberately, that he would not be sickened by it. This shop had been his cage for three years. Now it would be his house. Houses forgive their histories if you repaint the walls.

He set to work that same afternoon, and she stayed to help, and by evening they had opened every window and swept the floors and carried the worst of Garrick's effects out to the yard—the ledgers especially, those poisoned columns of dates and figures, stacked in a crate for the magistrate's clerk to collect. Ansel handled them with tongs, literally, a pair of brass tongs from the bench, and Maren laughed for the first time in weeks and he looked startled at the sound and then pleased, as though laughter in this room were itself a kind of repair.

It was near dark when he went to the bench drawer at the far end, the deep one that Garrick had always kept locked, and found it already open—he had forced it days ago during the first clearing-out—and lifted out the key blank and the little graver and the files.

"What are you doing?"

"A shop needs a lock that answers to its owner." He carried a stool to the window where the last grey light fell, and sat, and set the blank in the vise. "Garrick's key opens this door. Anyone who ever copied it can open it too. I'm going to cut a new one."

"You can do that? Cut a key from nothing?"

"I can cut a key from anything." There was something new in his voice—not pride exactly, but its cousin, the quiet satisfaction of a craftsman asked at last to practice his craft. "My father taught me. He said a key is the most honest thing a locksmith makes, because it only works for the person it was made for. Everything else can be copied or counterfeited. A key either fits or it doesn't."

He bent to the work, and Maren sat on the counter and watched. She had seen him repair watches, seen him work inside the great housing of the master clock with his sleeves rolled and his breath held—but this was different. This was creation, not restoration. The file moved in short, patient strokes, and brass filings gathered like pale sand at the base of the vise, and slowly, stroke by stroke, a shape appeared in the blank that had never existed in the world before. He tested it once against the lock, frowned, filed two more strokes, tested again.

The lock turned. Clean, smooth, with the small definite click of a mechanism agreeing with itself.

Ansel held the key up to the lamplight and looked at it for a long time.

"It should have your father's name on it," Maren said.

He turned. "What?"

"The shop. On the glass, the way Garrick had his own name. *Garrick & Son*, it used to say, years ago, before the son went off to London and was never mentioned again. Everyone in town remembers it." She slid off the counter and came to stand beside him at the window, close enough that their shoulders touched. "You should paint yours there. Both names. His and yours."

For a moment he was very still. She had learned to read his stillnesses—the frightened ones and the thinking ones and the ones that meant something had gone through him like current through wire—and this was the third kind.

"People will talk," he said, but his heart wasn't in it; she could hear that it wasn't.

"Let them. They talked about my family for seven years and see how well we've survived it." She touched the glass with one finger, tracing where the letters would go. "*Nakamura*. Right beside Ansel. Let them read it every morning on their way to market."

He looked at the window a long while. Then he said, very quietly, in Japanese—the language he almost never spoke aloud, the private tongue he kept folded away like a letter too precious to open—something she did not understand, except that it sounded like a question asked of someone not present, and that the tone of it was grateful.

"What does that mean?" she asked.

He considered. "It means: *Father, do you see this.* " He smiled, small and crooked. "And also it means yes. To the window. To both names."

* * *

They painted the letters themselves, three evenings later, because the signwriter on Fore Street had quoted a price that made Edmund snort into his tea and offered a timeline of six weeks besides. Maren brought the enamel paint—black, proper sign-paint, bought with her own saved coins—and Ansel cut stencils from stiff paper with a scalpel borrowed from his own bench, cutting each letter with the same unhurried precision he gave to escapements.

They worked by lamplight with the door propped open against the autumn chill, and the town walked past and slowed and looked, because of course it did. Mrs. Treloar from the bakery crossed the street expressly to peer. Two fishermen paused mid-stride. Old Cobbett himself, trustee and misanthrope, stopped dead on the cobbles, read the emerging letters, grunted—which from Cobbett was a eulogy—and went on his way.

*N A K A M U R A*

Below it, smaller:

*A N S E L — Clockmaker & Horologist*

By rights it should have been strange, Maren thought, watching him fill in the final stroke of the *A*: a half-Japanese apprentice of three weeks' standing hanging his name over a shop his disgraced master had held for twenty years. But it wasn't strange. It was the most natural thing she had ever seen, as natural as the tide filling a channel that had been cut for it. The name fit the glass the way his new key fit the lock. It had simply needed someone to cut it.

When the last letter was done, they stepped back onto the cobbles together to look. The lamplight inside caught the fresh paint and made it shine wetly, and beyond it the dim shapes of the benches, the lathe, the great wheel of the wall regulator waiting for its new keeper's hand.

"Do you remember," Maren said, "the first time you climbed the observatory steps? You told me afterward you counted them. Ninety-nine."

"I count everything. It's a failing."

"It's a virtue badly disguised." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You counted them because you thought you were walking into a room where you'd be tolerated. Ninety-nine steps to tolerance." She turned to face him fully. "Sunday, when you climb to wind the clock, try counting to something else."

"To what?"

"To one. Count to one. One keeper. One clock. One—" she hesitated, and found she was not afraid of the hesitation, only aware of it, "—one pair of us, if you're minded to say so."

Ansel looked at her. The lamplight from the shop fell across his face, and behind his eyes something turned over with the smoothness of a well-oiled train of wheels, engaging at last with what it had always been shaped to meet.

"One," he said. "I can count to one."

Above them, far up the hill, the observatory dome caught the moonlight, and inside it the master clock—his clock now, in every way that mattered—ticked on through the night, keeping the time that belonged, at last, to someone who would keep it honestly.
