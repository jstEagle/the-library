## 22. Winding the Master Clock

The ninety-nine steps had not grown shorter in a year. If anything, Ansel thought, they had grown steeper with familiarity, the way a road you know too well reveals every stone you have ever stumbled on. He climbed them now as he climbed them every Sunday morning, before the town below had fully shaken off its dreams, with the winding key slung over his shoulder in a canvas roll and the east wind coming up off the water carrying salt and kelp and the first faint smoke of the harbor bakeries.

At step forty he permitted himself to pause, as he always did, at the narrow window cut into the tower's seaward wall. Below, the town lay folded in early light: the crooked spine of Harbor Row, the slate roofs going silver, the masts of the fishing fleet swaying like reeds in the outer basin. And beyond the pier, beyond the breakwater, the sea itself—that vast grey page on which so much had been written and erased. A year ago this window had been nothing to him but a place to catch his breath. Now it was something like an accounting. Every Sunday he stood here a moment and counted what he had: a shop with his name on the glass, a trade that was his own, a family not by blood but by choice made public and permanent, and a woman waiting for him at the top of these stairs who had once taught him, in the worst week of his life, that silence could be a door walked through rather than a wall endured.

He counted, found the sum sufficient, and climbed on.

The observatory door at the top was unlocked—it was always unlocked now; Edmund Whitcombe had declared two years of locked doors quite enough for anyone—and Ansel let himself into the clock room with the ease of a man entering his own kitchen. The master clock filled the far wall, taller than he was, its oak case dark and dignified, its pendulum swinging in long patient arcs with a sound like a slow heartbeat kept for the whole coast. The great regulator had run without fault for three hundred and forty-one days. Ansel knew the number precisely, because knowing precisely was the whole of his trade.

And there, by the door where it had stood for a year, was the case.

It was small, walnut-glazed, built by Ansel's own hands during the slow weeks of January when the shop was quiet and his hands needed occupation worse than his sleep needed rest. Inside, mounted on velvet gone slightly faded at one edge from the light, sat the gear—the missing gear, the brass wheel no larger than a crown coin whose absence had unraveled everything. Garrick had pried it from the escapement train himself, Ansel had learned later, on the night he'd sealed the letter into the case; without it the clock would stop, a commission would be summoned, and the apprentice would be sent up the ninety-nine steps to find what had been planted for him to find. One small wheel, thirty-six teeth, and half a town's worth of ruin arranged around it.

Beneath it, written on a card in Maren's upright hand, was the date. *The fourteenth of June, 1912,* it said, and beneath that, in smaller script she had added only last month: *The day the truth kept better time than any lie.*

Ansel looked at it, as he looked at it every Sunday, and felt the old cold pass through him and out again, thinner each season, like winter leaving a house stone by stone.

"You're late."

He turned. Maren was curled in the keeper's chair by the south window, wrapped in her father's old coat against the pre-dawn chill, a ledger open on her knee though he could see from across the room that she had read nothing of it for some time. Her hair was coming loose from its braid in the way it did when she had been awake since five, which meant she had been up since five, which meant—his eyes went to the sill—there was a thermos of coffee and, he would wager the shop on it, bread still warm from the bakery wrapped in a cloth beside it.

"I'm precisely on time," Ansel said. "You're simply early. As usual."

"One of us should be." She closed the ledger and watched him cross the room, and there was in her watching—as there had been for some months now—a quality that never failed to unbalance him slightly, a steadiness without appraisal, the look of someone who had already decided and was merely enjoying the confirmation. "The tide's in early today too. You could see it turning from the window. Everything ahead of schedule."

"The tide doesn't keep my schedule. Nothing does. That's rather the point of clocks." He set down the canvas roll and knelt before the great case, and his hands did what a year of Sundays had taught them to do without instruction: found the lock, turned the key he had cut himself, swung open the long door. The smell came out to meet him—oil and old wood and brass, the particular scent of the machine that had changed his life twice over, once by breaking and once by holding a letter behind its own heart.

He began to wind.

It was not, strictly speaking, difficult work. The great weights rose on their drums, the crank turned with a resistance like a living thing leaning into his hands, and the mechanism gave back its small approving clicks as each ratchet took the load. But he had never treated it as mere work. His father had taught him—young, too young, at a bench in a country Ansel remembered mostly in smells—that a clock wound carelessly ran carelessly, and a clock running carelessly was telling everyone who consulted it a small lie about the nature of time itself. So he wound slowly. He wound as if the truth of the morning depended on it. In a sense it did; the observatory clock governed the harbor bell, the customs ledgers, the tide tables pinned up in the harbormaster's office where, one year ago, a man had delayed him with small talk while ruin traveled toward him in another man's pocket.

The harbormaster had retired in March. Ansel still could not pass him on the quay without a nod that carried, beneath its civility, the memory of a lie told smoothly for the first time in his life. He had made his peace with that lie. Most days.

"Edmund wants us for supper Thursday," Maren said, watching the pendulum rather than him, in the manner of people discussing important things sideways. "He's got letters from the Board of Trade. They've confirmed the harbor charter review. The reroute proposal is dead—formally, finally dead. Nobody will be moving the trade to Marlow Bay."

"Good," Ansel said, and meant it with the whole of his chest. It had been Garrick's paymasters, all of it traced backward now through Hale's patient months of inquiry—the men who had wanted the harbor strangled so their own wharves two towns south might fatten, who had bought a frightened clockmaker's pen and nearly hanged an honest family with it. The reroute had died with their convictions. But paperwork died slower than men, and until this letter, it had only been wounded.

"He also wants," Maren went on, with elaborate casualness, "to know when you intend to stop climbing these stairs on Sundays and start staying."

The crank caught in Ansel's grip. He let it rest a moment against the stopped weight, and listened to the pendulum count out the silence—one second, two, five—before he trusted his voice.

"Your father has been asking me that since August."

"And you've been answering him like a man asked to state his price for bread. *When it suits. When I'm able. When the time is right.*" She unfolded from the chair and came to stand beside him at the open case, and the warmth of her shoulder arrived before her words did. "Ansel. You've wound this clock fifty-two times. You've repaired every watch on Harbor Row twice. My brothers call you when their cart axle squeaks, because apparently a clockmaker is also a wheelwright now, in your spare hours. There is no righter time than the ones you keep refusing to read."

He looked up at her. Morning had come fully while he worked, and the east window threw a long gold blade across the floor between them, across the walnut case with its faded velvet and its small brass traitor sleeping under glass. Some mornings he still woke expecting the world to have reconsidered—to have remembered that he was the boy in the corner of the shop, the half-Japanese apprentice the town watched sidelong, the invisible man who fixed things and was not seen fixing them. Some mornings the expectation lasted until he unlocked his own door and read his father's name beside his own on the glass, painted in gold, and remembered that visibility was not a weather that happened to you. It was a thing you built, tooth by tooth, like a train of gears.

"I stayed," he said quietly. "A year ago I stayed because staying was the bravest thing I knew how to do. It doesn't feel brave anymore. It just feels—" He searched for the word and found, of all things, a horological one. "*Regulated.* Like a movement after cleaning. Everything in its place, doing what it was made for."

"And the question," Maren said, "is whether the maker intends the movement to run alone or mounted in something larger."

He laughed despite himself—she could always do that, pull the poetry out of his own trade and hand it back to him improved—and he finished the winding, the last weight rising to its mark, the click of the ratchet settling like a held breath released. He closed the case. He turned the key.

Then he took her hand, and answered the question properly, and the details of the answer belonged to the two of them and the pendulum alone.

* * *

Afterward they sat in the south window with the coffee and the warm bread, and the town woke beneath them in its ordinary miracle of small motions. The fishmongers' carts. The school bell. Old Prewitt walking his dog along the harbor wall exactly as he had every morning of recorded history. At the customs house the flag went up, and Ansel found his eye going there as it always did, to the white building where Inspector Hale had read a letter by lantern light and arrested a sergeant on a wet pier, and where—three days later, in front of half the town—Edmund Whitcombe had shaken a nervous apprentice's hand and called him family in a voice that carried to the fishing fleet.

Hale had left in September, promoted, transferred to the coast station down-channel. Before he went he had come to the shop and stood a long time among the ticking shelves, and then bought, of all things, a cheap alarm clock for his landlady, and paid precisely, and said at the door: "My brother used to say you could tell an honest trade by whether the work forgave you. Clocks forgive you every hour, Nakamura. They start over. Mind you don't waste the courtesy." Ansel had thought about that sentence more often than any other anyone had given him, except perhaps his father's lesson about silence.

His father. He thought of him now without the old ache, or with an ache that had been annealed into something useful, the way a spring is tempered—not soft, but no longer liable to snap. His father had believed silence kept people safe. And it was true, partly; silence had carried a boy through three years of a strange town with his skin intact and his dignity mostly so. But the churchyard visit last summer, the pocket watch left on the headstone, the words spoken aloud to the grass—*I stayed because I chose this place*—had settled the argument between the father's caution and the son's experience. Some silences were cages. Some silences were complicity—he had stood in a churchyard all one October night learning that. But some silences were the space inside a clock case where the truth waits, patient as brass, for the moment the case opens. This one, the clock's silence, the great patient tick that held the room and the harbor and the whole turning morning—this one kept time.

"You're doing it again," Maren said.

"Doing what?"

"Thinking in complete paragraphs and telling me none of them." She passed him the bread. "Out with one, at least. The short one."

Ansel considered. Below, the tide was fully turned and beginning its long withdrawal, uncovering the green-slicked piles of the pier, and somewhere out past the breakwater the pilot boat was heading out to meet a merchantman standing in from the continent. The world proceeded. It proceeded, he understood now—not by grand machinery, not by conspiracies exposed or tides obeyed, but by ten thousand small windings done faithfully by people who showed up. Garrick had understood the mechanism of ruin very well and had still lost, because ruin required everyone to stay silent and one apprentice had declined. That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it.

"The short one," Ansel said, "is that I'm happy, and I keep being surprised by it, and I think I should stop being surprised." He broke the bread and gave her the larger half, which was the correct proportion and always had been. "The longer one is for Thursday, at your father's table, in front of your brothers, where things are said properly or not at all."

Maren smiled at the harbor rather than at him, which was how she managed her best smiles, and said, "Then I'll tell Cook to expect you," and neither of them said anything else for a while, because the coffee was hot, and the bread was good, and below them the town ran on its ten thousand faithful movements, and above them, behind the long oak case, the master clock carried the morning forward one truthful second at a time.

At eight o'clock precisely, the harbor bell rang out across the water, governed by the wheelwork at their backs, and Ansel Nakamura, clockmaker of this town by choice and by claim, heard it as he would hear it every Sunday for the rest of his life: not as an order the world obeyed, but as a promise the world had finally kept.
