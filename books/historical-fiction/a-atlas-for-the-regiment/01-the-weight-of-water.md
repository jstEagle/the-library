## 1. The Weight of Water

The river woke her before the bell did.

Ulyana Sandoval had learned to sleep through most things — the groan of the dock timbers, the wind worrying at the barracks shutters, the coughing chorus that rose from the women's bunks each night like some slow animal turning over in its sleep. But the river she could not sleep through. It spoke a different language in the dark hours, low and gravel-voiced, and when it changed its mind about the weather, it said so plainly, and Ulyana's body had been trained, over nineteen years, to listen.

She lay still beneath the grey wool blanket and counted. Three breaths of wind from the north. That meant cold enough to sting the eyes on the walk down. Four breaths and they'd have called off the diving altogether; the Company did not spend divers on days the ice might come early. But three was a working number. Three meant the water would be cruel but survivable, and survivable was the standard the ledgers were written to.

She dressed in the dark, by feel, so as not to wake the others. Her diving shirt had dried stiff overnight where she'd hung it by the stove, and it crackled faintly as she pulled it on, like walking through dead leaves. The grease — tallow and something the Company bought cheap from the rendering works upriver, something that smelled faintly of old meat — went on next, worked into the skin with the flat of her hand until her arms shone dull yellow in the pre-dawn gloom. It was not warmth, exactly. Nothing they gave you was warmth. It was a delay. The grease delayed the moment the river took your heat, and your whole life, if you were indentured, was a study in delays.

The bell rang at first grey light, and the barracks emptied into a morning the color of unpolished tin.

* * *

The walk down to the water took eleven minutes if you didn't hurry, and hurrying cost you more than it saved. Old Maren walked beside her, as she always did, leaning on a cutting pole she'd claimed from the reed-beds twenty years ago and refused to surrender even after her hands grew too knotted to dive. Maren had been a diver once, one of the best, back before the rheumatism bent her fingers into question marks. Now she sat the shallows in a skiff and sorted what the younger ones brought up, and she talked, and talking was her way of keeping everyone alive.

"You breathe wrong today," Maren said, without looking at her. "I can hear it."

"I breathe fine."

"You breathe like a girl trying to prove something." Maren's voice was gravel and honey both, worn smooth by decades of saying true things nobody wanted to hear. "The river doesn't care what you prove. It only cares how long you stay under, and it will lie to you about that every single time."

They came out onto the shingle beach where the Company post stood — a sprawl of timber buildings the color of wet ash, the counting house with its shuttered windows, the drying racks, the long low shed where the day's take would be weighed and recorded in books none of them were permitted to read. Beyond it, the Verrin River ran wide and brown-green and patient, carrying snowmelt down from country Ulyana had never seen and would never see, toward cities that existed for her only as words on shipping manifests.

The overseer, Brandt, stood at the water's edge with his slate, checking names against his list. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a face like a dropped loaf of bread, and he never looked at any of them longer than he had to. This was, Ulyana understood, a kind of mercy. Men who looked too long started remembering faces, and remembered faces made trouble in the conscience, and consciences were expensive.

"Water's two degrees colder than yesterday," Brandt announced to no one in particular. "Short shifts. Nobody stays down past the count. I'll know if you do."

It was the closest thing to kindness the Company's structure allowed: a man announcing rules he hoped you'd break slowly.

* * *

The cold of the river was not a sensation. It was an argument, and the river won every argument inside ninety seconds.

Ulyana went down the first time before the sun cleared the eastern bluffs, working the shallows off the gravel bar where the mussels clustered in dark ridges. The technique was older than the Company, older than the post, older probably than the town that had half-drowned itself building the dock: a stone weight on a rope, a basket, lungs full of air and prayer. You dropped. You pulled mussels loose until your vision went to sparks. You kicked for the light, which seemed always to be moving away from you, a coin of white at the top of a well.

Nineteen years, and still there was a moment, halfway down, when her body screamed at her that this was wrong, that human beings were not made for this, that somewhere above was air and fire and a mother who needed medicine. And still there was the other moment, deeper, quieter, when the screaming stopped and the river became almost gentle — when the cold stopped being an enemy and became simply the condition of things, the way hunger was a condition, the way debt was a condition. She had learned to reach that second moment quickly. Some of the divers never reached it at all. Those were the ones who drowned, or quit their minds, or got sold upriver, though nobody used that last word aloud.

By midmorning she'd made nine dives and filled her basket a third of the way. The quota chalked on the board at the beach said twelve baskets per diver per day, which was a number written by someone in a warm office who had never put his face in the Verrin in October. Everyone knew the real arithmetic: ten baskets kept you out of trouble. Eleven earned a credit chit. Twelve earned nothing extra at all, because the surplus was folded invisibly into the Company's books, and the books, everyone knew without knowing, were the one thing in the world that never got wet.

At the surface between dives, she traded. Everyone traded. A strip of dried fish to Tomas, the quiet one, for a look at his gloves, which his late wife had lined with rabbit fur and which he now lent out, one dive at a time, to whoever's hands were worst. Two mussel pearls below grade — the tiny misshapen ones the tally clerks wrote off as grit — to the twins, Ferro and Liska, who between them could fix anything with a hinge or a latch and had promised to see about the draft in her mother's bunk corner. Small economies. Kindness measured in units small enough that the clerks never bothered to weigh them. It was the only currency the Company couldn't tax, because it never appeared on any ledger, and Ulyana held this thought the way you hold a warm stone in winter: close, and briefly, and knowing it would cool.

* * *

Her mother's cough had worsened with the month.

Sela Sandoval had dived until Ulyana was seven, until the year her father drowned — the year the river took him, they said, the year the Company paid out the widow's settlement of forty credits and a closed file. After that her mother had worked the sorting sheds, hunched over trays of shells, prying them open with a knife until her thumbs went bad, and the damp of the sheds had gotten into her chest and stayed there like a tenant who paid no rent and broke furniture.

Ulyana climbed to the infirmary hut at the noon break, her hair frozen into ropes that cracked when she moved. The infirmary keeper, a Company man named Pell with kind eyes and a coward's careful tongue, met her at the door.

"She's asking for you," he said. Then, lower: "And don't ask me for more of the lichen syrup. I've signed for what I've signed for."

"I'm not asking for yours." Ulyana opened her fist. In her palm lay three credit chits and a twist of paper containing four sub-grade pearls, traded for across the morning from four different divers, each of whom believed they were giving them to someone else's need. "There's a trapper comes through Thursdays. He has pine resin and bear root. Pell — please just tell me the dose. Don't tell me where it came from. I know how not to know things."

Pell looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind his careful face — recognition, maybe, of a skill he recognized because he practiced it himself. Knowing how not to know things was the whole curriculum of the post.

"One spoon of resin to three of rendered fat," he said. "Warmed, not boiled. At night, before the coughing hour." And then, because he was not entirely a coward: "If it goes to her lungs proper, Ulyana, no syrup in the world —"

"It won't," she said, and closed her hand, and believed it, because believing it was free and everything else cost pearls.

* * *

The evening came down early, as evenings did in the turning season, and the barracks filled with steam and smell — wet wool, tallow, fish stew stretched with barley to feed nineteen mouths from a pot meant for twelve. Ulyana sat by the stove with the resin warming in a tin cup, stirring it into fat while her mother dozed upright against the bunk frame, her breath whistling faintly, a sound Ulyana had catalogued the way a sailor catalogs winds: the whistle that meant tired, the rattle that meant frightened, the deep wet pull that meant the night would be long.

Tonight it was mostly whistle. Tonight was a good night. She told herself so twice, the way you tell yourself anything twice when once isn't taking.

Around her the barracks performed its nightly liturgy. Maren telling a story she'd told a hundred times, about the winter the river froze solid from the bluffs to the far bend and the whole post lived on pickled mussels and lies. The twins arguing over a broken lamp wick. Tomas silent in his corner, mending a net with hands that never stopped moving, as though stillness were a debt he couldn't afford. The lamps burned low because low was what the ration allowed, and in the low light everyone looked younger and older at once, the way people do when they've stopped expecting much.

Ulyana fed her mother the warmed mixture spoonful by spoonful, and Sela Sandoval swallowed without waking fully, and murmured something — a word, half a word, that might have been a name. Not Ulyana's name. Another one. A name from before, spoken the way you speak things in dreams, to people who are already gone.

"The world is bigger than this post," Maren said suddenly, from across the room, apropos of nothing, the way she sometimes did. "You know that, girl?"

"The post is the world," Ulyana said. It was a joke between them, or had been once. "The river runs through it. The Company owns it. What else is there?"

Maren snorted. "What else," she repeated softly, and turned back to her story, and let it go.

Later, banked stove, dark windows, the coughing hour passing gentler than feared — Ulyana lay listening to the river change its voice in the dark, and felt the particular peace that came from a day survived, a quota nearly met, a mother breathing easy, a small hoard of kindnesses banked against tomorrow. Weary contentment. She wore it like the grease: not warmth, only a delay, though she did not yet know what it delayed.

Because downriver, past the last lantern of the post, past the sandbar where the current ran fast and black, something was coming down the Verrin on the cold water. Something the river carried the way it carried everything — silently, without explanation, toward the people in its path.

It would arrive tomorrow.
