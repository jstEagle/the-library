## 2. A Stranger at the Ice Hole

The river arrived first as a sound.

Ulyana heard it before dawn, a groaning along the shallows where the ice sheets had begun to shoulder against each other in the night, and she lay in her bunk listening the way she listened every November — cataloguing the noises, deciding which were ordinary and which were warnings. Groan, crack, settle. Ordinary. The Verrin talking in its sleep. She pulled her mother's blanket higher over both of them, felt the rattle of the cough through the wool like something small and trapped trying to get out, and told herself: tomorrow I will trade for more of the willow bark. Tomorrow.

Then the horn sounded from the ice hole, three long notes, and nothing was ordinary anymore.

She was dressed before the third note finished. The barracks stairs took her down two at a time, boots half-laced, breath pluming white in the blue hour, and by the time she reached the shore perhaps forty others had gathered in a ragged crescent around the hole where the divers kept open water for the winter dredging. Lanterns swung on their poles. Faces she knew — old Maren with her shawl pinned like a flag of defiance, the twins pressed shoulder to shoulder for warmth, Tomas standing slightly apart the way he always did, watching everything and saying nothing.

"There." Maren's arm shot out, one gnarled finger pointing downriver. "There, in the shelf ice."

Ulyana saw it and felt her stomach drop through the soles of her boots. A dark shape lodged in the broken ice pan a dozen yards out, snagged where the current had shoved it against the edge of the solid sheet. It might have been driftwood. It might have been a seal carcass. But driftwood did not move its head, weakly, once, like a man trying to lift the river off his face.

"Rope," someone said. "Get rope."

"Company rope's for Company work," said one of the twins — Kiryl, probably; it was impossible to tell them apart until they spoke — and there was a bitter laugh in it, because everyone knew what that meant: no rope would be coming from the storehouse for a stranger. The Company did not pay for drowned men.

Ulyana was already pulling off her outer coat.

"Don't," Maren said, catching her wrist. Her grip was iron despite her years, hands deformed by thirty winters of diving. "The cold takes the strong ones first, girl. It's how you can tell it's hungry."

"I'm the strongest swimmer here and we both know it."

"That's why I said don't."

But Tomas had already gone flat to the ice, distributing his weight across the pan, inching toward the shelf with the rope looped under his arms. And Ulyana — because she could not stand still, because her body had never once in nineteen years allowed her to stand still while something suffered within reach — went after him, low and slow, the wind cutting sideways off the water with teeth in it.

The stranger was wedged in the rubble of pans like a coin dropped between floorboards. Up close he smelled of river mud and woodsmoke and something else, something wrong, an animal sweetness underneath. His lips were the color of slate. His eyes were closed. And his arms — this was the thing Ulyana would remember later, the thing she would turn over at night for weeks afterward — his arms were wrapped around a leather satchel the way a drowning child wraps around its mother. Both fists locked on the strap. Knuckles bloodless. Even the river could not pry it loose.

"He's got it frozen to him," Tomas called back. "Help me — the strap —"

Together they worked him free, and the current seemed to feel them taking their prize and fought them for it, sucking at their legs, shoving whole pans of ice grinding against each other with sounds like millstones. When the stranger's weight finally came loose and slid onto the solid ice, Ulyana's fingers had stopped registering as fingers. They hauled him the last yards hand over hand while the shore crew pulled the rope, and then he was on the stones and the crowd closed over them like water closing over a stone, and someone had brandy, and someone else had already run for the post surgeon, who would not come, because the surgeon was paid by the Company and the man on the stones was not.

"Is he breathing?"

"Barely."

"The satchel. Take the satchel off him before it —"

"No."

Ulyana did not know she had said it until the word was out, hard and flat as a hammer on rock. Half the crescent turned to look at her. The stranger's fists had not loosened; if anything, unconsciousness had made the grip absolute, the way rigor holds what living muscles only borrow. She knelt beside him, worked her own fingers under the strap where it crossed his chest, and found his pulse going slow and stubborn beneath the wet shirt, a heartbeat like oars in a lock, rowing steadily toward some shore only he could see.

"Girl, if he's carrying Company goods —" Maren began.

"He's freezing to death. You don't strip a freezing man." Which was true, and also entirely beside whatever point Maren was making, and they both knew it.

They carried him to the infirmary on a door taken off its hinges — Kiryl pried it loose with a crowbar and dared anyone to report him, grinning the whole time, because that was Kiryl's answer to everything — and laid him by the stove, and stripped the river off him, and rubbed his limbs with wool until feeling came back into them with the terrible ache of returning spring. Ulyana stayed. There was diving to do, quota to meet, and she stayed anyway, wringing out the man's clothes and hanging them by the fire, telling herself it was because someone should watch whether he lived. Telling herself many things.

He woke near midday, all at once, the way animals wake — no surfacing, no confusion, just eyes snapping open and his whole body convulsing upright, hands going instantly to the satchel, which sat on the cot beside him because they had never managed to take it from him and had mostly stopped wanting to.

"Easy," Ulyana said. "You're at the Verrin post. Mile sixty-two."

His eyes swept the room — infirmary, stove, window, her — with a speed and thoroughness that told her things about him before he said a word. Couriers, smugglers, soldiers: people whose eyes counted exits. Then, finding the satchel intact, some fraction of the tension left his shoulders, maybe a tenth.

"Davor," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "My name is Davor."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to." He tried to sit further up and failed, grimaced. "Everyone asks. Names first, then who do you serve, then where are you from. Save us the steps. I'm nobody's. I'm from everywhere north of here. And I need—" His eyes went to the window, measuring the light. "What day is it?"

"Fourth of Frostmonth."

"Fourth." He said it again, quieter, and Ulyana watched something go through his face that she recognized without understanding: the look of a man doing arithmetic and getting a number worse than he feared. "How long has the river held?"

"She'll freeze solid inside the week. Maybe less."

"The icebreaker. When does she sail north? Last sailing?"

"Before the freeze. Five days, if the Company doesn't cancel."

Davor closed his eyes. For a moment Ulyana thought he'd gone back under, but his hands had moved to the satchel and were working at it — not opening it, exactly. Checking. Thumbs moving along its seams the way a diver's thumbs move along a shell, reading it for cracks.

"You dove," he said suddenly, eyes still shut. "Your hands. The callus pattern. Pearl beds, cold water, company contract. You started young."

"Seven."

"Seven," he repeated softly, and something in the way he said it made the room tilt a degree, though Ulyana could not have said why. "So did she."

"Who?"

He didn't answer. He had begun to shiver again, the deep systemic shiver of a body that had been too cold too long, and Ulyana tucked the blanket higher around him with the brisk unsentimentality of someone who had done it a hundred times for her mother, and told herself the small tightness in her chest was nothing, was professional, was the ordinary tenderness of the indentured for anything else caught in the Company's machinery.

* * *

It happened that evening, and it happened because Ulyana was curious, and curiosity was the one luxury the Company had never managed to tax.

Davor slept like the dead — an unfortunate phrase, she thought, watching his chest rise and fall — and the satchel sat on the cot beside his hip, wax-sealed flap uppermost, and she had come in to change his bandages and the stove needed tending and she had to lean across him, and the seal was cracked already, split where the river's ice had done its patient work. One corner of the flap stood up like a page begging to be turned.

She did not mean to look. That was what she told herself, then, and for months afterward: *I did not mean to.*

Inside the satchel were letters, bundled and tied, oilskin-wrapped against the water that had found them anyway. Beneath the letters, a thickness of folded papers, dense with columns of figures. And on top of the bundle, loose, a single page in handwriting that stopped Ulyana's heart the way cold water stops it — one great clench and then nothing, nothing, nothing.

Slanted. Leaning hard to the right as if blown by a wind blowing forever from the west. The capital letters flourished, the tails of the y's hooked like fishhooks, the crossbars of the t's drawn in single impatient strokes. She knew that hand. She had traced it in sand when she was five. It had guided her own small fingers around the shapes of letters on the back of an old invoice, laughing at her, correcting her gently — *no, little bird, the tail goes under, like this* — a hand that had written her name on a Company indenture document and a promise on the flyleaf of a songbook and then, one gray morning ten years ago, had been reported drowned past the sandbar with no body recovered, and the river had kept him the way the river keeps everything.

Her father's handwriting. On a letter dated four months ago.

Ulyana did not remember sitting down. She was on the floor beside the cot with the page shaking in her hands, and Davor was awake, she realized, had been awake for some time, watching her with those quick courier's eyes that counted everything, and neither of them said anything at all. Outside, the Verrin groaned against its new ice, and somewhere down the dark river a lantern bobbed — the night watch, or something else, something carried silently toward the people in its path.

"Read it," Davor said quietly. "It's addressed to you."

The ground tilted. The past walked out of the water, dripping and alive, and put its slanted letters in her hands.

*Little bird,* the page began.
