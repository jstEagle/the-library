## 3. Letters from a Drowned Man

The fever broke on the third night, and Davor woke to the smell of tallow and river rot, which was, he reflected through the gray gauze of half-consciousness, considerably better than the smell of the river itself.

He was in an infirmary — a narrow room above the Company post's storehouse, one window glazed with frost ferns, two cots, the second of them empty. His clothes had been taken. In their place: a blanket that smelled of lye and someone else's sweat, and beneath it, his own body, bruised green from hip to shoulder where the ice had chewed him before spitting him out downstream. His ribs announced themselves with every breath, each one a small betrayal.

But the satchel was there. He knew it before he opened his eyes, because he could feel its absence from his hands like a phantom limb, and then he heard it — the soft drag of oiled canvas against floorboards, somewhere near the foot of his cot. Someone had it. Someone had *taken* it while he slept.

He sat up too fast. The room tilted. And there she was, sitting on a stool by the cold stove with the satchel open across her knees, a letter held up to the light of a single guttering lamp.

She didn't startle. He would remember that later — how she didn't startle. She only looked at him for a long moment, this drowned courier hauled half-dead out of her river, and said, "You held onto it even when you were unconscious. The men who pulled you out said you fought them with your eyes closed."

"Reflex," Davor croaked. His throat felt scoured with sand.

"It's not reflex." She turned the letter toward him. "It's devotion. There's a difference."

The handwriting on the page was slanted leftward, hurried, the letters leaning like trees in a wind that never stops. Davor knew that hand better than he knew his own. He had carried it through three winters, across two hundred miles of frozen road and thawed marsh, folded against his chest where the wax seals wouldn't crack.

"That's my father's writing," the girl said. Her voice did not shake, which was how he knew she was holding herself together with both hands. "My father is dead. He drowned ten years ago. They dragged the river for six days."

Davor leaned back against the wall. The fever had left a taste in his mouth like old coins, and behind it, gathering, the words he had been carrying almost as long as the letters themselves — words that were not his, that he had rehearsed in a safehouse lit by a single lamp, spoken by a man with his daughter's same dark eyes.

"Then the river lied," he said.

* * *

Her name was Ulyana Sandoval, and she did not cry, and she did not shout. She listened. That was the first thing Davor understood about her, and it frightened him more than the fever had, because a girl who listens like that — completely, without blinking, hoarding every word like a pearl — is a girl who has already decided what she will do with them.

He told it plainly, because there was no other way to tell it, and because the plainness itself was a kind of mercy. Kindness, he'd learned, was not softening the truth. It was delivering it whole and letting the other person decide where it hurt.

Ten years ago, a diver named Aldric Sandoval had noticed something in the quotas. Not in his own — in everyone's. Numbers that didn't add. Debts recorded against divers who had paid them off. Indentures extended by years on paper that bore no resemblance to the years worked. He had begun keeping copies. A man who spends his life underwater learns patience; a man who spends his life underwater for a company that owns his debt learns to count everything twice.

What Aldric found, buried in the ledgers' deeper columns, was worse than fraud. It was arithmetic with human beings as the units. The Verrin Trading Company had been selling indentured divers' debts north — upriver, past the magistrate's jurisdiction, to labor brokers who bought a debt and thereby bought the debtor. Whole families transferred on a signature. Men and women who thought they were diving their way toward freedom were being sold toward something else entirely, and the ledgers made it legal, and the ledgers made it quiet.

"Aldric couldn't take it to the magistrate here," Davor said. "The magistrate's clerk is the auditor's brother-in-law. So he did the only thing left. He went into the water one morning with his knife and his satchel of copies, and he did not come up where they expected him to. There's a stretch below the falls where the current goes under the rock shelf. Every diver knows it. Every diver knows you don't come back from it." He paused. "Except once."

Ulyana's hands had gone still in her lap. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves, and somewhere below, the night shift's boots scraped on ice.

"He surfaced four miles down, in the reed shallows, half-frozen, and a woman named Maren pulled him out and told no one — not even you?"

"She told no one," Davor confirmed. "Not even me, at first. I was nineteen. I ran messages between the fish camps, and I was cheap, and I asked no questions. Aldric bought my silence with a secret instead of coin, which was the only currency he had left. For ten years he has lived north, moving between safehouses, copying pages, building the case. Page by page. Seal by seal. Enough paper to hang the Company's charter from the rafters." He looked at the satchel in her lap. "I'm the last courier he trusts. Those letters are the final set. With them, the magistrate can void every forged indenture on the river. Including—"

"Including mine," Ulyana said quietly.

Davor hadn't told her that part yet. She had read ahead. Of course she had.

"Yes," he said. "Your contract is among the falsified ones. Your father knows. It's why he sent what he sent. It's why—" He stopped.

"Why he let us think he was dead for ten years," she finished. Her voice was still level. Still terribly level. "Why he let my mother bury an empty box. Why he let me grow up diving his own quota under his own stolen name, believing history happened to other people."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"You have to understand—" Davor began.

"No." She stood, and the satchel stayed in her lap a moment longer, her fingers pressed flat against the canvas as if taking its pulse. "Don't defend him yet. Let me be angry first. I've earned ten years of it."

* * *

She was angry until nearly dawn, though mostly silently, and Davor learned that anger, in some people, is just grief wearing armor it made itself.

He watched her move around the infirmary — feeding the stove, checking the frost on the window, straightening a blanket on the empty cot with unnecessary precision — and understood that she was doing what her father had done for ten years: working with her hands so her mind wouldn't drown.

"I used to write him letters," she said finally, without turning around. "After he died. When I was nine, ten. I'd put them in the river at the ice hole and watch them sink, because Maren said the water carries things to the people we lose." She laughed, short and hard, with nothing in it. "He was alive. He might have gotten them. Some fisherman upstream, some courier — he had couriers, apparently, a whole network, and my letters sinking into the mud while he sat in a warm house two hundred miles away reading *ledgers*."

"The house wasn't warm," Davor said. "And he read your letters. Maren carried them north the first three years. All of them."

That stopped her. Her shoulders, squared against him all night, dropped by a degree.

"He read them?"

"Every one. He has them in a tin box. He told me—" Davor hesitated, then decided the truth owed her nothing but itself. "He told me that if I ever met his daughter, I should tell her he kept every letter, and that he answered them all in his head, and that the answers were the reason he didn't stop. Ten years, copying pages in rooms with blacked windows, eating food bought with counterfeit names — and the thing that kept him going was a girl writing to a drowned man at the bottom of a river."

Ulyana sat down slowly on the stool. The fire had caught at last, and its light moved on her face, and Davor saw the fury in it begin to curdle into something more dangerous and more useful — not forgiveness, not yet, but the refusal to look away.

"He chose the cause over us," she said. It was not a question, and he did not insult her by pretending otherwise.

"He chose a cause that included you," Davor said. "Your name is in those ledgers, Ulyana. Your mother's. Tomas's. Old Maren's. Everyone you've ever dived beside. Your father looked at the water you swim in every day and decided someone had to drain it, and the price was that he'd never see you again, and he paid it, and he hated paying it, and he paid it anyway. You can hate him for it. He expects you to. But don't mistake the choice for absence. There wasn't a day he didn't—"

"Don't," she said softly. "Don't finish that sentence for him. If he wants to say it, he can come say it himself. Two hundred miles isn't a grave, courier. People cross it."

Outside, the sky was going the color of pewter, and the wind had shifted north, and Davor felt the change in it the way animals feel weather — a tightening along the spine. Somewhere south of here, on a road that would be impassable within days, an auditor was traveling with a company of enforcers, hunting a satchel of letters, following the cold trail of a courier who should have frozen in the river.

"How long do we have?" Ulyana asked. She had seen him feel it too. Of course she had.

Davor swung his legs off the cot, ignoring what the movement cost him, and reached for the boots someone had placed by the stove — her boots, he realized, too large for him by two sizes, stuffed with rags.

"The river freezes solid within the week," he said. "When it does, the last boat out is the icebreaker, and after that, this post is sealed until spring — sealed, audited, and searched. The auditor will arrive before the ice does. He always arrives before the weather." He looked up at her, and saw her father in her face for the first time — the same stubborn architecture around the jaw, the same eyes that counted everything twice. "We have perhaps five days to decode these letters, learn his routes, and decide who takes that last berth north. And Ulyana — the first thing the auditor will do when he lands is ask the overseer who pulled a stranger from the river."

The door latch rattled downstairs. Boots on the stair — heavy, unhurried, official.

Ulyana slid the satchel under the empty cot's blanket in one motion, smooth as a diver slipping under water, and Davor understood that whatever else the next five days held, they would not hold the girl who believed the Company post was the whole world.

The world, it turned out, had been coming for her all along.
