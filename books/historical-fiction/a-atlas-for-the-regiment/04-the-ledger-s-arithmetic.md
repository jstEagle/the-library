## 4. The Ledger's Arithmetic

The infirmary smelled of pine resin and the particular sourness of wool that had been wet too long. Ulyana came at dusk, when the shift bells had rung and the overseers retreated to their heated quarters, carrying a clay pot of barley soup she had traded two hours of evening mending to obtain. It was not kindness, exactly. Or it was, but kindness with a hook in it — she wanted what Davor knew, and they both understood that soup was simply the shape her wanting took.

He was sitting up against the wall, propped on the one clean blanket, and he watched her come in with the wary stillness of a man who had learned that doors opening were rarely good news.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"Neither should you." She set the pot on the crate beside his cot. "Yet here we both are."

His laugh was a dry rustle, quickly smothered. Up close, in the light of the single tallow lamp, he looked younger than he had half-drowned in the ice hole — twenty-five, perhaps, with a courier's lean hunger in his face and hands that never quite stopped moving, even now, even resting, fingers tapping out some silent rhythm against his knee. Route-keeping, she would learn to call it later. The habit of men who measured distance by heart.

"You saw the letters," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen her face when they'd pulled him from the water; he'd felt her fingers go rigid on the satchel strap before the others pried him loose.

"I saw my father's handwriting." She lowered herself onto the stool across from him, knees nearly touching his cot. "Ten years after they pulled his boot from the river and called it a drowning."

Davor was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the shutter, and somewhere down the row a diver coughed the wet, deep cough of lungs that had taken too much cold water over too many years. Her mother coughed like that. Ulyana made herself not think about it.

"His name was Aldric," Davor said finally. "Aldric Sandoval. Though for the last ten years he's gone by Vess, a widower who keeps bees, if you can believe it, outside a town called Kolvern." He met her eyes. "Two hundred miles north. Past the falls, past the toll bridges, past every place the Company's writ runs strong enough to matter."

The words did something strange to the room. The walls seemed to recede and tilt, as though the whole infirmary were a boat taking on water. *Alive.* She had spent ten years folding her father into a small, sealed shape — a boot, a river, a grief her mother carried like a stone sewn into her chest — and now the shape was coming apart in her hands, and inside it was not a drowned man but a beekeeper.

"He let us think—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "My mother lit a candle for him every year. Ten candles. She's still paying off the wax-debt to the Company store."

"I know," Davor said softly. "He knows. He talks about you. Both of you. Every time I've seen him, which is four times in six years, he asks after a girl named Ulyana who dives for pearls and writes letters no one can send." He paused. "I used to think it was sentiment. It wasn't. It was reconnaissance. He was building a case, and your life was part of the evidence."

* * *

They worked through the night by the light of two lamps, because one lamp's worth of secrecy was not enough and three would be conspicuous. Davor spread the satchel's contents across the floor: oilcloth-wrapped bundles, each page protected from the river by layers of waxed linen, and beneath the protection the paper was still damp-edged, still smelling faintly of the cold brown water that had nearly been his grave.

There were forty-one pages. Ulyana counted them twice.

"Company ledgers," Davor said. "Genuine ones, first — Aldric copied them himself over nine years, working in the records office at Fort Marren before anyone suspected him. Purchase agreements. Debt transfers. And then—" he tapped the thinnest bundle, "—the copies of the forged instruments. This is the proof."

"Forged how?"

"Forged the way everything is forged. Slowly, and with stamps." He drew a page toward the lamp and turned it so she could read. "Look. Here's a diver's debt, sold upriver. Read the name."

She read it. The name meant nothing to her — *Petra Ilves, indenture transferred to the Kessel Combine, sum outstanding: thirty-one marks* — but the date meant something, and the transfer clause meant something more, because Ulyana had spent nineteen years among people who spoke fluent contract the way fish spoke water. She could read a debt instrument the way other girls read ballads.

"This isn't a sale," she said slowly. "This is a forfeiture clause dressed as a sale. The debt transfers if the diver misses quota three seasons running — but look, the quota here is set at forty pearls a season. Nobody dives forty. Not in these waters. Not in any waters."

"Now the next page."

She turned it. Another name. Another impossible quota. Another transfer north, to buyers whose names she half-knew from the whispered geography of the divers' barracks — places divers went and did not come back from, or came back from changed, thinner, silent, with a new scar where a brand might sit.

"They're not selling debts," she said. "They're manufacturing them. Setting quotas nobody can meet, waiting for the forfeit, and selling the people." The words came out flat and strange, as though someone else were saying them through her mouth. "We're not workers. We're inventory being moved at a loss."

"And the Company auditor signs off on every transfer," Davor said. "That's the beauty of it, from their side. Nothing illegal ever happens. Paper does all the work. Paper and arithmetic." His mouth twisted. "Aldric used to say: show me a cruelty and I'll show you the column that hides it."

Ulyana sat back on her heels. The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself, and in its unsteady light the pages seemed almost to move, the columns of figures swimming like silt in current. Forty-one pages. Nine years of copying. One man pretending to drown so that this paper could live.

"Why me?" she asked suddenly. "You said my life was evidence. What did I ever—"

Davor didn't answer. Instead he reached into the last bundle, the one he had kept apart from the others, wrapped separately, and unfolded a single sheet with the care of a man handling a wound.

It was an indenture contract. Standard form, Company stamp, the dense legal script she had known since childhood — she had one herself, locked in the overseer's cabinet, renewed annually since she was seven years old.

The name on it was hers. *Ulyana Sandoval.*

Her first thought, absurdly, was that it must be a copy, a forgery planted among the true documents. But she read the clauses, and her stomach turned to cold lead, because she recognized the drafting. The seasonal quota — thirty-eight pearls. The transfer provision upon triple default. The assignee: the Kessel Combine, pre-signed, pre-stamped, dated eleven months ago.

"It's already done," she heard herself say. "Already executed. I'm not going to be sold. As far as the ledger is concerned, I've *been* sold. It's just—" she groped for the word, "—it's just waiting for the paperwork to catch up with my body."

"The auditor's catch-up," Davor said quietly. "That's why he's coming."

* * *

The auditor. Davor laid it out the way couriers lay out routes, in segments, each leg measured against the last. A man named Corvane — no, she corrected him, she'd heard the name in the mess hall gossip, it was Corvan — traveling downriver on the annual inspection circuit, due at the post within the week. Officially: a review of quotas and stores. Unofficially, per the intelligence Aldric's network had assembled over three years: a hunt. Someone in Fort Marren had discovered pages missing from the archive. Someone had traced the missing pages to a courier line. Three couriers had already been taken between here and the falls; Davor was the fourth, and the only one who'd made it into the water instead of into irons.

"He's coming here because the trail ends here," Davor said. "And because you're here. Corvan doesn't know yet that Aldric has a daughter at this post — or he suspects, which is worse. Either way, when he arrives, he'll start pulling threads. Your contract. Your mother's medical debt. The record of a drowned man named Sandoval who had access to the Marren archives ten years ago and whose body was never—" he stopped.

"Never found," Ulyana finished. "Because there wasn't one."

The lamp burned low. Somewhere far off, a wolf sounded along the frozen bank, a thin high thread of sound that seemed to stitch the whole night together. Ulyana looked down at the contract bearing her name and felt something shift in her chest — not the hot flare she would have expected, not fury, but something colder and more durable. An arithmetic of her own, clicking into place.

All those years she had believed history was a city thing. Something that happened behind stone walls, to magistrates and merchants, to people whose names got printed in the gazettes that arrived at the post months late and half-mildewed. She had thought her life was too small for history — quota and cough medicine and candle-debt, the same river, the same cold, year over year. She had thought willful blindness was the same as peace.

But the blindness had been designed. The smallness had been manufactured, stamped, filed. Her mother's cough was a line item. Old Maren's missing seasons were a column. The twins, Tomas, every diver sleeping in the long barracks tonight — they were all entries in a book written by men they would never meet, and the book had already decided where their bodies would end up.

History hadn't been happening in cities. It had been happening *to her*, in ink, for nineteen years.

"We get these to the magistrate at Kolvern," she said at last, and her voice surprised her with its steadiness. "Before Corvan reaches the post. Before the river freezes."

"That's the route," Davor agreed. "Two hundred miles. The icebreaker makes the last northern sailing in six days, and after that the river locks until thaw." He hesitated, and in the lamplight she saw him weigh something — the way a man weighs telling a passenger the truth about a crossing. "Ulyana. There's something else. Corvan won't come alone, and he won't come gently. When he lands, the first thing he'll do is seal the infirmary."

She looked at him.

"He already knows I'm alive," Davor said. "And the only person who's visited me is you."

* * *

She walked back through the dark rows toward the women's quarters with the cold biting through her coat and the numbers still turning behind her eyes — thirty-one marks, thirty-eight pearls, forty-one pages, two hundred miles, six days. Above the post the stars stood hard and white as driven nails, and the river ran black beneath its forming skin of ice, patient, keeping whatever it was given.

In the doorway of the quarters her mother sat wrapped in two blankets, waiting up, as she always waited up, with a candle burning down between her hands.

"You've been to see him," her mother said. Not a question either. Her voice was rough with the cough, but underneath the roughness there was something else — something that had been waiting ten years by a candle flame.

Ulyana crouched down beside her, close enough to feel the heat of the little fire, and found she could not make the words come in any order that was kind. So she told the truth the way the ledger had taught her: plainly, in full, all at once.

"Mama. Papa is alive. And the Company has already sold me."
