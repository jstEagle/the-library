## 5. What the Overseer Knows

The ledgers did not lie easily, and neither did Brandt, but he had learned to make them both try.

He sat in his office above the drying sheds with the quota book open before him and a lamp turned low — low enough that the figures swam, high enough that an honest man could still read them. He was not an honest man. He had stopped being one eleven years ago, on a night in late autumn much like this one, when a diver named Pell had come up from the ice hole with her hands too frozen to feel the rope and had looked at him with that particular blankness divers got when they understood they were going to die in the water and had made their peace with it. The Company's book said Pell owed nine more seasons. Brandt had written *deceased* beside her name instead, and entered a dead woman's quota as met, spread across the other entries like ash raked over a fire.

Since then there had been others. Not many. Never enough to draw the eye of a district clerk, never so few that the barracks starved. A forgery, done well, is a kind of mercy performed with arithmetic, and Brandt had become fluent in it. He told himself this every morning when he inked his pen. Some mornings he believed it.

Tonight he did not believe it, because tonight there was a second lamp burning downstairs in the counting room, and the man sitting under it had arrived without warning on the afternoon packet boat, wearing good wool and the expression of someone who had never once been cold.

*Auditor Vess,* the letter of introduction had read. *Empowered to examine all posts on the lower river.* Empowered. The word sat in Brandt's stomach like a swallowed hook.

Through the floorboards he could hear Vess moving among the shelves, though the counting room had been locked at dusk. The man had simply produced a key. Of course he had. The Company gave its auditors keys to everything except, apparently, patience.

Brandt closed the quota book and pressed his palm flat against its cover, as if holding something down.

* * *

Vess received him in the counting room at an hour when decent men were asleep, and seemed to consider this a courtesy rather than an imposition. He was younger than Brandt expected — forty perhaps, with a clerk's softness through the middle and a fencer's stillness in the hands. Two enforcers stood by the door wearing the grey coats of the Company constabulary, which no post on the lower river had seen in nineteen years.

"Overseer Brandt." Vess did not rise. "Your recovery rates are remarkable."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Your divers." Vess turned a ledger page with one finger. "Nineteen years on this stretch, and your mortality figures run a full third below the river average. Pearl beds kill men, Overseer. Cold water kills men. Yet here—" he tapped the page— "everyone lives long enough to pay off. Either you have found some secret of kindness the rest of the Company has missed, or your books are lying to me."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, ice creaked along the pilings like an old ship settling.

"The river is gentler here," Brandt said. "Shallow beds. Short dives."

"Mm." Vess closed the ledger gently, the way a man closes a door on a sickroom. "I'll want the last ten years of quota records by tomorrow evening. Originals. And I'll want to speak with your divers. Individually." He smiled then, and it was a professional smile, fitted to the face like a Company seal. "There is nothing to fear, Overseer. I am not hunting men. I am hunting paper. A courier went into the river near here some days ago — drowned, we assume. If any of your people pulled him out, or if anything he carried came ashore with him, the Company would be grateful to know."

"And if it didn't?"

"Then nothing." Vess spread his hands. "But papers are like bodies, Overseer. They always surface eventually. It is only a question of who is standing on the bank when they do."

Brandt walked back up to his office through the cold and did not light the lamp. He sat in the dark and thought about a courier's satchel, wax-sealed, and about a girl who had stood in his doorway two mornings ago asking for extra tallow for the infirmary — a small lie, he had assumed, the ordinary economy of the indentured — and how she had not quite met his eyes, and how her father had never quite met his either, all those years ago, in exactly the same way, right up until the day Sandoval walked into the river and did not come out.

Except that now, apparently, he had.

* * *

He had been there, that day. That was the thing Brandt could not put down, the stone he kept turning over. He had been standing on the dock when they dragged the water for Sandoval's body, and he had watched the grappling hooks come back empty three times, and he had signed the death certificate himself because there was no body to sign it for him, and he had felt — he had *noted*, he corrected himself, he was an overseer, he noted things — that the current below the falls ran slow that week, that a drowned man should have surfaced within two days, that the river gives back what it takes unless the taking was voluntary.

He had noted it. And then he had filed it away in the same drawer where he kept the falsified quotas and Pell's blank stare and every other small treason he could not afford to look at directly. Because looking at it meant acting on it, and acting on it meant choosing, and Brandt had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not choosing. He served the Company the way a beam serves a house: by bearing weight and never asking what the house was built over.

But the auditor was in the counting room now, and the house was being measured.

In the morning he walked the drying sheds early, before the shift bells, because walking let him think and thinking was dangerous and he preferred to do both where no one could see. Frost had furred the ropes silver. Down at the ice hole, the first shift was already gathering — old Maren chafing her wrists, the twins stamping in unison like a pair of ill-tempered horses, Tomas silent as always at the edge of the group, and Ulyana Sandoval checking her dive line with the methodical care her father had taught her, because of course he had, because everything in her was something he had put there.

She looked up as he passed. Nineteen years old and she had her mother's face and her father's caution, and when their eyes met she held his for half a heartbeat longer than deference required — not defiance, exactly. Assessment. As if she were deciding what he knew and what he could be made to forget.

"Overseer," she said.

"Diver." He kept walking. His heart was behaving disgracefully.

* * *

At midday he fetched the ten years of quota records from the strongbox and sat with them for a long time before carrying them down.

Here was the arithmetic of his life, laid out in columns. Eleven years of small forgeries: quotas marked met that were never met, debts quietly aged backward, one diver after another eased off the Company's books and into the gray country of the officially deceased while they lived on, unrecorded, in the barracks. If Vess read these pages with a careful eye, Brandt would hang — or worse; the Company had learned, somewhere in its charter law, punishments older and quieter than hanging. If Vess did not, the records bought him time, and time was the only currency he had ever managed to save.

And beneath that arithmetic lay another one. The courier. The letters. Sandoval alive, somewhere north, doing — what? Building a case. Gathering proof of things Brandt had only ever suspected: that the debts were being sold downriver, that the divers' children were being entered as collateral in ledgers kept in cities whose names Brandt barely knew. He had suspected it the way you suspect weather coming. He had never once asked to see the sky.

Ulyana Sandoval's name was in those pages. Her contract. He checked, though he already knew — entry dated six years after her father's drowning, debt transferred, terms amended, the amendment initialled by a district clerk whose signature appeared on a hundred such amendments across the river country. Sold. The daughter of a drowned man, entered as security against a debt her father had died — had *pretended* to die — protesting.

Brandt thought: if the letters say what Vess fears they say, then every forgery I ever committed was a pebble thrown at a fortress wall. And he thought: if I give Vess the girl, the auditor will cancel my errors in exchange for my cooperation, the way one cancels a debt, and the post will go on, and Maren will go into the water tomorrow, and I will go on writing *deceased* beside names until they write it beside mine.

He carried the records down to the counting room and set them on Vess's table.

"All ten years," Brandt said. "Originals."

Vess looked up at him with those patient, weighing eyes. "You hesitated at the door, Overseer. Four seconds, perhaps five." He drew the top ledger toward himself. "Men hesitate for two reasons. Guilt, or loyalty. I find it saves a great deal of time to know which." He opened the book. "You knew Sandoval, I take it. The diver. Before the drowning."

"Everyone knew Sandoval."

"Yes. And his daughter pulls quotas on your best bed, and two days after a courier drowns in your river she requests medicine for an infirmary that, according to your own supply logs, was fully stocked." Vess turned a page without looking at it. His attention stayed on Brandt the whole time, gentle and total, like the pressure of deep water. "I am not accusing anyone, Overseer. I am observing. There is a difference, and the difference is that observation costs nothing today and can be spent later." He smiled again. "The icebreaker makes its last sailing north in six days. After that, the river belongs to winter, and whatever happens here happens without appeal. I'd think carefully about which side of the ice you'd like to stand on."

Brandt returned to his office. He did not remember climbing the stairs.

He lit the lamp at last and took out a fresh sheet of paper and sat before it for a long while. Then he wrote, in his small careful hand, a note he did not send: *Sandoval — if any part of you is still reading the river, your daughter is standing where you left her, and the wolves have come with keys.*

He burned it in the lamp flame and watched the ash curl.

Six days. And below him, faint through the floorboards, he could hear the counting room door open and close, open and close, and boots moving among the shelves where the truth of nineteen years lay stacked in rows — and somewhere out in the barracks, a girl who had inherited her father's caution checking a dive line in the dark, deciding, the way he was deciding, whom to trust with what remained.
