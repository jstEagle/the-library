## 6. Found Family, Fraying Seams

The barracks smelled of tallow and wet wool and the particular mineral cold that lived in the stones no fire could fully drive out. Ulyana had known that smell her whole life — had assumed it was simply the smell of the world, the way she had once assumed the Company post was the world entire. Now she sat on the edge of Maren's bunk with a stranger's secrets folded against her ribs, and the smell seemed suddenly like something else: not the world, just a room in it, one room among ten thousand she had never seen.

Maren's hands were in her lap, knuckles swollen as river stones, fingers gone crooked from thirty years of prying shell from riverbed. She looked at those hands now while Davor spoke.

He told it plainly, which was the only mercy available to him. He told them about the ledgers — the true ledgers, kept in her father's slanted hand, recording how the Verrin Trading Company sold its divers' debts north to the slavers upriver when those debts grew inconvenient. How a diver too old to dive, too sick to work, was not released but *transferred*, the word stamped in red wax so it looked official. How the drowned man Tam Ashgrove — Ulyana's father, though she still could not make her mouth form the words — had copied those pages for two years before faking his drowning beneath the winter ice, sending them north packet by packet through couriers like Davor until the last satchel, the last crossing, the ice that broke beneath him.

"And the auditor comes within the week," Davor finished. "If he finds these pages, they burn. If he finds me, I burn with them. That's the whole of it."

Silence held the room. The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall, the river muttered under its new skin of ice, the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep.

Old Maren lifted her head. "Tam Sandoval," she said slowly, testing the name. "The quiet one. Used to mend the young ones' boots without being asked."

"He did," Ulyana said. Her voice came out strange, thin. She had said almost nothing since the ice hole; there was too much inside her and none of it would arrange itself into speech.

"Dead ten years," Maren said. "Or not." She turned her ruined hands over, palm up, as if reading them. "I was twenty-two when the Company bought my debt off a grain failure in the south. Forty years I've been paying it. They tell me every spring there's a balance remaining." She looked around the room, at each face in turn. "You all know what a transferred diver is. You've all watched someone go north and not write back. Don't tell me you thought they'd forgotten their letters."

Nobody spoke. The twins — Petra and Pavel, nineteen and inseparable, their faces still round with the softness childhood had only lately surrendered — sat shoulder to shoulder on the floor, and Ulyana watched the same calculation move across both faces at once, the eerie synchrony of twins thinking as one body.

It was Pavel who said it, or Petra; afterward no one could agree. "The auditor pays for informants. A year off your indenture. Maybe two."

"Aye," said the other twin. "Two years is two years."

"That it is," Maren said mildly. "And how do you suppose you'll spend them? Diving? At your age, another two years in that water and you'll have my hands at forty. Or do you imagine the Company rewards loyalty twice?"

"They don't reward anything," Tomas said.

Everyone turned. Tomas, who slept in the bunk nearest the door and spoke perhaps forty words in an ordinary week, was sitting very straight, his long hands flat on his knees. He had been hurt in a slide three years back — a shelf of gravel had come down on his leg — and he dove now on borrowed strength, the Company's physicians having judged him fit enough to keep. His silence had always seemed to Ulyana like emptiness. She understood now, watching his face, that it had been weight all along.

"My brother went north," Tomas said. "Eight years ago. Debt transfer. My mother wrote to the Company office four times asking where he'd been sent. She got the same letter back four times with the date changed." He paused. "I always thought he'd stopped writing because he was ashamed. That he'd fallen in with the canal people, or died in some accident he didn't want us to mourn." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "He didn't stop writing. They stopped letting him."

Ulyana felt the ground tilt again, that sensation from the ice hole — the past reaching up through the frozen surface with its grey hands. All those years she had grieved her father as a dead man and hated the river a little for taking him, and meanwhile history had been happening, enormous and terrible, in rooms adjacent to her own, conducted in the language of ledgers and red wax. She had believed herself a spectator to other people's stories. There were no spectators. There had never been spectators. There were only people who hadn't yet read the pages with their names in them.

"So," Maren said into the quiet. "We have among us a courier, a satchel of papers worth more than this whole post, and the daughter of the man who stole them." She looked at Ulyana. "And you've told us. Which means we're all guilty now, whether we lift a finger or not. Ignorance might have saved the twins." A thin, dry smile. "Not that they'd have earned much keeping it."

"We're not reporting anyone," Petra said — it was Petra, Ulyana was nearly certain — and Pavel nodded so hard his shaggy hair fell in his eyes.

"No," Pavel said. "But we should say it out loud. All of us. So it's done and can't be undone by fear later."

They said it out loud. It took only a moment — six voices in a cold room, agreeing to hang together — but Ulyana would remember the sound of it for the rest of her life: the way Tomas's low voice joined last, steady as a keel, and the way the silence afterward felt different from the silence before. Not lighter. Bound.

* * *

They began, in the days that followed, to build a small architecture of concealment, and like everything built by people who love each other, it was stronger than it was elegant.

Davor could not be seen at the post — his face would be known to the auditor's men if any description had gone south ahead of them — so he moved to the ice-fishers' huts downstream, and food made its way to him in Tomas's creel, tucked beneath the day's catch. Maren, whose infirmary duties let her come and go unremarked, carried broth and bandages and gossip. The twins invented a card game whose rules required them to sit near the boathouse window for hours, watching the road north, and swore so sincerely at each other's cheating that no overseer ever thought to wonder what else they were watching.

And in the evenings, when the lamps were trimmed low and Brandt's rounds had passed, Ulyana went down to the huts, and Davor taught her the routes.

He drew them first in fish-blood on the planking: the river country rendered as a spine with ribs, the great channel the spine, the tributaries branching off it like bones. "A courier thinks in water," he said. "Roads are watched. Rivers are watched too, but rivers change, and watchers' maps don't. Here — the old channel below Kessel's Landing, silted in twelve years ago. Every patrol map still shows it deep enough to boat a cargo. It's shin-deep and reed-choked. You could walk it naked at noon and no one would look twice, because no one believes it exists anymore."

"You walked it?" Ulyana asked.

"Twice. The second time my father's maps were wrong and I nearly drowned in a cut-off oxbow." He smiled without much humor. "Your father corrected my atlas. He had the river in his head better than men who'd piloted barges forty years. Said a pearl diver learns water differently — from underneath. You learn where it hides things."

She thought of her father teaching her letters on a slate, the same patient hand that had drawn hidden channels for smuggled truth. *The same hand*, she thought, and the thought was a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was locked.

"Memorize this," Davor said, tapping the fish-blood spine. "Don't copy it. Paper can be found; you can't be searched for what's in your skull. The relay stations — here, the widow Ostrer's farm, here the shrine at the fork, here the tannery at Bralla — you knock twice, wait, knock once. Never carry more than you can destroy. And never, ever take the same route twice running, even if the first one worked perfectly." He looked up at her. "Especially if it worked perfectly. Success makes people lazy, and lazy couriers are dead couriers."

"Why are you teaching me this?" she asked. It had been bothering her for two nights, working at her like a stone in a boot. "You're the courier. You'll be well again. Why does *I* need the routes?"

Davor was quiet a moment. In the hut's dimness his convalescent face looked older than it had half-drowned, the lines around his mouth carved deeper. "Because I may not be the courier much longer," he said. "Because your father trusted me to bring the pages north, and if I'm taken, the trust has to land somewhere, the way a thrown rope has to land in somebody's hands." He met her eyes. "And because you asked the question nobody else has asked. The others chose to shelter us. You're already asking what comes after sheltering. That's the difference between hiding and fighting, Ulyana. Your father knew it. It's in his blood somewhere, apparently."

She looked away, because her eyes were stinging and she would not cry in front of him. On the planking the fish-blood atlas was already drying to a faint brown ghost. She bent and studied it again, tracing the false channel below Kessel's Landing, memorizing, committing her father's river to whatever place in her kept his handwriting.

* * *

On the sixth night they gathered in Maren's corner of the infirmary — the warmest room, the safest, because even the auditor's men hesitated to search where the sick lay sleeping. Maren brewed her terrible chicory substitute and dealt out cups, and for a little while they simply sat, six people around a stove, and let themselves pretend it was ordinary. Petra cheated at cards. Pavel accused her loudly and wrongly. Tomas laughed — actually laughed, a rusty astonishing sound, like a gate opening after years — and everyone pretended not to notice so he wouldn't be embarrassed into closing it again.

Ulyana sat with her cup warming her palms and looked around the circle and understood that she was happy, or something near it, here at the edge of ruin. It made no sense and she did not question it. Nineteen years she had believed family was a thing the river decided — gave you a mother, took a father, left you the remainder. Now she counted a smuggler, a woman with stone-knuckled hands, twins who fought like one creature with two mouths, and a quiet man learning to laugh, and the arithmetic came out richer than what she'd lost.

"Winter will lock the river soon," Maren said, in the lull between hands. "Icebreaker makes its last sailing within the fortnight, then nothing moves north till thaw."

"Then we move before the river does," Ulyana said.

It was Tomas who went to the window — a small movement, unfinished sentence — and went still. "Lanterns," he said.

They followed his eyes. Down the post road, where the dark came up from the south like a tide, lanterns were swinging. Not one. A dozen, in careful formation, moving at the pace of men who did not need to hurry because hurry was for people who could be escaped.

Behind them, far down the river road, a second string of lights. The auditor had not waited for the week. He had come tonight.

Maren set down her cup very carefully, as if it might break, and looked at each of them the way she had on the first night. "Well," she said. "It's begun."
