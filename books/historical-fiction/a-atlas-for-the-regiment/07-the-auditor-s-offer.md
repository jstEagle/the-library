## 7. The Auditor's Offer

The summons came at the hour when the river went to glass — that brief stillness between the last dive and the lamplighting, when even the gulls seemed to hold their breath. A Company runner in a grey coat stood at the barracks door with a folded card on a tin salver, as though this were some fine house upriver and not a row of bunks that smelled of wet wool and lamp oil.

*Miss Sandoval. The auditor requests your presence. The counting house. After supper.*

Ulyana read it three times. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, the kind of paper that had never been dunked in a diver's satchel or used to wrap a heel of bread. She thought of Davor's warning, delivered low under the noise of the drying racks: *He'll come for you gently first. Men like that always do. It's the gentle ones you have to watch.*

Maren watched her fold the card into her sleeve. The old woman said nothing, only pressed a boiled sweet into her palm — a small amber thing, hoarded from some Christmas years ago — and squeezed her hand hard enough to hurt.

The counting house sat at the center of the Company post like a fat black beetle, its shingled roof furred with frost, its windows glowing warmer than any window had a right to glow in a place like this. Inside, Ulyana knew, there were brass fittings and ledgers bound in calfskin and a stove that burned coal rather than driftwood. She had been inside once, at nine years old, to sign her mark against her mother's debt. She remembered the smell of ink and beeswax and the way the clerk had looked through her as if she were a column of numbers that hadn't finished adding up.

She crossed the yard with her shoulders squared and her breath smoking, and she told herself she was not afraid. She was lying, and she knew it, and she walked anyway.

* * *

Auditor Vess was not what she expected.

She had imagined a Brandt grown crueler — thick-necked, loud, a man who wore his authority like a club. Instead, the man waiting for her behind the desk was narrow and neat, perhaps fifty, with spectacles perched low on a long nose and hands folded on the blotter before him like a man at prayer. He was pouring tea from a small porcelain pot — *porcelain*, here, at the edge of the world — into two cups, and he did not look up until she stopped in front of the desk.

"Ulyana Sandoval," he said, as though confirming an entry. "Daughter of Ilya Sandoval, diver, deceased these ten years. And of Marta Sandoval, currently carried on the infirmary rolls, consumption, third stage." He said it all without malice, without warmth, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "Sit, please. The tea is from the southern estates. You'll find it better than what they give the barracks."

"I'm not thirsty."

"Of course." He set the second cup aside without offense and removed his spectacles, polishing them on his sleeve, and for a moment he was simply a tired-looking man in a good coat. "Do you know what I do, Miss Sandoval? Truly do? Most people think an auditor counts things. Money, tonnage, pearls by weight. But counting is the least of it. What I actually do is find the difference between what a ledger says and what is true." He replaced the spectacles and looked at her, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without mercy. "There is a difference here. At this post. Someone has taken pages out of my company's records, and someone has been carrying letters written in a dead man's hand."

The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the walls, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," Vess agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. And if that were the end of it, we'd drink our tea and you'd go back to your bunks and none of this would concern you." He opened a drawer — unhurried, almost gentle — and laid two documents on the blotter between them. "But it isn't the end of it. Let me show you something. Not a threat. An arithmetic."

The first document was her own indenture contract. She knew every line of it; she had signed it at nine, pressing her thumb into ink because she couldn't yet write her whole name. Fourteen years of service against her mother's debt, the terms renewed twice by clerks who never explained why the number grew instead of shrank.

"This contract," Vess said, tapping it, "was sold four years ago. Do you know to whom?" He waited. She didn't answer. "To the Corvane factors, upriver. Along with eleven other divers' debts from this post. Your labor, Miss Sandoval, has been owned by slavers since you were fifteen years old. The Company simply hasn't told you yet. There was no need. The river freezes, the icebreaker comes twice a year, and debts travel north in the spring." He let that sit in the warm air between them. "I imagine your friend Davor may have mentioned something similar."

Her heart was a fist inside her ribs. She kept her face still. Nineteen years of diving taught you to keep your face still while your body screamed.

"The second document," Vess continued, turning it so it faced her, "is a release. Full cancellation of your indenture. Cancellation of your mother's debt — all of it, including the medical costs accrued since autumn, which are considerable. Passage for both of you on the icebreaker, north, wherever you like. Clean papers." He slid it toward her. "Signed by me. Effective the moment I countersign it."

The paper was beautiful. That was the obscene thing about it — the heavy cream stock, the precise clerk's hand, the blank space where his signature would go. It looked like freedom. It smelled like nothing at all.

"And in exchange," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"In exchange, the missing pages. The letters. And whatever you can tell me of where they came from — names, routes, the safehouse your father kept, if you know it." He folded his hands again. "That's all. No chains. No tribunal. The Company doesn't want a scandal, Miss Sandoval; scandals depress share prices. We want paper. Give me the paper, and you and your mother are free by morning, gone before the ice closes."

"She's dying," Ulyana said. It came out before she could stop it, raw.

"Yes," Vess said quietly. "Third-stage consumption, in a winter that will cut this post off for five months. If she stays here, she dies here, and you'll bury her in the frozen ground past the palisade with the other indentured dead. If she goes now, on a proper boat, with a doctor aboard and medicine in the hold—" He spread one hand, a small, honest gesture. "I am not offering you cruelty. I'm offering you the only version of mercy that exists in this world: yours. Other people's mercy is not yours to carry. That is a lesson most people learn too late and at great cost."

"You'd take Maren too. Tomas. The twins. Everyone who helped him."

Vess's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a flicker, like a fish turning under ice. "Everyone who helped him made their own choices. As you are making yours. I'll tell you something true, since you've been polite enough to listen." He leaned back. "Your father was a fool and a brave man, which are frequently the same animal. He stole from the Company because the Company steals from people like you every day of every year, and he thought pages in a magistrate's hand would stop it. Perhaps they would. Perhaps the charter falls, the debts void, the divers dance in the streets." He shook his head slowly. "And perhaps the magistrate is paid before the ice melts, and your father's pages become kindling, and everyone who touched them hangs. I have seen both outcomes. I cannot tell you which you'll get. Neither can he. But I can tell you exactly what you'll get, right now, tonight, guaranteed in writing." He tapped the release. "Two lives. Out. Free. Alive."

The stove ticked. Ulyana looked at the paper and saw her mother's face — the grey lips, the cough that bent her double at dawn, the way her eyes still lit when Ulyana brought her sweetened tea, as though sweetness itself were news. She saw the berth on the icebreaker. Warmth. Medicine. Her mother alive in the spring, writing to her from somewhere green.

And beneath it, like a body under clear water, she saw everything else: Maren's cracked hands. Tomas's silence. Davor's frostbitten fingers curled around a satchel he wouldn't surrender unconscious. Her father, alive two hundred miles north, believing — *trusting* — that his daughter would carry what he had bled ten years to send.

"He chose the cause over us," she said, and hated how young her voice sounded. "Ten years ago. He let us believe he was drowned."

"Yes," Vess said. "Men like him always do. It's the cause that keeps them warm at night; wives and daughters are just what they left ashore." He rose, straightening his coat, and came around the desk — close enough that she could smell the tea and the clean wool and, faintly, ink. "I'm not asking you to be braver than he was. I'm asking you to be wiser. Bravery is cheap, Miss Sandoval. It costs other people everything. Wisdom knows the price of a single life." His voice softened to almost-kindness, which was the worst thing yet. "Take your mother out of this cold. Let the men fight over their papers."

He returned to his side of the desk and wrote something on a slip, sliding it across.

"The icebreaker makes her final sailing day after tomorrow, on the morning tide, before the channel closes. My offer stands until her lines are cast off. After that—" He shrugged, elegant and final. "After that, the arithmetic changes, and I will be obliged to solve it less gently."

Ulyana took the slip. Her hand did not shake, and she was grateful to it.

At the door, Vess spoke once more, not unkindly.

"For what it's worth — your father's handwriting. I've seen it. On the old manifests, years ago. A beautiful slanted hand." A pause. "Dead men write such careful letters, don't they? Almost as though they want to be found."

She walked out into the dark. The frost had come down hard while she was inside; the whole yard glittered, treacherous and bright, and the river beyond the palisade was already wearing its winter skin at the edges. Somewhere in the barracks, muffled by walls, Maren was singing one of the old diving songs, low and cracked, the one about the girl who held her breath so long she forgot which world she belonged to.

Day after tomorrow. One slip of paper. Two lives, bought clean, at the price of everyone else's.

Ulyana stood in the glittering yard a long time, the auditor's deadline burning in her pocket like a coal, and did not yet know which way her feet would move when the morning came.

Behind her, the counting-house windows glowed warm as a lie.

Ahead, down by the water, a lantern swung at the edge of the dock — and beside it, moving fast and low along the pilings, a shape she recognized.

Davor was already running.
