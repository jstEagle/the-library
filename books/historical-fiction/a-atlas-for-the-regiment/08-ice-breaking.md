## 8. Ice Breaking

Davor had been a courier for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned that the body always knew before the mind did. The mind made maps, counted exits, weighed odds. The body simply went cold at the back of the neck and said: *run*.

It was the middle of the night watch when his neck went cold — the hour when even the river seemed to hold its breath beneath its new skin of ice. He lay in the loft above the drying sheds where Ulyana had hidden him three nights past, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tanning oil and old smoke, listening to the barracks below settle into its sleep. Somewhere across the compound, a dog began to bark. Then stopped. Then started again, higher, sharper, and was abruptly silent.

That silence was worse than any sound.

He rose without unfolding himself from the blanket, the way couriers were taught — leave no warmth behind you, leave no shape in the bedding — and crossed to the slatted window. The moon was a thin nail-paring low over the eastern bluffs, and by its light he saw them coming up the company road: six riders in the grey-and-black of the Verrin Trading Company, breath steaming, lanterns hooded, moving at a walk. Not hurrying. Men who hurry fear resistance. These men moved like an executioner's axe being lowered with ceremony.

Behind them came a seventh figure on foot, wrapped in a long coat, and Davor knew the set of those shoulders even at this distance. The auditor. Herr Voss, who smiled like a man showing you his teeth so you would not mistake them for anything else.

*Two days early.* The thought arrived flat and useless. Two hundred miles of river country between here and the safehouse, and the letters were not two hundred miles north; they were forty feet below him, sewn into the satchel Ulyana had hidden in the false bottom of the boathouse grain bin, because Brandt kept the boathouse locked and no one searched what the overseer had already inventoried.

Below him, through the floorboards, the barracks was stirring. He heard Maren's voice first — she slept badly, old divers always did, their lungs full of years of river — and then Tomas's low murmur, and then the twins' bunk creaking as one of them swung down. They knew too. Of course they knew. A compound this small heard everything: the dog, the hooves, the absence of the watchman's usual cough.

Davor took the satchel from under the floor plank and slung it across his chest. The wax seals pressed cold against his sternum through the canvas, and he thought, absurdly, of Ilya Sandoval standing on a dock ten years ago, pressing this same weight into Davor's hands, saying: *Whatever happens to me, these go north. Whatever happens to me. Do you understand what I am asking?*

He had understood. He just hadn't understood, until tonight, that *whatever happens to me* would someday mean *whatever happens to everyone else instead.*

* * *

The enforcers did not knock. They never do; knocking is a courtesy paid to people who own doors.

They came through the barracks gate in a wedge, boots ringing on frozen ground, and Davor was already in the yard when the first door splintered inward. Later he could not say why he'd come down instead of running for the boathouse — except that Maren was in the doorway of the women's quarters in her nightdress with her arms spread like a fence post, seventy years old and telling four armed men in a voice gone thin with rage: "Not past me. Whatever your paper says, it doesn't say past me."

"Old woman." The lead enforcer — a heavy man with a face like a thawed-out turnip — didn't even raise his voice. "Step aside or be carried aside. Either way you come with us."

"Come with—"

"Harboring fugitives is debt-fraud by extension, madam. The Company is generous enough to consider your presence here an asset rather than a liability. You'll be questioned at the audit house." He said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "Everyone will be questioned at the audit house."

Davor saw the geometry of it in a single glance: Maren being taken, the twins pinned against the well housing, Tomas already bleeding — a cut over the eye, nothing deep, but blood on white snow is a loud thing, and Tomas was swaying on his feet with the particular stillness of a man deciding whether falling is permitted.

And Ulyana. Ulyana was not there. Thank every drowned god, she was not there; she must have gone to the boathouse already, following the plan they'd agreed on if the worst came — and the worst had come two days early, but plans don't care about calendars.

Which left only him, and the satchel, and the question of how much a diversion weighs.

He walked out of the shadow of the drying sheds with his hands visible and his voice pitched to carry. "You're looking for a courier. Here I am."

The turnip-faced enforcer turned slowly, and Davor watched understanding move across that coarse face like weather. "The man from the river."

"The man from the river," Davor agreed. He let his coat fall open so they could see the satchel strap across his chest. "And this is what your auditor wants more than any of these people. So here is my offer: the divers walk free tonight — all of them, including the old woman — and I give you this without a fight."

"Davor, no—" Tomas started forward and one of the enforcers put a hand flat on his chest, almost gently, the way you restrain something you've already decided isn't worth breaking further.

"You're in no position to offer terms," the enforcer said.

"No," Davor agreed pleasantly. "But I'm in a position to run. And I'm very good at running. Ask anyone upriver. Eleven years, and they never caught me until I let myself be pulled out of a hole in the ice." That part was true, and he let the truth do its work: hesitation, calculation, the flick of eyes toward the audit house where the auditor waited. "Or you can take me now, satchel intact, and tell Herr Voss you solved his problem in one night. Your choice. But decide quickly, because my arm is getting tired of holding this strap."

It was a lie stacked on a bluff stacked on a prayer, and it worked the way such things work — not cleanly, not entirely. Maren was released, pushed stumbling toward the barracks with an enforcer's handprint bruising her upper arm; Davor memorized that handprint, filed it away in the ledger of accounts he intended to see settled. The twins were shoved after her. Tomas was not. Tomas, whose eye was still bleeding, whose only crime had been sharing bread and silence with strangers, was bound at the wrists and told he'd answer questions about conspiracy.

"Take me instead," Davor said, and heard his own voice crack on it, which frightened him more than anything else that night. "He's a boy. Take—"

"He's evidence," the enforcer said, and struck Tomas across the mouth when the boy tried to speak again, and Davor learned in that moment the precise difference between fury and helplessness: fury has somewhere to go.

They took the satchel first — of course they did, they weren't fools — cutting the strap and passing it between two men like men handling a live coal. Davor watched it go and felt the strangest sensation: lightness. Ten years of carrying someone else's courage, and now it was gone, and underneath it his ribs felt bare and human and afraid.

They bound his wrists in front — sloppy, rushed; the diversion had cost them their discipline — and marched him across the yard toward the lamplit windows of the audit house, where Herr Voss stood on the step in his immaculate coat, and even at forty paces Davor could see him smiling.

That was when he saw the boathouse door swing shut.

Only a sliver of movement, a wedge of darkness closing against darkness at the edge of his vision — but he had spent eleven years reading landscapes for a living, and he knew what he had seen. The boathouse door, which was supposed to be locked, which Overseer Brandt held the only working key to, closing from the *inside.*

Brandt had gone in. Or Brandt had let someone in. Or—

Herr Voss was saying something to him now, something smooth and courteous about cooperation and the Company's gratitude, and Davor answered in the flat courier's monotone that gave away nothing, while inside him two things screamed at once. One was grief: *the letters are gone, Ilya, I failed, they have the pages, it was all for nothing.*

The other was quieter, and it sounded like Ulyana's voice on the night she'd decoded her father's last letter, saying: *He wouldn't hide the real ones where anyone sensible would look. Papa taught me that. Sensible people search sensibly.*

The satchel they had taken was the decoy. It had to be. Ulyana had spent three nights sewing it, filling it with blank pages aged with tea and sealed with candle-dropped wax, grinning at him in the dark like her father risen from the river. *Let them chase paper,* she'd said. *We know where the true ones sleep.*

So why did his chest feel so hollow?

Because the true letters were in the boathouse. With Brandt. And beyond the boathouse lay the river trail, and beyond the river trail the frozen docks, and beyond the docks the icebreaker *Meridian*, taking her last berths before the channel closed for winter.

And somewhere between here and there stood a compromised man with a ring of keys and ten years of quiet falsified quotas on his conscience, deciding — right now, this minute, with the lamplight of the audit house on his back — which side of history he was willing to be found on.

* * *

They put Davor in the woodshed behind the audit house, because the cells were being prepared for the others. Two guards, one lamp, a bolt on the outside. Through the gap under the door he watched the shadows of boots cross the yard, back and forth, back and forth, busy as ants before rain.

An hour passed. Or two. Cold is a thief of time.

Then the boots stopped, all together, as if someone had given a signal — and Davor heard the audit house door open, and Herr Voss's voice, raised for once, sharp with something that was not anger but was adjacent to it: "What do you mean, *paper*? These are blank. These pages are *blank*, you idiot—"

Davor closed his eyes and laughed, once, silently, into the dark — laughed until it turned into something else, grief and pride tangled past separating. *Run, girl. Run like the river.*

The woodshed door rattled. The guards' voices dropped low, confused, and then one of them swore, and then — impossibly, wonderfully — the bolt scraped back and the door swung open and there stood Overseer Brandt, holding a lantern in one hand and a coil of rope in the other, his long face grey as ash and absolutely resolved.

"There's a skiff under the tarp at the boathouse slip," Brandt said, without preamble, without apology. "The ice won't take a man's weight yet, but it will take a boat's, barely, if you keep to the current seam. You have perhaps twenty minutes before Voss realizes the girl is gone and sends riders to the dock."

Davor stared at him. "You're letting me go."

"I'm unlocking a door," Brandt said. "What happens afterward is between you and the river." His hands, Davor noticed, were shaking, and his voice was steady anyway, the way a man's voice is steady when steadiness is the last thing he has left to give. "I falsified quotas for nine years to keep your divers breathing. Nine years of arithmetic lies. I always told myself the ledger balanced." He looked back toward the lamplit audit house, where chaos was beginning to organize itself into pursuit. "Tonight it doesn't. Tonight I pay the difference."

"Brandt—" Davor got to his feet, wrists still bound, absurdly wanting to shake the man's hand and equally wanting to shove him into the skiff and make him row.

"Don't." Brandt stepped back and, with great deliberation, drew his coat sleeve across his own mouth and cheek — smearing it red with the blood from a split on his knuckle Davor hadn't noticed until now. When he lowered his arm, he looked like a man who had fought and lost, and the fiction was complete. "They'll say I was overpowered. Let them. It's a better story than mine." He turned to go, then paused, and for a moment the overseer's mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired man who had known Ulyana Sandoval since she was small enough to ride her father's shoulders. "Tell her — tell her the boathouse was always supposed to be unlocked. All these years. Some of us were only waiting for permission."

Then he was gone, walking unhurried toward the shouts rising from the audit house, a lantern swinging in his fist, straight into the consequences he had chosen as deliberately as a man chooses a road.

Davor ran.

The boathouse, the tarp, the skiff, the black seam of open water threading the young ice like a vein. He rowed with bound wrists until the rope soaked soft and stretched enough to slip, and he rowed until the compound lights shrank to a smear of gold behind him, and then he shipped the oars and let the current carry him toward the docks and the last berth of the last ship, and the thought that drove the cold out of his bones was simple and terrible:

Ulyana was going to reach that dock with her mother dying and one berth to fill, and everything — everything — would depend on what she was willing to lose.
