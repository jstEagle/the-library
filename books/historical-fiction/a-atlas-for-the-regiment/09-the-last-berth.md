## 9. The Last Berth

The river died in the night.

Ulyana heard it go — a sound like the world's spine cracking, a long groan that rolled up from the delta and shivered through the pilings of the Company dock — and by dawn the water that had carried every pearl, every debt, every dream of escape for nineteen years had gone still and white as a dead man's eye. Ice stretched bank to bank, thick enough to walk on, too thick for any hull to break without help.

The *Verrin Star* was supposed to be gone by now. Supposed to have beaten the freeze south with the season's pearls and ledgers and profits, her holds full of other people's labor made portable. Instead she sat at the dock with steam bleeding from her joints like a wounded animal, because her captain had been paid — bribed, threatened, Ulyana didn't know which and hadn't dared ask — to wait one more day for a single passenger.

One berth. The last one north until spring thaw, four months away, and by then the magistrate's circuit would have come and gone through Verrin Landing like weather, taking testimony and moving on, and whatever wasn't in his hands by then would be as buried as her father was supposed to be.

Ulyana stood at the foot of the gangplank with her mother in her arms.

Not in her arms, quite — Sera Sandoval weighed almost nothing now, had been going light all autumn the way a candle goes light near its end, guttering down toward the wick — but Ulyana held her anyway, one arm banded around the sharp ridge of her back, the other keeping the satchel strap from sliding off her mother's shoulder. The letters rode there. Ten years of a drowned man's handwriting, sealed in wax, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than anything Ulyana had ever carried and weighing nothing at all.

"You're shaking," her mother said. Her voice came out in small white puffs. "Ulyana. You're shaking, little fish."

"It's cold."

"It's not the cold." Her mother's hand found Ulyana's face, fingers like paper, and turned it so they were looking at each other. Her eyes were still clear. Whatever the sickness was taking, it had left her mother those. "You've decided something. I've watched you decide things your whole life. You get very quiet, and you hold your breath, and then the world finds out after."

The dock boards creaked behind them. Ulyana didn't turn. She knew who it was the way you know footsteps in your own house — Tomas, moving slowly, his ribs still bound where the enforcers' boots had found him, one arm steadying old Maren, whose hands were shaking worse than Ulyana's own. They had walked her out from the barracks under guard at first light to see the ship off, and the guards had been given orders about their eyes: look anywhere but the gangplank. Brandt's doing, or Brandt's remaining reach. He sat now in the Company house answering the auditor's questions, and everyone on the dock understood that what he had unlocked last night in the boathouse he would pay for through the winter and possibly beyond it.

"The ice hole," Maren said. "They're holding him at the ice hole."

Davor. They had taken him in the raid, taken the satchel's twin — the decoy of blank pages Davor had let them capture along with himself, so that the real letters could travel — and now he sat chained in the shed by the diving hole, in the cold, waiting to be questioned properly when the auditor finished with Brandt. Waiting, Ulyana thought, to be made an example of. The Company did not forgive couriers. The Company did not forgive anyone who made its arithmetic public.

"I know," Ulyana said.

"The magistrate's party reaches the landing tomorrow," Tomas said. "Maren heard the guards."

"Then everything happens tomorrow." Ulyana looked up the gangplank, at the rail where the captain stood pretending to study the sky. "So it has to happen now."

Her mother laughed. It was a terrible sound, thin as the ice, and it broke something open in Ulyana's chest.

"Listen to her," Sera said to Maren, to Tomas, to the frozen river. "Everything happens tomorrow, so it happens now. Your father talks exactly like that, did you know? When he planned the drowning. *Everything happens tonight, so it happens now.* I thought he was being clever. He was only ever being afraid and refusing to show it." She turned her clear eyes back to her daughter. "Tell me what you've decided."

And there it was. Nineteen years of being a good diver, a good daughter, a body the Company counted like coin, and her mother had read her in ten seconds flat.

"The berth is yours," Ulyana said.

"No."

"Mama—"

"No." But her mother's hand stayed gentle on her cheek, which confused the word entirely. "Say the rest of it. Say all of it, if you're going to spend my last strength on a speech."

Ulyana's throat closed. She pried it open the way she'd once learned to pry oysters, hating the effort, needing what was inside.

"You're dying," she said. "I've known since the equinox and I've been trading medicine against it and it isn't working and I'm done pretending, the same way I was done pretending the Company post was the whole world. You can't make the crossing in spring. You might not make the winter even here. And the letters—" Her voice cracked; she pushed through. "The letters need someone the auditor can't touch. Someone already out of his reach. If I carry them and they take me at some checkpoint upriver, everything dies with me — Papa's work, Maren's debts, Tomas's ribs, all of it. But if you carry them, and I stay—" 

She made herself say it.

"—and I stay and stand in front of the magistrate and testify with my own name and my own scars and my own contract with the forged seal, then the letters aren't the only proof. I'm proof. A living witness standing in the Company's own yard. He can't bury me the way he'd bury a satchel."

"He could try," Tomas said quietly.

"He could try," Ulyana agreed. Her heart was slamming against her ribs like the ice had slammed the banks. "But there are divers here who owe the Company nothing but lies, and yesterday they chose each other. That's worth something. That might be worth enough."

* * *

Sera Sandoval did not weep, and neither did her daughter, though both of them came within a breath of it, and the space between that breath and the weeping was where they lived for the next quarter hour while the deal was struck.

It went like this: her mother would take the letters, because her mother was dying and dying people cannot be leveraged — the auditor could threaten her with nothing that wasn't already coming. Maren would give her the fever draught for the voyage, hoarded against her own bad nights. Tomas would carry Sera up the gangplank in his arms, hurt ribs and all, because he said — with the first full sentence Ulyana had ever heard him volunteer — that he had spent eleven years watching the Company decide where people went, and he wanted one morning of deciding it himself.

Ulyana would go back up the bank. She would find the magistrate's party before the Company found her. She would talk.

"Little fish," her mother said, when they stood at the top of the gangplank and the captain was calling time and the wind came off the ice carrying the smell of the deep water below it, "your father taught you your letters so you could write your way out of this place. I always thought he meant literally. I think maybe he meant something better."

"Papa taught me my letters," Ulyana said, "and I'm going to use them on the Company until there's nothing left of it to forge a ledger with."

"That's my girl." Her mother's hands framed her face one last time. They were cold and light as moths. "Now listen. This is the hard part, and I'll only say it once, so mind me. You will want to feel that you chose wrong. Tonight, and all winter, and maybe for years. You'll lie awake doing the arithmetic — *she was aboard, warm, going north, and instead I gave her away* — and you'll call it love, giving me the berth, and it is love, but it's also grief wearing love's coat, and grief lies. So hear this." She pressed her forehead to Ulyana's. "You did not give me away. You set me loose. Those are different words, and the difference is the whole atlas."

Then she kissed her daughter's brow, took the satchel strap in both hands like a woman accepting a sword, and walked down the deck toward the stern cabin without looking back — because, Ulyana understood with a pain like a hook set behind her heart, looking back was the one luxury her mother could no longer afford.

The lines came off. The *Star*'s engine turned over, coughing steam into air so cold it fell as glitter. Ulyana stood at the end of the dock and watched the hull shoulder through young ice, white plates tilting and grinding aside, the wake opening behind it like a wound that froze shut even as it opened. She watched until the ship was a smudge, until the smudge was nothing, until there was only the river again — dead, silent, hers.

She noticed she was breathing.

* * *

They found Davor at the ice hole, as Maren had said.

The shed stood at the edge of the diving grounds, a lean-to of tarps and timber around the black square of kept-open water where the winter crews worked the shallows. Two enforcers lounged outside with their collars up. Inside, visible through the gap, Davor sat on an upturned crate with his wrists ironed to a ring bolt, and he was — Ulyana saw it as she came around the tarp edge, Tomas's shadow beside her — he was smiling. Faintly. At nothing. At the black water, maybe. A man who knows the package got out smiles like that.

The enforcers straightened. One put a hand on his truncheon.

"You can't be here," the taller one said. "Auditor's orders. Restricted—"

"Read her the rest of it," said a voice from behind them, and there was Maren, ancient and small and furious, planted in the middle of the path with the twins flanking her and, behind them, a dozen divers drifting out of the barracks fog with their buckets and their knives and their nineteen years of learning exactly how much weight a man can carry and for how long. "Go on. Read us all the order. Out loud. I'll wait — I'm told I have all winter."

The enforcers looked at each other. Whatever the auditor had ordered, it had not accounted for arithmetic like this.

Ulyana knelt in front of Davor. Up close he was wrecked — split lip, eye swollen shut, frost in his beard — and the smile was still there underneath it all, stubborn as a pearl in a closing shell.

"She's aboard," she told him.

"I know. I heard the engines." His voice was gravel. "You gave up the berth."

"I gave her the berth. There's a difference."

"There is," Davor said, and something moved through his ruined face that was almost wonder. "Your father used to say the same words. *There's a difference.* He'd have liked hearing that." His eyes went past her, to the crowd of divers, to Maren's raised chin, to Tomas standing sentry with his bound ribs and his free arms crossed. "You built all this in a night."

"I didn't build anything. I just stopped believing the post was the whole world." She reached through the ring bolt and gripped his ironed wrists, cold iron, colder hands. "The magistrate arrives tomorrow. I'm going to find him tonight, before the Company does, and I'm going to tell him everything — the ledgers, the slaver sales, my contract, your name, Brandt's boathouse, all of it, out loud, in front of witnesses. And when he asks who else can swear to it, I'm going to point at this shed."

Davor laughed, low and careful, like a man laughing near ice that might crack. "You're going to break me out."

"I'm going to testify you were arrested protecting evidence that proves the Company sold human beings. What the magistrate does with that information is his business." She squeezed his wrists. "But Maren brought soup, and the twins brought a file, and Tomas brought his opinions about locks, so I'd say his options are wide open."

Behind her, the divers closed in around the shed like water around a stone, patient, inevitable, done with waiting for history to happen to them. Somewhere south, under a smudge of smoke on a white horizon, her mother was carrying their father's handwriting toward home. Somewhere in the Company house, a door was opening, and a man with a clean face and clean boots was stepping out into the cold, asking where the Sandoval girl had gone.

Ulyana stood up. She turned toward the lights of the landing, where the magistrate's sledges would come in with the morning, and began to walk.

Everything happened tomorrow. So it happened now.
