## 10. An Atlas Drawn by Hand

The river had never looked like this before.

Ulyana stood on the dock in the pale gold of a late spring morning — *her* dock now, though the word still fit strangely, like a boot borrowed from someone with bigger feet — and watched the water run brown and bright and free of ice. For nineteen years she had known the river as a thing that took. It took her father's name from the ledger of the living. It took her mother's lungs, one winter at a time. It took nineteen years of her own breath, held underwater while quotas ticked somewhere above her like a clock she was never allowed to read.

Now it only ran. She was learning to let it.

Below her, on the shingle where the Company's barges used to moor, the freed divers were arguing about fish. It was, she reflected, the most beautiful sound in the world.

"The pike come up the eastern channel first," Tomas was insisting. He walked with a cane now, his healed leg set crooked by an infirmary surgeon who owed the Company nothing and so worked slowly, but he walked, and he argued louder than ever. "My grandfather netted them every spring off the willow bend."

"Your grandfather," Maren said, "netted them wherever the Company told him to net them, which is why your family ate pike and dreamed of eel." She was seventy if she was a day, and the raid had taken four months of her life in a cell before the magistrate's writ sprang her, and it seemed to have made no impression on her whatsoever except a new appreciation for being disagreeable in daylight. "Eel is what we want. Eel is money."

"Eel is mud with opinions."

Ulyana laughed, and the twins laughed, and somewhere down the bank quiet Petra laughed without looking up from the nets she was mending, and for a moment the whole shore sounded like a household instead of a barracks, which was what it was now, though the paperwork saying so had taken the magistrate's clerks all winter to write.

* * *

The trial had ended in the second week of thaw, and Ulyana could still not think of it without her hands going cold.

She had testified for three days. Three days in a courtroom in the provincial capital, a room full of walnut paneling and men in gray coats, and her voice had come out smaller than she wanted and steadier than she expected, and she had learned something about testimony that no one had told her: that the truth, spoken plainly, is heavy enough to shift things. Not because it is eloquent. Because it refuses to be moved.

The letters had done the rest. Her father's slanted hand, page after page of copied ledgers, each falsified debt annotated in the margins — *this girl is eleven, this debt is invented, this sale is murder* — laid out on the magistrate's table like a body on a slab. The auditor's advocates had tried to argue forgery. Then Davor, released into custody two days after the icebreaker sailed, had produced the courier's marks, the route seals, the authentication codes of a trade the Company itself had built, and the argument had died quietly in its chair.

The charter was dissolved on a Tuesday. Ulyana remembered it was a Tuesday because she had spent nineteen years thinking Tuesdays meant quota inspection, and she had gone out onto the steps of the courthouse and breathed air that belonged to no one, and cried until a stranger — a fruit seller, gray-haired, unimportant — had pressed an apple into her hand and said, "There now. Eat something."

That was history, she understood then. Not proclamations. Apples pressed into hands. Whoever stayed long enough to do the pressing got to make it.

Brandt had not stayed. They had found him in the boathouse the morning after the raid, sitting very straight on an upturned crate, the keys still in his fist, waiting for the enforcers with the composure of a man who had spent twenty years rehearsing one honest act and finally gotten to perform it. The tribunal gave him five years. Ulyana had written to him once, care of the work camp at Kessel Ford, and received back a single line in a clerk's cramped hand: *Your father kept his books honestly. It was the least I could do to keep mine.* She had read it perhaps forty times.

* * *

Her mother's letters came north-to-south with the spring barges, one a month, each one smelling faintly of pine resin and the particular soap of the safehouse where she had spent the winter.

Ulyana read the newest one now, sitting against the pilings with her boots in the shallows, the paper warm from her pocket.

*My dove,* it began, in her mother's careful script, the letters rounder than they used to be, as if the mountain air had softened even her penmanship. *He came on Thursday. I heard the sledge before I saw him and my heart went stupid, and then there he was in the doorway, thinner, grayer, with that ridiculous beard, and I hit him with the fire tongs and then we did not speak for an hour because neither of us could.*

*He is real, Ulyana. He sits across the table from me and eats too fast and argues with the doctor about his lungs, exactly as he used to, and some nights I am so angry at him I cannot look at him, and some nights I hold his hand and think: ten years. Ten years he carried this alone so that other people's daughters would not carry what ours carried. I have decided to be furious and grateful in equal measure and let God sort the arithmetic.*

*We are coming south when the roads dry. He wants to see the river. He says he owes it an apology.*

*You wrote that you are drawing them a map. Your father laughed until he coughed when I read him that part. He says you get it from him, and I said no, she gets it from me, I was drawing maps in the margins of my prayer book when you were still afraid of water. Settle it when you come. Or don't settle it. Some arguments are better kept.*

*All my love, always, more than the ledger could hold — your mother.*

Ulyana folded the letter along its worn creases and sat a while with her face in the sun.

*Coming home.* The words still didn't behave. For ten years home had been a wound with a shape, and now the wound was filling in, and it itched, and it ached, and it was healing, and all of those felt the same.

* * *

The map took her most of April.

She drew it on the back of six salvaged Company charts — the old overseer's stock, printed with depth soundings and moorage fees, every square inch of it now worthless paper, which was the best kind to draw on. She stretched them across the floor of the former mess hall, weighted the corners with stones, and began.

She started with the river, of course. But not the river as the Company charted it — a brown line, a freight corridor, a series of toll points. She drew the river as it lived.

The willow bend where Tomas swore the pike ran, marked with a little fish and a note: *TOMAS IS PROBABLY WRONG, CHECK ANYWAY.* The gravel shoal below the old ice hole, where the current ran gentlest for swimmers and where she herself had first gone under at age five, her father's hands under her ribs. A cluster of reeds drawn carefully, one stalk at a time, where the eels sheltered — *MAREN'S CLAIM, DISPUTE AT YOUR PERIL.* The spring where the water came up cold and iron-tasting, that the Company had never put on any chart because the Company had never needed to drink there; its people drank from pipes it owned.

She drew the safehouse road north, from Davor's descriptions, each courier cache marked with his small precise symbols — a crossed hatchet for a hollow tree, a crescent for a stone cairn. She drew Brandt's work camp at Kessel Ford, and beside it, in ink she hesitated over and then used anyway: *A MAN WHO CHOSE LATE BUT WELL. VISIT HIM.*

She drew the infirmary, and the barracks, and the boathouse with its unlocked door. She drew the dock. At the dock she stopped, pen lifted, and then wrote the only name the map truly needed:

*HERE THE WATER ENDS AND YOU BEGIN.*

When she unrolled the finished sheets for the others, on the first genuinely warm evening of the year, with a fire going on the shingle and the smell of actual purchased — *purchased*, with wages — fish smoking over it, nobody spoke for a long moment.

Then Petra, who had not spoken a full sentence in Ulyana's hearing since the raid, reached out and touched the drawn reeds where the eels slept, very lightly, the way you touch something to confirm it is real.

"It's us," she said. "It's — everything the charts never bothered with. It's us."

"That's because we're the ones living here," Ulyana said. "Charts get drawn by people passing through. Maps get drawn by people staying."

Old Maren snorted, which from Maren was applause. Davor, who was leaving in the morning — couriers always left in the morning; it was, he said, the first law of the trade — looked at the map for a long time and then at her, and something passed between them that needed no words, though he said one anyway, quietly, so only she heard it.

"Your father taught me to read routes," he said. "You taught me what they're for."

* * *

Late that night, the fire burned down to embers and the divers drifted off to their bunks in the barracks that was no longer a barracks, and Ulyana stayed up with the map, adding the last things by candlelight.

She drew her mother's barge route south, arriving with the drying roads. She drew, at the very bottom edge, a small house she had not yet chosen, on a lane she had not yet walked, and labeled it *HOME (UNDER CONSTRUCTION)*, because optimism, she had learned, was mostly a discipline.

And at the top corner, where a cartographer would put a compass rose, she drew instead a small figure — a diver, mid-descent, arms open, rising or sinking impossible to say — and beneath it she wrote the sentence she had been carrying all winter, the one the magistrate's clerks had no form for filing, the one she suspected she would spend the rest of her life trying to earn:

*History is not what happens to you. It is what you refuse to let happen to anyone else.*

Outside, the river ran on in the dark, taking nothing, owing nothing, and down the coast, two hundred miles of road beginning to dry, a man with a ridiculous beard was packing a sledge for home — while at the edge of her map, in the empty white space beyond the last charted bend, Ulyana picked up her pen and began, line by patient line, to draw the country no one had ever bothered to chart at all.
