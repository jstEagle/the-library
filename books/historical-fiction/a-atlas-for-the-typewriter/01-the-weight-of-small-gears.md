## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The train gave up on the valley an hour past dawn, and after that there was only the mail coach, and after the coach, Sable Whitlock's own two feet and the mountain.

He had chosen the name Finch for the journey — *Mr. Finch, clock repair, contracted by wire* — because a finch was a small gray thing nobody looked at twice, and because he had learned long ago that a man's name was the first door through which trouble entered. He had nailed that door shut in Boston six years ago and had not opened it since, not even to look through the keyhole at what waited on the other side.

The road climbed through pines that held last night's snow in their arms like something borrowed. His breath came out in white flags of surrender. The case on his back — his tools, his whole trade, forty pounds of brass and steel and jeweler's screwdrivers wrapped in flannel — pressed against his spine with the familiar weight of purpose. That was the mercy of work: it asked nothing of you but your hands.

*You could still turn back,* said some voice in him, the one that sounded like his mother's piano teacher, thin and disapproving. *The bridge closes at first snow. You could be on the coast by Thursday.*

But the letter in his breast pocket had been signed by Director Hale himself, and the fee offered was triple the going rate for a master clock gone erratic, and men who were hiding did not ask why a remote observatory paid triple. Men who were hiding took the job and kept their eyes down and their hands busy.

He passed the frozen river around noon. It lay under its ice like a secret under a floorboard, and he thought — as he thought every winter, whether he wished to or not — of Iris standing on the millpond ice at nine years old, daring him to cross, her red scarf the only color in a white world. *Come on, Sab. The ice holds if you don't stop in the middle.* He had crossed. She had crossed. And years later, when the family name began to rot from the inside like a tooth, she had stood in the doorway of their father's study and dared him again: *Stay and fight for it.* He had not stopped in the middle then either. He had run the whole way across, and he had never once looked back to see whether the ice held her.

A crow lifted off a dead pine and scolded him all the way up the switchback.

* * *

The observatory appeared at dusk the way a ship appears in fog — first the dome, pale and enormous, then the lighted windows strung along the ridge like lanterns on a mast. Below it, the river gorge dropped away into blue shadow, and Sable saw the cable car house, its great wheel turning slowly, hauling supplies up from the valley station. In summer there was a road. In winter there was this: a steel thread over the void, and the mountain's permission to cross it.

The man who met him at the door was old and bent as a question mark, with forearms like knotted rope and eyes the color of tarnished brass.

"You'd be the repairman." Not a question. "Aldous Vane. I keep the instruments." He looked at Sable's hands before he looked at his face, which Sable found he liked. "Show me."

Sable set down the case, opened it, let the old man see the tools laid out in their exact order — the lathe attachments, the oil in its glass vial, the loupe on its chain. Aldous studied the arrangement for a long moment.

"Left-handed," he said. "Good. Most clocks are built by right-handers and repaired by them too. They fight the mechanism." He stepped back. "Director will want to see you at supper. Don't be late. He minds."

"I'm here for the clock," Sable said. "Not for supper."

"Everyone here is here for supper," Aldous said, unsmiling. "It's how we know we're alive."

* * *

The dining hall was low-ceilinged and warm, smelling of bread and lamp oil and the particular mineral cold that mountains breathe into everything. There were seven of them at the long table, and Sable counted them the way he counted gear teeth — automatically, looking for the flaw.

Director Hale sat at the head, silver-haired, precise, a man whose stillness seemed expensive, maintained at cost. To his right, a young woman with ink-stained fingers and her father's exact gray eyes was reading a journal and eating without appearing to notice she did either. Wren, someone said, when introductions came around. The director's daughter. Keeper of the records, Aldous murmured, keeper of everything.

"Mr. Finch." Hale's handshake was dry and brief. "You come recommended by a man I trust."

"I come recommended by my work," Sable said. "I hope that's enough."

"It is the only currency I accept." Something moved behind the director's eyes — not warmth exactly, but attention, the way a lens focuses. "The master clock has been losing four seconds a day. For an observatory, Mr. Finch, four seconds is not a delay. It is a lie."

"Then I'll teach it to tell the truth," Sable said, and heard, a half-second too late, how the words might land on a man who dealt in records and surveys and the difference between what happened and what was written down. But Hale only nodded, as if the answer had been expected, as if it had perhaps been scripted.

Wren looked up from her journal then, just once, and her gaze crossed his like a level finding true. He looked away first. He told himself it was nothing. He told himself a great many things that week.

* * *

The clock room sat at the heart of the observatory like a heart indeed, and when Sable opened its case that first night, alone, lamplight gold on the brass, he felt the old calm settle over him — the one place where no name could follow him, where he was only a pair of hands and a mind and the mechanism's own indifferent honesty.

The master clock was a beauty: a Riefler-pattern regulator, pendulum swinging in its vacuum jar, escapement ticking with a faint irregularity he could feel in his teeth before he could hear it. Four seconds lost a day. Somewhere in it, a wear, a flaw, a small dishonesty.

He worked until midnight, and the work was prayer, and he was almost happy.

Then, reaching deep into the case to seat a new pallet stone, his fingers found paper where paper should not be. An envelope, flat and yellowed, wedged between the movement plate and the case wall — placed there deliberately, by hands that knew the clock would one day be opened, and knew who would open it.

His name was on it. Not *Finch*. Not *Whitlock*, either — but the initial *S.*, in handwriting he had known all his life, because it had signed his birthday cards and his father's checks and, once, a survey map that had made the family fortune.

Below the *S.*, a single line:

*You were always the one who could hear what machines confess.*

Sable sat very still in the ticking dark, the envelope in his careful left hand, and outside the window the snow began to fall on the bridge road — falling, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, already thick and fast enough to close it behind him.
