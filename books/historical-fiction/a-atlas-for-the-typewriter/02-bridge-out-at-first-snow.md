## 2. Bridge Out at First Snow

By morning the snow had buried the road to the bridge, and by noon the bridge itself was gone — not gone in any dramatic sense, no collapse, no groan of iron, but simply closed, a plank nailed across the tollgate and a boy in an oilskin coat standing beneath it like a full stop at the end of a sentence.

Sable watched from the observatory's eastern window, the envelope still folded in his breast pocket where it had spent the night against his heart like a coal that would not cool. The boy below unrolled the notice, weighted its corners with stones, and trudged back down the mountain. *BRIDGE CLOSED FOR SEASON. REOPENS APRIL, WEATHER PERMITTING.*

April.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Five months. A hundred and fifty days of eating at a communal table, sleeping thirty feet from strangers, answering to a name he had invented on a train platform two years ago — Finch, after the birds that used to nest in the clock shop's eaves, the only creatures his father had never tried to own. A hundred and fifty days in which invisibility was not a discipline but a rumor, and rumors, he knew, traveled faster on a mountain than anywhere else, because there was so little else to do.

"You'll want heavier boots."

Wren Hale stood in the doorway with a tray of mugs, steam rising off them in the cold of the corridor. She had her father's eyes — gray, quick, the color of the sky just before it decides something — but none of his patience. She looked at people the way Sable looked at escapements: as mechanisms that would eventually show their working.

"I have boots," he said.

"You have city boots." She set the tray on the sill and pushed one mug toward him without asking whether he wanted it. It was tea with something dark in it — pine or juniper, something that smelled like the forest had been boiled for his benefit. "The drifts past the tree line come to the hip by January. Marta lost a cook to them in '09. Well — not lost. He walked into a drift and came out the other side three days later very embarrassed and minus two toes." She said it flatly, a fact among facts, and sipped her own tea. "You're the clock man. You should appreciate that. Toes are how you count."

"I don't count on my toes."

"No," she agreed, looking at him with that unnerving directness. "You count on your fingers. I watched you through the transom last night. All those little movements. Like you were playing something invisible."

He had thought the workshop empty above him. He filed the error away with the others — the ones he'd made since arriving, each small, each unlike him. Competence was supposed to be camouflage. Instead he was becoming conspicuous precisely because he was good, because a man who is good at one thing in a place with five people becomes *the* thing, becomes *the clock man*, becomes a fixture rather than a passerby. Fixtures got asked questions. Passersby got forgotten.

"The bridge," he said. "It closes early this year?"

"Early by nine days. Father says the river company wants the tollhouse roofed before real winter. He says a great many things about the river company that I won't repeat before breakfast." She turned to the window beside him. Below, the white road ran down and simply stopped, erased, as though the world had been drawn by someone who lost interest halfway through. "You came up intending to leave again."

It wasn't a question, which was what made it dangerous. Questions could be dodged; observations had to be contradicted, and contradiction cost more than silence.

"The work is what it is," Sable said. "A month for the master clock. Perhaps six weeks."

"And then?"

"And then somewhere warmer."

She studied him a moment longer — long enough that he felt catalogued, shelved, cross-referenced — and then shrugged and took her tea elsewhere, leaving his untouched on the sill, steaming faintly, patient as everything else on this mountain seemed to be.

* * *

The station had a rhythm, and rhythm was the enemy.

Sable knew rhythms intimately. He had spent his life inside them — pendulums, balance wheels, the metronomic tick of trains he rode under false names. But those rhythms asked nothing of him except that he keep time. This one demanded participation. There was the bell for meals rung twice, once as warning and once as fact. There was Marta's kitchen, warm and yellow-lit and smelling of onions and yeast, where everyone gathered at seven and again at one and again at seven, and where absence was noticed within minutes and remarked upon within the hour.

"You eat standing at the counter like a man expecting to be arrested," Marta told him on the third evening. She was broad and gray-browed and moved through her kitchen with the authority of weather. "Sit. Sit there. Between Aldous and the wall, so you can't bolt."

"I wasn't—"

"Everyone bolts the first week. Then they stop." She put a bowl in front of him — barley stew, dense enough to stand a spoon in — and fixed him with a look that permitted no negotiation. "You bolt slower than most, though. I've been watching."

Old Aldous was already seated at his left, a small man folded into himself like a letter, his hands ruined and beautiful, knuckles swollen into shapes no glove had fit for forty years. He ate slowly, with ceremony, as though each spoonful were being inspected before admission.

"Marta watches everyone," Aldous said mildly. "It is her second profession. Her first is making sure we do not die of scurvy." He turned his head toward Sable, and his eyes, milky at the rim but sharp at the center, found him with unsettling precision. "You have been three days among us, Herr Finch, and you have wound the regulator twice, oiled the transit drive, and re-hung the door to the instrument room, which has hung crooked since 1904. Either you are the most industrious repairman ever sent up this mountain, or you are hiding inside the work."

Sable's spoon paused above the bowl.

"Hiding inside the work is not dishonorable," Aldous went on, returning to his stew. "I hid inside clocks for eleven years, after the war. They ask nothing about who you were. They only ask if your hands are steady." He lifted one of those hands, trembled it deliberately, and smiled. "Mine stopped being steady in 1871. Yours are steady. That is all I know about you, and it is more than enough for now."

*Now.* The word sat in Sable's chest like a splinter. For now was exactly how long he had intended anyone to know anything.

* * *

But the mountain did not honor intentions.

On the fifth day the barometer fell and Wren recruited him — recruited, not asked; she framed requests as conclusions — to carry storm shutters along the catwalk to the dome offices while the wind came off the glacier in long white shouts. They worked in a silence broken only by the building's complaints, timber and iron ticking around them like an enormous loose mechanism, and somewhere in the middle of the third shutter, without preamble, she said:

"Father has your file."

He kept his grip on the shutter hinge. The wind pulled at it; he let it pull. "My file?"

"Every person who comes up here gets one. References, letters, the commission itself. He keeps them in the east cabinet." She hammered a pin home with short efficient strikes. "Yours is thin."

"Repairmen don't generate much paper."

"Repairmen generate references. Yours has none. Just the commission letter and a bank draft from a Zurich house, and a note in Father's hand saying *"quiet, competent, asks no questions"* — which, between us, is the sort of description Father usually writes about instruments he intends to trust and men he intends to use." She lowered the hammer and looked at him across the shutter, wind whipping a strand of dark hair across her face, and she did not brush it away, as though she wanted nothing obstructed on either side. "I'm not prying. I'm telling you that I noticed, and that noticing is my worst habit, and that if you wanted to give me a better story than the file does, now would be the time."

The wind screamed down the catwalk. Below them the valley had vanished entirely; the world had narrowed to planks and iron and the woman in front of him, waiting, patient in the way of someone who has learned that most secrets volunteer themselves eventually.

"I'm good with clocks," Sable said.

"Yes," she said. "That's the tragedy of it. You're good with everything except the question." And she took the other end of the shutter, and together they carried it in against the gale, and neither spoke again until the dome was sealed and the wind was only a sound, enormous and indifferent, outside the walls.

* * *

That night he sat in the workshop beneath the master clock, listening to it run — not repaired yet, only stabilized, its tick still carrying a hesitation on every fourth beat like a man limping toward a destination — and he took out the envelope with the *S.* on it and read the line beneath again.

*You were always the one who could hear what machines confess.*

His father's handwriting. His father's knowledge that he was here — which meant the commission had not found him by chance, which meant the letter of introduction, the Zurich draft, the whole careful architecture of his arrival had perhaps been built by someone else's hands and merely walked through by his own. He had believed he was coming to the observatory. It occurred to him now, in the ticking dark, that he might have been summoned.

And summoned men, he knew — he had grown up in a house of them, clerks and surveyors and quiet men with ledgers — summoned men did not get to leave when the season ended. Summoned men stayed until they were dismissed.

Above him the fourth beat stumbled, caught, resumed.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the closed road, filling in his footprints from arrival day, erasing the record of how he had come, so that soon there would be no evidence he had ever intended to go back.

In the east cabinet downstairs, behind a lock Wren could pick and he had not yet dared try, a thin folder held a name that was not quite his, and a director's note, and whatever else Director Hale had chosen to know about him.

Sable wound the clock, set the beat as true as he could make it, and understood that the mountain had begun counting his days — not down toward April, as he'd hoped, but toward something else entirely.
