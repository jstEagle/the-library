## 6. The Long Night Watch

The master clock kept its own hours, and on the nights of regulation we kept them with it.

My father had drawn up the schedule years before I was old enough to read it: every seventh night, someone must sit with the regulator through the small hours, coaxing the pendulum true against the cold, because mountain air contracts the rods and steals seconds the way a thief steals coins—one at a time, so gently you never notice the purse growing light. The duty had always been mine or my father's. But my father had been sleeping badly since the repairman arrived, pacing his study with his lamp burning past midnight, and when I offered to take his watch he surrendered it too quickly, with a look I chose not to examine.

So it was that at eleven o'clock on a Thursday in deepest February, I climbed the iron stair to the clock room carrying a thermos of coffee, a blanket, and a secret folded into my breast pocket like a letter I could not decide to send.

Sable was already there.

He sat on the low stool before the great case, sleeves rolled despite the cold, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, his hands moving inside the movement with the tenderness other men reserved for sleeping children. He did not hear me come up. The clock room swallowed sound—the gears took every noise and gave back only their own slow thunder, tick and tock and the vast breathing sweep between them. I stood in the doorway a long moment, watching him work by lamplight, and thought: *he looks like a man praying.* Then I thought: *or a man doing penance,* and I did not know why the second thought arrived, only that it settled into me like a splinter.

"You're early," he said, without turning. He must have seen my shadow cross the lamp.

"Regulation waits for no one." I set the thermos on the workbench. "Father's watch. He's not sleeping."

"No," Sable said quietly, and withdrew his hands from the clock, and something in the way he said it—two letters, no more—made me certain he knew why. The splinter went deeper.

* * *

The first hours passed the way hours pass in that room, which is to say they did not pass at all but pooled around us like still water. We worked in an easy alternation neither of us had planned: he read the pendulum's arc while I recorded the error against the sidereal tables, then we traded places, our shoulders brushing in the narrow space behind the case. His hands were beautiful hands—scarred across the knuckles, burn-marked near the thumb, a workman's hands but finer than most workmen's—and I caught myself watching them instead of the chronometer dial more than once.

"Coffee?" I said, around two o'clock.

"Thank you." He wrapped both palms around the cup as though it were something rescued from a fire. Outside, the wind had risen; it moved along the ridge like a living thing, worrying the eaves, and beneath it we could hear the river—not the polite trickle of autumn but a lower, heavier sound, ice groaning against its banks.

"It sounds different lately," I said.

"The river?"

"Mm. Louder. As if it were talking in its sleep."

Sable listened for a moment. "Thaw comes early this year," he said. "Warm winds off the valley. My father—" He stopped. The cup paused halfway to his mouth. "I've read that these rivers can rise fast when the ice lets go all at once."

I noticed the correction, the way I had learned to notice everything about him: the false start, the swerve away from himself. *My father.* Who was your father, Mr. Vane? What does he know about rivers? But I said none of it. Instead I pulled the blanket from my bag and spread half of it over my knees and, after a hesitation that cost me more than I let show, held out the other half.

He looked at it. He looked at me. For three full ticks of the master clock—the loudest three ticks in the world—he did not move.

"We'll freeze otherwise," I said, as though the matter were purely thermal.

He sat down beside me on the bench against the stone wall and drew his half of the blanket over his legs, and the wool bridged us in the middle, and neither of us acknowledged it, and both of us were aware of nothing else.

* * *

It is strange what people tell you at three in the morning. Perhaps it is the hour itself—that hinge where the night grows tired of pretending—or perhaps it is simply that darkness makes liars lazy. Sable began to talk.

Not about himself. Never about himself; I had learned that much in six weeks. But about clocks. About Aldous Fenn, the old man who had built this very regulator forty years ago and now lived in the cottage below the east wing with his bad lungs and his endless chess problems. About how Aldous had taught him, somewhere, sometime, in a story he told slantwise so that I could not fix it to any town or year.

"He found me when I was fourteen," Sable said, his voice pitched under the tick of the great wheel. "Half-starved, half-frozen, full of nonsense about myself. Most men would have turned me out. He fed me, put me to work sweeping shavings, and never once asked a question I couldn't bear to answer." A pause. The lamplight moved on his face. "He taught me that a clock doesn't lie. People lie. Clocks just keep time. Whatever you've done, wherever you've run—the escapement releases the same tooth every swing. There's something—" he searched, "—forgiving in that. Or merciless. I've never decided which."

"And which do you need it to be?" I asked.

He turned his head then, slowly, and looked at me the way men look at things they are afraid to touch. The lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, up the wall, over the great brass face of the clock, so that his shadow seemed to stand inside the dial among the numerals.

"I don't know," he said. "What would you need?"

And there it was—the opening, wide as a door. I felt the papers in my breast pocket as if they had grown warm, as if they were burning through the linen of my blouse into my skin. Three letters. My father's own hand, and another hand I now knew belonged to Emeric Whitlock, surveyor, founder-by-fraud, the name that ran through the observatory's founding documents like a hairline crack through fine porcelain. *Whitlock.* And the repairman who would not give his history, whose hands were too fine, whose silences were too practiced, who had been summoned here—summoned, I was nearly sure, deliberately—by a director who slept badly beside his own complicity.

*I found the letters,* I wanted to say. *I know the name Whitlock. Tell me who you are. Tell me you're not the son. Tell me anything, and I will believe it, because I want to—God help me, I want to.*

Instead I heard myself say: "I'd need it to be forgiving. I think. If I had done something I was ashamed of, I'd want the clock to keep counting forward anyway. To say: there is still time. There is still time to be someone else."

Something crossed his face then that I had no word for—not fear exactly, not relief, but the expression of a man standing at a door he has both longed for and dreaded, hand raised to knock, unable. The wind pressed against the tower. The pendulum swung its patient arc. And Sable Whitlock—if that was even the name, if the crack ran deeper than I guessed—opened his mouth to speak.

"I should tell you—"

"Yes," I said, barely a breath.

"—the third train of the strike wheel is wearing unevenly. I'll need to lap it tomorrow. It won't affect the transmission schedule."

The silence after was the longest of my life.

"Of course," I said. "Tomorrow."

He nodded and looked down at his scarred hands, and I looked up at the dial, and between us the blanket's wool bridge trembled with two heartbeats pretending to be calm. I had almost told him. He had almost told me. Two confessions had stood in the lamplight together and each had blinked, and now the clock struck four—four soft bronze notes falling through the tower like stones into deep water—and the moment closed over them without a ripple.

But love, I was beginning to understand, is not a lightning strike. It is a pendulum. It returns. It returns. However far you push it from center, it swings back toward the thing it falls toward, again and again, until either the mechanism fails or the truth comes out. I sat in the dark beside a man made almost entirely of unsaid words, and I loved him, and I waited.

* * *

We parted at dawn. The sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of skimmed milk, and the river below spoke louder than it had at midnight—a wet, muscular muttering, ice shifting in its sleep. Sable stood at the foot of the iron stair, pulling on his coat, and for a moment he looked as though he might say it after all, whatever *it* was, the whole weight of it.

"Wren." My name in his mouth sounded like something borrowed and treasured.

"Yes?"

"When the thaw comes—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "If the river rises faster than anyone expects, be ready. Don't wait for my father's permission to be sensible. Promise me."

It was such an odd thing to say, so specific, so freighted, that I felt the splinter turn all the way around. He knew something. Not only about clocks.

"I promise," I said. "And Sable—" I let the pause stretch until he met my eyes. "Whatever it is you should have told me tonight. There is still time."

He flinched as if the words had been struck off him like sparks from a grinding wheel. Then he smiled—an apology of a smile, brief and breaking—and climbed down into the grey morning, and I stood alone in the clock room with the pendulum swinging its faithful arc and the sound of the river rising underneath everything, patient as a debt, certain as spring.

By evening, the water gauge at the footbridge had climbed nine inches.

By midnight, Marta was waking the station.
