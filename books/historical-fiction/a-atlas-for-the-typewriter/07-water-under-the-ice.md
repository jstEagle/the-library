## 7. Water Under the Ice

I have cooked through forty mountain winters, and I can tell you this: the thaw is worse than the freeze. Cold is honest. It comes down from the peaks like a debt collector and takes what it takes, and you huddle and endure it. But the thaw — the thaw is a liar. It comes in sweet on a southern wind, whispering of spring, while all that snow hoarded up there all winter turns to water and goes looking for somewhere to be.

In the small hours of the third night of March, I heard the river under the ice, and it did not sound like a river anymore. It sounded like a crowd.

* * *

The kitchen was warm when they came stumbling down for breakfast, and warm kitchens make people honest. I had the big stove going since four, bread proving under cloth, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. Outside the window over my flour bin, the world was weeping. Every icicle on the eaves dripped at once, a hundred small clocks ticking themselves away.

"Listen," said young Fenwick, the night assistant, his ears still red from coming off watch. "You hear that?"

We all listened. Under the drip and the wind, low and continuous, was a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"The river's up," I said. I didn't need to go look. My knees had been telling me since midnight, that old ache finding a new register. "It's up under the ice, where you can't see it. That's the worst kind."

Director Hale came in before seven with his coat still buttoned wrong, and I knew before he spoke. Forty years of feeding people teaches you to read faces the way he reads barometers.

"How bad?" I asked, though my hands were already pulling out the heavy crocks, already counting without being told.

"The telegraph line to the valley is down — water's taken a pylon at the bend." He said it evenly, but his eyes went to the window and stayed there. "The cable car is functioning. For now. But if the lower anchorage floods, it won't be."

"For now," I repeated. In my experience, *for now* is the most dangerous phrase in any language.

"We'll send everyone down who wants to go. Three berths per run." He hesitated, and I saw him do the arithmetic I was doing: nine souls at the station, counting myself, counting the repairman. Three berths. One car. And between us and the valley station, a river learning its old bed again after a century of forgetting.

He left without touching the coffee I poured him. That's how I knew it was serious. Director Hale has never once refused my coffee in eleven winters.

* * *

By noon the whole station had the smell of fear about it, and fear, if you must know, smells like wet wool and unwashed urgency. People speak too fast. They touch their own belongings as if checking them for pulse. The assistants were hauling instrument records to waterproof boxes; Wren Hale directed the work with her sleeves rolled and her jaw set exactly like her father's, though she'd hate to hear it.

And Sable Whitlock — or whatever his name truly was, because I have cooked for enough drifting men to know one when I salt his soup — Sable worked.

That was the thing about him I noticed first, back in January when he arrived with his false smile and his toolbox and his careful way of never sitting with his back to a door: he worked like a man paying rent on his own guilt. Now he moved through the panic the way he moved through the clock's innerworks, unhurried, precise, hands steady while everyone else's shook.

I watched him from my doorway when the first lists were drawn. He stood at the edge of the yard where the ground had gone soft, looking down at the river. You couldn't see the river, not properly. You could see where it had *been* — the brown stain rising through the snow like ink through blotting paper, climbing the willows, swallowing the footbridge post by post. The ice that had held it all winter was breaking up in plates the size of barn roofs, grinding and groaning against each other.

He didn't gawk like the others. He counted. I could see his lips moving. Then he went inside.

* * *

Here is what I know about Sable Whitlock, gathered the way cooks gather everything: quietly, and with flour on my hands.

I know he eats little and thanks me twice. I know he mended the hinge on my pantry door in February without being asked and without mentioning it, so that I only discovered the fresh screws when I went looking for something to complain about. I know Aldous Merrick, my old friend the clockmaker, calls him *boy* in a voice gone soft as tallow, and that the boy lets him — lets no one else come within arm's reach, but lets Aldous lean on him on the stairs as if it were an arrangement older than both of them.

And I know this, which I have never said aloud: three weeks ago, in the deep cold, I found my storeroom fuller than my ledger said it should be. A sack of flour here, a string of dried apples there, candles that I did not remember buying. I thought my memory was going the way of my knees. Then I caught him at it — late, lamplit, easing his own tin of tea onto Marta's shelf as if it belonged there. My shelf. The one I keep for the sick.

"You'll run yourself empty," I told him from the dark, and I will give him this: he didn't startle, didn't lie.

"There are more mouths than mine," he said. "And fewer appetites."

"What does that mean?"

But he just closed the cupboard gently, like shutting a lid on something fragile, and went up to his clock.

Since then I've watched. When the rationing talk started, he was first to hand over his own stores. When Fenwick's boots split in the wet, Whitlock's spare pair appeared by the boy's bunk, oiled and resoled, no note. He gave the way some men steal — carefully, at night, hoping no one would ever tally it against him.

A man hiding something always spends. That part I understood. What I couldn't puzzle was why his spending looked so much like penance.

* * *

The river announced itself properly at dusk.

We were all at supper — such as it was, everything packed and weighed and labeled for the evacuation — when the sound came up through the mountain itself. Not the grinding of ice. A boom, deep as a church bell, felt in the floorboards and the teeth. Then another. The lamps swayed on their hooks and nobody spoke.

Wren got to the window first. "The ice bridge," she said. "At the narrows. It's gone."

Gone. A thing that had stood since before her birth, gone in the time it took to swallow. Below us the river ran open and brown and fast, carrying whole trees like splinters, and where the lower trail had been there was now only water and the white backs of waves. The cable car alone remained, that iron spider swinging on its single thread above the flood.

Three berths. Nine souls. I did the sum again and again, and it came out wrong every time, the way sums do when the currency is people.

Fenwick began to cry, quietly, into his sleeve, being nineteen and trying not to be. Marta's rule: feed them first, then let them feel what they feel. I put the last of the good stew in front of him and pressed his shoulder and pretended not to see.

It was Whitlock who filled the silence, and this is what I want written down somewhere, in case the flood takes the rest: he said, "The instruments still need watching. Someone has to stay regardless." Said it lightly, like a man discussing weather, while every head at that table turned toward him and understood what he was offering and could not bear to take it. Even Hale looked away first.

* * *

I woke past midnight to sounds in my kitchen and found him packing — not his own bag. He was portioning out food into satchels for the descent, weighing each one on my scales with a clockmaker's exactness, and setting aside the largest share for the others. His own bag, when I looked, was nearly empty. A shirt. A book. The rest of the space held tools.

"Tools," I said. "For a man giving up his seat."

"A clock that isn't maintained stops telling the truth." He wound the string tight on a satchel. "Someone should keep the station's time running until they come back. Records, transmissions, the meridian work. Instruments don't survive neglect any better than people do."

"And men? Do they survive neglect?"

His hands stopped. In the lamplight I saw his face do something rare — open, just for a moment, like a drawer pulled out and pushed shut again too fast.

"I mean to find out," he said.

There were boots on the stairs then — quick ones, Wren's — and a voice calling that there was light on the water, that the river was still rising, that Mr. Merrick's breathing had gone thin and they needed to decide, *now*, tonight, before dawn decided for them.

Whitlock straightened. Whatever had opened in his face closed, but differently than before — not like a drawer this time. Like a door someone has finally resolved to walk through.

"Marta," he said, picking up the satchels, "when they write down what happened here — and someone always writes it down — mind they get the weights right."

I didn't know what he meant. I followed him into the hall anyway, and that is how I came to be standing at the top of the stairs when the door burst open with the wind behind it, bringing rain and river-smell into the lamp-lit warmth —

— and there, dripping on the threshold, gray-lipped and shaking, half-carried by nothing but stubbornness, stood a woman with Whitlock's same sharp cheekbones and his same way of standing like a person braced to be turned away.

"Is this the observatory?" she said, and then her eyes found his face across the hall, and everything in her crumpled. "Sable."

The name fell into the room like a struck bell.

He set down the satchels very slowly, the way he did everything, as if even now he might still be able to put things back where they'd been. Behind me I heard Wren's breath catch. Down the corridor, old Aldous coughed once, softly, like a clock marking the hour.

The river roared below us, and outside, the cable car swung on its one thread, and I counted the berths again — three, always three — and waited to see what kind of man the flood had finally washed ashore.
