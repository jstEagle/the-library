## 8. Three Berths

The river told us before anyone else did.

I heard it first through the floor of the instrument room, a low grinding beneath the tick of the master clock, like millstones turning somewhere far below the ice. By dawn the sound had climbed into the walls themselves. Marta stood at the kitchen window with a wooden spoon still in her hand, watching brown water shoulder through the broken ice downstream, and said only, "It's early this year," in the flat voice of a woman who had counted floods the way other people counted birthdays.

By noon the director had posted the notice by the cable house: *River rising. Evacuation at first light tomorrow. The car carries three.*

Three berths. Nine souls at the station that winter — Hale, Wren, Marta, young Pell the assistant, two weather observers whose names I still stumbled over, Aldous Vane, and myself under my borrowed name of Merrick. And now, it seemed, the arithmetic was about to get worse.

* * *

Aldous sat in his corner of the workshop with a blanket around his shoulders, mending a hairspring he could no longer see properly, working by touch and forty years of habit. His cough had gone from occasional to constant since the thaw began, and each fit left him grayer, smaller, as though the damp air were drawing him out of himself.

"You'll take my berth," he said, without looking up. It was not a question and not quite an argument; it was simply a fact he had already filed away among other facts.

"I'll do no such thing."

"Sable." He used my real name now — he alone on the mountain did, ever since the night in the clock room when I'd confessed what I'd found in the compartment and who I was. He said it gently, the way one sets down something fragile. "I am seventy-three years old. My lungs are full of the winter. The mountain has had all the use of me it's going to get. You are thirty-one and you have not yet learned to be honest with anyone, which means you haven't finished living."

"That's a terrible reason to give me your seat."

"It's the best reason there is. Unfinished men are worth more than finished ones." He fitted the hairspring into its collet, blind fingers sure as water finding a channel. "Besides. Someone should stay with the instruments. Someone who understands them."

The instruments. The barometers, the seismograph, the rain gauge, the telegraph key that whispered the observatory's findings down to the valley every hour. If the flood took the lower road, someone would need to keep the record running from the heights, or a year of observations would drown unread. Hale had already begun, quietly, to look at me when he spoke of it. He knew what he was asking. He thought he knew what I would choose.

I went out into the cold to escape the kindness in the old man's voice.

* * *

Wren found me at the cable house an hour before dusk. Snowmelt roared in the gorge below, and the cable sang in the wind, a high thin note like a struck wire — the same note, I thought absurdly, as the fourth above middle C, and I could not stop hearing it.

"They've drawn the list," she said. She stood close enough that our shoulders nearly touched against the wind, which was its own kind of confession. Neither of us had named what had grown between us across those long night watches, but it lived in the silences now, fat and warm as a banked coal.

"And?"

"Papa's list. Wren Hale. Aldous Vane." She paused, and I watched her decide whether to soften it, and watched her decide not to. "And you. Sable Merrick."

"Your father is generous."

"My father wants you where he can't see you and where you owe him everything. Those happen to be the same place." Her breath came out white between us. "He thinks if he saves your name, you'll keep his secret. Our secret." She meant the letters, the survey fraud, the founding scandal she'd been pulling apart thread by thread for weeks. "But I don't want to leave owing anything. Not to him. Not to anyone."

"There's a third berth, Wren."

"I know what the third berth is." Her jaw set. "And I know what it costs, and I know you're already deciding to pay it, and I am telling you now, while there's still light, that if you give up your seat I will never forgive—"

She stopped. Because we both heard it: voices from the trailhead, down where the switchback met the flood plain. Shouting, thin with distance, and then a figure breaking from the treeline, stumbling, falling, rising again.

We ran.

* * *

She came up the last stretch of the trail half-carried by Pell and one of the observers, and even before they turned her face to the lantern light I knew her. Ten years is nothing. Blood keeps its own records.

Iris.

My sister — though no paper in the world would say so now, since Whitlock was a name I had buried in another valley — was soaked to the waist, her boots ruined, her dark hair plastered to her cheeks. She had forded the swollen tributary on foot. She was twenty-six years old and she looked, in that moment, exactly as she had at sixteen, standing in our father's doorway the night I left, saying *go, then, go* with her chin up and her eyes already apologizing.

"Iris." Her name came out of me before permission did.

She saw me and something moved through her face — relief and rage and ten years of unsent letters, all in the space of a breath. "They told me at the valley office a repairman named Merrick had wintered here." Her voice shook with cold and something colder. "Merrick. You couldn't even keep our name to hate us with."

"Iris, the river—"

"I know about the river." She pulled free of Pell's arm and stood swaying. "Why do you think I crossed it? The whole valley knows the cable runs tomorrow with room for three. Papa's accounts are frozen. There are men looking for him — for *us*, Sable, they're looking for the family too, they came to the house—" Her voice cracked clean through. "There is nowhere else. Do you understand? There is nowhere left for me to go, except wherever you are. Even after everything. Especially after everything."

Behind her, the crowd that had gathered at the cable house had gone very quiet. Wren stood at my shoulder. Aldous had come out wrapped in his blanket, coughing softly into his fist. Director Hale stood in the doorway of the quarters with the lamp behind him, and I felt his attention settle on Iris like a hand on a scale.

One berth. Two claimants. My sister, who had carried our father's ruin alone because I had run rather than carry any of it. And the choice I had already been building toward all week without admitting it — the old man in the blanket, who had sheltered a runaway apprentice when I was nobody, who had taught me that a clockmaker's whole creed is that every part matters, even the small ones, *especially* the small ones, and that you do not throw away a movement because it is worn.

Love, debt, and blood. I had three days ago believed I understood which was which.

"Sable." Wren's voice, low, for me alone. "Whatever you choose—"

"I need to talk to her."

* * *

We walked to the edge of the gorge, where the wind took our words and scattered them, though there was no one left to overhear. Below us the river had swallowed the ice entirely, a brown muscle flexing through the dark.

"When did you last eat?" I asked.

"This morning. In a barn. The farmer charged me." She laughed, and it broke in the middle. "You look well, Sable. That's the cruelest part. You look *well*. You ran off and made yourself new hands and a new name and you look well, and I stayed, and I look like this."

"I didn't make myself anything. I borrowed it all." I turned to face her. The truth had been climbing in me all day like water in a gauge, steady, unstoppable, and now it reached the mark. "Iris. Listen to me. Tomorrow you get on that car."

"I'm not taking your seat—"

"You're taking the third berth. Not mine. Aldous Vane was going to give me his, and I was going to refuse him, and then I was going to give you mine, and none of that is going to happen, because I've done the arithmetic you taught me when we were children — you always were better at sums than I was." I took her frozen hands in mine. "Someone has to stay with the instruments. It's in the regulations. The station cannot simply go dark; a year of records would be lost, and the valley needs the readings during the flood. It isn't sacrifice, Iris. It's the job. It happens to be mine."

"Liar," she whispered. But her hands gripped back.

"Yes. A little. But only a little, and I'm so tired of the rest." I breathed the cold air, and let the next part come, because if I did not say it now I never would. "I found the surveys, Iris. Here, in the clock. Father's handwriting. He falsified the founding measurements. The whole observatory stands on numbers he invented. And the director knows, and he wanted my silence, and I nearly gave it to him because silence is the family trade." Her eyes widened; I pressed on before she could speak. "I'm done trading in it. Whatever happens after the flood — the truth goes where it has to go, under my own name, the real one. Whitlock. Ours. Both of ours. If you'll stand in it with me."

For a long moment there was only the river and the singing cable.

"You absolute fool," Iris said, and began to cry, and laughed while crying, which was the most familiar sound in the world to me. "Ten years, and you finally tell the truth to your own sister on a mountainside during a flood."

"I work best under deadline."

She hit my shoulder, weakly, the way she had when we were children, and then held on to it instead.

* * *

At first light the cable car stood ready, swaying in the gray wind, its iron frame beaded with frost. The list had been amended overnight in Hale's tight hand: *Hale, W. — Vane, A. — Whitlock, I.* He had written the name himself, having heard it spoken on the platform, and when I saw it posted I understood that he knew exactly what I had chosen, and what it unmade of our bargain.

Aldous argued until his cough defeated him, and then pressed his brass loupe into my palm — "for keeping watch" — and let Pell help him aboard. Wren boarded last. She stood at the open gate and looked at me for a long moment while the operator called the time.

"The bridge opens when the water drops," she said. "Two weeks. Perhaps three."

"I'll keep the hours."

"Count them carefully, Sable Whitlock." And then, softer, so only I could hear it over the wind and the river and the groaning cable: "*All* of them. I'm coming back for every single one."

The gate closed. The car swung out over the gorge, three small figures in the mist — my sister's hand raised, the old man's blanket snapping like a flag, Wren's face turned back toward me until the fog took her.

Then the mountain was quiet, and the instruments waited, and far below the river rose toward me through the dark, patient as time itself.
