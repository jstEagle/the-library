## 9. The Hour of Decision

The river had stopped sounding like water. That was the first thing I noticed, standing on the cable-car platform with the wind tearing at my collar — the Meridian no longer murmured beneath the ice but roared, a live animal turning in its sleep, and every hour that roar climbed higher up the scree. Marta had come breathless from the instrument room to say the gauge read eleven feet and rising. The last car left at dusk. Three berths. Nine souls.

The platform smelled of wet iron and pine resin and the kerosene of the signal lamps, which Wren had lit herself though it was not yet her watch. Snow came down in fat, indecisive flakes that melted the moment they touched anything warm — our hands, the housing of the motor, the small crowd of us pressed together like passengers on a ship we all knew was sinking. Above us the cable ran off into the murk, sagging under its own grief, and the far anchor tower was already invisible, swallowed by the gray.

Aldous sat on the one bench, wrapped in two blankets, his hands folded over the head of his cane as if in prayer. His breathing made a sound like paper tearing. He looked at me and did not ask for anything, which was how I knew he understood everything.

Iris stood apart, dripping.

She had come up the goat trail from the valley road in the teeth of the thaw — half-waded, half-climbed, her hair plastered to her skull, her boots ruined, her eyes finding me across eight years and nine feet of floodwater with an expression I could not read and did not dare to. She had crossed a mountain to reach a berth she did not yet have.

"Whitlock." Director Hale said my name quietly, without the false surname, and I felt the syllables land on the platform like dropped tools. Wren's head turned. Of course she had heard. Of course she had known before any of us were ready. "The manifest," Hale went on, briskly, as if naming me had cost him nothing. "Miss Hale, Mr. Aldous, and—" he glanced at Iris, at her blue lips, and something in his face closed like a drawer "—your sister, I suppose."

"Three berths," Marta said, unnecessarily, counting us aloud with her eyes. Nine people. Three berths. The arithmetic sat among us like a corpse.

Wren came to stand beside me at the platform's edge. She did not take my arm. We had not arrived at touching, quite, though the long night watch had brought us close enough that I could feel her nearness the way one feels a fire through a door. Below us the river heaved, carrying whole trees end-over-end, and somewhere under that brown violence was the bridge that had closed in November and would not open until spring, if it opened at all.

"You should be on that list," she said.

"I know what I should be."

"Sable." My false name, in her mouth, sounded like a question she had been asking for weeks.

* * *

I want to say I deliberated. I want to tell you there was a long moment where love and debt and blood balanced on the pan of some inner scale, each weighing honestly against the others. But the truth is simpler and worse: I had been deliberating since the night I found my father's forged surveys in the clock's hidden compartment, since the night Director Hale offered me silence in exchange for protection, since longer ago than that — since the evening I walked out of my father's house in Boston with one valise and a name I meant never to answer to again. All those years of running had merely been the long approach to this platform. The decision had been making itself the whole time, gear by gear, the way a clock makes noon without anyone winding it.

"Iris," I said.

She looked up. Water still ran from the hem of her coat onto the boards, spreading dark around her boots, and I thought absurdly of all the puddles she had tracked through our childhood hallway, all the times our mother had scolded her for it, and how I would give anything now to hear that scolding again.

"The berth is yours."

"No." Her voice cracked on it. "No, Elias, you don't get to—"

"Elias." Wren said it softly. Not a question anymore. An arrival.

I turned to face her, because if I was going to lose everything I wanted, I intended at least to do it looking at her. The snow landed on her lashes and did not melt fast enough.

"My name is Elias Whitlock," I said. "My father is Alistair Whitlock, and thirty years ago he falsified the survey records this observatory was founded on — moved the meridian line two miles so the land would pass to men who paid him for it. I found the proofs in your father's clock three weeks ago. Your father knows. He has always known. He summoned me here to bury it, and I let him believe I would, because I was afraid, because I have spent eight years being afraid of everything, including—" my voice failed and I rebuilt it, plank by plank, the way Aldous had taught me to rebuild an escapement, tooth by tooth "—including wanting things. Including wanting you."

The river roared. Someone behind us — Marta, I think — had gone very still.

"The instruments need tending through the flood," I went on. "The transmitter, the barographs, the master clock. If the station goes silent, the valley towns below won't get the warning readings. Someone has to stay. Aldous can't — look at him. So it will be me. And you will go down that cable hating me, or not, and either way you will know the truth, which is more than I ever gave anyone." I took a breath that hurt going in. "I'm sorry. For the name. For the silence. For every hour of it."

Wren looked at me for a long time. The lamp hissed. The cable groaned in its tower like a ship's rigging.

"You knew," she said, "and you stayed anyway. To keep the instruments running." Something moved across her face — anger and grief and something warmer fighting for the same ground. "You absolute fool, Elias Whitlock."

Then she kissed me, once, hard, there on the platform in front of her father and the rising water, and stepped back, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "This isn't finished," she said. "None of it. The truth gets published, do you hear me? Both truths. Yours and mine. When the bridge opens, I'm coming back up this mountain, and you will be alive to see me do it."

"Yes," I said, which was the only promise I had ever made that I knew I could keep.

* * *

Getting Iris into the berth was like loading a cat into a crate — she fought me the whole way, hissed my name, called me a hypocrite and a martyr and several words our mother would have washed my mouth out for. I held her by the shoulders inside the car, in the yellow lamplight, with the water climbing the tower legs below.

"Eight years I waited for you to come home," she said. "Eight years, Elias. And you send me away?"

"I'm not sending you away. I'm giving you the future." I pressed the survey documents into her hands — the forged records, the proofs, everything I had pulled from the clock's belly. "Take these down. Don't give them to Father. Give them to a newspaper, a surveyor, anyone. You always were the brave one. You always were the one who told the truth even when it cost us dinner." I laughed, and it came out broken. "Go be brave a little longer. For both of us."

She gripped my coat with both fists, and for a moment her forehead rested against my chest, and she was twelve years old again, waiting up for me past bedtime. Then the attendant called the final boarding, and she stepped back into the car, and her chin came up the way it always did before a fight.

"Whitlock," she said, using the name like a weapon and a gift at once. "Live. That's an order."

The brake released. The car swung out over the flood — I watched it go, three small faces at the window, Wren's hand pressed flat against the glass until the fog took them. Aldous stood beside me at the rail, leaning on his cane, wheezing gently.

"Well," he said. "Now it's just us clockmen."

* * *

We went up to the instrument room while the light lasted. The storm arrived properly an hour after the cable car vanished — not snow now but rain, warm and wrong for January, the thaw gone rabid, the whole mountain weeping at once. Through the windows the world was black except when lightning showed us the river, and the river showed us nothing worth seeing.

Aldous regulated the barometers while I tended the transmitter, sending readings down the line every half hour: pressure falling, river at thirteen feet, fourteen, fifteen. Somewhere below, in offices lit by lamplight, strangers read our numbers and rang bells and woke their neighbors, and none of them would ever know the name of the man who sent them. There was a strange comfort in that. Anonymity, chosen rather than fled into, tasted almost like peace.

At midnight the master clock struck twelve, and its voice filled the stone room the way it always had — patient, incorruptible, striking the hour whether anyone listened or not. I sat beneath it with my back against the case, feeling the escapement tick against my spine like a second heartbeat, and Aldous dozed in the director's chair with his blanket around his shoulders, and outside the water climbed.

Fifteen feet. Sixteen. The generator coughed and died at half past two and we worked by lamplight, hand-cranking the transmitter, and still I sent the readings, because the numbers were true and true things deserve to be spoken.

Somewhere toward dawn the river reached its throat-clearing peak — seventeen feet, six inches, the highest mark on the gauge, high enough that water came halfway up the lower yard and stole the woodshed bodily. Then, so gradually I might have imagined it, the numbers began to fall.

Aldous woke at first light. The rain had thinned to mist; the river had dropped a foot; the world, wrecked and rearranged, existed again. He looked out the window at the drowned yard, then at me, slumped against his beautiful clock.

"You told her, then," he said. It was not a question. Old men who mend clocks notice when a mechanism finally releases.

"I told her everything."

"And she went anyway." He smiled, gap-toothed, ancient, wise. "They always come back up the mountain, boy. It's the ones who leave quietly you have to worry about."

I might have answered — I had some speech half-formed about deserving nothing, expecting nothing — but at that moment the transmitter key chattered under a hand not mine. I leaned over the tape and read it twice, because the first reading refused to be believed.

It was the valley relay station. The message was short.

BRIDGE INTACT BUT UNDERMINED — TRAVEL POSSIBLE WITHIN DAYS. MISS HALE REQUESTS: HOLD STATION. HOLD YOURSELF. SHE IS COMING BACK UP WITH THE FIRST SURVEY PARTY AND SAYS TO TELL YOU — HER WORDS — *THE CLOCK WAS RIGHT ALL ALONG.*

Aldous read it over my shoulder, laughing his torn-paper laugh, while outside the mist lifted off the water and the sun — pale, improbable, entirely on schedule — rose over the Meridian.

I put my hand flat on the clock's case, over the compartment where my father's lies had slept for thirty years, now empty as a confessed sin.

Then I reached for a pen, and fresh paper, and began to write my real name.
