## 1. The Question Too Many

The winter of the census came early to Vessany, riding in on a wind that smelled of coal smoke and river rot, and it arrived at our door the way all dangerous things did in those last months of the Republic—politely, with papers.

I had been carrying documents for seven years by then, and I want to be precise about what that means. I was not a smuggler. Smugglers choose what they carry; they weigh risk against profit and walk away from the bad bargains. A courier carries what he is given, and his whole art consists of being nothing: a body with dull eyes and a duller coat, a man whose face you could look at directly and forget before your tea cooled. For seven years I had been that man. I had memorized code sequences the way other men memorized prayers—*Kestrel-nine, Vellum-four, the gray door opens at dusk*—and I had recited them into the ears of deputy ministers and burned them off my tongue with cheap brandy the same night. Invisibility is a discipline like any other. You practice until no one practices noticing you.

So when the knock came on the third of Frostmonth, I answered it as myself, which is to say as nobody.

He stood on our landing with snow gathering on his shoulders—a broad, unhurried man in the gray uniform of the Bureau of Enumeration, holding a leather folio against his chest like a hymnal. He had a face built for forgetting: soft jaw, pale lashes, eyes the color of dishwater. Only later would I understand that this was its own kind of discipline, that Halvard had practiced his own invisibility longer than I had practiced mine.

"Noor Lockridge," he read from the folio. "This household?"

"That's me."

"Three registered. Yourself, Mirela Lockridge, Tavi Lockridge." He pronounced each name carefully, the way a man lays cards on a table, watching to see if any of them wobble. "May I come in? The form requires verification of residence."

Mirela's voice came from the kitchen doorway before I could answer. "Verification of *residence*," she said dryly. "As opposed to verification of what, exactly?" She wiped her hands on her apron, an old woman's gesture performed by a woman who was not old but had decided to seem so, and stepped past me into the hall. "Come in out of the snow, then. You'll ruin the government's boots."

Something flickered across Halvard's face—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. He stepped inside, and the temperature of our house changed.

Our flat occupied the top floor of a building that leaned slightly eastward, three rooms and a stove that ate coal like a starving thing. It was, on paper, unremarkable. On paper it had been Mirela's family home for forty years, passed down through a lineage she had never possessed, inhabited by a niece-by-marriage arrangement that existed nowhere except in ink that Mirela herself, in another life, in another ministry, had set down. She had forged our family the way she had once forged land titles: meticulously, with the correct inks and the correct tremor in her hand, so that even the flaws looked authentic. Seven years we had lived inside her forgery. I had carried state secrets through three ministries without my pulse rising above seventy beats a minute, and yet standing in my own hallway while a census taker removed his gloves, I felt my heart begin to run.

That should have told me something. I noted it, the way I noted everything, filed it away in the cold room behind my eyes where couriers keep the things they cannot afford to feel yet.

Tavi was at the table when we entered the main room, doing sums he had no intention of finishing, fifteen years old and all elbows and watchfulness. He looked up at the uniform and then at me, and I gave him the smallest shake of the head: *nothing*. It was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he went back to his arithmetic with the studied indifference of a boy who had learned young that adults' lies are sometimes load-bearing walls—you don't pull at them if you want the house to stand.

Halvard worked through the standard questions first, and I let the rhythm of them settle me. Number of rooms: three. Occupations: clerk, retired archivist, student. Sources of water: municipal. Any members of the household absent more than sixty days: no. He wrote in a small, fast hand, and the scratch of his pen was the only sound besides the stove ticking as it heated. Snow ticked against the window in counterpoint. Outside, somewhere down the street, a tram bell rang twice and faded, the city going about its ordinary business while the extraordinary sat at our table drinking tea Mirela had poured because refusing hospitality was itself a mark in a file now.

Then he turned the page.

And the questions changed key.

"Household lineage," he said. "Standard for this district. Mirela Lockridge—you are recorded as head of household by inheritance. The property passed from... " he consulted the folio, though I understood already that he knew exactly what it said, had perhaps memorized it, "...your uncle, Andras Lockridge. Deceased."

"My uncle," Mirela said. Her voice was perfectly level. I had heard her voice perfectly level through air-raid drills and ministry audits and the night Tavi arrived on our doorstep feverish and half-starved. Perfectly level.

"You were born in this house?"

"I was born in Vessany." Which was true, in the way that a forged coin contains real metal.

"The house, madam. The form asks about the house."

A pause. Outside, the snow thickened against the glass, erasing the street, erasing the city, erasing everything except this room and the small sound of a pen waiting.

"In this house," Mirela said.

Here is what I know now, what I did not let myself know then: she hesitated. Not in her voice—in her hands, which had been folded on the tablecloth, and which moved, just slightly, one thumb crossing over the other, the way a woman crosses herself. A habit from a country she had never officially lived in. And Halvard saw it. I watched him see it, the pen pausing above the page for half a second, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the pause had been the point of the entire visit—that the lineage questions were not bureaucracy but bait, and the whole apparatus of the census, the folio, the tea, the mild dishwater eyes, existed to make a woman's hands betray her.

And then I made my mistake.

"Her uncle kept poor records," I said. "The old man. Papers were lost in the flood year. Half the district's records were."

It was a good lie. It was even a plausible lie—I had checked, years ago, that there had been a flood. But it was a lie offered too quickly, by a man who had not been asked, in a household where the head of household needed no defending, and Halvard turned his dishwater gaze on me with the gentle attention of a man examining a specimen.

"And you are the nephew," he said. Not a question. Then, softly, almost kindly, the question too many: "By blood?"

The silence that followed lasted perhaps two seconds. I have been in rooms where two seconds of silence meant artillery. I had been trained—no, *tempered*, seven years tempers a man—to fill silences or to own them, whichever served. Every protocol I knew said: *say yes, say it bored, say it the way you'd confirm your shoe size.* Yes, by blood. Of course by blood. What an absurd question. Who would forge a family?

What came out instead was nothing.

My mouth opened and my trained silence—the silence I had carried codes inside of, the silence that was supposed to be my armor—came up empty. Because it was not my silence anymore, I realized. Somewhere in seven years of shared meals and mended socks and Tavi's fever and Mirela's terrible chess, the silence had stopped being a tool and become a home, and homes cannot be lied out of. I stood in my own kitchen and could not say the word *blood*, because saying it would make it a claim, and claims get checked, and under every check was the truth: that Noor Lockridge had been assembled from spare parts in a back office of the Ministry of Records by the woman sitting calmly across from him, that Tavi at the table was a foundling with no surname until he was nine, that our whole family was ink and nerve.

"Yes," Mirela said, filling my silence for me, smooth as poured wax. "By blood. My sister's boy. You'll forgive my nephew; he thinks genealogy is a vulgar subject. The family quarrels, you understand."

"Of course." Halvard wrote. The pen scratched for longer than *yes by blood* required. He wrote the way Mirela must once have written, I thought—someone who understood that a record is a weapon and a sentence is ammunition, and that the deadliest entries are the ones that look like weather notes. *Overcast. Household nervous. Subject volunteered information unprompted.*

He closed the folio. He thanked us for the tea. At the door, pulling on his gloves, he paused with his hand on the latch, and looked at me—not at Mirela, at me—and said, in the tone of a man discussing the weather:

"They're re-verifying the eastern districts this year. Old households especially. There will be follow-ups." A beat, snow melting on his shoulders, dripping onto our floorboards. "For some households, several."

Then he was gone, down the stairs, his boots fading into the white hush of the street.

None of us moved for a long moment. The stove ticked. Tavi's abandoned sums sat on the table, columns of innocent numbers. Mirela looked at her folded hands, at the space where the census taker's cup still steamed, and her face—for the first time in seven years—showed me the thing underneath the levelness, which was fear, old and patient and prepared, the fear of a woman who had always known this afternoon would come and had only prayed to be elsewhere when it did.

"Tavi," she said quietly. "Go to your room."

"It's my family too—"

"*Go.*"

He went. I heard his door close, too gently—he had learned that trick from me—and then it was the two of us and the cooling tea and the wet ring Halvard's presence had left on our table like the mark of a glass raised against us.

Mirela rose, crossed to the window, and watched the gray uniform diminish down the street. When she spoke, her voice had changed registers entirely; the archivist was gone, and something older stood in her place.

"He wrote for eleven seconds after I answered," she said. "Eleven seconds is a report. Eleven seconds is a file traveling to the Ministry of Records tonight, and a flag on our block by morning." She turned from the window, and her eyes were very bright and very calm, the calm of deep water over things that drown men. "Get your coat, Noor. We have perhaps an hour of ordinary evening left, and there are things you need to burn, and things you need to know about where I really worked—and there is a key in this floor that has been waiting seven years for exactly this kind of afternoon."

* * *

Later—after the fire in the stove had eaten the photographs and the letters and the small blue notebook I had never asked her about—she knelt at the edge of the kitchen floorboards and pried loose a plank I had walked over ten thousand times. From beneath it she drew a key, iron, long-shanked, black with age, and pressed it into my palm, closing my fingers over it as though she were closing a wound.

"The Ministry of Records," she said. "Third sub-basement. Everything the Republic says about us, Noor, and everything it says about itself—it lives in that building. And tonight, I think, the men who write the files are coming for ours."
