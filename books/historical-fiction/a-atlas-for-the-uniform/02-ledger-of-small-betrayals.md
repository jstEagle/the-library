## 2. Ledger of Small Betrayals

The stove in the back room had not been lit for cooking. Mirela made certain of that before she knelt before it, her knees complaining against the cold tile, and began feeding it the papers.

She worked by lamplight with the shutters drawn, though she told herself the darkness was habit rather than fear. Habit was a comfort she could still afford. Fear, properly rationed, kept a woman honest; unrationed, it turned her into one of those trembling creatures who signed whatever was put in front of them at the review offices. She had seen such women, once, from across a long room with high windows. She had stamped their files herself.

That was the thought she had been avoiding all afternoon, since Halvard's boots scraped on the front step and his voice—polite, unhurried, the voice of a man reading from a form—had asked the question too many. *And the lineage of the household head? Maternal line preferred.*

Seven years of practiced silences, and Noor had failed to find one. She had watched it happen: the boy standing in the doorway with his courier's blankness sliding off his face like wet paint, his mouth opening on nothing. Halvard had smiled the way clerks smile when they see a sum that does not add, and written something down.

*Something.* In the Ministry of Records, thirty years ago, Mirela had learned that everything depended on what a man wrote down, and nothing depended on what he meant. A marginal notation. A tick in a column meant for ticks. The census rolls did not record suspicion; they recorded *discrepancies*, and discrepancies were routed upward on Tuesdays.

Today was Tuesday.

She fed the stove another handful. Her old letters from Vessany's provincial years—harmless, sentimental, but bearing an address that no longer existed because the street had been renamed twice. Her correspondence with a bookseller in Ostrava who had been arrested in the spring; even innocence was contagious now, and paper carried infection beautifully. A photograph of a young man in a Records uniform, buttoned to the throat, whose face she had loved before she learned what the uniform fed on. She held that one a moment longer than the others. Then she let it go, and the flame took his face first, the way fire always takes faces first, and left only the uniform burning, which felt correct.

Tavi's drawings she would not burn. The boy filled the margins of his schoolbooks with bridges and harbor cranes and impossible flying machines, and if the state wanted to catalog a fifteen-year-old's imagination as sedition, then let them come and explain themselves to her. Some things were worth being caught holding.

Noor came home after dark. She heard him at the door doing his seven-year ritual—the pause, the two soft knocks, the third knock that meant *only me*—and she had time to close the stove's grate and arrange her face into something calmer than she felt before he entered.

He stood in the doorway of the back room, coat still on, snow melting in his dark hair, and looked at the stove, and at the ash already gray and thick in its belly, and at her hands, which smelled of smoke no matter how she'd washed them.

"You're burning things," he said.

"I'm deciding things," she said. "Burning is just how decisions end."

He hung up his coat with the exaggerated care of a man who needed his hands occupied. "Halvard wrote something down. I saw it. He wrote while I was still standing there, like he didn't care that I saw."

"He didn't care," Mirela said. "That's the point of it. Intimidation used to be done in cellars, Noor. Now it's done in doorways, politely, in front of witnesses, so that the terror is documented and the document is terrifying." She rose from the hearth, joints cracking like small green wood. "Come. Sit. There are things you should have known years ago, and today they've become urgent."

He sat across from her at the table where they had eaten ten thousand meals, and she studied him a moment—the face she'd known since he was a hollow-eyed foundling of eleven who could open any lock in the quarter but couldn't look anyone in the eye. She had taught him silence the way other mentors taught rhetoric. She had never taught him this, because she had hoped he'd never need it.

"Do you know why your silences work?" she asked.

"Because I don't lie. Liars need stories, and stories unravel."

"Because you don't lie," she agreed, "and because someone spent three decades making sure the records that could contradict you don't exist. Silence is only safe inside a burned archive, child. You've been living in the shelter of my fires without knowing it."

His eyes narrowed. Noor was not quick the way clever men were quick; he was deep, the way wells are deep, and it took a moment for anything to reach him. When it did, he went very still.

"The Ministry of Records," he said slowly. "You knew the forms. That day at the registrar's, when Tavi's papers almost failed—you corrected the clerk on the margin code. You said it was from helping in an office during the war."

"I said what was survivable," Mirela said. "I was not a helper. I was Assistant Keeper of the Ninth Registry, Household Lineage and Movement, for nineteen years. I know where every family in this city is buried in paper. I buried some of them myself."

The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick without looking at it. Old hands remembered.

"There were three of us who kept the destruction schedules," she went on. "When the Republic began eating itself—this was before you, before any of this—certain registries were ordered purged. Not preserved, understand. Purged. Names removed from the rolls so that families could be made to have never existed, which is the cheapest way to make people disappear: you don't kill them, you unfile them. My colleagues refused and were reassigned to provinces that no longer appear on maps. I refused more quietly. I falsified the destruction certificates. On paper, forty thousand files burned. In truth, they went into a sealed annex beneath the Ninth Registry, under my personal seal, and I have spent thirty years waiting for someone to ask me about them."

"And now?"

"Now Halvard has written down a discrepancy in our household," she said, "and you should understand what happens next, because it will happen exactly, mechanically, the way everything in this country happens exactly and mechanically, right up until it becomes murder and everyone professes surprise."

She drew toward her a sheet of plain paper and a pencil stub, and drew as she talked—a square, a smaller square beside it, a line connecting them.

"The census taker writes his report tonight or tomorrow. It goes to a district compiler, who enters households with discrepancies into a review queue. Review means a second visit, deeper papers, interviews with neighbors. If the discrepancy stands—and ours will, because ours is real, because I built our lives on forgery the way masons build on stone—then the household is marked." Her pencil pressed harder. "Marked households go onto a schedule. The schedule goes to the garrison liaison. And after the garrison liaison, there is no next step anyone will admit to. People marked in the spring queues were gone by autumn. Their flats were re-let by winter. I have read the paperwork, Noor. Disappearance, in Vessany, is a filing error with boots."

Noor sat with his hands flat on the table, the posture he took when he was steadying himself. "How long?"

"A review visit takes two weeks to schedule. Perhaps less, now, with the year ending and quotas to fill. Quotas," she added, seeing his face, "yes. Even erasure has productivity targets."

"And the files. The ones you saved. The real lineage rolls—if they still exist, they prove that half the marked households are legitimate. They're evidence."

"They are the only evidence," Mirela said, "and they sit behind a door in the Ninth Registry annex that opens to two keys. One has hung around my neck for thirty years inside a locket I have told everyone contains a portrait of my dead mother." She laughed, briefly, a dry sound like paper turning. "My mother never sat for a portrait in her life."

"And the other key?"

Mirela closed her eyes. Here was the betrayal she had rehearsed for three decades and still could not perform gracefully: handing a child the weight she had carried alone. But the alternative was carrying it into whatever room Halvard's notation opened, alone, and losing it there.

She unpinned her collar and drew out the chain. The locket was brass gone soft with handling, warm from her skin. Inside, coiled in oilcloth where a portrait should be, lay a key the length of her smallest finger, black iron, its teeth cut in a pattern she could draw blind.

"The counterpart is in my old office," she said. "Third floor of the Records building, Room 9-C, inside the frame of the window that overlooks the courtyard—pried out of the plaster, sealed under paint, painted over again every year by my own hand whenever I was permitted to visit, right up until my retirement. The office was reassigned, but the window was never replaced; the budget for windows has always been scandalous, thank God for institutional miserliness." She pressed the locket into his palm and folded his fingers over it. "If anything happens to me, Noor—if the review comes early, if I am detained, if I simply do not come home from market—go to Room 9-C. Take both keys. Open the annex. And get those files out of this country."

"Me." His voice was flat. "Why me? Why not burn them, like you're burning everything else?"

"Because burning them is what they want," she said, "and because I did not keep forty thousand families alive in the dark for thirty years to hand them a merciful fire at the end. Those names are ammunition. Someone will eventually need proof of what Vessany did to its own people, and paper outlives regimes. Paper is the most patient witness there is." She held his gaze. "Besides. You carry codes between ministries every day and no one sees you. That is a rare gift, child. Most men cannot be invisible. You were built for it—God forgive me, but I helped build you."

He looked down at the locket in his fist. When he spoke again his voice was careful, stripped bare. "The census taker. Halvard. Will he report it?"

Mirela was quiet a moment. She had spent one afternoon in Halvard's presence and a lifetime among his kind, and there was a thing in his face she had not expected to see again in this country: reluctance, worn thin as glove leather, but there.

"He'll report something," she said at last. "Men like him always report something; the form requires it. But whether he reports a discrepancy—" she turned her hand palm-up, weighing air, "—that depends on questions I can't answer yet. Which means we assume the worst and act tonight."

* * *

They worked past midnight, by lamplight, in the rhythm of two people who had shared silence for years and needed few words. Mirela dictated; Noor memorized. The route to the Records building through the coal yards. The courtyard sentry's shift change at the half hour. The exact wording of the maintenance pass she still possessed, yellowed and folded in the lining of her winter coat, authorizing *M. Vessany-lineage-none, Records staff, retired* to access archival floors—an anachronism the guards had stopped reading years ago.

Room 9-C. Window overlooking the courtyard. Third pane from the left, lower corner, beneath six layers of paint.

"Say it back," she said.

He said it back, perfectly, in the flat voice that meant the words had already moved into the vaulted place inside him where he kept other people's secrets. She felt the strange maternal pride and the stranger grief of it—that her legacy to this boy was a talent for hiding, honed like a blade, when what she had once dreamed of giving him was a country that didn't require hiding in.

Near dawn she sent him to bed and remained at the table, listening to the house settle. Snow against the shutters. Tavi breathing through the wall, deep and untroubled, dreaming of flying machines. Somewhere across the city, a clerk was opening a queue.

It was then she noticed, in the cold grate, that one item had not burned completely. A corner of heavy stock, ministry watermarked, curled at the edge of the ash. She reached in with the poker and drew it out: a fragment of an internal routing slip, decades old, her own initials in the corner—*M.K., Ninth Registry*—the rest consumed except for a single legible line:

*...schedule forwarded to garrison liaison, effective year's end...*

She had written it herself, thirty years ago, certifying a destruction that never happened. She had forgotten it existed. And the words on it—*effective year's end*—were the words now circulating through every ministry in the capital, gathering speed like weather.

Mirela sat very still as the light came gray through the shutter seams, the scrap trembling in her old fingers.

Year's end. Nine days away.

Whatever was coming, it would come before the New Year—and the key around her neck had grown suddenly, unbearably heavy.
