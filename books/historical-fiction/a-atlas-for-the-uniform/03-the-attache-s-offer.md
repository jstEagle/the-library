## 3. The Attaché's Offer

The Ministry of Interior smelled of wet plaster and cold iron in the last week of November, and Noor Lockridge had learned to breathe it the way a fish breathes water—without thinking, without tasting. He moved through the corridors with the particular gait of a man who has made himself furniture: shoulders slightly stooped, eyes fixed on the middle distance, hands occupied with a leather satchel that had carried nothing more dangerous than lunch for three years running. The guards at the west stair nodded at him without seeing him. That was the whole art of his trade. Seven years of being unseen, and he had grown so good at it that sometimes, alone in his room at night, he wondered whether he still existed at all except when Mirela called him to dinner.

The summons came as they always came—a slip of grey paper left in the courier's alcove, unsigned, bearing only a room number and an hour. *Room 214. Four o'clock.* He had delivered a hundred such slips himself, and he knew their grammar: the vaguer the instruction, the higher the floor, the worse the news.

Room 214 was not on any map of the ministry he knew. It sat at the end of a corridor where the gas lamps had been replaced by electric bulbs that buzzed like trapped wasps, and the door bore no nameplate, only a brass number polished bright by anxious hands.

He knocked once.

"Come in, Master Lockridge." The voice inside was accented—Vessany spoken the way a foreigner speaks it, each vowel held a half-beat too long, consonants softened as if apologizing. "And close the door. What I have to say doesn't belong to this building."

The man behind the desk was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in the grey of a country Noor could not place—not the black of the northern federations, not the olive of the southern republics. Grey, like weather. Like the sky over a border you haven't crossed yet. He did not wear a uniform, but everything about him suggested one: the squared shoulders, the hands folded flat on the blotter, the way his chair faced the door rather than the window.

"I'm afraid you've mistaken me," Noor said, which was what he always said, which was the first sentence of every silence he'd ever kept. "I only carry documents between floors. I don't—"

"You carry codes." The man said it mildly, the way one remarks on rain. "Between ministries. Third Floor Records to the Office of Censuses, Tuesdays and Fridays. The Census Annex to the Chancellery, whenever the little grey slips appear. You've done it for seven years, since the autumn the old registrar died and his nephew took over the routes." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Please sit down, Master Lockridge. Or should I say—"

He paused. Let the pause do its work.

"—should I say the name your household uses? The name Mirela Voss's papers say she raised?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, somewhere below, a tram bell rang twice, absurdly domestic. Noor found that his legs had carried him to the chair without his permission, and he sat, because standing suddenly seemed dangerous, like being upright in open ground during shelling.

"You know about the census," Noor said. Not a question. His mouth had gone dry as ash.

"I know that Halvard Osten visited a certain house on Larkspur Lane nine days ago and asked one question too many. I know what he wrote in his margin notes—I know because I pay men like Halvard, and some of them are honest, and honesty can be bought more cheaply than loyalty." The attaché opened a drawer, unhurried, a man who owned time the way landlords owned buildings. "I know that households flagged for lineage review are transferred to the Office of Consolidation within six weeks. And I know what happens after transfer."

"They review the papers," Noor said. "There's an appeals process. There's—"

"There is a furnace," the man said, "and there is a ledger, and the appeals process is a queue that ends at the furnace. Forgive me. I didn't ask you here to frighten you. Fear makes couriers stupid. I asked you here because you are very good, and very desperate, and those two qualities together are rarer than either alone."

He set something on the desk between them. A book—no, a ledger, bound in oxblood leather, its corners worn round the way books wear when they have been carried secretly through many hands. Stamped across its cover, in faded gilt: *OFFICE OF CONSOLIDATION — SCHEDULE OF TRANSFERS.*

Noor did not touch it. His hands stayed in his lap, folded, obedient. But his eyes went to it the way a starving man's eyes go through a window to bread.

"The census isn't a census," the attaché said. "It never was. It is an inventory. Every household whose lineage cannot be traced three generations inside Vessany goes into this book, and this book becomes a schedule, and the schedule begins on the first of February. Your Mirela Voss is page forty-one. Her ward—the boy, Tavi—" he turned a page without touching it, letting Noor read the columned names, the neat bureaucratic script, dates and districts and numbers that refused to mean what they meant— "is page forty-one also. Listed under her. As dependents so often are."

Noor's heartbeat had become a sound in the room, louder than the buzzing bulbs. He thought of Tavi that morning, arguing cheerfully about whether sparrows dreamed, butter on his chin, fifteen years old and utterly unaware that his name lived in a government ledger between two columns of ink. He thought of Mirela burning papers in the kitchen stove, feeding the flames page by page with the serenity of a woman saying grace.

"What do you want," Noor said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it.

"My name is Serevan. I am attached to the Legation of—well. The country doesn't matter; by spring it will be called something else anyway. My government believes, correctly, that when this purge happens, the world will be told there was no purge. No list, no schedule, no order. Only rumors, and refugees, and mass graves that official histories will call epidemics." He laid one finger on the ledger's cover. "This book is the proof. Authentication seals, ministry countersignatures, dates. It cannot be forged because it *is* the forgery—the state's own hand against itself. But paper proves nothing in a drawer. Paper proves everything in a foreign press office. It must leave Vessany before the year turns. After New Year's Day, the borders close. All of them. Completely."

"So take it yourself. You're an attaché. Diplomatic pouches exist."

"Diplomatic pouches are searched precisely because everyone assumes they aren't. Three of my colleagues have already been sent home—one of them feet-first—for less." Serevan's eyes were pale grey, patient, terrible. "But a courier who carries nothing but lunch, who has walked past every checkpoint in this city for seven years without being seen—that man is invisible. Until now, invisibility has been your gift. I am asking you to spend it."

*Asking.* Noor almost laughed. The word hung in the room like a lamp without light behind it.

"And if I refuse?"

Serevan spread his hands—an honest gesture, or the shape of one. "Then nothing happens today. Nothing happens tomorrow. You go home, you eat dinner, you listen to the boy complain about his arithmetic tutor. And in six weeks, maybe five, men come to Larkspur Lane with a warrant and a wagon, and Mirela Voss's appeals process begins, and Tavi—" he glanced down at the open page, gently, as if checking the time— "goes where unclaimed minors go. There is a facility north of the city. I won't describe it. Describing it would be cruelty, and I need you calm."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts. Noor gripped the arms of the chair. He had spent seven years learning every dialect of silence—the silence that deflects, the silence that agrees, the silence that waits—and now every one of them failed him, because silence was exactly what Serevan was offering him, and silence had a price tag written in a fifteen-year-old's handwriting.

"What would I have to do?" he said instead. Words at last. His own words, chosen, and therefore his.

"Three checkpoints. The river bridge at Vellmark—you know it, your Tuesday route crosses upstream of it. The archive district gate. And the border house at Kessel Pass, which closes at midnight on New Year's Eve and does not reopen until the purge is finished." Serevan drew a thin envelope from beneath the blotter and placed it beside the ledger. It was fat with papers. "Inside: transit permits for three persons. One retired archivist. One minor ward. One courier, occupation unspecified. They are genuine—my legation's stamp, my government's seal, verifiable at any consulate on the other side. When the ledger reaches my press contacts, your family walks out of history alive. That is the entire transaction."

"And if I'm caught at a checkpoint?"

"Then the permits burn with you, and the ledger finds another courier, and I will attend your household's appeal personally to express regret." He said it without malice, which was somehow worse. "I don't promise safety, Master Lockridge. I promise stakes. Everything else is weather."

Noor looked at the ledger for a long time. The gaslight—or electric light, the wasp-buzzing bulbs—caught the gilt lettering and made it flare. He thought about the codes he carried every week, strings of figures committed to memory and burned from paper, meaningless to him, sacred to the ministries. He thought about how a man becomes a corridor: people pass through you, things are hidden in you, and no one ever asks the walls what they know.

"Why me?" he said finally. "Truly. Not the flattery version."

Serevan considered the question, and to his credit, seemed to actually consider it.

"Because you were trained by someone who worked in Records," he said. "Your route discipline, your document handling—it's archivist's training, not courier's. Someone taught you to think like the people who built these lists. Which means you're the only man in this city who might understand what he's carrying well enough to protect it." A thin smile, the first one. "Also because you said 'what would I have to do' instead of 'how much will you pay.' Men who ask the second question sell you twice."

Noor stood. His knees felt borrowed. He looked down at the ledger, at page forty-one, at the two names that were his entire country, his entire religion, his entire reason to have learned the grammar of silence in the first place.

"If I take it," he said, "the checkpoints will cost me. You know that. Everyone knows that. Nothing passes three gates in wartime without paying toll."

"Yes," Serevan said quietly. "I know. I'm sorry for it. Choose what you'll pay carefully—some things, once surrendered, are not returned at the border."

He slid the envelope across the desk until it touched Noor's hand. The paper was cold.

Outside Room 214, the corridor buzzed its wasp-song, and the tram bells rang again far below, and somewhere across the city a stove was cooling in a kitchen on Larkspur Lane where an old woman had just fed the last of her past to the fire. Noor tucked the envelope inside his coat, against his chest, where the lunch usually rode, and began the long walk back through the ministry that had never once seen him.

Tomorrow was Friday. He had codes to deliver, and now a ledger to learn by heart, and forty-one days until the year died.

At the west stair, the guard nodded through him as always.

This time, Noor nodded back.
