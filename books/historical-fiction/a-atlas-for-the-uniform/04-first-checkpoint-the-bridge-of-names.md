## 4. First Checkpoint: The Bridge of Names

The river came into view an hour before the bridge did, a long grey seam of light between the hills, and Noor felt the ledger against his ribs the way one feels a second heartbeat—insistent, foreign, not entirely his own.

Serevan had sewn it into the lining of his coat in three places, then unpicked two of them. "One seam," the attaché had said, tapping the remaining stitch with a lacquered fingernail, "is confidence. Three is paranoia. Guards smell paranoia the way dogs smell fear." Noor had not asked how many dogs there were at the bridge. He had simply memorized the route: down through the terraced vineyards where the winter vines stood like rows of crucified men, along the towpath, past the customs house with its green shutters, onto the span itself. The Bridge of Names, the carters called it, though no sign said so. It had earned its title honestly enough—the census rolls hung here now, posted on boards at either end like weather reports, updated each morning by clerks who worked with the unhurried diligence of men who knew their ink would outlive them all.

He joined the queue of foot traffic at half past nine, when the fog was still lifting off the water in slow rags. Around him: a woman with geese in wicker cages, their orange bills poking out like questions; two brothers hauling a handcart of coal; an old man who walked as though his knees were negotiating separately from the rest of him. Ordinary people carrying ordinary things across a river. Noor made himself one of them. Seven years of ministry corridors had taught him that invisibility was not a talent but a discipline—you lowered your shoulders, you let your gaze rest two steps ahead, you became a man thinking about nothing more remarkable than the price of bread.

The guards had set up a table at the bridge's near end. Two of them, in the new winter uniforms, dark as spilled coffee, with the republic's crest stitched over their hearts—and below the crest, small enough to pretend it wasn't there, the crest of something newer. A clerk sat behind them with the rolls open before him, turning pages with licked fingers, and beside the clerk a brazier burned, and above the brazier the fog gathered and thickened until the whole checkpoint seemed to float on smoke.

"Name," said the first guard, to the goose woman.

"Pella Voss."

The clerk ran his finger down a column. The finger stopped. Moved on. Stopped again. "Voss. Pella. Household of four, Gull Street, third district. You travel alone?"

"My husband's dead. The children are with my sister."

A pause. The guard looked at the geese, at her hands, at her face, which had gone carefully smooth. Then he waved her through, and the geese muttered their disapproval, and Noor counted his own heartbeats and found too many of them.

The coal brothers went next. Then the old man, whose name produced such a long search through the rolls that Noor watched the clerk's finger travel nearly the whole page, back and forth, back and forth, like a shuttle weaving something none of them could see yet. Eventually the old man passed too. It was possible, Noor told himself, that this was only procedure. That most names passed. That the stopping finger meant nothing.

But Mirela had said otherwise, the night before, standing at her window with her back to him so that he watched her words come out as breath on the glass. *The census isn't counting people, Noor. Counting is arithmetic. This is theology. They are deciding which souls officially exist, and everything else follows.* She had turned then, and he had seen something in her face he had never seen in seven years—not fear exactly, but its older sibling, the thing fear grows from when it has been fed for decades.

"Noor Lockridge," she had said quietly. "Whatever happens at that bridge, remember what a name is. It is a door. And a door can be closed from either side."

Now the coal cart rattled away over the planks and the guard looked up.

"Next."

Noor stepped forward. The fog had thinned enough that he could see the far shore, a smudge of roofs and poplars, and beneath the bridge the river moved with the patient muscle of something that had carried armies, grain barges, drowned secrets, and did not distinguish among them.

"Name."

"Noor Lockridge."

Even as he spoke it, he felt the strangeness of hearing his own voice say the syllables—as if he were reporting someone else, describing a colleague who happened to wear his coat. The clerk's finger began its journey down the column. Noor kept his eyes on the brazier, where a knot of damp wood hissed and spat, and he thought: *the L comes after K, the roll is alphabetical, Lockridge will be under L, please be under L, be there, be ordinary, be a name like any other name.*

The finger stopped.

It did not move on.

"Lockridge," the clerk said, in the tone of a man reading a prescription aloud. "Noor. Household of three. Candlemaker's Row. Census notation—" and here he tilted the page toward the guard, and Noor saw, upside down and reversed, a small mark in the margin, a hook like a fisherman's barb, the same mark he had watched Halvard's pen make seven days ago in his own kitchen while Mirela's teacup trembled almost imperceptibly in her saucer. *Notation for review,* Mirela had translated. *Which is to say: pending.*

"You are listed," the guard said. It wasn't unkind, precisely. It was worse than unkind—it was administrative. "Households under review do not cross without supplementary documentation. Do you have supplementary documentation?"

"No," Noor said.

"Then I'm afraid—"

"I have transit papers from the Foreign Ministry." He had rehearsed this the way he rehearsed everything, in the dark, walking the routes in his mind until they wore grooves. Now he produced the folder Serevan had given him, diplomatic buff paper, the seal embossed deep enough to catch light. "Courier status. Ministry business. My household listing is a clerical error—I am traveling on assignment."

The guard took the folder the way a man takes a fish he suspects of being bad. He turned it over. Held the seal toward the light. And Noor understood, with the cold clarity that arrives sometimes in the worst moments and simply stands aside to watch, that the papers were good but not good enough, that seals impress other clerks but not soldiers, that the barb in the margin outweighed any embassy's stamp, because the barb had been written by hand, by Halvard, in his kitchen, in the last afternoon when his family had still been safe.

The guard opened his mouth—

And closed it again, because a third man had come up from the customs house, older, heavier, wearing the same dark uniform but wearing it like furniture rather than clothing, and he had been watching Noor, Noor realized, since before he reached the table. Since the queue. Perhaps since the vineyards.

"Lockridge," the older man said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"There was a Lockridge family registered in the capital. Nineteen years ago. Father a typesetter." The man's eyes were the color of dishwater and about as readable. "You'd have been a child."

*Say yes. Say anything. Say the truth, even—truth is the last thing they look for.* But Noor's practiced silences, those seven faithful years of held breath and lowered eyes, chose that moment to fail him, and what came out instead was nothing at all, and the nothing stretched, and the guard shifted his weight, and somewhere behind them the goose woman's birds complained at the delay.

"Typesetters are listed trades," the old man continued mildly. "We have the trade registers too. Everything is written down somewhere, young man. That is the genius of the age. Nothing is lost anymore." He smiled without warmth. "Except, occasionally, on purpose."

He beckoned the clerk, murmured, waited while pages turned—trade registers now, then something else, a third book Noor hadn't seen before, bound in red, kept under the clerk's elbow like a hymnal. The fog was burning off fully now. Sunlight lay hard and white on the bridge planks. Noor could see all the way to the far shore, could see the road climbing away from it into the hills, the road that led to the second checkpoint, the burning city, Mirela waiting—or not waiting, Mirela never merely waited—and Tavi, fifteen years old and incapable of understanding that some doors must close.

The clerk looked up. Shook his head slightly. The red book stayed shut.

"Curious," the old man said.

Then he did something Noor would turn over for years afterward, examining it from every angle like a coin of doubtful minting. He took Noor's transit folder, opened it, read the assignment line—and took out a pen.

"The courier exemption applies," he said, "to couriers. Courier Lockridge of the Foreign Ministry crosses freely." He wrote as he spoke, crossing something out, writing something else. "The boy from Candlemaker's Row, however—that household record appears to contain a duplication error. Name assigned twice. One instance must be struck." He initialed the margin, right over the fisherman's barb. "Regrettably, it cannot be struck from the household copy. Only from the traveler."

He handed the folder back. He did not hand back anything else, because there was nothing else; the name had lived on paper for nineteen years, and the paper had just been amended, and amendments in the republic were not corrections—they were erasures with good handwriting.

"Go," the old man said. "Cross quickly. The roll is recopied every morning."

Noor walked. His legs performed the mechanics of walking while the rest of him lagged behind on the near shore, standing at that table, being deleted. Halfway across the bridge the planks gave way to iron and his footsteps rang differently, hollowly, and the river below ran green-grey and indifferent, and he gripped the rail because the world had developed a slight tilt. A name, Mirela had said. A door. He had crossed the bridge carrying his own name and arrived on the far side without it, and the strange thing—the thing that made him stop, briefly, in the middle of the span with the wind pulling at his collar—was that he felt lighter. Unburdened. As if the state, in taking the name, had accidentally taken the weight of it too.

*Noor Lockridge is flagged. Courier Lockridge passes.* Whoever the guards stopped tomorrow morning would find the barb already struck through. Whoever they searched would find only a courier. He had been given, of all things, a kind of mercy—dressed in bureaucracy, delivered without explanation, costing him the one thing he had believed was safely his.

He was still standing there, wind in his teeth, ledger pressing its false heartbeat against his ribs, when he saw the figure on the far bank.

Small. Dark-haired. Standing at the foot of the ramp with a canvas bag slung over one shoulder and an expression Noor knew better than his own reflection—the set jaw, the lifted chin, the furious, terrified stubbornness of a boy who had followed his brother through a winter country because he refused, absolutely refused, to be left behind.

Tavi.

* * *

The ledger suddenly weighed nothing at all and everything, and Noor started forward across the last of the bridge, already composing the scolding, already dreading the tenderness underneath it, already knowing that every careful route he had mapped was now ash—because if Tavi had found him here, Tavi had walked past the census board at the near end, and Tavi's name was still on it, unstruck.
