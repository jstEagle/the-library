## 5. Tavi Follows

The house told me before anyone did.

I'd learned to read it the way other boys read weather. The way Mirela's slippers whispered instead of tapped. The way Noor polished his boots twice in one evening, which he only did when the roads were bad or the news was worse, and sometimes both. The lamp in the front room burned past midnight three nights running, and nobody scolded me for being awake to see it.

Seven years in that house had taught me that families were made of small sounds. Mirela's cough before she lied — a soft clearing, like sweeping dust under a rug. Noor's habit of counting under his breath when he was afraid, though he thought I didn't know; I'd heard him do it over the wash basin, *one-two-three-four*, his lips barely moving, like a man reciting something he wasn't allowed to write down. And my own sound, the one I contributed: silence, mostly. Fifteen years old and practiced at it, because in a household built on forged papers and borrowed names, you learn early that questions are a kind of theft.

But that week the house went quiet in the wrong way. Not the comfortable quiet of people who trust each other. The held-breath quiet of people hiding the same thing from one another.

It started with the smell of smoke that wasn't from our stove. I came downstairs before dawn and found Mirela standing at the kitchen basin feeding papers into the fire, one at a time, so the flames wouldn't leap. She wore her good shawl, the grey one with the frayed edge she saved for official visits, as if burning documents required formality. Her hands moved with terrible calm.

"You should be asleep," she said without turning around.

"The chimney smells different."

"Old paper burns differently than wood." She fed another page to the fire. In the light I caught a corner of an official seal, the double-headed bird of the Republic, before it curled black. "Go back up, Tavi."

I didn't go back up. I stood in the doorway and watched seven years of whatever-it-was turn to ash, and Mirela let me watch, which frightened me more than chasing me out would have. When the last page was gone she washed her hands at the basin for a long time, scrubbing between each finger, and said, "Some things are safer forgotten," in the voice she used for history lessons, except this time she was talking about herself.

That afternoon Noor came home with ink on his cuff and a look I'd never seen on him: not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a man who has agreed to something and is already grieving it. He kissed Mirela's cheek. He ruffled my hair, which he hadn't done since I was twelve, and that was how I knew.

He left two days later, before first light, wearing the coat with the hidden seam. I knew about the seam because I'd found it once while mending — a channel sewn into the lining, flat and long, sized for paper. I'd asked him about it and he'd said, "Maps," and smiled, and I'd known it wasn't maps.

I watched him go from my window. He walked toward the river district without looking back, and there was a moment, just at the corner where the lamplighter's pole stood crooked, when he paused. Half a second. As if the city itself had snagged on him. Then he turned the corner and was gone, and the pause stayed behind like a splinter in my chest.

Mirela made breakfast as if nothing had happened. Porridge, brown sugar, the good spoon. "He'll be back by week's end," she said.

She was lying. Her cough came right before it, the little sweep of dust under the rug.

Here is what nobody in that house ever understood about me: I am not a child to be protected. I am a person who was chosen. Mirela pulled me out of the census orphanage when I was eight, off a list, with forged papers she signed herself, and she told me once — drunk on plum brandy one winter night, the only honest hour I ever got from her — "You weren't given to me, Tavi. I committed a crime to get you. Remember the difference." I remembered. Being chosen is a debt, and debts are paid in kind: you don't get left behind by the people who chose you. You don't get carried like cargo, sealed in a safe house, kept *safe* while they walk into checkpoints built for people like us.

So I packed.

Not much. The coat with the torn pocket because it looked poorest. Bread wrapped in cloth. My mother's button — the single relic of before, a brass thing worn smooth — sewn inside my collar where no searcher would think to feel. And the map. I'd copied it weeks ago from the atlas in Mirela's study, tracing the river road east with charcoal onto butcher paper, folding it into eighths. I told myself it was practice, cartography homework, but my hands had known better even then. You trace a route enough times and your feet learn it.

I left Mirela a note. It took me an hour to write four sentences. *Gone after Noor. Don't follow me, you'll be marked. I'll keep him alive. — T.* I put it under the sugar tin where she'd find it within the day, and then I stood in the doorway of the house that had been the only true place I'd ever lived, and I memorized it. The water stain on the ceiling shaped like a hand. The door that stuck unless you lifted the handle. Mirela's spectacles folded on the table like a sleeping insect.

Then I closed the door, and the click of the latch sounded exactly like the end of childhood, which is to say: ordinary, and final.

* * *

The river road east was full of people pretending not to flee.

That's the trick of a country dying slowly — everyone leaves politely. Families walked in their best clothes with luggage too small for the length of their faces. A woman ahead of me carried a birdcage with no bird in it, and when I asked her why, she said, "So they'll believe I'm coming back." Everyone on that road believed they were coming back. That belief was the heaviest thing any of us carried.

I fell in with a grain merchant's family for the first day, close enough to look like belonging to them, far enough not to be questioned. This too I'd learned from Noor: invisibility isn't hiding, it's arranging yourself inside someone else's story. I became a nephew. I carried their smallest boy on my shoulders through the mud flats and the mother gave me half a sausage and called me a good lad, and I thought: *this is what a name is worth. A sausage and a stranger's kindness.* Noor had surrendered his whole documented self for less than that, probably. For a nod at a bridge.

Because I knew about the bridge. I'd listened at doors my whole life; it was the one skill my upbringing rewarded. First checkpoint, the Bridge of Names, where guards cross-reference travelers against the census rolls. Second checkpoint, the archive city — Vellmar, where Mirela had worked before us, before me, in the Ministry of Records whose existence she denied having ever entered. Third checkpoint, the border, New Year's Eve. Three gates, and Noor walking through all of them carrying something in a hidden seam, and every adult in my life deciding I would hear none of it.

The trouble with trailing a man trained in invisibility is that he doesn't leave tracks. But he leaves habits. He counted under his breath when he was afraid; he also stopped at every shrine, every roadside niche with its guttering votive candle, and touched the stone with two fingers. An old habit from somewhere before us, some faith or superstition he'd never explained. So I followed the shrines. East along the river, then south at the fork where the pilgrim path crossed, candle wax and cold mornings, always a day behind him, sometimes half a day, reading his devotion like a written trail.

On the third night I nearly lost him. Snow came sideways off the river and erased the road, and I sheltered in a hay barn with a widow and her goats, and lay awake doing Noor's counting — *one-two-three-four* — until it turned into a prayer I didn't have words for. If he passed the second checkpoint without me seeing him, I'd lose him to the border and the year would turn and whatever the adults were so afraid of would happen on the far side of a gate I couldn't reach.

I caught him at noon the next day, in Vellmar, by accident and by design both.

By design, because I'd figured out he'd have to pass the fountain square where the archive building stood — you could see it from everywhere in that city, a grey bulk like a tombstone for paper, its windows ranked like teeth. By accident, because when I finally saw him, crossing the square with his collar up and his head bowed, my body acted before my sense did. I shouted his name.

His actual name. The one from before, the one he'd surrendered at the bridge — I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know until later, but the sound of it tore out of me raw, the childhood name, the one I'd only ever heard Mirela use in anger.

He stopped. The whole square seemed to stop with him. And then he turned, and the look on his face when he saw me — fifteen years old, snow-crusted, breathless, furious — was not joy. It was horror wearing joy's coat, grief arriving ahead of relief, and even then, even at twelve paces in a crowded square, I understood what I'd done. I hadn't brought love to him. I'd brought complication. I'd taken the careful route he'd built and snapped it in my fists like bread.

"Tavi." He crossed to me fast and gripped my shoulders hard enough to hurt. "What are you— how did you—" He looked over my head at the crowd, at the windows, at the archive with its teeth. "You shouted my name. Here. Of all the—"

"You left," I said. My voice cracked and I hated it. "You both left. You left me to be *safe*. I'm not cargo, Noor. I'm not a parcel you store in a cellar until the roads are clear. I'm your brother, and brothers don't get left behind."

Something moved through his face then, quick as fish under ice. Because I'd never said it before either. Seven years in that house and we'd orbited the word *family* without ever landing on it, the way you orbit a fire.

"They're burning it," I said, jerking my chin at the archive, because I'd heard it on the road, in the mouths of porters and innkeepers, the way news travels faster than any courier: the Ministry of Records, scheduled, tonight or tomorrow, officially an accident. "And Mirela worked there. Whatever she gave you the key for — she'll go back for it herself, won't she? She'll go back inside."

Noor went very still. The stillness I knew. The counting stillness. His lips moved, almost silently: *one-two-three-four.*

"Tavi," he said quietly, "how much do you know?"

"Everything," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he took my arm anyway and pulled me toward the shadowed side of the square, away from the archive, away from the eyes in its ranked windows.

"Then walk," he said, "and don't say another word until I tell you."

And above us, behind those teeth of glass, in the grey heart of the record city, a light was already flickering that had nothing to do with lamps — and neither of us knew yet that Mirela was already inside.
