## 6. Second Checkpoint: The Archive Burns

### NOOR

The light in the archive windows was not lamplight. I had carried codes past enough watchmen to know the difference between the amber patience of a lamp and the restless, jumping orange of something that eats. It climbed the ranked glass like ivy, fast, greedy, and beneath it the Ministry of Records — Mirela's ministry, though she had not set foot inside it in nine years — began to glow from within like a lantern made of paper.

Which was, in a sense, exactly what it was.

"Tavi. Stop." He had been walking too fast, pulling me by the sleeve through the shadowed side of the square, and now he stopped so abruptly that I nearly overtook him. In the firelight his face was all planes and panic. "That's no accident."

"I know," Tavi said.

"You don't know anything." My voice came out harder than I intended, harder than I had ever used with him, and I saw it land — saw the flinch travel down through his shoulders like a struck bell. But there was no time to soften it. The square was filling with people who had come to watch, because people always come to watch, and every one of them was a pair of eyes that might be attached to a list. "You go back to the rooming house. You stay there with your hand on the door until I come."

"Where are you going?"

I looked at the burning archive and felt the answer form in my chest before my mouth agreed to it, the way a code forms under the fingers before the mind has finished translating. *Mirela.* She had given me a key three nights ago, pressed into my palm across the kitchen table with her old woman's certainty: *If anything happens, if they mark us, you go to my office, third floor, north range, and you take what's in the drawer.* I had thought she meant papers. I had thought she meant someday.

But Mirela had never once in seven years waited for someday.

"She went for the key's counterpart," I said. "She told me she would. I didn't believe her." I closed my fist around the iron shape in my pocket until its teeth bit my palm. "Stay here. Count to six hundred. If I'm not out—"

"If you're not out, then I'm coming in after you," Tavi said, "and you can't stop me, because you'll be dead."

There was nothing to say to that, so I said nothing, and silence — my oldest craft, my only inheritance — carried me across the square toward the fire.

The service door at the archive's flank stood ajar. Someone had gone in ahead of me; someone had propped it with a brick as casually as a baker propping his shop. Smoke breathed out of the gap in slow grey exhalations, and the heat came with it, thick, tasting of glue and leather and something sweeter underneath — burning paper, thousands upon thousands of pages, the state's whole memory going up in the order it was kept.

I pulled my collar over my mouth and went in.

The stairwell was still passable. That was the horror of it — how *orderly* it was. The fire had been set by professionals, floor by floor, section by section, so that the destruction would be complete but the building itself would stand, a shell to be reused. They were not burning the ministry. They were burning *us*, and keeping the house.

Third floor. North range. The smoke lay along the ceiling in a low grey tide and I moved through it bent double, one hand on the wall, counting doorways the way I counted everything — one-two-three-four — until my fingers found the brass plate she had described, worn smooth, and the lock that was older than the Republic and kinder to old keys than new ones.

The drawer inside held a ledger bound in grey cloth, thin as a psalter, and a single sheet loose between its pages covered in columns of figures I did not understand and one line I did: a certification of authentication, stamped with the seal of the Assistant Registrar of Households — Mirela's own signature, thirty years younger, beside a countersignature I did not recognize. Serevan had told me the ledger meant nothing without its authentication page. Here it was. Here it had been all along, waiting in the dark like a seed.

I heard the ceiling groan above me, a long animal sound, and ash began to fall like black snow.

And then, through the smoke from the direction of the atrium, a voice I knew better than my own, calling my name.

"Mirela!" I shouted, and the smoke took my voice and gave back nothing.

### TAVI

He counted to four hundred and then he broke his own promise, which did not surprise me, because Noor makes promises the way other people make excuses — sincerely, and meaning to break them.

I had followed him through the service door at a count of fifty, keeping the wall between us, watching his bent silhouette climb the stairs into the orange throat of the building. I am fifteen and small for it; I have been invisible my whole life by necessity, which is a skill no child should own and every listed household teaches. The crowd in the square had their faces turned up to the fire. Nobody watched a boy slipping sideways through a doorway.

Inside, the heat was a hand pressing on my chest. The smoke hung at head height and below it the air was almost clear, and I walked through the ground floor of the place where Mirela had worked for thirty years — where she had forged her first false record and her last true one — and I saw shelves collapsing in slow motion, ledgers opening as they fell like birds shot mid-flight, their pages fanning, browning, curling into flame. Names. All of them names. Somebody's grandmother was burning on the left. Somebody's entire lineage was curling at the edges two steps from my boot.

And somewhere above me were the two people who had chosen me when nobody else would have, and I could not breathe properly and did not care.

I found them on the third-floor landing, in a corridor where the smoke had descended almost to the floor and moved like weather. Noor was on his knees, and he had Mirela's arm over his shoulders, and Mirela — Mirela was laughing. Coughing so hard she doubled, and laughing through it, an awful sound.

"The fool," she wheezed, "the absolute fool, they set the south range first, they always start with births, do you understand? Births and adoptions. They're unmaking everyone who was ever *made*—"

"Later," Noor said. "Talk later. Walk now."

I got under her other arm. She weighed nothing, this woman who had carried our whole household on her back for seven years; the fire had hollowed her. Her hair had gone from grey to white at the temples where cinder had kissed it, and there was a burn along her forearm she was pretending not to notice, and her eyes found mine and went wide and then — worse than anger — wet.

"Tavi," she said. "Oh, you stupid, brave boy."

"Don't," I said, because if she said anything else I would cry and crying takes air and there was no air. "Walk."

We made the stairs. Behind us the north range window burst inward and the fire drew a long breath, and the whole corridor behind us turned the color of the inside of a bell.

### NOOR

We came out through the service door into cold air that tasted like salvation, and the crowd's noise swallowed us — shouting now, because the third floor had gone up all at once, a bloom of sparks rising over the roof tiles like a field of wheat deciding to be stars.

Mirela sank down against the wall in the alley's mouth, dragging breath. I knelt beside her. And it was then that I opened my coat to check the ledger, as I had checked it a hundred times in a hundred doorways, and my hands went still in the way that means the worst has already happened.

The grey book was there. The authentication page was not.

It must have come loose in the stairwell — a single sheet, loose among the pages, and I had run through falling ash with my collar open, and somewhere between the third floor and the cold it had slipped free. One page. Thirty years old. A signature in faded ink and a wax seal that was, at that moment, either lying somewhere in the corridor we had crawled out of or feeding the fire that had just eaten the north range.

"Noor?" Tavi's voice, very small.

"The page," I said. My own voice sounded far away, a courier's voice reporting a fact about cargo. "It's gone."

Mirela caught my wrist. Her grip was shockingly strong. "Look at me," she said. "Thirty years I kept that page safe and tonight I went back for it like a woman going back for a photograph, and do you know why? Because paper is not the thing, Noor. Paper was only ever the map." Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "*I* authenticated that ledger. My hand signed it. If the page is ash, then the truth of it lives where it always lived — in the person who can swear to it. They cannot burn me twice."

Above us the archive roared, and sparks came down like the sky shedding, and Tavi put his arms around both of us, and the three of us knelt in an alley while the Republic burned its own memory, holding to each other the way embers hold to a log.

It was Tavi who saw them first. Two men at the far mouth of the square, moving against the flow of the crowd, heads turning side to side, reading faces the way Halvard had read ours. Not firefighters. Not spectators.

"They're looking for someone," he whispered.

And one of them raised his hand, and pointed — not at the burning archive, not at the fleeing clerks — but at the three of us, kneeling together in the dark like a family portrait waiting to be taken.
