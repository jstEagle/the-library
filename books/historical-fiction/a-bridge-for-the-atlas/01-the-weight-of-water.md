## 1. The Weight of Water

The fog was still on the water when Kestrel went in.

He liked it that way — the harbor blind, the town blind, the whole gray world reduced to the sound of his own breathing and the cold pressing close around his ribs like a second skin. At dawn there were no clerks on the pier, no tally-men with their brass-clicking counters, no one to watch him work and decide what his work was worth. There was only the sea, which kept no books.

He rolled once at the surface, let the air out slow through his nose, and dropped.

The shallows off Gullstone Point were his own discovery, three years back — a shelf of broken coral and old shell where the pearl oysters grew thick as roof tiles, fat-lipped and dark. The Atlas Company's divers worked the deep beds beyond the point, the ones the company boats could reach, the ones the company had measured and mapped and entered into its ledgers down to the last fathom. Nobody had bothered to measure this. The company did not believe a boy of seventeen could find what its paid divers missed, and so far nobody had told them otherwise.

Kestrel meant to keep it that way.

He moved along the bottom with long, easy pulls, not swimming so much as pouring himself through the water, the way his father had taught him before his father's chest began to rattle. *Don't fight it,* Dunmore always said. *The sea weighs nothing if you agree to be weighed.* It was the one piece of wisdom the old man still delivered without bitterness, though these days he delivered most things from a chair by the stove, coughing into a rag he pretended not to inspect afterward.

Kestrel found the first cluster by feel more than sight — the light up here was milk-thin, barely enough to turn the coral from brown to bone. His fingers walked over the shells, reading them the way Tobias read his ledger pages: quickly, surely, by touch. Too small. Too thin. This one — yes. He pried it loose with the short blade at his belt and tucked it into the net bag at his hip, and the shell came away clean, leaving no wound in the reef worth mentioning.

That was the trick of it. Take enough to live, never enough to notice.

His lungs began their slow complaint. He counted it away, the way you count anything — one for the oyster, two for the bag, three for the swim back to the anchor stone — and kicked for the surface, breaking through into the fog with a gasp he kept quiet out of habit. Sound carried on still water. The whole town might be asleep, but the town had ears even in sleep.

* * *

Briony was on the rocks when he came up, cross-legged with a mending needle in her fist, working her way down a net that had more holes than net. She was fifteen and had hands like an old woman's, knuckles swollen from twenty years of work she'd only done ten of. She didn't look up when he hauled out onto the flat stone beside her, dripping.

"Eight," he said.

"Eight is nothing."

"Eight is eight."

"Eight is breakfast." She bit the thread and pulled it tight. "It isn't rent."

Kestrel tipped the bag out between them. The oysters sat in a wet heap, and Briony finally looked, because neither of them could help looking — it was the same every time, the small greedy hope that today's heap would be big enough to mean something. He pried the best shell open with the knife's flat. Inside, in the muscle's fold, sat a pearl the color of weak tea.

"Good one," Briony said, and there was something in her voice she'd have denied.

"Decent." He wiped it on his thigh and held it up to the graying sky, turning it. A decent pearl bought a month of flour. A good pearl bought flour and lamp oil and a week off the fear. They'd had maybe four good pearls in his whole life, and every one of them had gone straight across the counter at the company house and come back as ink in a book — a smaller number where there had been a bigger one, the debt creeping downward like something drowning very slowly.

"Father coughed in the night," Briony said, too casually.

"I know. I heard."

"He says he'll take the boat out tomorrow."

"He's not taking the boat out."

"He says—"

"He's not." Kestrel put the pearl in the tin box they kept inside a hollow stone, under a rock shaped like a sleeping dog, because even here, forty feet from their own door, you didn't leave money where money could be seen. "I'll do the deep beds myself next week. The weather's turning anyway; he'd only get soaked and make it worse."

"You can't work the deep beds alone."

"I've done it."

"Twice. And both times you came up white as a drowned man and lied about how long you'd been under." She jabbed the needle through the mesh hard enough to bend it, straightened it, went on sewing. "You lie badly, Kes. You always have. You get this look like you're counting."

He did not tell her he'd been counting since he was nine years old. Counts of breath, counts of pearls, counts of days until the quarter-day when the collector came down from the company house with his ledger under his arm and his clerk behind him carrying the pen box, and the whole harbor lined up like children outside a schoolroom to hear what they owed.

Everyone owed. That was the shape of life in Merrow's Rest, and everyone understood it the way fish understood water — completely, wordlessly, without imagining anything else. The Atlas Trading Company owned the boats, or rather owned the paper saying who owned the boats, and the paper said the boats belonged to itself until such time as the debts were paid, and the debts were arranged so that they would never quite be paid, the way a horizon is never quite reached. Father had explained it once, years ago, standing in the company house with his cap in his hands: *We don't owe them money, son. Money you could pay. We owe them time.* Kestrel hadn't understood then. He understood now. Time was the one thing a diver had plenty of and the one thing the company knew how to take.

"We need eleven," Briony said. "By quarter-day. Eleven good ones or we're into the spring debt, and you know what happens when you go into the spring debt."

He knew. Spring debt compounded. Spring debt was how the Pell family had lost their boat outright, and how old Marta Senn had ended up shucking oysters in the company shed six days a week for wages that covered exactly half her own bill. The company called it *carrying*. The harbor called it what it was.

"I'll make eleven," Kestrel said.

"From the shallows?"

"From wherever I have to."

She stopped sewing and looked at him then, really looked, the way she did perhaps twice a year — the rest of the time her eyes were busy elsewhere, on nets, on knots, on the middle distance where their mother used to stand before the fever took her. When Briony looked at you like that it was less affection than audit. "You're going to do something stupid," she said.

"I'm going to do arithmetic."

"That's the same thing, in this family."

* * *

The sun burned the fog off by mid-morning, the way it always did, and the harbor appeared all at once — the crooked line of the seawall, the drying racks strung with fish like laundry, the company house up on its rise of green lawn with its white columns and its slate roof, the only building in town that had never once leaked. From the water it looked almost kind. From the water you couldn't see the barred window on the records floor, or the flagpole where the Atlas colors ran up every morning at seven sharp, or the clerk who stood at the top of the steps sometimes just watching the harbor, making his small notes.

Kestrel scrubbed the salt off in the rain barrel and put on his good shirt, meaning the one with only two patches, and weighed out the morning's take on the chipped scales in the kitchen while Father watched from his chair and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

"Nine," Father said finally.

"Nine and a half. The tea-colored one will weigh up heavy."

"It'll weigh what it weighs. The scale doesn't care what you hope." He coughed then, deep, the wet rattle of it, and pressed the rag to his mouth, and when he took it away Kestrel saw him fold it fast, hiding whatever was on it. "Tobias on duty today?"

"Should be."

"Give the boy my regards." Father said it lightly, but his eyes went to the window, toward the company house, and stayed there a moment too long. Twenty-two years he'd been diving for Atlas. Twenty-two years of pearls crossing that counter, and the number in the book was larger now than the day he'd signed. Kestrel had seen the entry once, when he was small, standing on a stool to see over the counter: *Dunmore, A. — principal 340, carried interest —* and after that a column of figures like a staircase going down into the dark. He hadn't known then that staircases could go down forever.

"I'll be back before noon," Kestrel said, and pocketed the tin.

The walk along the shore road took half an hour, past the drying racks and the chapel with its leaning spire and the row of company cottages where the Pell family's windows were still boarded from when they'd left, or been left. A pair of gulls fought over something in the tideline. A dog followed him for a hundred yards hoping for a habit that didn't exist. It was an ordinary morning in an ordinary town at the edge of an empire Kestrel had never seen and couldn't have found on a map, and he thought — he would think, later, with a bitterness he had no room for yet — that history was a thing that happened to other places. To capitals. To men in rooms with maps. Not here. Here there was only fog and fish and the slow arithmetic of debt, and the century could turn over like a sleeper without anyone in Merrow's Rest feeling the bed move.

The company house doors stood open against the warmth. Inside smelled of paper and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of old varnish, and behind the great counter Tobias Fenwick was already at work, sleeves rolled, copying entries from one ledger into another in a hand so fine it looked printed. He was Kestrel's age and had the pale, indoor look of a boy raised among shelves, and when he saw Kestrel he did the thing he always did: glanced past him at the inner office, then back, a flicker of conspiracy passing over his face like a cloud over water.

"The tea-colored one," Kestrel said, laying the pearls out on the counter. "Weigh it honest."

"I always weigh it honest. It's everyone else who—" He caught himself, lowered his voice. "Wait."

He waited. Tobias weighed each pearl, murmured numbers, made marks in a day-book, and the whole ritual had the quality of church: solemn, precise, entirely indifferent to the people performing it. Nine and a quarter ounces' worth. Kestrel watched the figure go into the book and felt the familiar small vertigo — that a thing born in the cold and the dark and the weight of the sea could end up as a pencil stroke in a warm room, its whole history reduced to a number that would be subtracted from another number he'd never be allowed to see the bottom of.

Then Tobias turned the ledger slightly — deliberately, casually, the way he'd been doing for three years now — and Kestrel looked where he wasn't supposed to look.

He was learning letters that way. It had started as a game: Tobias tapping a name with his pen, mouthing it across the counter. *Senn. Pell. Dunmore.* Now Kestrel could read the column headings, could pick out words like *principal* and *interest* and *arrears*, could read his own father's entry from memory, every figure of it. It was the closest thing to magic he had ever touched — that black marks on paper could hold a man's whole life, could say it, could fix it — and some mornings walking home he spelled street signs in his head, shop names, the faded letters on the chapel wall, drunk on it.

But today, following Tobias's pen down the page, he stopped at a line near the bottom. A name he knew. A neighbor. And next to it, in the margin, a small notation in red: *recovered by seizure of goods.*

He knew all the words separately. Together they meant something he had to sound out slowly, the way a child sounds out a sentence too heavy for it.

"Tobias," he said quietly. "What does that mean? There — the red."

Tobias looked. Something crossed his face — not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the look of a boy who knows where a door leads and wishes he'd never opened it. He closed the ledger. Behind him, from the inner office, came the scrape of the senior clerk's chair.

"Come back at closing," Tobias said. "Not now."

And Kestrel stood there with his empty tin and his full pockets of nothing, holding the strange new weight of a question he couldn't yet read, while out in the harbor, far past the point, a smudge of smoke was rising against the bright water that no one in Merrow's Rest had ever seen before.
