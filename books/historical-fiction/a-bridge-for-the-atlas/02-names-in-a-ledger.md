## 2. Names in a Ledger

The pearls came up out of the water in Kestrel's cupped palm like small cold moons, and by the time he'd walked the mile from the shallows to the company house they were warm as living things. That was the trick of them. Everything the sea gave you started cold and ended warm in your hand, and everything the Atlas Company gave you worked the other way around.

He counted them on the walk. Twelve good ones, three of them large enough to sit in the hollow of a thumbnail, and one — the last, found wedged in a crack of reef where his father would never have thought to look — so pale it was almost blue. He kept that one apart from the others, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth inside his shirt, because he had already decided what he was going to do with it and because decisions were easier to keep when you didn't speak them aloud.

The company house sat at the head of the harbor road like a fat man at the head of a table. Two stories of gray stone, slate roof, windows tall enough to stand in, and none of them open even in August because the men inside paid other men to be uncomfortable for them. The Atlas flag hung off the eave, damp and motionless. It was the same color as the fog most mornings, which Kestrel had always taken for a kind of honesty.

He stamped his feet on the stone step to shake the sand off, out of habit more than manners. Inside, the air smelled of ink and lamp oil and, underneath it, something sweet and expensive that came in crates marked with cities Kestrel couldn't pronounce. Sugar, maybe. Or tobacco. Things that grew where the sun was ordinary.

"Deliveries are after nine," said the clerk behind the counter without looking up.

"It's half past," Kestrel said.

The clerk looked up then. He had the face of a man perpetually interrupted mid-thought, which perhaps he was; the company house ran on paper the way the harbor ran on tide. "Dunmore's boy."

"Kestrel."

"I know your name." The clerk held out his hand, palm up, an open book. "The bag."

Kestrel set it on the counter. He watched the clerk untie it, spill the pearls into a shallow wooden tray lined with dark felt, and go at them with a loupe screwed into his eye socket like something grown there. One by one, into little compartments. The blue one last. The clerk held it a long time under the lamp.

"This is a fine stone," he said.

"My father found it." It wasn't quite a lie. His father had been in the water beside him when he'd pulled it from the reef crack. In the Dunmore family this counted as finding together.

"Mm." The clerk wrote something in a narrow book, then turned to a shelf behind him and took down another book — and this was the moment, though Kestrel didn't know it yet, that his whole life tilted on its side like a boat shipping water. Because this second book was enormous. It lay flat on the counter like a sleeping animal, bound in green leather gone brittle at the corners, its pages thick as sailcloth, and when the clerk opened it the sound it made was the sound a church door makes.

"What is that?" Kestrel asked, before he could stop himself. Questions were not encouraged here. Questions implied you didn't already know your place, and your place was written down somewhere, presumably.

"The ledger," the clerk said. Then, because Kestrel's face must have done something interesting, he added, with the first flicker of anything like warmth: "You've never seen it?"

"I've seen books."

"You've seen Bibles and cargo manifests. This is different." He turned the ledger around so it faced the counter's edge, and Kestrel leaned in, and the world got very quiet.

Columns. Columns and columns, ruled in faint red lines, and in them — names. Not stories, not pictures, just names, each one followed by numbers, and the numbers went on and on, page after page, year after year, in a dozen different hands, some of them beautiful and some of them cramped as bird tracks. *Abernathy, Colm. Ashford, Maren.* Names he knew. Men who dove the deep beds. Women who mended the company nets. Children who shucked oysters for a penny a bucket. Every name had a sum beside it, and the sums were all shaped the same way: a figure owed, crossed out and rewritten larger, crossed out and rewritten larger again, like a tide that only ever came in.

"There's my father's crew," Kestrel said. "There's the Hollises. There's—" He stopped.

Because there he was.

*KESTREL DUNMORE*, third line from the bottom of the current page, in ink so fresh it still shone slightly. Beside it, a number. He knew the number. He knew it the way you know the weight of a stone you carry every day — his father's original debt for the boat lease, plus interest compounded each quarter, minus nothing, ever, because the price the company paid for pearls always arrived just shy of what the debt required. But seeing it was different from knowing it. Knowing it lived in his chest, where it could be carried. Seeing it, it lived on a page in a locked room, in a building he did not own, in a handwriting that wasn't his, and the page did not care about him at all. The page would go on being true long after he was dead. Perhaps especially then.

"That's me," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened.

"That's everyone," the clerk said, not unkindly. He closed the ledger, and the sound was softer than the opening, like something being put back to sleep. "Company property, all of it. The names too, near enough."

Kestrel stood there a moment with his empty hands. The blue pearl was somewhere in the tray now, compartmented and catalogued, become inventory. "What happens to it?" he asked. "After you buy it."

"Goes to the city. Then further." The clerk made a vague gesture eastward, toward everything. "Some lady in a place I couldn't spell will wear it at her throat and never know a boy carried it warm in his shirt."

Kestrel nodded. He walked out onto the step and stood in the fog a while before going home, and the strange thing — the thing he turned over that night and many nights after — was that he hadn't felt robbed. He'd felt *read*. Like someone had opened him to a certain page, noted the figures, and moved on.

*

The letters started a week later, and they started with Tobias Quill.

Everyone in the harbor knew Tobias the way you know the moon — visible, distant, belonging to another system entirely. Clerk's son. Only child of Aldous Quill, who kept the great ledger and three lesser ledgers and whose signature appeared at the bottom of every debt in town. Tobias was fourteen, a year older than Kestrel, soft-handed, quick-eyed, with the pale indoor look of someone raised above the damp line. He wore shoes every day. He had once, famously, corrected his father's arithmetic in front of a company inspector, and instead of a beating he had received a smaller desk of his own.

Tobias came down to the shallows on a Tuesday, in those shoes, carrying them actually — barefoot in the wet sand, shoes hooked over two fingers — and stood watching Kestrel work the shallows until Kestrel surfaced and spat and said, "You're standing where the oysters live."

"So I am." Tobias crouched, examined the water around his ankles with scientific detachment. "How deep can you go?"

"Deeper than you can hold your breath."

"That's what I wanted to know." He said it without mockery, which somehow made it worse. "My father says the divers' children can't learn their letters because their heads are full of water pressure. I told him that was nonsense. He said prove it."

Kestrel wrung out his hair. "Prove it how?"

"Come to the counting room Thursday. After hours. My father plays cards at the Meridian until eleven." Tobias was already turning to go, then paused. "Bring something you want to read."

"I don't have anything I want to read. I can't read."

"No," Tobias agreed pleasantly. "That's the problem we're solving."

*

The counting room smelled of the same ink and lamp oil and expensive sweetness, but at night, emptied of clerks, it became a different country. Lamplight pooled on the counters. The shelves threw long shadows like ship rigging. And on the great table in the middle of the room, unlocked, unguarded, open — the ledger.

Tobias had laid out paper and pens and a pot of ink the color of midnight. "We start with names," he said. "Names first because you already know what they mean. The shapes come easier if the meaning is already in you."

He dipped a pen and wrote, in a careful round hand: *KESTREL.*

"That's you. Seven letters. Say them with me. K. E. S..."

It should have been tedious. It was not. It was, Kestrel thought later, the closest thing to diving he had ever done on dry land — that same slow descent into a place where the rules were different, where the light came from somewhere else, where you could drown if you weren't careful but oh, the things that lived down there. Letters were creatures. They had shapes like shells and like hooks, like the bones of fish. *S* was a current. *L* was a cliff edge. When Tobias wrote *BRIONY* and Kestrel sounded it out — slowly, syllable by syllable, feeling his sister assemble herself out of marks on a page — he laughed out loud, one sharp bark, and clapped his hand over his mouth because it felt like shouting in church.

"She's six letters," he said. "She's heavier than she looks."

"Everyone is," Tobias said. "In here." He patted the ledger.

And that was the trouble, or the beginning of it. Because the ledger was the only book in the room besides the copybook Tobias brought, and Tobias taught the way his father's trade had taught him: with examples from stock. So Kestrel learned *debt* before he learned *dream*. He learned *interest* and *arrears* and *default*. He learned to read the entries of men whose faces he knew — old Colm Abernathy, who'd saved him from a cramp in the deep beds when he was nine; the Hollis brothers, who sang off-key on the boats — and each entry was a small biography told entirely in sums. *Advanced: boots, 2s 6d. Advanced: flour, 1s 3d. Pearl sales credited against arrears: insufficient.* Insufficient. He learned that word early and hated it thoroughly, the way you hate a specific rock that has cut your foot.

"Read this one," Tobias said one night, tapping an entry near the front of the current volume, and Kestrel bent over it, moving his lips, decoding letter by letter, getting faster now, three weeks in, dangerously fast —

*Hollis, Edith. Widow. Debt assumed upon death of husband, per contract clause nine. Balance: £14 7s. Note: no dive rights. Repayment per net-mending schedule, est. completion: eleven years.*

Eleven years. She was fifty if she was a day. She would mend nets until she was sixty-one and die owing the company four months' flour.

Kestrel straightened up. The lamplight seemed thinner suddenly. "Does she know? The number of years?"

Tobias shrugged, and the shrug was the worst part — practiced, easy, the shrug of a boy already learning to carry things without setting them down. "It's in her contract. Everything's in the contract. Reading it is free." A pause. "Knowing how is the hard part, apparently."

"Apparently," Kestrel said, and heard how flat his voice had gone.

They worked on. But something had shifted in the room, some pressure change Kestrel felt in his ears the way he felt it before a storm. He looked at the ledger differently now. Before, it had been a wonder — the whole town folded between two covers, every life legible, legible meaning *knowable*, and knowing things had felt like power. Now he saw it for what the sea was: indifferent. It recorded you. It did not care for you. You could drown in it and it would make a note of the date.

Near the end of that night's lesson, Tobias turned back to the current page — to the fresh ink, the newest entries — and said, casually, "Find yourself again."

Kestrel ran his finger down the columns. *Dunmore, Kestrel.* Third line from the bottom. And beside it the number, the number that owned him, the number that had eaten twelve years of his father's lungs and was patient enough to eat his too.

He read it aloud. He read his own name aloud, and the sum, and the word *insufficient* at the end of the entry, and hearing it in his own voice did something that seeing it had not. It made it real in his mouth. It made it a fact he had agreed to.

"That's not my name," he heard himself say. "That's my name with a chain through it."

Tobias looked at him for a long moment. Lamplight moved on his face. "No," he said finally, quietly, and there was something new in his voice, something that hadn't been there in three weeks of lessons — not pity, exactly. Recognition. "But it's the name they'll answer to."

Neither of them said anything else for a while. Outside, fog pressed against the tall dark windows. Somewhere across the harbor a boat bell rang twice, lazy, unhurried, marking nothing.

Then Tobias cleared his throat and turned to a clean page in the copybook, and said, "Next week we do verbs. You'll want those. Verbs are how you tell the difference between what happened and what was written down."

Kestrel walked home along the harbor road with the letters swimming behind his eyes — twenty-six small animals, all of them tame now, all of them his. He was giddy with it. He was rich in a way no ledger recorded. He rehearsed the alphabet against the rhythm of his footsteps and thought: *I will read everything. Contracts, clauses, columns. I will read the fine print of the whole world.*

He did not yet know that in five months' time soldiers would carry this very book out of this very room under their arms, and that every name in it — his father's, Briony's, his own, chained through with its sum — would decide who boarded a ship and who stayed to breathe the smoke. He did not know that the first word he would ever truly need to forge would be *deceased*, written in his own hand, about himself.

He only knew that for the first time in his life, the world had opened one of its locked drawers and shown him what was inside.

He reached home past midnight, hung his wet clothes by the stove, and lay awake listening to his father breathe in the next room — that familiar laboring sound, each inhale a small argument won.

Tomorrow he would dive. Tomorrow the water would be honest with him, cold and total, asking only what he could pay in breath.

But tonight he whispered the letters into the dark, over and over, like a prayer in a language only he and one clerk's son could speak — and far out past the harbor bar, unseen in the fog, something with sails was already coming.
