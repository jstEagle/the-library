## 3. Smoke on the Horizon

Briony Dunmore saw the ship before anyone else did, which she would remember later, because seeing it first felt like being blamed for it.

She was on the eastern point with the mending basket, where the light came clean off the water and you could work a needle without squinting. The nets were spread over the gorse like the shed skins of enormous animals, and she was three knots into the worst of them—a haul from the Corran boat that had come up fouled with something sharp, glass maybe, or a scrap of iron—and her fingers were moving without her. That was the trick of mending. You let the hands go stupid and the mind went wherever it liked. Kestrel dove the way other boys ran downhill; Briony mended the same way, half asleep, wholly elsewhere.

Elsewhere, this morning, was the horizon.

There was smoke on it. Not the low gray smear of a steamer working the coastal run—that came and went twice a week and nobody looked up—but a standing column, black as ink dropped in milk, too far off to show the hull and too steady to be weather. She watched it for the length of a knot. It didn't move. Then it moved, very slightly, southward, the way something moves when it is not drifting but steering.

She put down the net. Her hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind coming off the water, and she sat with that coldness for a moment, trying to name it, the way you try to name a smell. Dread, she decided, arrived whole, before understanding, before reason could get its boots on. You felt it in the body first. The body always knew before the mind agreed to.

The warship would not come into the harbor until evening. Briony knew this because she watched it all day, off and on, between knots—she counted eleven knots finished and hated every one of them—and by late afternoon it had grown from smoke to shape, a dark wedge with a bone in its teeth, dragging the sea white behind it. By the time she carried the mended nets down the cliff path, half the town was already at the seawall, and the word going mouth to mouth along it was *gunboat*, said in the tone people used for words they'd only ever read.

* * *

At home their father had the good lamp lit and the company ledger's payment stubs laid out on the table like cards he meant to play. He did this at the end of every month: tallied what they owed against what Kestrel had brought up from the beds, arranged the figures, found them wanting, and slept anyway. It was a ritual of order. Aubrey Dunmore believed in accounts the way other men believed in saints—not that the accounts loved you back, but that keeping them faithfully kept you honest, and honesty was a kind of armor.

"The *Corriemuir*," Briony said. She set the basket down. "A gunboat. It came around the point at four o'clock and anchored in the roads."

"I heard." He didn't look up from the stubs.

"Everyone was at the seawall. Mr. Corran says the army's been taking towns down the coast. Supply ports, he said. They requisition the harbor and the storehouses and—" She stopped, because her father had put one finger on a stub, gently, the way you quiet a dog.

"Mr. Corran," he said, "once told me the herring would come back in 'ninety-three."

The lamp hissed. Upstairs, through the floorboards, Briony could hear Kestrel moving, washing the salt off, the small domestic thunder of him. Her father straightened the stubs so their edges matched. He had hands like old rope but he handled paper delicately, always, as if paper were the fragile thing and the world were tough.

"History doesn't come here, Briony-girl." He said it kindly, the way you correct a child about the moon. "Look at it." He nodded toward the window, toward the fog lying against the glass like a damp cloth. "No rail line. No deep-water quay worth the name. A harbor you can walk across at low tide if you don't mind mud to the knee. There are six hundred people here and half of them owe the Atlas Company more than they'll earn in ten years, which means the Company will never let anything happen to us, because we're collateral. Armies don't requisition collateral. They requisition assets. We're a debt."

He said it with such certainty, such a tidy craftsman's pleasure in the shape of it, that she almost believed him. That was the thing about her father nobody outside the house ever saw: not the hardness but the comfort he took in systems, in the belief that the world was legible if you kept your figures straight. He'd taught Kestrel the dive tables the same way. Depth and time, time and depth. Nothing in the water is arbitrary. Follow the arithmetic and the arithmetic brings you home.

"And if it anchors anyway?" Briony said.

"It's surveying." He gathered the stubs, squared them, tied them with the string. "They survey every few years. Charts wear out. Come morning there'll be fog and it'll be gone, and you'll have given yourself a day of fright for nothing, and fright costs more than most things, girl. Remember that. Fright is the only expense that never earns back."

She remembered that, later. She remembered it exactly, in his voice, while everything he'd built the sentence out of came apart around them.

* * *

The fog did come in the morning, and the gunboat did not leave.

It sat in the roads like a fact, gray-black, its outline softening and reforming as the fog worked, and the town adjusted itself around it the way water adjusts around a stone. The fish crews went out. The Atlas Company house opened at its usual hour and the clerk's boy swept the steps. Mrs. Tarrant sold bread. Somewhere behind the fog-wall the ship's bell rang the half hours, a sound none of them had heard before and all of them began, immediately and without deciding to, to set their days by.

Briony went to the seawall with the other girls from the net-shed and they stood in a row like laundry, watching the shape breathe.

"It's the *Corriemuir*," said Enid Mowbray, who read the shipping news. "Three guns. They re-coaled at Harwick on Tuesday—my cousin saw them take on."

"Taking on coal for what?" said Nettie. "There's nothing out there but water and us."

"There's a war," Enid said, with the relish of someone handing around bad news wrapped in ribbon. "Down the coast. You'd know if your family read anything."

"We read."

"You read ledgers."

Briony let them argue. She was watching the ship's boats. Two of them had been lowered sometime in the night or early morning—she could see the davits swung out—and now one came pulling through the fog toward the harbor mouth, oars lifting and falling in perfect unison, and sitting in the stern was an officer whose uniform, even at that distance, even in that light, was a color Briony had never once seen in her town. Not navy blue. Not merchant black. Something grayer, harder, a color that had no work here and therefore no meaning, and the absence of meaning frightened her more than the guns would have.

The boat came in. The officer stepped onto our stones—the stones Kestrel had played on, the stones their mother had been carried across to die on—and he took out a notebook, and he wrote something in it, and then he walked, unhurried, past the seawall, past the shed, past the Company house with its brass fittings and its swept steps, looking at everything and owning it with his eyes, and the whole town watched him do it and no one said a word.

"He's counting," Nettie whispered.

He was. Storehouses first. Then the slipway. Then the net-drying racks, of which he wrote down the number, which was thirty-one, which Briony knew without counting because she had mended on those racks since she was seven and could have named the nets hanging on each one. He did not look at the people. That was what she noticed afterward, telling Kestrel about it with her hands shaking around a cup of tea: the man never once looked at a person. People, whatever he'd been sent to count, were not on the list.

* * *

That evening their father listened to all of it—the boats, the notebook, the counting—and then he did the worst thing he could have done, which was nothing new. He explained it.

"Hydrographic survey," he said. "Soundings. They'll want the depths marked for the charts, same as I said. A gunboat doesn't anchor in five fathoms, Briony. Whatever they're doing, it isn't for us, and what isn't for us can't touch us. That's the whole of it." He was repairing the handle of Kestrel's second-best knife with waxed twine, wrapping it turn over turn, and his hands were steady, and steadiness in her father had always been a kind of sermon. "The Company holds paper on every roof in this harbor. Paper is stronger than any gunboat. Men with guns come and go. Paper stays."

Kestrel sat in the corner sharpening his diving knife on the whetstone, slow strokes, saying nothing. He'd been quiet since the officer walked through—quiet in a way Briony recognized and distrusted, because it was the quiet he went into before he did something. When their father finished the handle he held the knife up to the lamplight, testing the wrap, and said, almost to himself:

"A man keeps what he can account for. That's all safety is, girl. Arithmetic. Keep your numbers true and the world stays in its column."

Briony thought of the officer's notebook, and how he hadn't needed to look at a single face.

"Whose numbers?" she asked.

Her father looked at her over the knife, and for just a moment—one breath, no more—something moved behind his certainty, some small animal sensing weather. Then he put the knife down beside the stubs, edges aligned, everything in its column, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

The rumor came on the third day, and it came the way everything came to the harbor: wet, salt-damaged, and out of a fisherman's mouth.

Corran's boat had spoken a steamer off the point, and the steamer's mate had come from down the coast, from Tarrowmouth, which had been taken for a supply port in September. Taken: that was the word the mate used, and it traveled through the harbor faster than the fish ever had. Requisitioned, the mate said. Harbor, stores, records. Every trading house made to open its books to the army. Every indentured soul registered. And after Tarrowmouth, the mate said, the army wanted a deeper port, with shallows for divers and room for a depot, and there was a list, and—

He didn't say their harbor was on the list. That was the cruelty of it. He simply let the list hang there, unfinished, and every listener filled in the last name themselves.

By nightfall the town had split cleanly along a seam nobody had known was there. Half of them said it couldn't happen—the Company wouldn't allow it, the Admiralty wouldn't dare, the war was down the coast and would stay down the coast. The other half were already counting what they owned and what could be carried.

Their father belonged to the first half with his whole heart.

"They took Tarrowmouth," Briony said at supper, "because Tarrowmouth has a breakwater and we have a mudflat—"

"Tarrowmouth," her father said, "had a magistrate who couldn't keep his mouth shut and a harbor master who owed money to the wrong men. That's why Tarrowmouth happened. Weakness invites. We are not weak—we are *indebted*, which is entirely different. Debt is a chain that binds both ends, Briony. The Company will fight this harder than we ever could, because if the army takes this port, the army takes the Company's own ledger, and there is not a clerk in that fine house who will permit that to happen." He tapped the table with one rope-knuckled finger, once, like a gavel. "Nobody is coming to take anything from us. Our names are in a book that belongs to the Atlas Trading Company, and books are the one cargo in this world that armies find too heavy to lift."

Kestrel put down his fork. Very quietly, not looking at anyone, he said: "You've seen the ledger, Da?"

"I've signed it often enough."

"Then you know it isn't heavy at all." He picked the fork back up. "It's one book. I could carry it under one arm."

The silence that followed lasted three full beats of the ship's bell out in the roads. Their father stared at his son the way a man stares at a crack in a foundation—as if by staring he could make the crack be a shadow. Briony looked from one to the other and felt the dread from the eastern point come back, colder now, closer, wearing a shape she finally understood: not fear of the ship in the roads. Fear of this. Of the two of them at one table believing opposite things, her father in the paper and her brother in the carrying of it, and only one of them could be right, and either answer cost them everything.

"Fog tomorrow," her father said at last, standing, gathering his plate. "It'll burn off by noon. So will all this talk."

But he checked the stubs twice that night, and he put the string on them tighter than usual, and Briony, awake in the loft, heard him do it.

* * *

On the fourth morning the fog burned off by noon, exactly as promised, and the gunboat was still there, and it had been joined by a second ship.

This one flew no colors she recognized. It anchored farther out, fat-hulled and slow, a storeship, and all afternoon lighters went back and forth between the two vessels like ducks in a row, and the town stopped pretending and simply watched. The net-shed worked in near silence. At the seawall, men who had argued for three days now stood shoulder to shoulder without speaking, which was worse.

Toward dusk Briony climbed the eastern point again, alone, because she wanted to see it whole before dark and because she could not bear the sound of the harbor talking. From up there the two ships sat black on copper water, patient as headstones, and the town below her was small enough to cup in two hands: forty roofs, one company house, one drying yard, one family with its lamps lit and its stubs tied and its faith in arithmetic.

She thought about her father's word. *Collateral.* He had said it like a shield and she heard it now like a sentence. If the Company's ledger was what protected them—if their safety lived inside a book—then the book was the battlefield, wasn't it? Not the harbor. Not the mudflat. The book. And armies, her father notwithstanding, had been burning and stealing books for as long as there had been either.

The ship's bell came across the water, marking the hour, and beneath it, fainter, she heard the Company house door close for the night.

She stayed on the point until the light went. In the morning, she learned, soldiers would come ashore in force, and by noon the doors of the Atlas Trading Company would stand open to men in that colorless gray, and the great ledger with every diver's name in it—all the names, all the debts, all the arithmetic that kept her family safe—would be carried out under a soldier's arm, light as a bird, into a wagon, and gone.

She didn't know that yet. But standing in the last light with the smoke of two ships against a darkening sky, Briony Dunmore understood one thing ahead of everyone else, the way she had seen the smoke first: history was not coming to their harbor.

It was already there.
