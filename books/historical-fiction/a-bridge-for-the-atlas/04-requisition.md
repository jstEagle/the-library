## 4. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt obscene. Wars, Kestrel had always assumed, arrived on days with weight to them—Sundays, maybe, or the anniversary of some battle old men still argued about. Not a Tuesday. Not a morning of gray drizzle and gulls arguing over fish guts at the tideline, the harbor doing exactly what it always did, indifferent and exact.

He was on the company steps before dawn, as he was every delivery day, a basket of shell pearls wrapped in oilcloth against his hip. He'd counted them twice in the dark. Forty-one. Enough to knock two months off his father's debt if the clerk's scale ran honest, which it never did, but which ran honest enough that you didn't dare say otherwise.

The company house sat above the harbor like a fat man at a table, three stories of gray stone with the Atlas crest carved over the door—a ship and an anchor, wreathed in something that might have been wheat or might have been rope. Kestrel had walked past it ten thousand times. It had never once occurred to him to be afraid of it.

There were soldiers on the steps.

Not one soldier, not two, but eight of them, in the dark blue of the colonial army, rifles slung, faces set in the particular blankness of men doing a job they'd been told was routine. A supply wagon stood in the lane behind them, its canvas cover lashed down, and two more soldiers were carrying things out of the doors—things Kestrel recognized with a strange, slow horror.

Files. Ledgers. The great brass cashbox from the counting room. A clerk's stool, absurdly, carried out by a corporal who held it like a dead animal.

Kestrel stopped halfway up the lane, the basket suddenly heavy.

The oldest soldier on the steps—sergeant's stripes, gray at the temples, the bored eyes of a man who had done this in four towns already this year—looked at him without seeing him. That was the first vertigo. Kestrel Dunmore had spent seventeen years learning how not to be seen, and he had never once considered what it would feel like when it worked too well.

"Company business is closed," the sergeant said. "Come back when there's a flag up."

"There's no flag," Kestrel said.

"No," the sergeant agreed. "There isn't."

* * *

By noon the whole town knew, because the whole town had come to look, pressed along the seawall and the lanes in the particular silence of people watching their own lives get carried out a door. The Atlas clerks stood in a huddle by the warehouse, Tobias among them, his father Mr. Pellam's hand clamped on his shoulder—not affectionately, Kestrel thought. Like holding down a lid.

Tobias caught his eye across the crowd. Fifteen years of shared secrets lived in that glance: the letters traced in spilled sugar on the counting-room floor, the sums whispered over ledger entries while Pellam took his midday brandy. *Later*, the look said. Or maybe it said *I don't know either.* With Tobias you could never be sure which.

A captain came out of the company house at half past twelve. Kestrel learned his name within minutes, the way a town learns anything: Verrin. Captain Elias Verrin, of the 3rd Colonial Supply Regiment, a tall man with a neat mustache and the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it.

He read from a paper. The paper had seals on it, red wax stamped so hard the wax had cracked.

"By order of the Provisional Military Administration," he said, "the township of Greyharbor and all its works are hereby requisitioned for the service of the blockade. All commercial records of the Atlas Trading Company are seized as instruments of state accounting." He lowered the paper. His eyes went across the crowd the way a man counts sheep. "All persons indentured or contracted to the Atlas Trading Company will present themselves for registration at the company house between tomorrow and the seventh day. Registration is mandatory. Persons failing to register will be classified as absconded."

He said *absconded* the way other men said *dead*. Maybe it was the same thing here. Nobody asked. Nobody ever asked, in these moments; the questions came later, alone, in the dark, where they could do the most damage.

"What does registration mean?" someone called out—an old voice, Mother Anselm, who had buried two husbands and feared nothing living. "What does it mean for us?"

"It means your status is confirmed and recorded," Verrin said pleasantly. "It means you exist."

Kestrel felt those words go through the crowd like cold water through cloth. *You exist.* As if existence were a thing you could fail to confirm. As if forty years of hauling nets and diving beds and dying of the lung-rot in company houses could be erased by the absence of a signature in a book.

His father wasn't in the crowd. His father was at home, mending a cracked air-hose with tarred twine, and when Kestrel got back and told him, Father didn't even look up.

"They've taken the papers," Kestrel said. "The ledger. Everything."

"Mm." The twine pulled tight. "Army'll give it back after. They always do."

"How do you know?"

"Because armies don't dive, boy. They need us. Papers are just paper." But his hands slowed on the twine, just slightly, just enough that Kestrel saw it, and understood that his father was performing calm the way Kestrel himself performed invisibility—skillfully, and for an audience of one, and not entirely convincingly.

* * *

Registration day. The line outside the company house began forming before light, families standing in the drizzle with their documents bundled in oilcloth like relics: indenture contracts, debt schedules, birth records, the marriage lines of grandparents, everything the Atlas house had ever made them sign. Kestrel watched them from the mouth of the lane and thought about what it meant that every family owned its papers the way a drowning man owns his debts—clutched, cherished, useless.

Inside, they said, the process was simple. You gave your name. A soldier found it in the ledger. A clerk—Pellam and two others, working under a bayonet's polite supervision—confirmed the entry. Then you signed a new page, a requisition annex, transferring your indenture from the Atlas Trading Company to the Provisional Military Administration. Same words. Different master. The ink hadn't even changed color.

"You should go early," Tobias said. He'd found Kestrel behind the salt sheds, where they'd met a hundred times to practice letters on slate. He looked wrong—washed-out, wrung, like laundry left out in rain. "First day. Before they get tired and start making mistakes."

"What kind of mistakes?"

"The kind where a name isn't found in the book." Tobias wouldn't look at him. "If they can't find your entry, they can't confirm your status. Unconfirmed means unregistered. Unregistered means—" He stopped.

"Absconded."

"Runaway," Tobias said. "They're using both words now."

Kestrel looked at his friend and saw something new under the familiar face: fear wearing a clerk's collar. "You've seen the ledger since they took it."

"I've been made to carry it." Bitterness, sudden and sharp. "My father's been made to open it. They put a soldier at the table beside him and told him to turn pages. Eleven hundred entries, Kes. Divers, haulers, net-menders, widows on pension debt. Every name this town has, in one book, and now the book belongs to a man whose job is to feed a war." He finally looked up. "You know what I couldn't stop thinking? All those years teaching you your letters out of it. I thought I was giving you something. Turns out I was just showing you where you lived."

"Tobias—"

"My father says register fast and keep your head down and it'll pass. He says the army needs the harbor working and paperwork is paperwork." Tobias's laugh was awful, small and dry. "But he checked our own entry twice last night. Twice. After twenty years of writing it, he checked it."

* * *

Kestrel registered on the second day, with Briony beside him, because Father refused to leave the air-hose unfinished and Mother was abed with her hands swollen from the nets. The line moved in silence. Nobody wept; nobody shouted. That was the worst part, Kestrel thought—the competence of it, the way terror had been processed into queueing.

At the table, a young lieutenant ran his finger down a column of names.

"Dunmore," the lieutenant said. "Kestrel. Son of Aldous Dunmore, diver, bed-debt outstanding, sum of..." He read the number aloud, flat, and Kestrel felt the old shame rise and then something stranger underneath it. For eleven years that number had lived only in the Atlas house, a private wound dressed daily by Pellam's pen. Now it was being read aloud in front of soldiers. Now it belonged to the army too.

"That's me," Kestrel said.

"Sign." The annex page lay open. At the top, in printed type: *TRANSFER OF INDENTURED LABOR TO THE PROVISIONAL MILITARY ADMINISTRATION.* At the bottom, a line waiting for his mark.

Briony signed before he did—her name small and furious, pressed so hard into the paper the nib nearly tore it. She was fourteen and she wrote a better hand than most of the clerks, because Kestrel had taught her what Tobias had taught him, nights, by lamplight, on slate. Their parents didn't know. It had seemed like a secret worth keeping. Standing at that table, watching her name go into a government book, Kestrel wondered for the first time whether literacy was a gift or a door you couldn't shut again.

He signed. The lieutenant blotted the page, moved his finger to the next entry, and said, "Next," without looking up.

That was it. That was the whole ceremony. Eleven years of his family's life, transferred in the time it takes to swallow.

Outside, the drizzle had stopped and the light came down hard off the water. Briony walked beside him, saying nothing, until they reached the seawall, and then she said it:

"He didn't check anything. Did you see? He didn't even read the entry. Just the name and the sum."

"He knows the book's true. Why would he check?"

"Because books can be wrong." She turned and faced him, and her eyes had a shine he didn't like, fury or tears or both. "Kes. Think about it. Our whole lives—whether we eat, whether Father dives the deep beds, whether we're runaways—they're all written in that one book, and nobody checks the book. The soldiers don't. We can't. It's just... paper that everyone agrees to die by."

Kestrel looked out at the harbor. The requisition wagon was gone; in its place, soldiers were stacking crates on the warehouse quay, and a signal party was raising a military pennant over the company house where the Atlas crest used to catch the light. Somewhere beyond the horizon, a war was happening that none of them had asked for, and it had come here not as fire or blood but as a man reading names aloud from a ledger.

"It's not the guns," he heard himself say. It came out slow, like a discovery being made in real time. "Everyone thinks it's the guns. It's not. It's the book. Take the rifles away and the soldiers are just men in the rain. Take the book away and they've got nothing on any of us. The book is the army, Bri. The rest is decoration."

Briony stared at him. "When did you start talking like that?"

"Since I watched a lieutenant decide who exists in about four seconds."

She laughed—one short, startled bark—and then pressed her hand over her mouth like she'd sworn in church. But she was scared. He could tell. So was he. And beneath the fear, in a place he didn't want to look at yet, something else was stirring: the same feeling he'd gotten the first time Tobias traced the alphabet in sugar, wonder at what letters could do, except inverted now, black instead of white. Letters could do this. Letters had done this. A pen had conquered the town faster than any rifle could have.

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep. He lay in the loft listening to the house—the creak of his father turning over, the sea working the pilings below—and tried to make the arithmetic come out right.

Eleven hundred names in the ledger. One copy, seized, now sleeping under guard in the company house. If the book was the army, then the book was also the town—all of it, every debt and birth and death since before Kestrel was born. Whoever held the book held them. And whoever could change the book—

He stopped. Lay very still. The thought had arrived whole, fully armed, the way dangerous thoughts do, pretending they'd always lived there.

*Whoever could change the book.*

Below, his father coughed in his sleep, that wet rattle the deep beds gave men as a parting gift. The debt was in the book. The conscription lists would come from the book. Who dove, who hauled, who got fed, who got called *absconded*—all of it, every fact of their lives, flowing out of that one bound volume of paper in a guarded room above the harbor.

Kestrel stared at the rafters and understood that he had spent his whole life believing history happened somewhere else—in capitals, in parliaments, in wars with names—and that it had in fact been happening here all along, quietly, in a counting room, one ledger entry at a time. He had simply never been allowed to read it.

Now he could read. Now he knew exactly which book it was.

And three days later, when Tobias climbed the bluff path to the salt sheds with his collar up and his eyes wild, and said in a voice Kestrel had never heard him use—low, urgent, grown-up—"I need to show you something, and you have to swear you'll forget it the moment you see it"—Kestrel followed him down toward the occupied company house without asking a single question.

He already knew what the answer would be. There was only one thing in this town worth swearing to forget.
