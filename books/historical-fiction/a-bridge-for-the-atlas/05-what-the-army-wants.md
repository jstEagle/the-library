## 5. What the Army Wants

They posted the notice on the company house door at dawn, and by seven the whole harbor had pressed itself against the building to read a document nobody could read.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the crowd with his father and watched men he'd known all his life crane at a sheet of paper as if it were a drowning swimmer. Old Marsh, who had thirty years of shell scars on his palms. The Pell brothers, who fished the north shoal and hadn't spoken to each other since a woman left between them. Even Mother had come down from the nets, which she never did, her hands still smelling of tar.

The paper was pinned square and level, and that was the first wrong thing about it. Atlas Company notices went up crooked and came down in the rain. This one had been affixed with four brass tacks by someone who believed in what he was doing.

"It's got a crest," Briony said. She'd come too, though Kestrel couldn't remember asking her. Fifteen years old and she moved through crowds like water through rocks, arriving beside him without seeming to cross the space between. "A real one. Stamped."

"Can you make anything out?"

"Some of it." She squinted. Tobias had been teaching them both, though Briony pretended otherwise and Kestrel let her pretend, because a girl who could read was a girl who asked questions in this town, and there were fewer safe places for those than for girls who couldn't. "It says—here—" Her finger hovered over the middle of the page. "*All able-bodied male persons registered under indenture or debt.*"

The words went into Kestrel like cold water into an ear. *Persons.* Not divers. Not men of the harbor. Persons. As if they'd been translated into another language overnight and this was what they became: persons, able-bodied, registered.

Father said nothing at all. That was the second wrong thing. Dunmore was a man who had opinions on weather, bait, company rates, the moral character of eels. He stood looking at the paper with his arms folded and his jaw working, and when Kestrel touched his elbow he flinched as if the touch had been a blow.

"There'll be an announcement," someone said behind them. "The officer's coming out."

* * *

Captain Verrin came out onto the steps of the company house at eight o'clock precisely, and Kestrel understood within ten seconds of seeing him that everything his father believed about history had been true only so long as no one like this existed.

Verrin was not large. That surprised him. The soldiers had arrived with their boots and their bayonets and their appetite for requisitioning, and Kestrel had imagined the man at the top of it all as something oversized—a bull of a officer, loud, red-faced. Instead Verrin was slight, gray-templed, neat as a ledger line, with the pale indoor complexion of a clerk and a voice that never once rose above the volume of a man reading accounts aloud. He wore his sword the way Tobias wore his ink-stained cuffs: as a fact about himself he no longer noticed.

He didn't shout. That was the thing Kestrel kept circling back to afterward, the thing that made his stomach turn even days later. Verrin didn't need to shout. He stood on the steps with three soldiers at parade rest and perhaps four hundred townspeople packed into the cobbled square, and he spoke quietly, and everyone listened because the quiet itself was the threat. You raise your voice when you're unsure of being heard. Verrin had never been unsure of anything in his life.

"People of this port," he began, and paused, and consulted a card in his gloved hand, and Kestrel felt something twist in his chest when he understood that the man was checking how to pronounce the name of their own town. "Your harbor is to be developed as a station for the northern blockade. This will bring employment. It will also bring requirements."

He let the word sit there. *Requirements.* A clean little coffin of a word.

"The naval operation requires divers to inspect hulls, clear obstructions, and recover material from the channel depths. It requires laborers for the works at the mole. These positions will be filled from among registered indentured men of working age, beginning tomorrow at this hour. Men will present themselves at the company house with their registration numbers. Those who fail to present will be recorded as absconded, and treated accordingly."

Treated accordingly. Four more words with nothing inside them but weight.

Kestrel's father unfolded his arms. "What depth?" he called out, before anyone could stop him, because Dunmore had never once in his life failed to ask a question about water. "The channel beds run forty fathoms past the point. Our men dive shell, not salvage. There's a difference, Captain, and it's measured in lives."

For a moment the square was absolutely silent. Somewhere a gull screamed and quit.

Captain Verrin looked at Father the way you look at a mark on a page that needs correcting.

"The depths are a matter for the Crown," he said mildly. "As are the men. Your registration status will be reviewed with the others, Mr.—" he glanced at his card again, and Kestrel felt the crowd around him contract, every person waiting to see if their name would be the next one swallowed—"Dunmore. Yes. Present yourself tomorrow."

It wasn't anger. That was the terrible part. There was no anger anywhere in the man, no cruelty he was enjoying, no malice he was feeding. He was doing arithmetic. Father had objected, so Father had become a number that needed watching, and now the number would be processed first.

The soldiers stepped down off the porch and began moving through the crowd—not pushing, just walking, and somehow the crowd made room for them the way flesh makes room for a knife—and the assembly ended the way weather ends, not with a crack but with everyone slowly realizing it had already happened.

* * *

That night the house felt smaller.

They ate salt fish and barley and nobody tasted any of it. Mother kept reaching across the table to move things—Father's cup, the lamp, the bread—small adjustments, arranging the world as if arrangement could hold it together. Briony pushed her food into patterns and ate nothing. And Father talked, which was worst of all, because Dunmore talking was Dunmore refusing to think.

"It's work, is all it is," he said. "Hard work, deep work, but we've done hard and we've done deep. They want hulls inspected, fine. I've inspected hulls since before that man could grow a beard. You go down, you look, you come up. The army pays better than the Company ever did, if the rumors hold."

"The rumors don't hold," Briony said. "Ask the fishing crews from Marrow Point. They were conscripted in the spring. Nobody's seen wages. Nobody's seen the crews."

"That's rumor itself, girl."

"It's all anybody has, and you're choosing not to believe it because believing it means doing something."

Mother said, "Briony," low, a warning, and Briony shut her mouth, and Kestrel sat in the middle of it feeling something shift in him that he couldn't yet name.

Here was the thing he had built his whole life on, the load-bearing wall of every day he'd lived: obey the terms. Dive your quota. Pay your installments. Sign your name—or make your mark—where the clerk points. His father had taught it to him at nine years old, in the boat, in the gray light: *The water doesn't care about you, boy, and the Company does, in its way, and that care is worth more than love. Stay inside the lines and the lines will keep you.* Every diver in the quarter lived by some version of that creed. It was why they endured. It was why they survived winters and debts and the sea itself.

And tonight, in the space of one quiet man's ten-minute speech, Kestrel had watched the creed die on his father's face and seen his father refuse to bury it.

Because Father had stayed inside every line ever drawn around him. Forty years of lines. And the reward for it was a captain consulting a card to find his name, and a note taken, and *present yourself tomorrow*—not as a summons, even. As a processing instruction. Obedience hadn't bought safety. It had bought efficiency. It meant the army knew exactly where to find him, exactly what he was worth, exactly how many fathoms of him remained to be spent.

"They took our names," Kestrel said suddenly.

Everyone looked at him.

"The ledger," he said. "When the soldiers came, they took the ledger. All the registrations, all the debts—all of it's in that building now, under army seal. Father's name is in that book. Mine. Yours." He looked at Briony. "Everything they decide about us tomorrow, they decide by looking us up. Like stock."

"That book's been hanging over us my whole life," Father said. "Company property or army property, what difference? A debt's a debt."

"No." Kestrel heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "It's different now, and you know it's different. The Company owned our work. This man owns our *persons*. Able-bodied male persons. We're not labor to him, we're inventory, and tomorrow he starts counting."

The lamp hissed. Mother had gone very still, her hands flat on the table as if steadying it, or herself.

"You've been spending time with that clerk's boy," Father said slowly. "Tobias."

"Yes."

"He fill your head with this?"

"Tobias reads," Kestrel said. "I read. That's all the filling that's needed. The trouble isn't in my head, Father. It's on the steps of the company house, wearing gloves."

Father looked at him for a long moment, and Kestrel saw something he had never seen in his father's face before, not once in seventeen years: fear that had given up pretending to be caution. Then Dunmore looked down at his plate and said, "Eat your fish," and the conversation was over, and the silence it left behind was the loudest sound in the house.

* * *

Kestrel didn't sleep. Near midnight he gave it up and went out and walked down to the water, because the water was the only place his thoughts ever organized themselves.

The fog had come in off the shoals, thick and soft, and the harbor lights stood inside it like coins at the bottom of a bowl. Soldiers' lanterns burned at the company house—two on the porch, one moving behind the shuttered windows where the records were kept. Guarding paper. Four armed men guarding paper, while the boats rode unguarded at their moorings and the pearls lay in the strongroom and the whole town slept under a seal of wax.

He thought about the ledger. He'd seen it twice in his life, both times through the clerks' door Tobias was never supposed to leave open. A great bound thing, calf-hide corners, columns ruled in red, and somewhere in it a line that was him: *Dunmore, Kestrel. Diver, class three. Debt outstanding:* and then a sum that had followed him since birth. He remembered the vertigo of it, the first time—the wonder of letters curdling, entry by entry, into the understanding that he was ink. That somewhere in a locked room there was a version of him that fit on one line, and that the line was the true one, and the boy standing at the door dripping seawater was only its representative.

Tomorrow the army would open that book and start subtracting.

His hands had closed into fists without his noticing. He made himself breathe, made the fists open. Rage was useless; rage was for men who had somewhere to put it. What he felt had nowhere to go. It sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed, and the fog closed over the harbor lights one by one, and he thought: *I am sixteen years old and I have never once decided anything that mattered.*

Then he thought about Tobias, who copied pages, who had access, whose handwriting could pass for his father's.

He didn't finish the thought. He turned it face-down in his mind like a card not yet played, and went home, and lay in the dark listening to his father's slow breathing in the next room, counting the breaths the way you count the rise and fall of a diver's float, waiting for it to surface, surfacing, surfacing.

* * *

They came at nine o'clock, two hours early, and they came for him in front of God and the whole street.

Not Verrin—two privates and a sergeant with a list, marching up the lane through the morning gulls and the fish-smell and the children playing, ordinary as postmen. Father was mending a float rope on the step, and he stood up when their shadows reached him, and Kestrel saw him begin, automatically, to reach for his cap—the courtesy of an obedient man—and stop halfway, hand hovering, because he no longer knew what he was being courteous toward.

"Dunmore. Registered diver, class three." The sergeant didn't look up from his list. "You're wanted for assessment."

"I'm early," Father said. "The notice said noon."

"The notice says what the notice says. You're wanted now. Bring any certificates of competency."

Certificates. Kestrel's stomach dropped. Assessment meant classification, and classification meant the deep beds—because Father was right about one thing, the thing he'd shouted across the square: there was a difference between shell diving and salvage work, and the difference was measured in lives, and a class-three certificate with forty years of deep-water endorsements was exactly the sort of line item a blockade operation would reach for first.

Mother came out with her sleeves still wet to the elbow. "He'll eat first. He'll take an hour."

"He'll take now, madam."

Briony was in the doorway, white-faced, saying nothing, and Kestrel realized with a lurch that she had learned already—two days into the occupation—when speech was dangerous.

Father straightened his collar. It was unbearable, that: he tidied himself for them. He got his coat, his good coat, the one for funerals and the Company audit, and put it on, and Kestrel watched his father perform dignity for an audience that had not asked for it and would not record it. At the gate he stopped. Turned back.

"Mind the quota," he said to Kestrel, because that was the language between them, the old language, the lines-that-keep-you language. "The season won't wait for the army, and the debt won't either."

And then, quieter, with his hand briefly heavy on Kestrel's shoulder, gripping harder than the words admitted: "Look after your mother. Look after your sister."

"Come home," Kestrel said. It came out younger than he meant it to. "Just—come home after. Whatever they say. Come home."

Father almost smiled. "Water doesn't scare me, son. It never has."

No, Kestrel wanted to say. The water was never the thing.

He watched the soldiers walk his father up the lane, past the neighbors standing at their doors, past the children gone quiet by the breakwater, until the fog took them the way it took the harbor lights, gradually and then completely. The sergeant's list had other names on it. Down the coast he could hear, faintly, another knock, and another.

Kestrel stood in the lane a long time. The rage was still there, the swallowed stone of it—but beneath the rage, colder and far more frightening, something else had cracked loose overnight and was now shifting, settling, rearranging itself into a new shape. He examined it the way he'd examine an unfamiliar object brought up from the bottom.

It was this: obedience had been his whole architecture, and he had watched it fail in under a minute, politely, without raising its voice. Which meant the rules were not rules. They were tools, and tools belonged to whoever held them.

There was a boy across town who held a pen.

Kestrel turned toward the company house, and began to walk.
