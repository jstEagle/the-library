## 6. Tobias's Bargain

The army had turned the counting room into a map room. Tobias Verrin—no relation to the captain, though he'd stopped correcting anyone weeks ago—copied names into a ledger of his own now, in a hand that had grown faster and uglier since the soldiers came. He sat at his father's old desk by the window because it was the only desk that hadn't been requisitioned for maps, and through the salt-fogged glass he could see the harbor, the gray water, the black hulls at anchor, and beyond them the low shape of the divers' quarter where Kestrel Dunmore lived.

He was supposed to be copying page forty-seven. He was, instead, reading page forty-six again.

*DUNMORE, KESTREL. Age 16. Indentured to Atlas Trading Co., harbor lease 7. Outstanding balance: £11 8s. Status: eligible for conscription, able-bodied.*

Eligible. The word sat on the page like a hook sitting in water, patient, certain that eventually something would swim into it.

Tobias had taught Kestrel to read at this very desk, three years ago, with the ledger open between them like a forbidden book—which it was, which it had always been. *This word is harbor,* he'd said. *This one is your father's name.* And Kestrel had looked at the ink as if it were the sea itself, vast and dangerous and suddenly legible. Tobias had felt, in that moment, like a magician. He understood now that he'd been something else entirely. A tour guide to the cage. Look, here is the door of your prison, spelled out in a fine copperplate hand. Isn't it beautiful?

A boot heel clicked in the doorway. Tobias snapped the ledger shut.

Sergeant Hale stood there with his usual expression, which was the expression of a man who has smelled something slightly off and intends to find it. "Captain wants the annex copies by evening."

"They'll be ready."

"Your father says you're quick." Hale's eyes moved over the desk, the ledgers, the window. "Quicker than useful, some might say."

"Then some might say it to my father," Tobias said, and kept his voice flat, because he'd learned that flatness was the only armor the counting room issued.

Hale held the look a moment longer, then went. Tobias let out a breath and opened the ledger again.

It had started innocently, the way everything did. The captain wanted copies made of every document before it went into the sealed annex—copies for himself, Tobias understood now, though he hadn't understood it then. Duplicate records of supplies ordered, supplies received, men registered, men paid. And Tobias, who had spent his whole life among papers, was the one who made them.

It took him two weeks of copying before he noticed what a careful boy notices. The company had shipped four hundred sacks of rice to the garrison. The captain's copy said two hundred. The payroll listed sixty laborers; the captain's copy listed thirty-eight, and the difference in wages went somewhere that wasn't any laborer's pocket. Phantom men. Sold rice. The numbers didn't lie, exactly—they just told a second story underneath the first, the way a ledger always does if you know how to listen.

And here was the thing that kept him awake: the original documents, the true ones, went into the annex under lock and key, and Captain Verrin was the key. If anyone ever asked questions—if the war ended, if an inspector came—the captain would open the annex and produce whatever story he liked. The truth would be ash before it was ever read.

Unless someone copied it first.

Tobias had been doing exactly that, at night, a single sheet at a time, hidden inside the fair copies he legitimately produced. He had perhaps twenty pages of evidence folded into the false bottom of his father's old deed box, and no earthly idea what to do with them. Evidence in the hands of a clerk's son was not evidence. It was a confession. It was a rope with his name on it.

What it needed was someone the army never watched.

* * *

He found Kestrel at the tide pools below the breakwater, where the divers' children went when they wanted to be unobserved, which meant Tobias had to work to be unobserved too, doubling back along the fish-gut lane and coming down the scree like a thief, which he supposed he now was.

Kestrel was crouched over a pool with a knife, prying limpets, and he didn't stand when Tobias approached, which from Kestrel was a courtesy—he stayed low so that anyone watching would see one boy scavenging, not two boys meeting. Three years of friendship had taught Tobias to read those small grammars.

"You shouldn't come here," Kestrel said without looking up.

"Nobody follows me. I'm furniture." Tobias crouched beside him. "I need to show you something and I need you to be stupid about it afterward. Can you be stupid? You've always been smarter than me, it'll be a strain."

That got the ghost of a grin. Then it faded, because Kestrel had seen his face properly. "What is it?"

"Not here. Tonight, after the ten o'clock patrol passes the company house. The side door, the one with the broken latch I've been meaning to report for eight months and haven't."

"Tobias." Kestrel put the knife down. In the low light his face was all angles and worry, older than sixteen, older than anyone should have to be. "If this is about the registration—"

"It's about your name. It's about getting it out from under them." Tobias heard his own voice shake and hated it. "Come tonight or don't. But if you don't, you'll be on a conscript list by spring, diving for their blockade until the water takes you or the war ends, whichever comes last."

Kestrel looked out at the harbor, at the black ships, and said nothing, which was the loudest answer he had.

* * *

The side door of the company house opened onto the corridor that ran between the record room and the stair. Tobias had timed it: Hale's patrol crossed the rear yard at ten, checked the front, and wouldn't circle back for forty minutes. Forty minutes. He had done more with less.

Kestrel came in smelling of pitch and cold seawater, and for a moment they stood in the dark together, listening to the building breathe—the tick of the stove cooling, the murmur of the sergeant's card game upstairs—and Tobias thought absurdly of when they were twelve, sneaking in here to read adventure serials by candle stub, back when the worst thing in this building was a debt column.

"This way," he whispered.

In the counting room he lit the lamp low, turned it down to a bead of flame, and unlocked the drawer where he kept the captain's copies. He laid the pages on the desk one at a time, like a dealer showing cards.

"Rice," he said. "Four hundred sacks came in on the *Meridian* in October. This is what Verrin reports: two hundred. Where are the other two hundred? Sold, most likely, to the coastal traders that anchor past the point—there's a landing recorded at Miller's Cove that matches nothing official. Here: payroll. Sixty laborers on the breakwater works. Thirty-eight paid. Twenty-two phantom men drawing wages to an account I can't trace but I can trace the handwriting, and it's the quartermaster's. And here—" he laid down the last sheet—"the requisition order itself. Every diver in this harbor, every indentured man, listed by name and debt. Your father's on it. You're on it."

Kestrel read slowly—he was good now, better than Tobias ever acknowledged aloud—but he read this page fast, because he already knew what it said. He'd known since the day the soldiers seized the ledgers and a corporal called out his name like a number.

"You said you could change it," Kestrel said quietly. "You said—last night at the pools you said my name could come out from under them."

"I said I'd show you something first. Because it's not free, Kestrel, and I won't pretend it is." Tobias pulled the deed box from under the desk, lifted the false bottom, and brought out the folded sheets—his own copies, his own evidence, twenty pages of the captain's arithmetic. "This is what I need. These pages, out of this town, into hands that aren't Verrin's hands. There's a revenue inspector stationed at Corvane, across the bay. My uncle. He doesn't love the army and he loves less a captain who skims a war. If these reach him—"

"And if they don't reach him? If they're found on the road, or found in your coat?" Kestrel's voice was very even. "They hang thieves here now. They hung the Chandler boy in November for less paper than that."

"For theft of army property, yes." Tobias met his eyes. "But altering a conscription record isn't theft. It's forgery against the requisition act, and that's worse, and I'm the one holding the pen, so understand what I'm telling you: I will make you dead, Kestrel. On paper. *Deceased*, in my own hand, in the fair copy that goes to the annex. Diving accident—it won't even be questioned, this harbor kills men every winter. And when the conscription muster comes for the able-bodied divers of lease seven, your name won't be read, because the record will say the sea already took you."

The lamp flame guttered and steadied. Somewhere upstairs, a soldier laughed at a card.

Kestrel stared at the blank space on the page where his death would go. "And my father?"

"Your father's entry stands. I can't touch it—two alterations in one household, in one week, that's a pattern, patterns get noticed." Tobias heard the cruelty of it arrive in his own mouth and couldn't soften it, because softening it would be lying. "One dead son, one conscripted father. That's a tragedy. Two dead sons is an audit."

For a long moment Kestrel didn't move. Then he said, "You've thought about this. How long?"

"Since the day they seized the ledgers. Since I watched Corporal Bray run his finger down the column and decide people's lives with his finger." Tobias's voice cracked and he pressed it flat again. "I used to think this room was just a room. Paper and dust and my father hunched over his sums. Then I watched an army walk in and lift one book off one shelf, and every family in this harbor bent like grass. One book, Kestrel. I copy it six hours a day. Do you know what that's like? Holding the thing that owns everyone you know, and being trusted with it precisely because you're nobody?"

"Yes," Kestrel said. "That's what it's like being poor. Only we don't get trusted."

The words landed harder than any blow Tobias had taken. He sat down heavily on his father's stool, and for a moment neither of them spoke, two boys in a lamplit room full of other people's lives.

"I'm frightened," Tobias said, because it seemed owed, as a matter of accurate accounting. "I want you to know that. I'm not brave. I've just done the sum, and the sum says doing nothing is also a choice, and I can't carry it."

"I know." Kestrel picked up the pen. Turned it. Set it down. "My sister reads faces the way you read columns. She'll know something's wrong within a day."

"Then don't tell her."

"She mends our nets. She counts every shell in the tin. She's the reason we eat." Kestrel's jaw worked. "Lying to her is lying to myself, and you're asking me to do both."

"I'm asking you to survive." Tobias leaned forward. "Listen to me. We have spent our whole lives being handled. The company handles your family, the army handles the company, the war handles the army, and none of it—not one stroke of it—has ever once asked us. This is the first thing I have ever chosen. It may be the last. Are you in?"

Kestrel looked at the ledger, at the neat copperplate of his own name, and Tobias watched something settle in his friend's face—not resolve, resolve was cleaner than this. It was more like a man stepping off a known shore.

"Show me where my death goes," Kestrel said.

* * *

They worked fast and in silence. Tobias ruled the margin, dated the entry, wrote *DUNMORE, KESTREL — lost at depth, 14 Dec., body not recovered*, and the words looked obscene and holy at once, a lie shaped exactly like the truth, since the sea did keep boys from this harbor every year and nobody ever asked it why. He initialed it with his father's mark, which was the deepest forgery of all, and blotted it, and it was done, and nothing in the room changed, and everything had.

"There," he said hoarsely. "By tomorrow noon it's in the annex. After that it can't be unmade except by fire."

"Then let's hope nobody audits us." Kestrel tried to laugh and couldn't quite. He picked up the folded bundle of evidence instead, turning it over in his hands—twenty pages of a captain's greed in a clerk's careful hand. "Where do these go?"

"Thursday. The supply launch runs to Corvane with dispatches. I'm on the manifest as escort for correspondence." Tobias took the bundle back and slid it into the deed box, into the dark, where it lay like a coal. "After Thursday it stops being ours. After Thursday it belongs to whatever happens next."

Kestrel stood to go, then paused at the door, silhouetted against the corridor's black.

"Tobias. If this goes wrong—whatever they do to you—I want you to know I'd have done the same for you. If I'd been given the pen."

"No, you wouldn't have," Tobias said gently. "You'd have written your sister safe and left yourself to drown. That's the difference between us. Go home. Don't look at the patrol. Be stupid."

The door closed. Tobias sat alone in the counting room with the lamp turned to a bead, and listened to the building breathe, and understood, with the clarity of a figure finally balancing, that he had just crossed a line from which the ledger keeps no return column. He was fourteen years old and he would never be a child again, and somewhere above him a sergeant dealt another hand of cards, and the annex downstairs held every name in the harbor, and one of them was already dead.

He blew out the lamp.

Two nights later, crossing the yard at dusk, he saw Captain Verrin standing at the annex door with the manifest in hand, and he saw the captain's finger stop on a line, and stay there.

On his father's initials. On the new entry. On a dead boy's name.
