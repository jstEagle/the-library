## 7. The Forgery

The copy room smelled of tallow and dust, a narrow cell behind the counting floor where the Atlas Trading Company kept its duplicates. Tobias had the key. He always had the key — his father's key, lifted from the hook by the door while old Merrick slept off his evening gin, hung now around his son's neck on a bootlace like something holy.

"Lock it behind us," Tobias said.

Kestrel turned the key in the lock and heard it click, and the click sounded louder than any wave he'd ever heard at dawn, because this was the first door in his life he had ever closed on purpose against the world.

Two lamps. A table scarred with decades of ink cuts. And between them, open, the copied ledger — not the great book itself but its twin, the working duplicate that Captain Verrin's clerks amended daily while the original sat under army seal in the requisitioned strongroom. It was the copy the army trusted. The copy that would travel to the conscription board in three days' time, when the muster lists were drawn.

Kestrel's name was on page forty-one.

He had known that since he was nine years old, since the first night Tobias traced a wet fingertip down a column of names and said, *That one's you.* But knowing a thing and looking straight at it were different animals. There it was: **DUNMORE, KESTREL. Age 17. Debt outstanding: 84 marks, 6 shillings. Status: INDENTURED — CONSCRIPTABLE.**

Conscriptable. Someone had entered that word recently, in a different hand, in iron-gall ink darker than the company brown. The army's hand. As if Kestrel were a barrel or a bale of hemp, a thing assessed for weight and serviceability.

"You're certain the board reads from the copy," he said. Not a question. He'd asked four times already.

"The original stays sealed for the inquiry into Verrin's accounts. Nobody opens the strongroom before the muster." Tobias pulled the stopper from the ink bottle and set it down with exaggerated care, the way their fathers handled blasting fuse. "After the lists go out, it won't matter what the original says. You'll be dead on paper, and dead men don't dive blockade wrecks."

*Dead.* The word went through Kestrel cold, and then, shamefully, it did not go through him at all — it settled, it warmed him, it felt like a coat.

He hated himself for that warmth even as he stood there letting it soak in.

Tobias dipped the pen. "Hold the page flat."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the harbor fog pressed against the shutters, and somewhere below them a sentry's boots rang on the company steps, paused, moved on. Kestrel flattened the page with both palms, pressing down on his own name as though he could hold it still, keep it from doing what they were about to do to it.

Tobias wrote small. That was the first marvel — how small he could write, how precisely, each letter a grain of rice. He was mimicking the conscription clerk's hand, which he'd studied through two evenings of "fetching documents" for Verrin, and he'd practiced the letterforms forty times on scrap until Kestrel could no longer tell the practice sheets from the true entries.

A line through **INDENTURED — CONSCRIPTABLE.** A marginal note in that borrowed hand: *Deceased. Fever, 14 Dec. Confirmed by district surgeon. Entry closed.*

Entry closed.

Three words and a ruled line, and Kestrel Dunmore, who had been owned since before he could walk, ceased to exist.

"There." Tobias set the pen down and blew on the ink, and his breath fogged in the cold room, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

Then Kestrel began to laugh. It came up out of him low and helpless, an ugly sound in the little room, and he clapped a hand over his mouth and laughed harder behind it. Tobias stared at him, alarmed, and then started laughing too, silent shaking laughter, two boys bent over a dead man's page with tears standing in their eyes.

"We're hanged for this," Kestrel managed. "You know that. If anyone ever—"

"No one will ever look." Tobias wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "That's the whole point of ledgers, Kes. Nobody looks. They just believe."

*Nobody looks. They just believe.* Kestrel carried those words home through the fog like coins in his mouth, tasting them, turning them over. All his life he had believed the opposite — believed that everything about him was looked at, weighed, recorded; that his debt was a leash someone held every hour of every day. And here was Tobias telling him the leash had never been real. Only the belief in it. Only everyone agreeing to be held.

It should have been freedom. It felt like theft.

* * *

His mother was asleep when he came in, and Briony was not. She sat up in the loft with the net-mending needle still in her hand, watching him climb through the loft hatch with the particular stillness she used when she had decided not to ask questions yet.

"You were out till past midnight," she said.

"Company business."

"At midnight."

"Tobias needed help moving records." It was nearly true. He was surprised how easily the lie came, how smooth it felt in his mouth, like a stone worn by water. *This is what lying is,* he thought. *It's just words you don't have to pay for.*

Briony looked at him a long moment. She was fifteen and she had their mother's way of seeing straight through fog, and he knew — knew with sick certainty — that she saw something new sitting on him like a gull on a rail. But she only said, "There's bread," and went back to her net.

He ate the bread without tasting it. Below, in the dark, his father coughed in his sleep — the short, wet cough that had started after the processing sheds, after whatever they'd made him breathe down there in the testing tanks. Eighty-four marks and six shillings of debt, and now a father whose lungs were going soft. The arithmetic of the Dunmore family, all of it written down somewhere in a hand nobody would ever look at.

And none of it applied to him anymore. That was the thing he kept circling back to as he lay in his bunk listening to the house settle. Somewhere across town, in a locked room, a ruled line ran through his obligations like a knife through netting. When the muster board read the copy, they would pass over DUNMORE, KESTREL the way a man passes over a grave marker — with respect, briefly, and no interest whatsoever.

Dead men paid no debts. Dead men dove no wrecks for Verrin's blockade. Dead men could walk the streets of the quarter in plain sight, invisible in the one way that mattered, and no soldier's list would ever find them again.

He fell asleep smiling, and hated the smile.

* * *

The next three days were the strangest of his life.

He rose at dawn and dove the shallows as always, because a dead man still had to eat, and brought up oysters with hands that felt lighter than they had since the warship first appeared. The water was the same water. His body was the same body, the same ache in the shoulders, the same salt sting in the old cut on his palm. But everything above the surface had changed its weight. When Sergeant Haley's patrol came down the quay with their muster book, checking faces against pages, Kestrel stood in the queue of men out of pure habit — and watched the corporal's finger slide down the column, past DUNMORE, KESTREL, without stopping, without seeing, and move on to the Weir brothers below.

They didn't see him. That was the intoxication of it. He had spent seventeen years being seen in exactly one way — as a sum, a liability, a name on a page — and now he was seen in no way at all. He walked through the occupied town like a ghost through a wall. A soldier shoved past him on the company steps without a glance. A clerk took his delivery of pearls, weighed them, marked the payment against the *family* account — the account that still lived, that would live until the last Dunmore was erased — and never once connected the diver to the dead boy on page forty-one.

He was free. He was free the way a drowned man is free of weather.

On the second day he caught himself whistling at dawn, actually whistling, hauling in the anchor line with a tune in his throat, and stopped so abruptly he nearly went over the gunwale. His father noticed anyway. Old Dunmore sat wrapped in a blanket in the stern where he could no longer help with anything but bait, and he gave Kestrel a long, puzzled look, and said nothing, and the nothing was worse than a question.

On the third night, Tobias came to the drying shed where Kestrel mended gear, and his face was wrong.

"What is it," Kestrel said. Not *what's wrong* — he'd learned that much from the army already. Wrong was a condition; *it* was a thing you could point at.

"They posted the provisional muster lists tonight." Tobias leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed tight, a boy holding himself together. "The Weirs are on it. Both of them. Half the quarter, Kes. And your father."

"I know. I saw the—"

"And there's a supplement." Tobias's voice dropped even though the shed was empty and the tide was the only listener. "Verrin ordered a verification pass. Cross-checking the copy against the original before the final lists go to the board. He says—" and here Tobias swallowed, and Kestrel saw for the first time real fear in his friend's face, fear with a shape and a name — "he says there may be irregularities. Errors introduced into the duplicate. He wants the originals compared line by line, entry by entry, before the century turns."

The lamp between them hissed. Out on the water, the blockade ships rode their lights at anchor, patient as spiders.

"He knows," Kestrel said. "Somehow. He doesn't know it's us yet, but he knows something's altered, and when he compares—"

"When he compares, he finds a dead boy who isn't dead, and a clerk's son with the only key to the copy room." Tobias pushed off the doorframe. "I can delay it. Maybe. A day, two. After that—"

"After that we're both ghosts," Kestrel said, "and not the useful kind."

He looked down at his hands — the diver's hands, scarred, competent, steady — and understood that the freedom he had bought with a ruled line and three borrowed words had lasted exactly three days, and that everything he had felt walking unseen through the occupied town, the lightness, the whistling, the shameful joy of it, had never been freedom at all. It had only been the pause between the trap and the spring.

Tobias was already moving toward the door. "Burn the practice sheets tonight," he said. "Everything with the letterforms. Everything."

Kestrel nodded. Then, because some part of him still wanted, desperately, to be the boy who had laughed in the copy room: "Tobias. Whatever happens — we got three days no one can take from us."

Tobias paused in the doorway, lamplight catching half his face, and for a moment he looked much older than seventeen.

"Verrin wants a name," he said quietly. "By Friday. And Kes — I've seen how he asks."
