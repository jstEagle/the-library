## 8. Briony's Discovery

Briony found the page on a Tuesday, because she would remember it was a Tuesday — the day she'd gone to the market for lamp oil and come home with none, distracted by the sight of soldiers unloading crates at the company house, and so had gone looking for the candle stubs her mother kept in the chest under the window seat.

The chest held candles, yes. And mended nets, and Father's old weight belt that no one had touched since his lungs went soft, and a folded square of oilcloth that did not belong to any of them.

She knew Kestrel's handwriting before she knew what she was reading. That was the strange part. She'd watched him learn his letters across Tobias Fenwick's knee in the company house yard, five summers ago, and she'd been jealous then — jealous of the secret and of the boy who shared it — and now the jealousy came back to her as something worse: recognition. She could read too, though she'd taught herself by copying the hymn board at the chapel and the price signs at market, and no one had made a ceremony of it, and no one knew.

The page was a ledger copy. Company stock paper, ruled in columns, the watermarks pale under her thumb. She carried it to the window where the light came in off the harbor, gray and even, and she read it twice because the first time she thought she must have misread it.

*DUNMORE, KESTREL.* The entry ran down the column the way all their entries ran: debt incurred, interest accrued, wages offset, balance owed. She knew this arithmetic better than the hymnal. She could have recited it. Their whole lives were that column, the numbers climbing when Father bought gear on credit, falling when Kestrel brought in pearls, never once reaching zero.

Except this copy did not say *balance owed* at the bottom.

It said *DECEASED.*

And beneath it, in the space reserved for remarks, someone had written — in ink slightly too brown to be the clerk's black, in a hand that trembled only at the end of the word, as if the writer's nerve had run out exactly there — *Lost at depth. Body not recovered.*

Briony stood very still with the page in her hands.

The harbor worked outside the glass the way it always worked. A skiff came in. Gulls argued over fish heads on the breakwater. Somewhere up the hill the soldiers were hammering crates shut, and the sound carried over the water flat and regular, like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

*Kestrel,* the paper said, *is dead.*

Which meant the debt did not pass to him. Which meant the conscription lists — posted now every week on the company house door, names in neat rows, men called up for the blockade work, for the diving details, for the loading gangs that worked until their hands split — those lists could never hold his name again. Dead men dived no blockades. Dead men owed nothing. She understood it instantly, entirely, the way you understand a thing that has been done to you rather than for you.

Then she understood the rest of it, the part underneath: if Kestrel Dunmore was deceased, then the family debt — the whole of it, Father's gear, Mother's medicine last winter, the interest compounding month over month like weed on a hull — fell where the ledger said it fell. On the surviving indentured members of the household.

On her.

She set the page down on the windowsill very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

* * *

He came in at dusk smelling of salt and lamp smoke, which she noted, and filed, and did not yet use. He'd been out past the shallows again; she'd seen him from the nets, far out where the water went dark-green and no licensed diver was supposed to work, and she'd told herself he was checking Father's old lines. Now she knew better. Unregistered men earned coin the ledgers couldn't see. Her brother had become a ghost in more ways than one.

He hung his coat on the peg. He saw her face, and stopped.

"You went through my things," he said.

"Your things are our things." She was sitting at the table with the oilcloth square in front of her, laid open, the forged page facing him like an accusation. "There's no such thing as yours anymore. There hasn't been since they took the book."

Kestrel didn't sit. He stood with his hand still on the coat, and Briony watched her brother — fourteen months younger than her, and always smaller, and always faster — decide what lie to tell. She watched the decision happen. It took less time than it should have. That frightened her most of all.

"It's not what it looks like," he began.

"It says you're dead."

"Paper can say anything."

"That's exactly what it is." She pushed back from the table and stood, because if they were going to do this she would not do it sitting down like a child waiting to be scolded. "Paper says we owe the Atlas Company eleven years of Father's lungs. Paper says I'm registered, so I can walk through the gate without being dragged. Paper says who goes on the list Monday, and who gets put on a ship, and who stays. You've known that longer than I have — Tobias taught you. So don't stand there telling me paper is nothing when you've just spent it like money."

"Keep your voice down."

"There's no one home but us." Mother was at the Hendersons' doing washing for soldiers' wives now, paid in scrip, coming home with red hands and saying nothing. Father was asleep in the back room, sleeping more and waking less, his breath going through him like wind through a wreck. "Sit down, Kestrel."

He sat. That surprised her. He looked young when he sat, suddenly, the lamplight finding the angles of his face and making them sharp, and she hated him a little for looking young, because it would have been easier to be angry at a man.

"Who else knows," she said.

"No one. Tobias copied it for me. The original—" He stopped. Something crossed his face, quick as a fish shadow, and was gone before she could name it. "No one else knows. It's safe."

"The original is in the records house under a soldier's boot heel, isn't it. This copy means nothing except to us." She wasn't asking anymore. "So here's what I know. I know my brother died last week in some inkwell, and nobody told me. I know that when the debt collectors finish with Father's account — and they will, they're already circling, Mrs. Pell told me the company sent a man to look at the boat — they'll find his account has one living diver attached to it instead of two. Me. I know that dead men don't pay debts, and I'm the only Dunmore left on the page."

Kestrel said nothing. The fire ticked in the stove.

"Say it," she said. "Out loud. Say who pays."

"The debt doesn't pass fully," he said. "Not immediately. There's a provision—"

"I've read the provision. It's the same provision that bound Father, and his father. It passes to kin of working age." She heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used at market when she wouldn't be cheated, and she let it. "I'm sixteen in March. I'm of working age. Congratulations, brother. Your funeral cost me everything you thought you were saving."

"It was supposed to protect you." His voice cracked on the last word and she watched him hate himself for it. "It was supposed to keep me off the lists so I could work, so I could earn, so when the ship comes there'd be berths for—"

"For whom?" She hadn't meant to ask it that hard. She asked it anyway. "For you and Father? For you and Mother? Sit here and do the sums with me, Kestrel, since you love sums so much now that you write them in other people's blood. One berth costs what? More than a dead man earns. More than a registered girl mending nets. You haven't freed this family. You've cut it loose from yourself and let it drift."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "None of this is fair. That's the whole point of it. They built a machine that runs on unfair, and you tried to beat it by feeding it your own name." She sat down across from him then, finally, because the anger had burned off whatever was under it, and what was under it was fear, and the fear wanted a chair. "You should have told me."

"You'd have tried to stop me."

"I'd have helped you do it properly." She almost laughed. "You used a brown ink. Do you know how many documents pass through that office in a week? Do you know what the clerks notice? Brown ink on company stock. Tobias's hand shaking on the final letter. If Verrin ever compares the copies — and he will, men like that count everything —"

"He won't compare them."

"You don't know that."

"I know Tobias."

"Do you?" Briony leaned forward. "Tobias Fenwick, whose father keeps the books for the men who own us. Tobias, who taught you letters out of a ledger because it was the only book in town that had your name in it. Kestrel, I love that boy like a cousin, and I'm telling you: people like him survive by being useful, and when being useful stops being safe, they choose their fathers. Everyone chooses their fathers."

"Not everyone."

"Prove it," she said. "Go on. Prove it, and meanwhile prove this too: that you're alive. Because I need you alive. Not on paper. Actually alive, actually here, actually eating supper at this table instead of lying drowned in a column of figures while I pay for your headstone with my whole future."

The words landed. She saw them land. And in the silence after, the fire ticking, Father's ragged breathing faint through the wall, Briony felt the argument turn over in her chest and show its underside — because she had not spent the last hour angry only about the debt. She was angry about the erasure. Her brother had taken the one thing the company couldn't take, the fact of himself, and handed it over voluntarily. He'd made himself invisible to save them, and he hadn't even asked if that was a price anyone should pay alone.

"We fix it," she said, quieter.

"How? The original's locked in the annex. I can't get near it without—" He stopped again, same place as before, same fish shadow crossing his face.

"Without what?"

"Nothing."

"Kestrel." She reached across the table and took his wrist, the way she used to when they swam out past the buoys as children and the current caught them, hold on, hold on. "Whatever you and Tobias have done or are doing — I'm not asking you to confess it. I'm asking you to stop carrying it alone, because you're bad at it. Look at the ink."

He laughed then, short and broken, and rubbed his face with his free hand, and for a moment he was just her brother, tired, twenty months into a war that had walked into their harbor and sat down like it owned the view.

"It doesn't matter anyway," he said. "Even if we fixed the copy. Even if the forgery vanished tomorrow. The lists go up Monday. My name's already marked absent from the processing rolls. Absent without registration reads as runaway. Runaways hang, Bri, or worse — they get sent to the works. There's no road back to being visible. That's the trick of it. That's the whole trick. Once they lose you in the book, you stay lost."

Briony sat back.

She looked at the forged page, lying there between them like a body at a wake. Deceased. Lost at depth. Body not recovered. Her brother's life, unwritten in brown ink by a boy with shaking hands, for love, which changed nothing about how the world would treat it.

Then she looked at her brother, alive, thin, scared, stubborn, carrying three people's futures on a frame meant for diving shallows, and she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the moment she read the page — the same arithmetic, she realized, that he must have done the night he decided to die on paper. Who fits. Who's worth. Whose name buys the most.

There was another way to solve it. There was always another way; sums were generous that way.

"Then we don't fix it," she said slowly, feeling the idea take shape as she spoke it, terrible and clean. "We leave yours as it is. You stay dead. You stay free — no lists can touch you, no collector can attach you, you can work and earn and no ledger anywhere sees a shilling of it. That part of your plan was right, Kestrel. It was just aimed wrong."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you erased the wrong Dunmore." She kept her voice steady. She was proud of how steady it was. "If someone in this family has to vanish from the book, it should be the one whose disappearance frees the most. You're dead — fine, the debt needs a living diver, and Father can't dive. But I'm registered, and registered girls get assigned too, did you know that? Domestic service details. The officers' houses. There's a requisition schedule going up Monday with the conscription lists, and my name is on it either way — either as labor or as property. Unless."

"Unless what." His voice had gone very quiet. He knew. She could see that he already knew, and was watching her walk toward it anyway, hoping to head her off.

"Unless I'm dead too." She turned the forged page toward her, studied the trembling letters, the brown ink. "Tobias owes you a favor, or he'll owe you one. Another line. Another remark in another margin. *DUNMORE, BRIONY. Fever. Body released to family.* Two ghosts in one household. No living divers on the account. The company writes the debt off as unrecoverable — they do it all the time, Mrs. Pell told me, half the old families on the point are 'unrecoverable.' And then we're both free, Kestrel. Free to work, free to earn, free to buy berths with money no ledger can trace. You've already proven it can be done."

"No." He was on his feet before she finished. "Absolutely not. No."

"Why not? It's the same sum."

"Because it's not the same sum!" And there it was, out at last, the thing underneath the whole evening, naked in the lamplight: he could bear to be erased but not to erase her. "Because you're — you mended the nets this morning and sold Father's boat-share without blinking and you keep this house standing and if you think I watched them take Father apart in that processing shed so that I could feed my sister into the same mill—"

"You didn't watch them do anything. You hid. Like I'm hiding now. Like everyone hides." She stood too, and they faced each other across the table across the forged page, across the whole ruined arithmetic of their family. "You don't get to be the only sacrifice, Kestrel. Love doesn't work like that. Either we do the sums together or we drown separately, and I have watched enough drowning this year to last me."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it change under the pilings, the great slow breath of the harbor, indifferent as a ledger.

It was Kestrel who broke first — not with words but with a look, over her shoulder, toward the window. Toward the harbor. Toward the records house beyond it, dark against the last light, one window on the upper floor burning yellow: the annex. The room where the original pages lived.

"Bri," he said, and his voice had changed, gone low and fast, the voice he used right before a deep dive, when there was no more time for talking and only the plan remained. "When did you say the new lists go up?"

"Monday. Why?"

"Because Tobias came to me at the nets this morning, and he couldn't talk — soldiers ten feet away the whole time. All he could do was give me this." He dug in his coat pocket and set a scrap of paper on the table, folded small, unfolded fast. Four words in Tobias's careful clerk's hand:

*They audit the annex Friday.*

Briony read it twice. Outside, the gulls went up off the breakwater all at once, shrieking, as if something below them had moved.

"Friday," she said. "That's tomorrow."
