## 9. Father Returns

They brought him back on a Tuesday, in the back of a requisitioned fish cart that still smelled of herring and ice.

Kestrel saw the cart turn onto the harbor road and knew before it stopped. He knew by the way the driver sat — not like a man hauling cargo but like a man hauling something he didn't want to look at. He knew by the soldiers trailing behind at a loose walk, rifles slung, bored, done with him. And he knew by the silence of the divers' quarter, how the net-menders put down their work without being told, how Briony came out of the house with her hands still wet from the wash basin and stopped dead on the threshold.

The cart creaked to a halt in front of the Dunmore door.

"Here you are," the driver said, too loudly, as if volume could make the delivery ordinary. "One diver, returned."

Father sat up against the cart's rail and tried to stand under his own power. That was the worst of it, later — that he tried. Kestrel saw him set his boots to the board and push, and saw his chest seize halfway up, a wet tearing sound in it like a net catching on wreck iron, and then he folded, and Kestrel was there, catching most of his weight, enough of it that his knees cracked against the cobblestones and he didn't feel it until that night.

"I can walk," Father said.

"I know," Kestrel said. "We'll walk in a minute."

It took both of them and Briony holding the door to get him inside, onto the bed he'd shared with Mother's memory for nine years, the mattress bowing under him like it had forgotten how to hold a man lying down in daylight.

* * *

The army's processing had taken eleven days. In that time the story around town had assembled itself in fragments, the way stories did here, each neighbor contributing a piece they'd heard from someone who'd heard it from a soldier's cook. The conscripted divers had been taken to the eastern anchorage, past the point, where three transports sat rusting at anchor behind a boom of chained barges. They were needed to clear the harbor floor — old scuttled hulks, cable fouling, whatever the blockade operation required. They dove in shifts. They dove in water that was colder and deeper than the beds, with army men counting them down and up, and the counting was done by whistle, and a man who came up late was docked pay, and a man who came up very late was not docked anything because there was nothing left to dock.

Two men hadn't come up at all. Everyone said the names quietly: Sorrel Vane, who was nineteen. Old Merek, who everyone had assumed was too old to be taken and had been proven wrong about that and nothing else.

Father had come up every time. That was what he kept saying, the first evening, propped against the pillows with his shoulders at a wrong new angle, as if his body no longer knew how to be horizontal in its own bed. "Every time, Kess. Every whistle. I was never last."

"I know," Kestrel said.

"The Vane boy panicked. That's all it was. Panicked at depth, shot up, and his lungs—" Father made a gesture with one hand, a small explosion, fingers opening. His hand shook doing it. He looked at the hand as if it belonged to someone whose habits he disapproved of, and closed it. "I came up slow. Every time. Slow is the whole craft. I taught you that."

"You did."

"Then there's nothing wrong with me." He said it with the flat certainty of a man laying a stone in a wall. "A week of rest. Maybe two. Then I'll go back to the beds."

Briony, standing in the doorway with a bowl of broth she'd been reheating for an hour because he wouldn't eat it hot or cold, caught Kestrel's eye over their father's head. She didn't say anything. She was fifteen and she already knew how to keep her face still, which was a kind of inheritance neither parent had meant to leave her.

The sound in Father's chest when he breathed lying down was the sound Kestrel had heard once in a dropped jar — the fine crackle of something that would hold liquid no longer.

* * *

The company clerk came on Thursday.

Not Tobias — Tobias had been reassigned to the annex with the army records people and hadn't been by in two weeks, and Kestrel had told himself this was busyness, and had told himself it convincingly perhaps four times out of every ten. This was the elder clerk, Mr. Harrow, who smelled of ink and camphor and carried the ledger's smaller twin under his arm, a daybook where accounts were transcribed and updated.

He stood in the kitchen because Father couldn't receive him in the bedroom without it becoming something else, some occasion, and nobody wanted the occasion. Harrow laid the daybook on the table, licked his thumb, turned pages with the tenderness other men reserved for photographs of the dead.

"Dunmore, Alistair. Diver class one, seniority eighteen years." He ran a finger down a column. Kestrel could read now — Tobias had given him that, letter by letter, in the dim back room of the company house, using the ledger itself as a primer — and so he could see the numbers, though he looked at them sideways, the way you look at the sun. "Conscription wages credited against account for eleven days. Less equipment levy, less rations drawn, less—" Harrow paused, and something almost like embarrassment crossed his face, quickly disciplined. "Less a medical assessment fee."

"They charged him," Briony said, "for looking at him."

"The examining physician is a civilian contractor," Harrow said, which was not a no. He cleared his throat. "The assessment notes diminished capacity. The deep-bed classification has been withdrawn. He'll be reclassified to shore work if shore work becomes available, which I'm instructed to say is unlikely during wartime conditions." He turned the book around so they could see it, which was a cruelty dressed as courtesy, or courtesy dressed as cruelty; Kestrel genuinely could not tell. "As head of household he was guarantor on the account. With his classification changed, the guarantee reverts."

"To whom?" Kestrel asked.

Harrow looked at him. For just a moment the clerk's professional face slipped and underneath was a tired man in his fifties who had spent thirty years writing numbers next to names and had long ago stopped asking himself what the numbers were for. "To the household. Jointly. You're both of working age and registered." He closed the book. "The full balance stands at—" and he said a number.

Kestrel heard it the way you hear your own name called from far off across water: recognizable, distant, requiring a response he could not yet make. It was more than the boat was worth. More than the house. It was roughly, if he did the arithmetic the way Tobias had taught him to do arithmetic, four years of everything the three of them together could earn, in a good year, with good prices, with nobody sick.

"The company offers extended terms," Harrow added, gathering his book. "Interest accrues, but the schedule stretches. Many families find it—"

"Yes," Kestrel said. "Many families."

Harrow paused at the door. "I'm sorry about your father," he said, and Kestrel believed him, and understood that belief changed nothing at all, and understood that this — a sorry man with a book, going up the path to the next door — was what the machinery looked like up close. Not soldiers. Arithmetic.

* * *

For the first week, Father got up every morning.

That was the corruption beginning, though Kestrel didn't have the word for it yet. He got up before dawn, out of a habit forty years deep, and dressed, and went out to the shed where the gear hung, and stood among the weights and knives and mesh bags in the gray light, and touched nothing. Then he came in and sat by the stove until it was light enough to justify mending something, and he mended badly, his hands steady enough for knots but his breath short after a single flight of stairs to nowhere, since there were no stairs in the house and he climbed them anyway, somehow, in his mind.

"It's the cold air," he said. "Sea air will fix it. Salt in the lungs."

"That's not—" Briony started, and Kestrel shook his head at her, a small movement, and she bit the sentence in half.

By the second week Father had stopped getting up quite so early. By the third week he had started talking about the war.

This was the thing nobody had prepared any of them for: their father, who had held in his entire life exactly one opinion about the capital (that it was far away), began holding forth at the stove on shipping lanes and naval strategy and the justice of the cause, repeating back, Kestrel realized with a sickness in his stomach, the very words the army men must have fed the conscripts at the anchorage between dives, the words that came free with the rations. *Short campaign. Decisive action. Honor to the port that serves.* Father said *we*, now. We hold the strait. We need the boom finished by spring. As if there were a we, as if the whistle-counting and the wet crackle in his chest had enrolled him in something instead of spent him.

"Pride in the work," he said one night, apropos of nothing, staring into the stove. "I taught you that. There's no shame in a man diving for his country. There's shame only in diving badly."

Kestrel sat very still. He thought: you used to say the company price sheets were theft with manners. You used to say, spit into your palm, hand on my shoulder, thirteen years old and shivering on the jetty: *the sea doesn't owe us a living, son, but it doesn't lie to us either, and that's more than you'll say for any man on shore.* You used to weigh a pearl on your tongue-word alone and know its worth without their scales. Where had that man gone? Had the army taken him along with the ledger? Was there a page in some annex with his name on it, and had they inked over the old father and written in a new one, marked *loyal*, and was that the real forgery, the one no one would ever burn?

"I'll take the morning beds tomorrow," Kestrel said. "And the mid-shallows after. Briony's selling at market now, the company stall, she gets a better rate than I did."

"A better rate," Father repeated, softly, and something moved behind his eyes — suspicion, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "The army rates are fair. Fair compensation for skilled labor."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "Fair."

Afterward Briony washed the bowls and said, without turning around, "You have to stop agreeing with him."

"He hears disagreement and he tries to get up."

"I know." She set the last bowl in the rack. Her hands stayed on the edge of the basin. "I know, Kess. But you can't build a person back out of agreement. He's drowning and none of us can dive for him." Her voice didn't break. That was somehow worse. "There's the berth money we've been saving. For the boat shares. If we put it against the account—"

"It won't cover a month of interest."

"If we put it against the account, then we've done something. Doing something is the only medicine he believes in."

Kestrel thought of the tin under the floorboard — one hundred and six days of his own diving in the deep margin beyond the company beds, illegal water, unregistered catch, money no ledger could trace because officially neither he nor the pearls existed. Their whole future. He thought of Tobias's face in the lamplight two weeks ago, saying *they trust me with the copies*, and the plan coiled somewhere beneath all this like a weight line paid out and waiting.

"Not yet," he said. "Give me until the end of the month."

She looked at him then, really looked, with the flat gray appraisal she'd learned from the sea. "You're doing something," she said. It wasn't a question, and she didn't ask what, and he loved her for it and was frightened by it in the same heartbeat.

* * *

On the last night of the month, Father had his bad spell.

It came after supper, without drama. He stood to bank the fire and simply didn't finish standing — slid down the stove's flank to the floor, breathing in shallow silver pulls, lips gone the color of the mussels' shells. Briony ran for the physician's widow down the lane while Kestrel knelt and held his father's head and felt, through his own knees and palms, the terrible laboring lightness of the man, as if the sea had gotten into him after all these years and was slowly replacing what it found.

The widow came, listened, dosed, pronounced. "The sacs are torn," she said in the kitchen afterward, quietly, the way people spoke near sickrooms even when the sickroom was empty. "They don't mend, Kestrel. Some men live years smaller than they were. Given the bleeding, given his age—" she arranged her kindness into order, "—I'd measure in seasons. Perhaps less, if he goes near deep water again, even to watch."

Seasons. One season, maybe. The word sat in Kestrel's chest like a swallowed stone.

When he came back into the room, Father was awake, propped high on pillows, watching the door with eyes that were horribly clear.

"She told you," he said. Not a question. "Don't lie. Your mother always said you lie like a door swinging shut."

"She told me."

Father nodded, slowly, as if confirming a tide table. He looked at his own hands on the blanket, the diver's hands, scarred white along every finger from a thousand cold closures, and Kestrel watched pride and its ruin negotiate a final settlement in his father's face.

"I was never last up," Father said. "Every whistle. Say it back to me sometime, when I'm gone, to whoever asks. Alistair Dunmore was never last up."

"You were never last up."

"No." A breath, thin and careful, budgeted like the household accounts. "But I was last, Kess. At the end. All those years of coming up slow, and it was the slowness that killed me anyway. The bottom keeps what the bottom keeps." His eyes closed. Opened again, finding his son. "Don't dive angry. Angry divers die. Promise me."

"I promise," Kestrel said, who had been diving angry for a month.

Father slept. Briony appeared in the doorway, drawn by the quiet, and Kestrel told her what the widow had said, in the fewest possible words, because that was the family currency now. She absorbed it standing very straight, the way she'd absorbed every hard thing since Mother, by making herself narrower.

Then, in the dark kitchen after, with the lamp turned low, she took his sleeve.

"There's a ship," she said. "Evacuation. Marta Corr's cousin works the charter office — berths assigned by ledger status, first weeks of the new year. Kess, if the status can be read right, if there's any way a name can end up in the right column—"

She didn't know. Of course she didn't know. She couldn't know that her brother was already dead on paper, struck through in a forged copy in a lamplit back room, *deceased* in a clerk's careful hand; that the plan he'd been paying out line toward all month was built on that erasure; that the arithmetic of love had already begun running in his head and would not stop.

"The end of the month," Kestrel said. "I told you. Give me until then."

"You gave yourself until tonight," Briony said. "I watched your face change."

Down the hall, faint through the wall, came the sound of their father breathing — the dropped-jar crackle, slower now, keeping its own ledger, entering every hour in red ink.

Kestrel pulled his coat off its hook. Tomorrow he would dive the illegal beds before dawn, unregistered, unrecorded, a dead man earning invisible money toward a berth he wasn't sure any of them should take. But first there was a back window and a fog-thick night and a quarter mile of wet grass toward the lights of the company house, where a boy who taught him letters was waiting — or failing him, or both.

He went out into the dark, and the dark closed over him like water.
