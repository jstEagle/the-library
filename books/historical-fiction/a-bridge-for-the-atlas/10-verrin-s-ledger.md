## 10. Verrin's Ledger

The copy room smelled of tallow and damp paper, a smell Kestrel had once thought of as Tobias's smell—their shared smell, from the winter evenings when a clerk's son taught a diver's son his letters by tracing ledger entries with a blunt pencil. Now it was the smell of the army's business, and the boy who let him in through the coal door wore a soldier's greatcoat two sizes too large, sleeves rolled to the elbow like a man pretending at a uniform instead of the other way around.

"Third shelf," Tobias said. "Red binding, brass corners. Verrin calls it the annex." He said it fast, the way you set down something hot. "I'm not supposed to know it exists."

"You're not supposed to be here either."

"My father is at vespers. We have an hour." Tobias lit the lamp, turned its wick low, and stood with his hands jammed under his arms as if holding himself shut. "Kestrel. Once you've seen it, you've seen it. There's no going back to not knowing."

"I've been not knowing my whole life," Kestrel said. "It's the one skill they gave us for free."

He pulled the annex off the shelf and felt its weight before he opened it—not the weight of paper but the weight of a thing that had been used to move men. He set it on the table under the lamp and began, as he'd been taught three winters ago over entries in his own family's debt, to read.

* * *

It took him less than an hour to understand what Captain Verrin had built, because it was built the way everything in the harbor was built—on the simple principle that nobody who mattered would ever check.

The requisition ordered four hundred men for the blockade operation: divers for the channel works, laborers for the powder magazine, boat crews for the transport runs. The annex listed them by name, by rate of pay, by rations drawn. Four hundred names. But Kestrel knew the harbor's able-bodied men the way he knew the deep beds, rock by rock, and he counted what he saw.

There were perhaps two hundred and sixty.

The rest were phantoms—invented men, or men long gone, or men dead of the lung fever whose names Verrin had simply never released. Their pay accrued on paper. Their rations were drawn from the company stores on paper. And on the facing pages, in Verrin's own cramped hand, the paper resolved itself into truth: *sold, 60 sacks flour; sold, 12 casks salt pork; sold, cordage, assorted.* Sold to whom, the annex did not trouble to say. Sold to the coastal traders, Tobias told him quietly, who paid in coin that never touched a government form. The phantom workers fed real markets, and the real workers—Kestrel's father among them—drew half-rations while their invented brothers ate full ones somewhere in the arithmetic.

"There's more," Tobias said. He turned pages with a pencil eraser, careful not to smudge. "The supply manifests. The army shipped out six hundred pairs of diving boots in October. Six hundred. You've seen the divers' feet. Half of them are wrapped in rags."

"I've seen my father's feet," Kestrel said.

"Then you've seen where the boots went." Tobias didn't smile. He hadn't smiled, Kestrel realized, since the requisition. "Verrin sells them back to the company stores. The company charges the families. The army pays twice, the families pay three times, and every transaction leaves a little sediment in the captain's pocket. That's all graft is, when you see it written down. Sediment. It settles."

"And nobody checks?"

"The officer who should check is Major Quill, who drinks with Verrin and owes him money. The civilian authority is the Atlas Company, whose clerks—" Tobias stopped, and his ears went red. "Whose clerks do what they're told. My father does what he's told. Everyone does what they're told, and the ledger just gets heavier."

Kestrel sat with the book open before him and felt something he couldn't immediately name. It took him a moment to recognize it, because he had so seldom felt it: power. Not the shadow of it, not the secondhand kind that came from knowing how to read someone else's numbers—but the actual weight of it, sitting under his palms. This book could end Verrin. One copy, placed in front of the right admiral, the right magistrate, the right newspaperman off one of the mail steamers, and the captain's career would come apart like wet rope.

"Copy it," Tobias whispered. "The graft pages only. Names, dates, amounts. Leave out anything that mentions how you got access. I'll take it to—"

"I know what to do with it," Kestrel said, and heard his own voice come out flat and cold, and was briefly surprised by it.

* * *

He copied through the small hours in the loft above the net shed, by a candle stub sheltered inside a broken barrel so the light wouldn't show from the water. Briony slept below; their father coughed in the main house in the rhythm Kestrel had learned to sleep against, the long rattle and the short relief, the long rattle and the short relief. He wrote small and even, the letters Tobias had taught him, and as he wrote he found he was thinking about letters themselves—about how a word like *sold* was the same shape whether it moved flour or men, and how a hand that could make those shapes could unmake a captain, and why had no one ever told him this? Why had they taught him knots instead? A knot holds what's in front of you. Letters held things that weren't even there anymore. Letters held the dead and the invented alike, four hundred men, two hundred and sixty of them real.

By the third hour he had eleven pages. Sold supplies. Phantom rations. The boots. The cordage. The quiet double-entry of Verrin's private wealth accumulating in the margins of a war. He read it back and it was clean, it was damning, it was exactly what Tobias had promised—and then, folding it, his thumb caught on the last page, and he stopped.

Because the eleventh page proved more than graft. To prove the graft, he had copied the phantom names—the invented workers, the dead men kept on the rolls. And beside the phantoms, in his neat schoolroom hand, he had transcribed the real names too, the ones that anchored each fraudulent entry: *Merrick, diver, conscripted Oct.R; Aldous, laborer; Dunmore, diver, processing pending.*

His father's name. His father's lungs, entered in evidence against the captain, filed away in Kestrel's coat like a stolen fish.

And below that, in his memory if nowhere else, the knowledge of how the copy had been made. A coal door. A lamplight. A boy in a borrowed greatcoat who was not supposed to know the annex existed, saying *I'm not supposed to know it exists,* which was itself a confession, which was itself, if it came to it, proof.

Kestrel sat very still in the barrel-shadowed light and understood, with the slow certainty of a diver feeling the pressure change, what he was actually holding.

Not a weapon against Verrin. A rope with two ends.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the loft until the window went grey, the eleven pages on his knees under both hands like something that might swim off, and turned it over the way you turn a shell looking for the crack.

Deliver it, and Verrin falls. That was the whole plan; it had been the whole plan since Tobias first laid it out, breathless, in the dark of the coal stair. And it would work—it would work precisely because the theft was real. An inquiry would need to know how the evidence left the records house. The answer was a boy with keys and a father in the company's employ and a history of small mercies toward a diver's son. The army would not ask whether the thief had acted from conscience. Armies asked one question about documents that walked, and the question was *who carried them*, and the answer was *Tobias Vane*, and there was a yard behind the guardhouse where they had hanged a smuggler in September, and everyone in town had been invited to watch, and the point of inviting everyone was so that everyone would remember it.

Tobias could hang for this. Not figuratively. Hang.

Unless Kestrel claimed the copying alone—which was impossible, because an unregistered diver could not have entered the records house without help, and claiming the help was naming the helper, and around it went, a circle drawn in chalk on a wet deck.

Or unless he didn't deliver it at all.

That was the other end of the rope, and Kestrel was honest enough with himself to feel how it pulled. Keep the pages. Keep the knowledge. Verrin suspected the forgery in the ledger—had said so, almost gently, the way a man mentions weather—and the eleven pages in Kestrel's coat were worth more than pearls now, worth more than berth money. They were the one thing a captain could be afraid of. And a captain who was afraid negotiated. Two berths. Three, maybe. A blind eye to the deep-bed diving. A man who held nothing bargained; a man who held this could dictate.

The thought arrived fully formed, wearing respectable clothes, and Kestrel made himself look at it directly, the way you look at a cut to judge how deep.

He thought: *I could feed my family.*

He thought: *This is exactly what Verrin does.*

It wasn't the same, some voice insisted—Verrin stole from the desperate, Kestrel would be bargaining for the desperate, the ends were different, the direction of the money flowed differently. But the mechanism was identical, and he could feel the mechanism, could feel how smoothly it ran once you stopped asking whose name was on the entry. Sell the truth or withhold the truth; either way, people became lines in your arithmetic. Tobias had become a line. His father's ruined lungs had become leverage. Even Briony, asleep below with her burned registration and her hard new silence, had become a reason—a justification, which was a kind of currency, and he was rich in it.

Power corrupts even those who only hold its shadow. He didn't think the sentence in those words, not yet, but he felt its meaning settle on him like cold water at depth: the pressure that changes a man doesn't care whether he descended on purpose.

Near dawn he heard Briony on the ladder and shoved the pages inside his shirt. She came up with two cups of chicory, handed him one, and sat looking out the loft window at the harbor, where the warship rode at anchor with its black sides and its patient smoke.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I worked."

"On what?"

"A bridge," he almost said, and didn't know why. "Copies," he said instead. "Of things."

She drank her chicory and watched the water and let the silence do its work, because she had learned that from him. Finally she said, without turning: "Whatever it is, Kess—it's written down somewhere now, isn't it? In your head or on paper. Something somebody doesn't want known."

He said nothing.

"Tobias came by the nets yesterday," Briony said. "Looking pale as gull-bone. Asking whether you'd gone through with it." She turned then, and her eyes were their mother's eyes, and their mother was three years drowned, and Kestrel had to look away. "He's frightened of you. Did you know that? His own friend. Frightened of what you're carrying."

"He shouldn't be."

"Nobody carries anything that heavy without it changing the shape of their shoulders." She stood, brushed her skirt, paused at the ladder. "Father says you get rid of a thing like that or it gets rid of you. Father also says obedience keeps you safe." Her mouth twisted. "So weigh whose sayings are still worth anything."

Her footsteps went down the ladder. Below, the coughing started again, long rattle, short relief, and Kestrel sat with eleven pages against his heart and the chicory going cold in his hand, paralyzed in the exact center of the rope—deliver and risk hanging his friend, withhold and become a smaller Verrin, bargain and teach himself that truth was stock-in-trade.

He was still sitting there when the knock came at the net shed door below. Three knocks, spaced evenly, official. Briony's voice went up, then another voice, a soldier's voice, and then a third voice that needed no introduction because it had announced every order in the harbor for three months.

Captain Verrin, saying, pleasantly, from below: "Master Dunmore. When you're decent. I'll only keep you a moment—I find myself short of certain supplies, and I'm told you're a young man who knows where things can be found."

The pages lay warm and hidden against Kestrel's chest, and he realized, rising, that the captain had come to make a trade—and that whatever Verrin wanted, the price would be named in the only coin Kestrel had left.

Someone else's loyalty. Someone else's name.

He started down the ladder.
