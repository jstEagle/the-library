## 11. The Raid

Briony heard the boots before she saw them.

That was the first lesson of occupation: you learned to hear through the soles of your feet. The divers' quarter was built on pilings over the tidal flats, and the whole neighborhood was a drum—every footstep on the boardwalk traveled down through the planks and into the water and came back up into your bones. She had spent her life reading the water that way, the way her brother read it from underneath. She knew the difference between a fisherman's tired shuffle and a company man's brisk clip, between a drunk's stagger and a debt collector's purposeful march.

She knew what soldiers sounded like by October.

They came in threes. Always threes. Two to hold you, one to write it down. That was the arithmetic of the army—everything in triplicate, everything witnessed, nothing real until ink touched paper. Briony had come to hate the sound of three sets of boots more than she hated Captain Verrin's name, which she never said aloud anymore, because saying a thing gave it weight and she was trying, these days, to carry less.

She was mending nets on the porch when they passed. Not stopping—passing. Headed for the Maren house at the end of the row, where old Sefa Maren lived with her son's widow and two grandchildren, and where, as the whole quarter knew by now, there lived also a young man named Ivo Cade who had come down from the herring camps six weeks ago with no papers at all.

* * *

The registration order had gone up in September, nailed to the company house door in letters so formal they might have been carved: ALL PERSONS RESIDENT WITHIN THE REQUISITIONED DISTRICT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR ENUMERATION. FAILURE TO REGISTER SHALL CONSTITUTE EVIDENCE OF FUGITIVE STATUS.

Fugitive status. As if the town were a prison and everyone born in it an escapee.

Father had gone. Of course he'd gone—he went where he was told, always had, and look where it had landed him, half a man coughing his lungs out in a bed that smelled of camphor and salt rot. Kestrel had not gone, because Kestrel was dead now, officially, on paper—a fiction Briony still could not say out loud without feeling the floor tilt under her. And Briony herself had stood in line for four hours in the rain and given her name to a clerk who wrote it down wrong—*Bryony Dunmore*, with a y like a flower—and she had corrected him twice and he had ignored her both times, and in the end she had walked away registered under a name that was almost hers, which felt like being recorded by someone who wasn't listening, which was, she understood later, exactly what it was.

Ivo Cade had not gone. Ivo Cade had no name to give them. He'd washed up in the quarter with a story about the herring camps closing and a face that said the story was only half of it, and Sefa Maren, who had buried a husband and a son to the sea and owed the Atlas Company money on both funerals, had put him in her loft and fed him from her own bowl and told no one.

Everyone knew. That was the thing about the quarter—everyone knew everything, and everyone kept it, the way you keep water behind a weir. For six weeks the knowledge sat there, held back by nothing but the shared understanding that the army's appetite was bottomless and you did not feed it if you could help it.

The boots stopped at the Maren door.

* * *

Briony did not go to watch. That was the second lesson: watching made you a witness, and witnesses got written down too. She sat on the porch with the net in her lap and her hands moving in their old patterns—over, under, draw tight—and she listened.

Sefa's voice first, high and cracked with age but steady. Then a soldier's voice, flat, reciting something. Then silence, the particular terrible silence of a house deciding what it is going to do.

Then Ivo's voice, low, and Sefa's again, louder now, and then the sound every person in the quarter had learned to dread: the sound of a door being forced. Not opened—forced. Wood splitting along the grain, the latch tearing out of the frame, and then the boots inside, and then shouting, and then a woman screaming—not words, just sound, the widow Maren, and the children crying in the way children cry when they understand more than anyone wants them to.

Briony's hands had stopped moving. The net hung slack between her fists.

They brought him out past her porch. There were four of them this time, not three—Ivo must have struggled—and they had his arms bent up behind his back at angles arms weren't meant to go, and his face was bleeding from the mouth, and he was not struggling anymore because there was nothing left to struggle against. Sefa Maren was on her knees on the boardwalk behind them, and the strange thing, the thing Briony would carry for the rest of her life, was that Sefa wasn't reaching for Ivo. She was reaching for the soldier carrying the book—the clerk-soldier with the satchel—and she was saying, over and over, *write me down instead, write me down instead,* as if the ledger were a mouth that could be bargained with, as if it had ever once accepted a substitute.

The soldier didn't even look at her. He was checking his list.

They took Ivo Cade down the boardwalk toward the requisitioned warehouses by the deep-water pier, and Briony watched him go, and she noticed—because noticing was all she had—that he turned his head once, deliberately, and looked back at the quarter. Not at Sefa. At the whole row of houses, the nets, the drying racks, the smoke rising from the chimneys of people who had hidden him and failed. He looked at it the way you look at something you are saying goodbye to, and then the soldiers turned him around the corner by the salt shed and he was gone.

He never came back. Not that day, not that week, not ever. No one was told where. No record was offered. When Sefa Maren went to the company house to ask, a lieutenant informed her that no person matching the description of Ivo Cade appeared in any register, and therefore, formally, no such person existed, and therefore, formally, nothing had happened to him.

That was the day Briony Dunmore understood the true shape of the thing they were living inside. It was not cruelty. Cruelty would have been simpler—it would have meant the army wanted something from them, even if what it wanted was their suffering. But this was worse. This was erasure dressed as procedure. The army didn't need to kill you. It only needed to fail to write you down, and then whatever happened afterward happened to no one at all.

* * *

She burned her registration card that night.

Not immediately. First she sat with it a long time, turning it over in her fingers in the lamplight while Father slept his drugged half-sleep in the back room and Kestrel was out—out doing whatever it was he did now, coming home at odd hours with money he wouldn't explain and bruises he wouldn't show, diving the deep beds illegally, she was nearly sure, wearing the face of a dead man because the living ones had no room left on any list.

The card was cheap stock, army gray, soft at the corners from her pocket. BRYONY DUNMORE, it said. OCCUPATION: NETMENDER, DEPENDENT OF INDENTURED DIVER. STATUS: REGISTERED. And below that, in smaller print, the sentence that had seemed like boilerplate in September and looked different now: THIS CARD MUST BE PRODUCED ON DEMAND. FAILURE TO PRODUCE SHALL CONSTITUTE EVIDENCE OF FUGITIVE STATUS.

On demand. Whenever they wanted. Wherever they wanted. Her whole legal existence folded into a piece of gray paper that some corporal could ask for on a whim, and if she'd lost it, or it had rotted from the damp the way everything rotted in the quarter, or if a soldier simply decided to say she hadn't shown it—then she became Ivo Cade. A person to whom nothing could happen, because officially there was no person there.

The card wasn't protection. She saw that now. It was a leash, and the other end of it was in a filing drawer in the records annex, and the men who held that end had demonstrated, with Ivo, exactly how much the leash mattered when they chose to ignore it.

But burning it—burning it was its own kind of madness. A registered person could be evacuated when the ships came. Everyone whispered about the ships now; the rumor had gone through the quarter like blood through water. Berths assigned strictly by ledger status, the company men said. Your card was your ticket off this rock. Burn the card and you burned your place in the line, your name in the book, your claim to be counted among the saved.

Briony held the card over the lamp and thought about her mother, who had died owing the company eleven pounds and whose grave marker was a plank of driftwood because the company charged for stone. She thought about Sefa Maren kneeling on the boards, offering herself to a ledger that didn't take trades. She thought about the clerk writing *Bryony* with a y, not listening, never listening, and about how somewhere in the annex there was a page with her almost-name on it, and how that page could be copied, sold, amended, lost, used—by Verrin, by whoever came after Verrin, by anyone with a stamp and a conscience with a hole in it.

If they wanted to erase people, she thought, then let them find out what it cost to erase someone who refused to be written in the first place.

She touched the corner of the card to the flame.

It caught slowly, the way cheap paper does, curling brown at the edge before the fire took hold, and she held it as long as she could bear to—not because she doubted, but because she wanted to feel it, wanted the small burn of the heat climbing toward her fingers, wanted the choice to cost something. Then she dropped it into the iron pot by the stove and watched her name go up in a breath of smoke.

Bryony. With a y. Let the army search its books forever for a woman who had never quite existed.

* * *

Kestrel found the ashes before she could explain.

She heard him come in past midnight—the third board from the door, the one that sang—and then the pause, the long silence of him standing over the pot, and she knew he was reading the gray flakes the way he read everything now, looking for the trap in it.

"You burned it." His voice was careful. Careful the way ice is careful.

"I burned it."

"Briony." He came into the lamplight, and she saw the dive-bloat still in his face, the red rimmed eyes of a man who'd been deeper than his body liked. "The ships. The berths go by the ledger. Without the card—"

"The card didn't save Ivo Cade."

"Ivo Cade wasn't registered. That's my point. That's exactly—" He stopped. She watched him arrive at it, watched the understanding move across his face like weather. Registered people got evacuated. Unregistered people got disappeared. And she had just walked herself, voluntarily, across that line.

"They can't sell what they can't list," she said. "They can't trade me, or barter me, or amend me. You've seen what the amendments look like, Kestrel. You're the one who taught me." She hadn't meant to say that last part—it slipped out with an edge she didn't fully intend—but she didn't take it back either, because it was true, and because some part of her had been angry at him since the night she found the forged page and understood that her brother had bought his safety by unmaking himself, and that the family's debt had not diminished by one penny.

"That's different," he said quietly.

"It's exactly the same. You erased yourself to slip the hook. I erased myself to slip the hook. The only difference is you feel guilty about yours and you want me to feel guilty about mine."

He sat down across from her, heavily, the way Father used to sit down, and for a moment he looked so much like Father that her chest hurt. "There has to be a way back," he said. "From this. There has to be a version where we get on the ship."

"Is there?" She kept her voice low, for Father sleeping. "One berth, Kestrel. Maybe two, if the money comes. Four of us. You've been doing the sums longer than I have. Tell me the version where the ledger gives us back what we need."

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

"Then here's mine," she said. "They think paper is power. Fine. So we take the power back the only way left. Nobody in this quarter writes their names anymore. Nobody registers, nobody amends, nobody signs. Let them raid every loft and loft-hole from here to the salt shed—they'll find people, but they won't find *persons*. You can't conscript a ghost. You can't bill a ghost. You can't put a ghost on a list, and if enough of us become ghosts—"

"They'll stop caring about lists," Kestrel finished slowly. "And start caring about ropes."

"Maybe." She met his eyes. "Or maybe they'll discover that a town that isn't in their books doesn't owe them anything. You memorized the ledger—all those names, all those debts. You carry it better than the paper does. Well. Now I'm carrying mine." She tapped her own breastbone, where the card used to sit. "And Sefa's. And Ivo Cade's, who isn't in any book anywhere, and is therefore mine to remember too."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the tide was turning, and the pilings creaked their old slow song, and somewhere down the row a baby cried and was hushed.

"You sound like the fire sermon old Anselm used to give," Kestrel said at last, and there was something in his voice she couldn't name—fear, maybe, or its cousin, awe.

"Anselm's dead."

"Then somebody else has to say it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and when he looked up at her his face had changed—harder, and more awake. "Because there's something you need to know. Something I was going to tell you tomorrow, but it can't wait now. The records annex. The original ledger pages, the requisition copies, everything Verrin uses to own us—it's all in one building, Briony. One building, one lock, and I know when the guard changes."

The lamp flickered between them.

"And I'm going to burn it," Kestrel said.
