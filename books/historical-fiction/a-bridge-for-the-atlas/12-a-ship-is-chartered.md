## 12. A Ship Is Chartered

The news came with the morning tide and a man named Corr Whelan, who ran the message trade between the harbor and the garrison road because his leg was ruined and his mouth was not.

Kestrel was mending the punt when Corr came down the slipway, hopping on his stick, shouting before he reached the waterline.

"They've chartered her," he called. "A ship! The *Meridian*, out of the capital, black-hulled, four hundred tons if she's an ounce. Coming before the year turns. Evacuation berth for every soul the ledger says is owed passage—and not one soul more."

The words moved down the beach faster than Corr did. Kestrel watched them go, door to door, net shed to smokehouse, the way fire moves through dry grass—each household taking the flame of it and passing it on before it could burn them. By the time the sun cleared the headland, the whole divers' quarter was standing outside in the cold, talking at once, and nobody was listening to anybody.

Briony found him at the water's edge. Her hands were red from the nets and shaking, though whether from cold or something else he couldn't tell.

"Four hundred tons," she said. "How many berths is that?"

"Fewer than us."

"How many fewer?"

He didn't answer, and that was the answer.

* * *

By noon the company house had hung a notice in the window, and the crowd pressed against the glass to read what only three or four of them could. Tobias read it aloud from the steps, his voice flat as a clerk's, which was maybe the only way he could get through it:

*By order of the military administration: passage aboard the vessel Meridian will be assigned strictly according to ledger standing. Families whose accounts are settled or settled-in-part shall receive priority of berth. Registered persons in default shall be considered second. Unregistered persons shall not be conveyed.*

*One berth per registered adult. One berth per two registered minors.*

That last line did the worst work. Kestrel heard it land all down the street, in the little catches of breath, the sudden silences where families were doing sums they'd never done before. One berth per two minors. He was seventeen now by the ledger's count; Briony fifteen. Together they made one and a half berths. Their father, lungs ruined, registered, in default, was worth less than nothing—a debt wearing a man's coat. Their mother had been dead six years, but the ledger still carried her name because debts outlive the debtor.

"Settled-in-part," said old Maren Cade behind him, spitting into the shingle. "Who among us is settled-in-part? We're all of us in default down to our teeth."

"Not all," someone said, and Kestrel turned to see who'd said it, and everyone turned, and the someone shut their mouth too late.

That was the first crack. He would remember it afterward—the moment the quarter stopped being a place where people stood together against the wind and became instead a line of separate houses, each counting its own arithmetic behind its own door.

* * *

The bribing started quietly, the way everything quiet starts.

Hedda Sorrell, who kept the smokehouse and had three sons and no husband, went door to door the first evening with a jar of smoked eels wrapped in cloth, offering it as a gift, asking nothing, and everybody understood exactly what she was asking. The Widow Fenn, who could read better than most and had once worked in the company laundry, began charging a penny to copy out people's account numbers "so as you can check your standing"—a service worth nothing that she sold for a coin because a coin might buy a berth off somebody desperate enough to sell one.

Kestrel sat with Briony by the stove and listened to it all come through the walls.

"Maren Cade told the registrar's man that the Pell family has a cousin in the hills who never registered," Briony reported. She said it without inflection, the way you report weather. "Maren Cade, who pulled Brack Pell out of the water when we were children. Pulled him out herself, mouth to mouth."

"She wants a berth for her granddaughter."

"Everyone wants a berth for someone." Briony stared into the stove. "That's the thing about it, Kess. Nobody's doing evil. They're just doing sums."

And there it was—the thought he'd been circling for two days without wanting to land on it. He had believed, in the way you believe things you learned before you could remember learning them, that cruelty was a kind of person. That the men who took his father, the clerk who inked their names into columns, the captain in his gray coat—all of them must be monsters somewhere deep, monsters all the way down. But watching the quarter dissolve, he understood the machine differently. You didn't need monsters. You needed only scarcity. Put four hundred tons of ship against nine hundred souls and you didn't have to make anyone cruel. You just had to make them count.

The ledger didn't turn neighbors into betrayers. It turned them into accountants.

* * *

His father heard the news from bed and laughed—a sound Kestrel hadn't heard from him in months, and worse for that.

"One ship," Dunmore said. His voice was gravel and wet rope. "One ship, and half this town will drown each other for the rail. I've seen it. Two men and one air line, down in the deep beds. You know how that ends."

"You're not diving anymore."

"No," his father said, and looked at his own hands, which had been hands worth looking at once. "No. Now I'm the weight, aren't I. Every family's got one now. The old one. The sick one. The one whose berth arithmetic doesn't work." He closed his eyes. "Your mother should have lived. She'd have been worth a whole berth on her own."

"Don't talk like that."

"Somebody in every house is going to say it before the *Meridian* docks," his father said. "Somebody in every house is doing the sum right now, and pretending they aren't. Go on, boy. Leave me be."

* * *

On the third day, the denunciations started.

They began as questions asked in the wrong tone of voice. *Isn't it true the Sorrells never paid their winter installment? Isn't it true young Pell dove the east beds without registering? Isn't it true—* and then, on the fourth day, a list appeared nailed to the chapel door, eight names written in a fair round hand, each name followed by the word *UNREGISTERED* and the phrase *known to frequent*, and after that the registrar's man came around with a notebook and the questions stopped being questions.

Kestrel read the list twice. None of the names surprised him; that was the horror of it. Everyone in the quarter could have written it. Everyone in the quarter, given a night and a grudge and the promise of consideration, would have written most of it. He himself knew a ninth name the list didn't hold, and the ninth name was his own, dead on paper these many weeks, unregistered in fact, diving the deep beds by lantern light for coins that no ledger traced—and he had walked past the chapel door and felt, for one long cold moment, the animal relief of the hidden, that it wasn't him up there in ink.

Then he felt sick at the feeling, and then he felt sick at feeling sick, and he understood that the machine had him too. It didn't need his betrayal yet. It was patient. It had shown him the shape of the thing he might become, and the shape fit.

* * *

He went to find Briony and couldn't, and the not-finding lasted an hour that aged him a year. When he finally tracked her down she was at the north slip, sitting with her back against the overturned punt, and she was burning paper in the lee of the hull—a small careful fire, feeding it sheet by sheet.

Her registration. And their father's. And the copies of the account pages she'd been keeping since autumn, the ones with the interest figures circled, the ones she'd used to do the family sums.

"What are you doing," he said, though he could see.

"If we're not listed," she said, feeding another page to the flames, "we can't be ranked. Can't be compared. Can't be weighed against Maren Cade's granddaughter or Hedda's three boys. Let them try to sort ash."

"Briony—that's your berth you're burning. Ours. If the ledger says nothing—"

"Then the ledger can't say no." She looked up at him, and her face was calm in a way that frightened him more than tears would have. "You taught me the letters, Kess. You and Tobias both. So hear me: I'm done being written. Whatever happens with this ship, it happens to us as people, not as entries. People can choose. Entries can only be sorted."

The fire ate the last page and she scattered the ashes with her boot, and the wind took them out over the water, over the beds where their father had earned their lives and lost his lungs, out toward the open sea where a black-hulled ship was even now loading its scarce, terrible mercy.

* * *

That night Kestrel climbed to the ridge above the town and looked at the harbor, because he needed to see it small. From up here the quarter was a scatter of lamps, the company house a block of darker dark, the garrison fires a row of orange eyes along the requisition road. A town. Just a town. Fog-bound, poor, half of it leaning toward the water like tired old men.

And somewhere down there in the dark, seven hundred people were lying awake deciding—not whether they were good people, because they all still believed they were good people, but what goodness cost, and who would pay, and whether love of one's own children was a bigger number than loyalty to everybody else's.

Scarcity. That was the whole engine. Not cruelty, not the gray coats, not Verrin's cold smile. Just four hundred tons against nine hundred souls, and the arithmetic doing what arithmetic does.

He thought of Tobias, trusted with the copies. He thought of the forged page, *deceased*, in his own remembered hand. He thought of Verrin, who suspected, who watched, who needed only one name.

Down in the harbor, riding at anchor where the fishing fleet used to cluster thick as stars, lay a single navy tender, and a signal lamp on its deck was blinking steadily toward the roadstead—messages going out across the black water about berths, manifests, standing, priority. Even from the ridge he could feel it: the century was closing like a ledger, and there was room in it for so few.

When he came down the hill, Tobias was waiting at his door, and he had never seen the clerk's son look like this—gray-faced, hatless, holding a satchel against his chest the way you hold a wound.

"Kess," Tobias said. "I have to tell you what I did."
