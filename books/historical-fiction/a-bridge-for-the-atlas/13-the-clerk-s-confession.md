## 13. The Clerk's Confession

Tobias Vane had been rehearsing the confession for eleven days, and every rehearsal had failed.

He'd tried it standing in front of the washstand mirror in the clerk's quarters above the company house, watching his own mouth move around the words like a man chewing something spoiled. *I gave him the pages. I gave Verrin the pages.* The mirror didn't flinch. That was the worst of it — that he could say it to glass and glass would forgive him, and Kestrel Dunmore would not.

He'd tried it walking the seawall at dusk, when the garrison bugle sounded retreat and the soldiers' boots made a rhythm on the parade ground that drowned out everything human. He'd tried it counting pearls into assay trays, each click of shell against brass a small metronome ticking toward the moment he could no longer postpone. He'd tried it in prayer, though Tobias hadn't prayed since he was nine years old and his mother died, and the words came out sounding like an inventory: one sin, amended pages, surrendered under duress, value negligible.

Value negligible. That was what the army called a man's name.

Tonight he would tell him. Tonight, because tomorrow the berth lists went up on the company house door, and after that there would be nothing left to confess — only consequences to survive.

* * *

The walk from the company house to the divers' quarter took twelve minutes if you took the cliff path and fifteen if you took the road, and Tobias always took the cliff path because the road passed the guard post and the sentries had started asking clerks for their papers, which was absurd, since the clerks were the ones who made the papers. He carried a lantern with the shutter turned low, a coin of light that showed him three feet of wet rock at a time. Below him the harbor breathed. Above him the requisition annex sat black against the stars, its new padlock catching the lantern glow like a small yellow eye.

He hated that building now. He had loved it once. He had learned to read in it, or rather he had taught himself to read in it, pulling down ledgers from the lower shelves while his father tallied freight and pretending he was looking for misfiled entries. A ledger was just a book that told the truth badly. That was the first thing he'd understood about them, at ten years old, and it had taken him nine more years to understand how much damage a book could do when it lied well.

Kestrel was waiting where they always met, in the lee of the net-drying shed where the tar smell covered conversation from any passing patrol. He came out of the dark the way he came out of water — no splash, no warning, just suddenly present, as if he'd been there all along and had simply decided to be visible.

"You're late," Kestrel said.

"I counted the assay trays twice." It wasn't an excuse. It was a symptom. "Kestrel, I have to tell you something."

He watched Kestrel's face do the thing it did when trouble arrived — not fear exactly, but a kind of narrowing, the whole face contracting toward the eyes, a man making himself smaller so the blow would land on less of him. Kestrel Dunmore had been making himself small his entire life. Tobias had watched him do it in the company house a hundred times, head bowed over the counter, voice pitched to the register of furniture, invisible in plain sight. And then last autumn Tobias had put a ledger page in front of him and said, *this word is your name,* and Kestrel had stopped being invisible for one terrible second, staring at himself reduced to a sum, and neither of them had ever quite recovered.

"Verrin has the original pages," Tobias said. "The ones we amended. I gave them to him."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the tide to turn. Tobias knew the sound of the tide turning; he'd grown up with it; it was a soft exhalation under the pilings, the sea deciding which way to lean. In that exhalation Kestrel said nothing at all, and the nothing was worse than shouting.

"When?" Kestrel finally said. His voice was very level. Level voices were the dangerous ones; Tobias had processed enough conscription interviews to know that the men who screamed were the men who still believed someone might listen.

"The night after we forged the entry. Four days ago."

"Why."

Not *how*, Tobias noticed. Not *what did you give him*. Why. Because Kestrel Dunmore thought in causes the way other men thought in coins.

"My father." Tobias heard his own voice go thin, the way it did when he read casualty figures aloud to the captain and made himself think of them as figures. "Verrin called my father in. Showed him a transfer order — a good posting, inland, away from the war, the kind of posting that means a man keeps his pension and his health. And then he showed him a second document. An investigation file. My father's countersignature on freight manifests that didn't match the stores. Phantom barrels, phantom sacks. Except they weren't phantom, Kestrel. They were real. They were Verrin's. Sold off the back of the annex for months, and my father's signature on the receiving slips because my father signs whatever the army puts in front of him, that's his whole life, he signs things—"

"Breathe," Kestrel said quietly.

"I'm telling you why."

"I know why." Kestrel looked out at the harbor, at the evacuation ship riding at anchor with her riding lamps lit, a constellation moored to one spot of water. "You gave him me instead of your father."

"No." And here was the part Tobias had never managed to say in any rehearsal, the part that made the mirror-blurred version of himself look away. "I gave him both. I stood in that office and I let him believe it was a choice between them, and I chose, and I've been telling myself it wasn't a choice because what else could I do, and that is exactly what everyone in this town says when they inform on their neighbors, isn't it? What else could I do. I've become one of those sentences."

Kestrel was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something flat and careful, a diver's voice, conserving air. "What exactly does Verrin have?"

"The original copy of the conscription annex. Page forty-one. Your entry, amended. 'Dunmore, K., deceased, 14 November, fever.' In my hand over your hand. Verrin has it folded inside his dispatch case. He showed it to me. He wanted me to see that he'd known all along."

"He's known for four days and hasn't arrested anyone."

"He doesn't want arrests." Tobias felt the awful clarity of it, the thing he'd been circling for eleven days finally landing. "Arrests make noise. Noise makes inquiries. Verrin wants leverage. He's holding the page the way a moneylender holds a note. He's waiting to see what we'll do to buy it back."

"And what did he ask you to do?"

"Nothing yet. That's what frightens me. He thanked me." Tobias's stomach turned again at the memory, the captain's dry handshake, the smell of brandy and gun oil. "He shook my hand and said the company needed steady men, and that he hoped I'd continue to be useful, and he wrote something in his pocket book, and I walked out of that office understanding that I work for him now. Not the company. Him. There's a difference, Kestrel. I used to think there wasn't."

* * *

They sat down on the upturned dory beneath the shed wall, the way they had when they were boys, when the worst secret between them was that Tobias had taught Kestrel his letters using the debt column and Kestrel had never once complained about learning to read his own servitude. For a while neither spoke. The lantern between them made a small warm country with borders two feet wide.

"I keep trying to find the version where I didn't do it," Tobias said. "I lie awake and run the night again and look for the door I missed. There isn't one. That's what I need you to understand. Verrin had my father cold. One word from him and my father loses his position, his pension, maybe his liberty — those manifests are forgery-adjacent even though he didn't know, and nobody in an army court cares about didn't know. I could burn the town down and it wouldn't un-sign those slips. So I handed over the pages, and I told myself the pages weren't the thing. The amendment lives in our heads. You're still marked deceased in the true record, the one that matters—"

"The one that matters is the one Verrin reads from," Kestrel said. "I learned that in October, standing in a queue with a number chalked on my coat."

"Yes." Tobias swallowed. "Yes, that's fair. That's exactly fair."

"It's not about fair." Kestrel rubbed his face with both hands, and in the lantern light Tobias saw how much thinner he'd gotten since the deep-bed diving started, the cheekbones standing out like keel timbers. "It's about what happens next. Does Verrin know I know?"

"Not yet. He thinks you don't suspect. He thinks the forgery is his private card to play."

"Then we have a little room. Not much." Kestrel stared at the anchored ship. "You understand what you've done, though. You haven't just given him a page. You've given him proof that names can be moved. Before tonight, the ledger was a wall. Now it's a door, and Verrin holds the key, and everyone in this harbor who can pay will start asking him to open it. Berths, exemptions, dead brothers resurrected for a price. You've shown the machine it can be bribed."

"I know." Tobias's voice cracked and he let it. "I've had four days to know it. Do you want to know the worst part? The genuinely worst part? When he shook my hand, some small filthy part of me was relieved. Relieved. Because my father was safe, and because being trusted by power feels — Kestrel, it feels *good*. That's the discovery. That's what I came here to confess, more than the pages. Everyone thinks corruption is men like Verrin forcing it on people like us. It isn't. It's offered. It's offered gently, with a handshake, and it fits your hand like it was made for it, and the only thing that ever stops you taking it is remembering whose name is written in the column."

Kestrel was silent. Then he said, "Who taught you that sentence?"

"You did. Standing in front of the ledger, nine years old, looking at your own name priced at eleven pounds four shillings. You looked at it and you said, 'That's not me. That's what they think I cost.' I've been trying to live up to that sentence for ten years and I failed last Tuesday in a warm office with brandy on the desk."

"That's not—" Kestrel started, and stopped, and started again. "Tobias. You were nine years older than that sentence too. You taught me the letters. Every name I can read, I can read because of you. Whatever you did in that office, you did it for your father, and I would have done it for mine, and Briony would do it for either of us, and that's the trap, don't you see? That's the whole machine. Nobody betrays anybody out of wickedness. Verrin doesn't need wicked men. He needs men who love somebody. Love is the raw material. Everything else is processing."

Tobias found he couldn't speak. Ten years of friendship, ten years of shared contraband letters and stolen hours, and Kestrel had just forgiven him in the same breath as condemning him, and the forgiveness hurt worse than anger would have, because anger would have let Tobias punish himself cleanly and forgiveness meant they were both still inside the machine together.

"There's something else," Tobias said, when he could. "About the copies."

"What copies?"

"The evidence. Verrin's graft. The manifests, the sale records, the phantom workers on the blockade payroll. I told you I could get copies from the annex files." Tobias pulled his collar tighter against the wind coming off the water. "I can't. Or — I can, but it doesn't matter anymore, because two days ago Verrin reorganized the annex. New locks, new filing order, and the old manifests are gone from the general files. He's consolidated them somewhere I can't reach. Which means he's been preparing for exposure since before I gave him the pages. Which means—"

"He expected betrayal," Kestrel said. "He built the office scene before he staged it. He knew you'd trade. He just needed to watch you do it, so he'd know what you're worth and what you'll carry."

The lantern guttered. Tobias trimmed the wick with a fingernail, hands steadier than they had any right to be. "I used to think I understood power because I filed its paperwork. I thought the man who knows where everything is written owns the truth. But Verrin doesn't care where anything is written. He cares what people are afraid of. Paper is just how fear is stored."

* * *

They talked until the lantern oil ran low, and what they built in that time was not a plan so much as a shape of a plan, edges unfinished. The berth list posted tomorrow. One ship. Ledger status deciding everything, and Kestrel legally dead, and Father Dunmore's lungs failing by inches, and Briony's registration burned in a stove in November because she refused to be listed in a book that had taken a neighbor with no papers and returned nothing. None of it fit together. None of it fit anywhere. But they mapped it anyway, the way you map a reef by feel in bad light, marking the places that would sink you.

At the edge of the shed's shadow, before they parted, Tobias caught Kestrel's sleeve.

"If it comes to it," he said, "if Verrin moves, and there's a choice between saving yourself and telling him it was me — you tell him it was me. Promise me. I've already lost the thing I was protecting. My father is safe whether I hang or not; that was the trade; I made it; I'll stand behind it. But you have a sister on that ship's list and a father in a bed, and I won't have my cowardice spend your family twice."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment, and Tobias saw something move behind his eyes that he couldn't read — grief, arithmetic, love, all three running in the same current.

"Don't make promises for me," Kestrel said. "That's what Verrin does. He decides what people will trade before they've chosen." He stepped back into the dark, and his voice came out of it, low and final: "Go home, Tobias. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow the list goes up, and we'll both learn what our names are worth on paper."

Tobias climbed the cliff path with the dead lantern swinging at his knee. Behind him the harbor lay black and patient, and above it the annex stood with its yellow-eyed lock, and somewhere inside that building, in a dispatch case with a brass clasp, a dead boy's name waited in a dead man's handwriting for the morning.

He did not sleep. At dawn, from the clerk's window, he watched the crowd gather at the company house door, and he watched a soldier climb a ladder with a sheaf of papers and a hammer, and he heard the first nail go in like a shot.

The list was going up. And pinned to its top corner, in the captain's own hand, was a notice no one had predicted:

*All persons claiming exemption by reason of death must appear for verification of record within three days.*

Verrin was calling the dead to account.
