## 14. Dead Man Diving

The notice went up on a Tuesday. By Wednesday, Kestrel had stopped being dead in any way that mattered to the army and started being dead in the only way that paid.

He dove the deep beds.

No registered man was permitted past the second reef — that had always been company law, and now it was army law too, chalked on the requisition board in Captain Verrin's tidy hand: *Diving beyond the marker buoys is forbidden. Violators will be treated as runaways.* But the second reef was picked clean. Twenty years of Atlas Company quotas had stripped the shallows the way locusts strip a field, and what pearls remained grew fat and lonely in the cold dark past the buoys, where the water turned from green to ink and the light came down in threads instead of sheets.

Only three men in the harbor had ever worked those depths regularly. One was Kestrel's father, who now could not climb the stairs to his own bed without resting twice. One was old Sef Marlowe, drowned there eleven years ago. And one was Kestrel Dunmore, aged seventeen, deceased according to the ledger of the Atlas Trading Company.

A dead man needs no registration. A dead man leaves no name at the pay window, no entry at the tally desk, no trace in any book Verrin might seize. Whatever he earns belongs to no one — which is to say it can belong to whoever holds it in his fist when the counting is done.

That was the theory. The practice was colder.

* * *

He went out before dawn each morning, hauling the skiff by hand along the shingle so the oar-locks wouldn't knock and wake the patrol on the seawall. Briony came as far as the tideline with him, holding the lamp low, her face a pale smear in its glow.

"Three hours," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. It had stopped being a question the fourth morning, when she'd realized asking changed nothing.

"Three hours."

"And you come up between every descent."

"I come up."

She didn't believe him. He could see she didn't believe him, and he let her not believe him, because the truth was that he'd already started stretching the intervals, shaving minutes off his surface rests the way a miser shaves copper off a coin, and if he admitted it out loud she would find some way to stop him, and there was no way to stop him that didn't cost more than the diving did.

The arithmetic sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Three berths on the evacuation ship: forty crowns each for steerage passage, or one hundred and twenty total. He had, after six days of illegal work, thirty-one crowns and four shillings sewn into the lining of his oilskin. At this rate — good days, full baskets, no storms — he would have enough by the end of February. The ship sailed the last week of January.

So he dove longer. Deeper. He learned to read the cold layers the way his father had tried to teach him years ago, back when the lessons were about pride and not survival: *the sea lies to you, boy, but it lies consistently, and a man who knows its lies can trust it better than a man who tells them.* He found a bed off the third shelf nobody had touched since the warships came, mussels crusted over pearl-bearers like coins buried under moss, and he worked it until his fingers went numb past feeling, prying and pocketing in the dark, his world shrunk to the width of his arms and the burn in his chest that said *up, up, up.*

He counted his breaths on the way down. Sixty strokes to the bed. He counted his breaths on the way up, because the temptation, he was learning, was to stop counting — to let the surface be a rumor somewhere above him and just rest, just hang in the gray water where nothing was owed and nothing was due and no ledger held his name.

On the ninth day he came up with a basket worth eleven crowns, and Tobias was waiting on the shingle.

* * *

"You look terrible," Tobias said.

"You should see the other diver."

"There is no other diver. That's the whole problem." Tobias fell into step beside him as Kestrel dragged the skiff above the wrack line. His clerk's coat was buttoned wrong, Kestrel noticed. His hands were shaking. "There are soldiers at the verification tables asking questions. About a deceased diver, young, working the deep beds. Fishermen talk, Kes. Old Pell saw your skiff past the buoys Tuesday and told a sergeant for a flask of rum."

Kestrel set the basket down and began sorting pearls by lamplight, because his hands needed work or they'd start shaking too. "What did Verrin say?"

"That's the thing." Tobias crouched, lowered his voice even though the beach was empty. "Verrin isn't looking to arrest you. He's looking to *hire* you. There's a wreck off the point — an army transport, went down in the storm last month with a strongbox aboard. Pay chest. He wants it recovered quietly, before the navy auditors arrive in spring, and he can't order registered divers to do it because then there's a record. But a dead man—" Tobias made a small, bitter gesture. "A dead man doesn't sign anything. A dead man's wages are whatever falls out of his pockets."

"He offered you this?"

"He offered to forget you exist. Which from Verrin is practically a benediction." Tobias looked out at the water, at the place where the buoys bobbed black against the graying sky. "Kes, don't take it."

"Why not?"

"Because men who work for Verrin off the books have a way of staying off the books permanently. You know what I'm saying. A witness is just a ledger entry he hasn't inked yet."

Kestrel kept sorting. Eleven crowns. With the wreck money — and a pay chest would hold hundreds — three berths became possible. Four. He could buy his mother's passage too, and Father's if Father would go, and—

"No," he heard himself say. And then, surprising himself more: "Not yet. Tell him I'll consider it."

Tobias stared. "You just said—"

"I know what I said." He closed the basket. "Tell him I need to think. That buys me a day."

It bought him two. He used them to dive the third-shelf bed until his ears bled, and he told himself the blood in his sputum on the second evening was from the cold air, and Briony looked at him across the net-mending with an expression he refused to translate, and on the twelfth day the weather broke.

* * *

The storm came out of the northeast without much warning, the kind of squall that turns the harbor to boiling milk in an hour. Kestrel was on the third shelf when it hit.

He felt it first as a change in the pressure overhead, a great slow drumbeat through the water column, and then the visibility died all at once — not fogging but *churning*, the whole sea stirred like tea, and the thread of light above him snapped and was gone. He was in absolute blackness at nine fathoms, alone, with a basket half full and his lungs half empty.

Panic is a thing his father had trained out of him early, or thought he had. *Breathe out slow going up. Never race the surface. The sea takes the ones who hurry.* Kestrel exhaled and kicked for the light that wasn't there, counting strokes, sixty to the boat, except the boat was no longer where he'd left it, or the current had moved him, or the world had simply been rearranged while he worked, and at stroke fifty his lungs began their screaming, and at stroke seventy he was still rising through black water that gave him nothing, no light, no sound but his own heart slamming like a fist on a door.

He surfaced once — he thought — gasped something that was mostly harbor water, went down again. Came up on the hull of the skiff itself, clawed over the gunwale the way a landed fish claws, and lay in the bilge while the storm tried to capsize him, coughing salt and blood into the scuppers.

He lay there a long time. Long enough for the rain to soak him through. Long enough to do the arithmetic properly, finally, without the diving-numbness to blur the figures.

One hundred and twenty crowns for three berths. He had perhaps fifty, counting the pearls drying in the hidden cache under the floorboards. Even with the wreck — even selling his lungs to Verrin by the fathom — he could not close the gap in five weeks. Not alive. The sea had just shown him the exchange rate, and it was exact: every crown cost breath, and his account was nearly drawn.

Three berths. Mother. Father. Briony.

His father couldn't survive the voyage; Dr. Quennel had said as much, gently, in the way doctors say things they've said too often. His father wouldn't leave anyway — wouldn't leave the town, wouldn't leave the beds, wouldn't leave the graves of his own father and grandfather out on the point. So one berth was spoken for in every direction except the paper, and the paper was the whole world now.

Which left two berths and three living Dunmores, minus one who was legally dead and therefore couldn't board a ship at all, since boarding required a ledger entry and his entry read *deceased* in his own forged hand.

He laughed, alone in the bilge, and it hurt his ribs, and he kept laughing anyway because the alternative was the other thing.

Two berths. Two names to choose. And every hour he waited, the choice got made for him by somebody else's arithmetic — Verrin's, or the sea's, or the century's.

He rowed home through the dying storm, shipping water, bailing with his hat, and when Briony ran down the shingle to meet him he stood in the surf and lied to her face with the ease of long practice: "Lost a basket. That's all."

She looked at him the way she'd looked at the forged page. She knew. She always knew. But she took the oars from him and hauled the skiff up herself, and said only, "Supper's cold," and he understood that she had done the same arithmetic he had, and reached the same impossible sum, and was waiting — as he was waiting — for someone to say it out loud.

* * *

The answer arrived before either of them spoke it.

He was at the verification tables the next morning — not to verify anything, a dead man keeps clear of tables, but to watch, because watching was the one trade left to him. Verrin stood at the head of the queue in his gray greatcoat, checking names against the resurrected ledger, and when a fisherman named Aldous Crane failed to produce papers matching his entry, two soldiers walked him off the dock and nobody asked where. The queue shuffled forward. Nobody looked up.

And then Verrin raised his eyes from the ledger, found Kestrel in the crowd the way a hawk finds a vole, and smiled.

"Dunmore," he called, pleasantly, loudly enough for the whole square. "I'm told the deep beds have been productive lately. Come see me tonight. Bring your answer about the wreck."

He turned back to his book. The queue moved. Somewhere behind Kestrel, a woman began to cry quietly, and the soldiers did not look up, and the harbor fog rolled in off the water and covered everything the way it always had, as though history were still something that happened elsewhere.

Kestrel stood in the cold a moment longer, doing sums he already knew the answers to, and turned toward the company house, where the man who owned his death was waiting to buy the rest of him.
