## 16. The Choice

Verrin's boots faded down the alley and Kestrel stood in the doorway of the net shed for a long time afterward, watching the fog close over the place where the captain had been, as if the sea itself were trying to swallow back the words.

Two berths. That was the offer. Two names on the *Meridian*'s manifest in exchange for one name spoken aloud: *Tobias Hale*.

He went inside and sat down across from his father, who was propped against the wall on the old pallet with a blanket over his legs and his chest rising and falling in that shallow, whistling rhythm that had become as ordinary to the house as the sound of the tide. Father's hands lay open on the blanket, palms up, the way a man's hands do when he has stopped gripping things.

"You've been talking to the army," Father said. His voice was a ruin now, sanded down to gravel. He'd heard everything through the thin boards; there was no pretending otherwise in this town, in this room.

"He came to me."

"And you said nothing yet." Not a question. Father's eyes were still clear, whatever the rest of him had become — clear and tired and terribly patient. "You're going to sit up all night deciding."

"I don't have to decide anything."

"Boy." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he looked smaller. "I taught your hands to dive. Don't teach your heart to lie."

Kestrel looked away. Outside, the fog horn sounded once from the harbor mouth, the army's horn now, mounted on the requisitioned pilot station, marking time like a slow clock winding down toward the century's end.

* * *

Briony found him at midnight on the seawall behind the smokehouse, where he'd gone because the walls of the house felt like they were pressing inward. She sat down without asking, pulled her coat around her, and put a heel of bread in his hand because that was what she did — fed people first, argued second.

"He offered you two berths," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because Mrs. Corren saw Verrin walking toward our quarter alone at dusk, and there is only one thing in this town an officer visits a diver for privately." She tore a piece off her own bread and didn't eat it. "Two berths for Tobias."

Kestrel said nothing. The silence was answer enough.

"If you take them," Briony said, "it's Mother and me who go. You stay with Father. Or Mother and Father, and we—" She stopped. Recalculated. He could hear her doing it, the same arithmetic he'd been running all evening, the terrible ledger-math that had colonized both their minds. "No. You'd stay regardless. You'd never leave him. So it's Mother and one of us, or Father and one of us."

"Father can't dive anymore. If they're gone and I'm dead on paper, who—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip over shallow water. "Don't say *dead on paper* like it means nothing. I burned my registration, Kestrel. I know exactly what it means. It means soldiers, someday, coming down this street with a list. It means being a rumor instead of a person."

The fog horn sounded again. Somewhere down the quarter a dog barked itself out and gave up.

"There's another thing," Briony said, quieter now. "Dr. Amory saw Father today. At the processing station, before they let him come home."

Kestrel turned. "What did he say?"

"He said the water got into places it shouldn't have. He said—" She pressed her lips together, and for a moment she was nine years old again, mending nets with bleeding fingers, refusing to cry where anyone could see. "He said Father has until spring. Maybe summer if he stays completely dry, and you know he won't, because he'll try to work the shallows the moment we stop watching, because working is the only prayer he knows."

Kestrel heard the words and felt nothing, which frightened him more than grief would have. Then the nothing cracked and everything came through it at once: the image of his father at dawn on the diving rock, thirty years old and laughing, shaking the sea off his shoulders like a dog; the image of the same man last week needing two tries to stand up. Until spring. The century wouldn't even turn before the sea finished collecting its debt.

"So it's one berth," Kestrel said. His voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "Not two. One."

"One," Briony agreed. "And Verrin is offering two for a name."

* * *

He walked the town after that, because sitting still had become unbearable. Past the company house with its new sentries and its lit windows where the annex held every name in the harbor in its iron drawers. Past the chapel, dark. Past Tobias's window, where a lamp still burned at one in the morning, and where the silhouette of his friend bent over copying work made something in Kestrel's chest twist like a hook in flesh.

*Tobias.* Who had taught him letters with a wet finger on a tabletop. Who had risked everything twice — once to forge the entry, once to smuggle the graft evidence — and who had already given Verrin the original pages to save his father's job, a betrayal so gentle and so human that Kestrel hadn't even been able to hate it. Tobias would confess to anything if asked directly. Tobias could not lie under pressure; it wasn't cowardice, it was constitution. He was built honest the way some men were built strong.

And Verrin knew it. That was why the captain suspected him. That was why the trap worked: name Tobias, get two berths, and whether or not Tobias had actually done it, he would hang or be transported, and the machine would grind on with its paperwork intact.

Kestrel tried to make the arithmetic come out clean.

*Name Tobias. Two berths.* But Father couldn't survive the voyage — Dr. Amory would say so, the ship's surgeon would say so, and besides which, Father wouldn't go. He'd refuse at the gangway and shame them all. So the second berth would fall to Mother and then there was still Briony unaccounted for, and Briony had no papers, no registration, no legal existence since she'd fed her own name to the stove. A berth assigned by ledger status could not be given to a woman the ledger said did not exist. Verrin would know that better than anyone. The offer was rotten at the core; it always had been. Two berths, but only one of them usable, and a friend destroyed for the privilege.

*Say nothing. Keep Tobias.* Then no berths at all, except the money Kestrel had earned dead-man-diving the deep beds — enough, barely, for one berth bought gray-market from the harbormaster's clerk, no ledger required. One berth. One name. Choose.

His mother, who had carried this family through twenty years of debt with hands that smelled of salt and lye, who had not wept once through any of it.

Or Briony, who was nineteen and whole and whose lungs were sound, whose whole life was ahead of her like an unbuilt ship, who had burned her registration rather than be owned — and who would be safest of all of them precisely because she was already invisible to the record. No paper trail meant the army had no claim on her. It also meant she had no rights, no protection, and no way off this coast except a berth someone bought for her outright.

Or himself. The thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do, dressed in reason. *Take the berth yourself. Live. Carry the names across the water, find Briony later, send for Mother later—* Later. Later was the word liars used.

He ended up on the diving rock at four in the morning, the cold coming up through the stone into his bones, and looked at the water that had raised him and ruined him and kept every secret worth keeping. The deep beds were out there past the point, black and silent. He thought about the pearls still lying in his cache under the ledge, three seasons of illegal diving wrapped in oilcloth, enough for one berth and a little over. Enough for one.

The fog lifted just before dawn, the way it sometimes did, and for ten minutes the horizon showed hard and blue and endless, and Kestrel understood with total clarity what he had known since the night of the raid and had been refusing to say aloud:

There was no version of this where everyone lived. There was no clever trade, no forged line, no stolen evidence that would buy back what the war had taken. The ledger had taught him that a man could be reduced to a sum. This night taught him the rest of it: that love, too, could be reduced to a sum, and the sum was one, and somebody he loved was going to watch the ship leave without them.

All he could choose was how to bear it. And what to burn on the way down.

That last thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. *Burn.* The annex. The iron drawers full of names — his name, Briony's absence, every diver in the quarter, every unregistered soul the army claimed to own. If the annex burned, the claims burned with it. No ledger, no conscription. No record, no ownership. Verrin's whole machine ran on paper, and paper was the one thing in the world that feared fire more than divers feared drowning.

It was madness. It was treason. It would cost him everything he had left, including possibly his life.

But it would not cost him Tobias. And it would not require him to choose between his mother and his sister — not yet, not tonight, not by naming one and damning the other. The fire would tear the arithmetic apart at the root.

The sun cleared the headland. Down in the harbor, the *Meridian* rode at anchor, her berths filling, her manifests being written in fair hand by clerks who had never met a single person whose name they wrote.

Kestrel stood up from the diving rock. His body ached as if he'd surfaced too fast from a very great depth.

He had until the tide turned to decide nothing, and until dark to decide everything — because tonight the records house kept only two watchmen, and the annex windows faced the seaward side, and Kestrel Dunmore, who officially did not exist, had spent his whole invisible life learning exactly where the shadows fell.

He turned toward home to look at his father's face one more time, and memorize it, just in case memory was all any of them were going to be allowed to keep.
