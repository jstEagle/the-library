## 17. Fire in the Records House

The lamp oil came from three houses and no one asked why.

That was the thing about the divers' quarter in its last winter — people had stopped asking questions they didn't want answered. Mrs. Calloway handed Kestrel a stoppered jug without meeting his eyes. Old Pell gave him a second one wrapped in sacking, as if it were fish for the table. Briony counted out coins from the berth fund to buy a third from the chandler, and her hands were steady, which frightened him more than if she'd wept.

"You don't have to do this," she said that night, in the room where their father slept propped on pillows like a man already half-carried to his grave. "Tobias said the copies—"

"Tobias's copies prove Verrin stole." Kestrel turned the jug in his hands, watching the oil shift inside like something alive. "They don't unmake a single name in that annex. There are forty-one divers in this harbor with no papers since Briony burned hers, and me, and the Marlow boys, and old Pell who can't remember what year he signed. The army's claim doesn't live in Verrin's graft. It lives in ink. So we burn the ink."

"And then what? You'll be a dead man who's also an arsonist."

"Then I'll be free," he said, "and I'll have made them say so."

He did not tell her the rest. He did not tell anyone the rest until it was done.

* * *

The requisition annex stood behind the company house like a tooth gone bad at the back of the town's mouth — a long, low building of grey timber the army had thrown up in October, when they realized the occupation needed somewhere to keep its paperwork dry. Inside were the ledgers of names, the registration rolls, the conscription schedules, and the master copy of the Atlas Company's indenture book: every debt, every diver, every family in the harbor rendered into columns and sums. Captain Verrin had had a lock fitted and a sentry posted at dusk, because Verrin understood, better than any man in the garrison, where power actually lived.

Kestrel cased it for six nights. He walked past with mended nets over his shoulder. He watched the sentry's rounds, timed the gap between his lantern passing the north wall and reappearing at the corner — eleven minutes, twelve on cold nights, and every night that week was cold enough to crack a bucket.

On the seventh night Tobias was waiting for him at the tide-stairs, hood up, face pale as paper in the dark.

"I brought you something," Tobias said, and held out a bundle: a spare clerk's coat, army issue, and a requisition slip filled out in a hand indistinguishable from the duty officer's. "Coal delivery for the annex stove. Nobody questions a clerk hauling coal at night. They question everything else."

"You could hang for this."

"I've been hanging for weeks," Tobias said. "I just haven't dropped yet." He almost smiled. In the lamplight from the harbor he looked older than his father, older than the century. "Kestrel. Listen to me. Once you light it, you can't take it back. Not the fire. The other thing."

"I know."

"The other thing being that you're going to stand there and tell them it was you."

"I know."

"Why?" It wasn't accusation in his voice. It was something worse — grief. "You could vanish. Dead men are invisible. You taught me that, or I taught you, one of us. Walk away and let them wonder. Let them suspect me, even — I'm a clerk, clerks lose papers, it's practically a disease. But you'd rather—"

"Rather what?"

"Rather be seen." Tobias shook his head slowly. "All those years I taught you letters so you could read your way out of here. And you're going to write yourself onto the biggest page in the colony instead."

Kestrel took the coat and the slip. "You taught me letters," he said. "But you never taught me to disappear. That part I learned from the ledger."

He put on the coat. It smelled of another man's tobacco and someone else's war. He climbed the tide-stairs into the wind, and behind him, for the first time in their whole friendship, Tobias did not follow.

* * *

The sentry passed the north wall at half past eleven. Kestrel counted.

Eleven minutes. Twelve.

At thirteen he crossed the open ground carrying two jugs of lamp oil under one arm and a sack of coal dust he didn't need, because coal dust explained oil stains, and oil stains explained nothing worse than a clerk's carelessness — though God knew he meant to claim the whole of it by morning. The lock yielded to Tobias's slip and a forged countersignature that would have made the boy proud. Inside, the annex breathed paper: the smell of it, dry and dense and ancient-feeling, though the oldest book in the room was barely nine years old and had his father's signature on its fourth page.

He found the requisition annex ledger first — the fat black volume Verrin had shown off to the garrison surgeon, listing every unregistered diver in the district as army property pending processing. Then the master indenture book. Then the registration rolls. He stacked them on the long table like a man laying out a body.

There was time to look. That was the cruelty of it — the sentry wouldn't circle again for ten more minutes, and Kestrel found he couldn't stop himself from opening the black book one last time, running his finger down the columns. *Dunmore, K., diver, status: deceased (clerical error), note: investigate.* His own death, still official. *Dunmore, B., female, minor, unregistered.* Briony, erased by her own hand. Forty-one names after hers. Pell. The Marlows. The Oduya twins, evacuated to the cannery, no address recorded. Names he knew and names he only knew from this book, which had become the only place some of them existed at all.

And there it was — the thought that had been circling him all week like a gull over a gutted fish. If he burned this, those names went nowhere. They lived in him. He had spent four months memorizing the ledger the way other men memorized psalms, and he knew them all: all hundred and six divers, all their debts, all their dead. The army kept records. He would keep memory. Records could burn. Memory had to be carried.

He unstopped the first jug and poured.

The oil went across the table, down the stacks, into the shelf-gaps where the loose muster sheets lived. It soaked into a hundred years of the harbor's servitude and pooled on the floorboards, black and patient. Kestrel struck a match against the box, and his hand was steadier than it had ever been on a dive line, and he thought — absurdly, at the end — of dawn shallows, of pearls coming up green in his palm before they dried, of the first time Tobias traced a letter under his finger and said *that's you, that word right there.*

"That's me," Kestrel said aloud, to no one. "This is me."

He dropped the match.

The paper caught the way dry paper does — not gradually but all at once, a roar rather than a flame, light slamming up the walls. Heat shoved him backward toward the door. He stood one moment longer than was sane, watching the black book curl and bloom orange along its spine, watching *Dunmore* go brown, then gold, then nothing, and he felt the strangest sensation of his life: not horror, not triumph, but weight leaving him. Every sum ever set against his name going up as light. He was worth nothing now. No one owned him. He was the freest pauper in the colony.

Then the smoke found him and he ran.

* * *

He made it as far as the company house steps before the bells started, and then he sat down on the cold stone like a man waiting for a ferry, because that was the plan, the mad beautiful terrible plan, and plans deserve to be kept.

Soldiers came at the double from the barracks. Clerks came in nightshirts. Verrin came last, greatcoat over his shoulders, face lit orange and hard as a coin, and he stood looking at the annex burning — really burning, timbers catching now, sparks climbing the black sky like the whole harbor's debts ascending — and Kestrel watched him understand in real time what had been lost. Not a building. A grip. The army's whole hold on every unregistered soul in the district, unraveling into ash and updraft.

"Who did this?" Verrin's voice carried flat across the yard. Nobody answered. The clerks stared at the fire. The soldiers stared at Verrin.

"I did."

Every head turned. Kestrel stood up off the steps, slow, joints aching from the cold, and let them see him — the dead diver, the ghost from the ledger, in a stolen clerk's coat that nobody had noticed yet.

"Dunmore." Verrin said the name the way you'd spit out a bone. "You're supposed to be deceased."

"I am. Ask your ledger." Kestrel nodded at the fire. "Oh — you can't."

Somebody among the soldiers laughed, one short bark, and strangled it.

Verrin crossed the yard and stood close, close enough that Kestrel could smell the wine on him and see the calculation running behind his eyes like water through a sieve. This was the moment, Kestrel knew, when Verrin would try to make it small. Make it about Tobias. The clerk's coat — Verrin's gaze dropped to it, snagged, came back up. Gotcha, that look said.

"Where did you get that coat, boy?"

"Took it," Kestrel said clearly, loudly, before the suspicion could travel anywhere it might catch. "Out of the stores. I've been in and out of your buildings all winter, Captain. Your sentries never saw me because officially I don't exist — your own records say so. I forged my entry myself. I learned letters from your clerk's son, but the forgery was mine alone, and tonight the fire was mine alone. One man. One lamp's worth of oil. Write it down somewhere — oh." He smiled, and hated himself for how much he enjoyed it. "You haven't anything to write on."

"Restrain him," Verrin said quietly.

Hands took his arms. He didn't resist. Through the soldiers' bodies he searched the crowd of clerks for Tobias and found him — pale, rigid, playing shocked like a professional, because he was one — and Kestrel gave him the smallest shake of the head. *Not you. Me. All of it, me.* Tobias's eyes closed for half a second. That was all the goodbye they needed.

The annex roof fell in with a sound like the sea breaking, and a column of sparks went up so high that Kestrel knew it would be visible from the fishing boats, from the ridge farms, from everywhere the occupied town could see — and would see, and would talk about, and would remember. Let them come tomorrow wanting proof of who owed what. Let them bring their questions to the ash.

"You understand what you've done," Verrin said, low, in his ear. "No berth. No ship. No place on the evacuation rolls — not that you were ever on them. You've burned yourself onto this island, Dunmore. You'll die here."

"No," Kestrel said. "I'll live here. There's a difference. You wouldn't know it — you've only ever been stationed places."

The blow, when it came, hardly hurt at all. He'd been hit harder by the water.

* * *

They put him in the coal store behind the barracks, which was fitting, since that's where his forged delivery slip said he'd been going. It was dark and warm-ish and smelled of his whole imagined crime, and he sat against the wall listening to the town fight the fire — axes, buckets, the pump wagon's bell — and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for a week.

One berth on the last ship. He had earned money no ledger could trace — enough for one, barely, after the near-drowning in December, after the blood he'd coughed up quietly on the tide-stairs like his father before him. He was now a confessed arsonist facing whatever justice the army invented for men who burned its property, which meant his own berth was a fiction anyway. His mother was dead years since; his father would die within the year regardless, Dr. Prue had said so in words of one syllable, and Father himself refused to speak of ships at all. That left one person in the world the berth was for, and she was going to fight him about it, and he was going to win, because for once in his life he held something she couldn't argue with: he was already lost, and she was not.

Boots in the yard. A key in the door. Light spilled in — lantern, then Verrin's face above it, and beside Verrin, to Kestrel's astonishment, the garrison paymaster, holding a telegram flimsy in a hand that shook.

"Well," Verrin said, and his voice had gone strange — smaller, careful, a man reading his own obituary by firelight. "It seems your little blaze was seen farther than either of us guessed. Somebody on the mainland has been waiting for proof against me, boy, and tonight the wires started humming. Graft. Phantom workers. Sold supplies. Where there's smoke—" He laughed, without any humor in it at all. "They're recalling me. Disgraced, but not hanged. Your friend the clerk will want to know his copies reached shore."

Kestrel kept his face still, which cost everything he had.

"But here's what matters to you," Verrin went on, crouching to bring them eye to eye, and for the first time all winter there was something like respect in the man's ruined face. "In eleven days the ship comes. And in eleven days I leave too — which means for eleven days, nobody in this garrison gives a damn about a coal-store arsonist or a dead diver or a burned book. Do you understand what I'm telling you? For eleven days, nobody is watching."

The lantern swung. Outside, the annex glowed like a hearth, and snow began to fall through the smoke, settling white on the ashes of everyone's debts.

Kestrel leaned back against the coal and started counting the days.
