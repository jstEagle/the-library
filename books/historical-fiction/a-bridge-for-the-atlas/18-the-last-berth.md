## 18. The Last Berth

The gulls woke her before the bell did.

Briony Dunmore had slept in her clothes for three nights now, and she woke the way the whole town had learned to wake that winter — all at once, with her heart already moving, as if some part of her had been standing watch while the rest of her slept. The loft was cold. The window above her cot showed a sky the color of pewter, and beyond it, out past the breakwater, the *Meridian* rode at anchor with its single lit porthole burning like a coin dropped in dark water.

One berth. One ship. One year left in a century, and the army had counted the town's people the way they counted everything else — by what fit.

She dressed without lighting the lamp. She'd gotten good at that. Her fingers knew the buttons, knew the tear in the left sleeve she'd mended twice, knew the weight of the small oilcloth packet she'd sewn into her coat's inner lining two nights ago, when Kestrel was out on the water and couldn't see her do it. Inside the packet: their mother's ring. And beneath the ring, folded to the size of a stamp, a page of paper covered in Kestrel's careful, upright hand — the letters Tobias had taught him, the letters he now taught back to the harbor children on salvaged slate when the soldiers weren't looking. Names. Forty-one of them. Every diver in the ledger, every debt, every sum owed, every berth denied — copied not from paper but from memory, because memory was the one thing the requisition officers hadn't figured out how to seize.

*If I go,* he'd said, *the names go with me. That's the point.*

But he wasn't going. She'd known it before he'd said it, known it the way you know weather coming off the water before you can see it. And last night, standing at the foot of Father's bed, watching the old man's chest rise and fall in that shallow, whistling rhythm the deep beds had taught him, she had stopped pretending otherwise.

Downstairs, the house smelled of coal smoke and nothing else. There was no food to speak of; the army ration line had moved to the far side of the harbor, past the checkpoint, and Kestrel wouldn't take them through it — wouldn't show an unregistered face to a bored soldier with a list. So Briony boiled water, and that was breakfast.

Kestrel came down while the kettle was still rattling. He looked like he hadn't slept, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has finished arguing with himself and lost to the part that was always going to win.

"You're up," he said.

"The ship doesn't wait for sleepers."

"Neither did I." He set a canvas bag by the door — hers, packed, though she hadn't asked him to. He'd done it while she pretended to sleep. "There's bread in there wrapped in cloth. It'll be hard by tomorrow but it keeps. And the letter from Mrs. Calloway's cousin in Port Merrow — the address. You send word when you land. Any way you can."

"I will."

"And the money's sewn—" He stopped. Looked at her. "You felt me doing it."

"I felt you doing it."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive. He sat down across from her at the table, and for a while neither of them drank the tea, because drinking it would make it real, and real things have to be gotten through.

"Father won't come down," Kestrel said finally. "I went up twice. He says the sea took his lungs and it can keep them." He turned his cup in a slow circle. "I told him you'd want to say goodbye."

"He's saying goodbye," Briony said. "In his way. By staying where he is so I can't see him cry."

Kestrel looked at her then — really looked, the way he used to look at pearls, turning something over until it gave up what it was worth — and whatever he found in her face made him look away first. Fifteen years old, and she'd learned to read her family better than any clerk could read a ledger.

"Kestrel." She kept her voice level. "Say it out loud. Say you're staying."

"I'm staying."

"Why?"

"Because someone stays." He said it simply, without drama, which was the worst way he could have said it. "The names have to be carried by somebody who's here to carry them. If we both go, the town ends when the ship pulls out. If I stay, it doesn't. Not all the way." He paused. "And Father dies in the spring. We both know it. He shouldn't die alone."

"That's three reasons and none of them is the true one."

"The true one is all three."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted it so badly her hand closed around the edge of the table instead, knuckles white, wood biting cold into her palm. Because he was right, and being right was his oldest infuriating habit, and because she had spent a month watching him erase himself — dead on paper, invisible to lists, diving the deep beds illegally to buy a berth no ledger could trace — and she understood now that some men don't sacrifice themselves out of nobility. Some men do it because it's the only arithmetic that lets them keep loving the people they love without drowning in it.

"When the century turns," she said, "and this war ends or doesn't, and someone writes down what happened here — it'll be written by whoever held the pen. Not by us. Not by the divers." She stood up. "So you hold the pen, Kestrel. You hear me? You write us down. All forty-one. Write them so well that a hundred years from now nobody gets to pretend we were never here."

He nodded once. That was all. But she saw his jaw work, and saw him blink hard and fast, twice, the way he did when he refused to let anything surface.

* * *

The dock at dawn was a country of its own.

Soldiers in gray-blue greatcoats held the gangway with fixed bayonets, checking names against a clipboard — the clipboard, Briony thought, that had replaced the ledger, the same power in a smaller book. Families crowded behind a rope line, breath steaming, voices pitched low because high voices drew attention and attention drew lists. A woman Briony half-knew was arguing with a sergeant about her son's registration, her voice cracking on every third word. Somewhere near the warehouse a child was crying in the flat, exhausted way of a child who has been crying for hours.

The *Meridian* sat low in the water, overloaded, filthy with coal dust, flying a flag Briony didn't recognize from any atlas — some charter company's colors, bought and paid for, the only ship in a hundred miles willing to run civilians out ahead of the blockade. Its rail was lined with faces. People who had gotten aboard yesterday and hadn't slept either, watching the dock like passengers watch a shore they're not sure they'll ever see again.

Kestrel walked her to the rope line and stopped where the soldiers' eyes couldn't follow them past.

"Ring's in the oilcloth," he said. "Left pocket. Don't tell anyone you have it — gold travels badly through checkpoints."

"I know."

"The names are under the insole of your right boot. Memorize them anyway. Paper burns."

"I know, Kestrel."

"The cousin in Port Merrow—"

"—keeps a room over the chandlery. I know." She turned and faced him fully. "You've told me all of it four times. Tell me the thing you haven't told me."

For a moment he didn't answer. Out on the water the fog was lifting off the breakwater in long gray banners, and the light came through it the color of pearl — their mother's pearl, their father's trade, the whole economy of their lives reduced to a shimmer on cold water. Somewhere out there, under the gray, lay the deep beds where Kestrel had nearly drowned twice this winter, diving as a dead man, holding his breath against the arithmetic of love.

"The thing I haven't told you," he said slowly, "is that I'm not brave. Everyone's going to say I was brave, staying. I'm not. I just couldn't stand being saved." His voice caught and steadied. "You go be brave. It's harder."

"It isn't fair," she said.

"No."

"I'm fifteen."

"Yes."

"I should get to hate you for this."

Something broke open in his face then — not tears exactly, but the place tears come from, the quarry they're cut from. "Hate me later," he said. "Take your time. I'll be here."

A corporal was working down the rope line with the clipboard. "...Dunmore, B.? Dunmore, B.!"

Briony picked up her bag. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; grief and gratitude had gotten so tangled in her chest she couldn't tell which one was shaking and which one was holding on.

She reached up and took her brother's face in both hands — he was taller than her now, taller than Father had ever been, but he bent to her, let her, the way he used to bend when she was small and he carried her on his shoulders through the shallows at low tide. She pressed her forehead against his.

"Write us down," she whispered.

"Every name," he whispered back. "I swear it on the water."

Then she stepped through the rope line, and the corporal checked her name, and a soldier's gloved hand pointed her up the gangway, and she climbed — one plank at a time, the *Meridian*'s hull groaning under her feet like something alive and reluctant — and at the top she turned around.

Kestrel stood on the dock with his hands in his pockets, small suddenly, ordinary, a boy on a cold pier in a town that history had decided to use up and discard. He raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. More like an oath.

She raised hers back.

They pulled the gangway. The ropes came off the bollards. The *Meridian* shuddered, coughed steam, and began to slide sideways from the dock, and Briony stood at the rail and watched the harbor town grow smaller — the company house with its broken windows, the records house still black-scarred from the fire everyone pretended not to understand, the divers' quarter with its crooked chimneys, and one figure on the dock who did not move, who did not wave again, who stayed until the fog took him the way the fog takes everything, gently and completely.

Only when the breakwater passed astern did she go below, find a corner between crates, and open the oilcloth packet.

Her mother's ring caught the lamplight — a thin band, a small pearl, worn smooth at the shoulders by twenty years of hands. And beneath it, the page. Forty-one names in her brother's upright, unbreakable script. She read them aloud to herself, barely breathing the words, learning them the way he'd learned them: not as ink, but as debt. As inheritance.

At the bottom of the page, beneath the last name, in smaller letters, he had added a line she hadn't seen him write:

*I was never invisible. I was only unread.*

The ship rolled. Somewhere behind them, in a town she would not see again for years, the century was dying — and in a house above the harbor, a man who had refused to leave his bed was keeping his own kind of vigil beside a brother who had chosen to stay, both of them waiting out the longest night of the year together, while far away in the capital the new one arrived without asking anyone's permission, the way history always did.

Briony closed her hand around the ring and watched the water, and began, quietly, to memorize.
