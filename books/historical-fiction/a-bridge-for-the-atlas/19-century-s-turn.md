## 19. Century's Turn

The year did not die quietly. It went out the way a drowning man goes out — with noise at first, then a terrible stillness.

Kestrel heard the news before dawn on the last day of December, from Marta Cade, who heard it from the fish-boy, who heard it from a soldier's own mouth in the tavern line: Captain Verrin was recalled. Effective immediately. A transport would collect him and his staff within the fortnight, and the requisition administration would pass to a Major Ostrand, arriving from the capital.

"Recalled," Kestrel repeated, because the word felt foreign in his mouth, like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

Marta shrugged, pulling her shawl tighter against the harbor wind. "That's what they said. No reason given. No reason ever is."

But Kestrel knew the reason. Tobias had shown him the copies three nights ago, spread across the floor of the records house like a hand of winning cards — inventories that didn't match invoices, phantom divers drawing rations, the blockade's supply contracts signed to a firm whose only address was Verrin's cousin's house in the provincial capital. Tobias had made duplicates of everything before surrendering the originals, had hidden them inside the binding of a hymnal where no soldier would think to search for evidence of sin. Then he'd sent them up the chain himself, through the postal service, addressed to the Admiralty Board, signed *a servant of the Company.*

"He'll hang," Kestrel had said.

"No," Tobias said, and there was something old in his face, something that hadn't been there a year ago. "He'll be transferred. That's how it works, Kes. They don't punish men like him. They just move them somewhere the weather's worse."

He'd been right, of course. He was always right about institutions now; it was the education the army had given him without meaning to. Verrin would leave the harbor with his pension intact and his reputation merely dented, and some other garrison would learn his handwriting soon enough. Justice, Kestrel was learning, was not a ledger that balanced. It was a ledger that someone else kept.

And yet.

And yet there was this: the copies had survived. The names were read. Somewhere in a marble office a thousand miles away, a clerk with Tobias's own gift for columns had frowned at an invoice and written *irregular* in the margin, and that single word had done more damage to Captain Verrin than a year of Kestrel's hatred. Paper had built the cage. Paper, at least once, had rattled its bars.

Kestrel stood in the doorway of the Dunmore house and let the news settle over him like cold water. It should have tasted sweeter. He tried to find the sweetness and could not. All he found was the smell of lamp oil and boiled barley, the sound of his father coughing behind the thin partition wall, and the knowledge that the transport collecting Verrin would sail on the same tide as the century, and neither ship would carry anything back for them.

*

His father wanted to sit up to see the year out.

"It's tradition," Dunmore said, propped against his pillows, though Kestrel knew of no such tradition and suspected his father had invented it that morning, the way he'd lately invented reasons to be moved from bed to chair, chair to window, as if motion itself could be banked against the coming dark. His chest rattled when he breathed. Dr. Ennett had used the word *consolidation,* which meant nothing to any of them, but she'd said it softly, and soft words from doctors were the worst ones.

"You should be lying down."

"I've been lying down since October. I'm sick of the ceiling." He gestured at the room's one window, where the harbor showed grey between the rooftops. "Besides. Your mother used to say the year turns whether you watch it or not. I'd rather watch."

*Your mother used to say.* She'd been dead eleven years and she was in every sentence now, the way water is in every plank of a boat. Kestrel didn't correct him. He pulled the chair to the window and helped arrange the blankets, and hated how light his father had become, how the man who once hauled Kestrel out of twelve fathoms by one arm now weighed less than the anchor chain he used to coil one-handed.

Outside, the occupied town was preparing for midnight in spite of itself. Paper lanterns had appeared along the fish market — forbidden, technically, under the assembly ordinances, but the soldiers had stopped enforcing half their own rules now that the war had moved on and left them guarding a harbor full of poor people and paperwork. Someone had gotten a fiddle. Somewhere up the hill, the company house windows glowed with the lamps of clerks packing files into crates, the whole apparatus of the requisition being disassembled and crated like machinery sold off at auction.

"They're taking the ledgers, you know," his father said. "All of them. Back to the capital."

"I know."

"So it wasn't for nothing. What you did." Dunmore's eyes stayed on the window. "The fire. Whatever it cost you. The book that named us is gone, or going, and either way it isn't sitting in that house anymore with our names in it like marks on cattle."

Kestrel said nothing. There was no honest answer. The requisition annex was ash, yes — but the master ledger had already been crated for evacuation when he burned it, so the army's claim had survived his defiance and his sister both. Briony was three hundred miles east by now, in a city Kestrel couldn't picture, carrying their mother's ring and forty-one names memorized in a diver's rhythm, the way other people carried psalms. And Kestrel remained officially dead, unregistered, unpaid, a man the paper world had erased — free, in the way a ghost is free.

"The debt," his father said, and stopped. Coughed. Went on anyway, because stubbornness was the last muscle left to him. "The company debt. With me like this—"

"Papa. Don't."

"Somebody has to say it aloud. Your brother's dead on paper—" the irony had stopped being funny to either of them weeks ago, "—Briony's gone, and I'm worth less than nothing, I'm a subtraction. If they come for the balance—"

"There's no balance." Kestrel kept his voice level. "Ostrand's administration hasn't even landed. Nobody knows what the rules are. And when they do land—" he heard himself say it before he decided to, heard the lie assemble itself out of love and exhaustion, "—the account was settled. Before the annex burned. I saw the entry myself."

His father looked at him for a long moment. Dunmore had spent forty years reading water, and he read his son the same way now — surface first, then the depths beneath it, the shadow of the thing underneath.

"All right," he said at last. Which meant he knew. Which meant he'd known for some time, and had chosen, in the manner of dying men, to accept the version of the truth he could carry.

Kestrel turned to the window so he wouldn't have to keep lying with his whole face.

Below, on the frozen quay, Tobias Vane was coming up the hill with a lantern, small and halting against the wind.

*

Tobias came to the door the way he came everywhere now — quickly, hood up, eyes doing a clerk's sweep of the street before he knocked twice, pause, once. Their signal, absurdly, from the old forgery nights. Some habits had survived the year better than either boy had.

"He sails tomorrow," Tobias said, inside, stamping feeling back into his feet. "Verrin. Tide after next. They gave him a frigate escort, if you can believe it. Like he's cargo worth protecting."

"Did you go see him? Say goodbye?"

Tobias made a sound that was almost a laugh. "I stood in the records room while he inventoried what he was allowed to take. He looked straight through me. Kes, I worked for that man eighteen months. I copied his graft into fair hand with my own fingers. And he doesn't know my name." He unwound his scarf. "That's the part nobody tells you about betraying an institution. It can't betray you back. It never even learned your face."

"You sent the copies anyway."

"I sent the copies anyway." Tobias warmed his hands at the stove Kestrel had fed for him without being asked. "They'll probably promote whoever replaces him for finding the problem. That's the other thing nobody tells you."

From behind the partition came the rattle of his father's breathing, long and tidal. Both boys were quiet until it settled.

"How is he?" Tobias asked.

"Ask me at low tide. Some hours are better." Kestrel sat down on the crate that served as second chair. "Tobias. Why are you here? On this night of all nights. You should be home. Your father will need you when the transfer papers come."

"My father's packing. He's been offered the branch office at Cape Merrow. Smaller books, smaller mistakes." Tobias hesitated, and for a moment Kestrel saw the boy who had once traced letters in spilled pearl-oil, teaching another boy to read his own servitude. "I'm here because tomorrow, when the frigate sails, every record of what happened in this harbor goes with it. The true ones and the false ones. And I thought somebody besides us two ought to mark the occasion."

He reached into his coat and produced a bottle — real wine, army issue, traded from a sentry for cigarettes Tobias did not smoke. He set it on the table between them like an offering.

"We're not toasting Verrin," he said. "We're not toasting the army, or the company, or the century, God knows. What's left?"

Kestrel thought of Briony on the water somewhere east, alive, which was the only column that mattered. He thought of forty-one names. He thought of the slate in his sea chest downstairs, salvaged from the schoolhouse, already crowded with the beginnings of a list he added to when the house was asleep.

"The divers," he said. "The ones the ledger carried and the ones it dropped. Toast them."

"To the divers," Tobias agreed, and poured.

The wine was rough and sweet. Behind the partition, his father slept or pretended to. Outside, the fiddle had picked up a tune, and someone was singing badly, gloriously off-key, and the lanterns swung in the harbor wind like a fleet of small brave ships that would never leave port.

*

Midnight came gray and cold, not dramatic at all.

There were no bells — the church bell had been requisitioned in autumn for scrap and never replaced. The new year announced itself the way most things did in the harbor: quietly, on schedule, indifferent to attendance. But on the quay below, people came out anyway. Divers and netmenders and widows, faces upturned in the dark, and the soldiers on the wall watched them and did nothing, and for one held breath nobody was anybody's instrument.

His father insisted on standing for it. Kestrel held him upright with one arm behind the thin shoulders, feeling the ribs like rungs of a ladder, and together they watched the dark water where the year turned over unseen, the way the bottom of the sea turns over unseen, carrying everything down and holding everything, keeping what the shore forgets.

"There," Dunmore said softly, when the shouting on the quay had faded. "Done. We watched it turn. Your mother owed me that dance, but she always was late to things." He let himself be lowered back to the pillows, spent, breath whistling. "Kes. Come here."

Kestrel leaned close.

"You know I know," his father whispered. "About the entry. About all of it. A man hears things even dead on paper." His hand, weightless as kelp, found Kestrel's wrist. "You burned a government building to keep your family off a list. I raised a pearl diver." Something moved through the ruined face that was not quite a smile and was worth more. "Don't you dare apologize for arithmetic."

"I'd do it again," Kestrel said, and his voice cracked on the plainness of it. "That's what frightens me."

"Good. Frightened men live." The grip tightened, then couldn't anymore. "Spring," Dunmore murmured, already sinking. "When the water warms. Take me down to the shallows. I want to see the beds one more time before—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to; they both knew the sentence's last word, had known it since the conscription processing tent, since the pressure did its work in lungs already scarred by twenty years of honest diving. Kestrel tucked the blanket to his father's chin and sat back down in the chair, and outside the century began without ceremony, the first hour of it indistinguishable from the last hour of the old one.

Somewhere east, Briony was waking to a new year in a city of strangers, wearing their mother's ring. Somewhere in the harbor roads, a frigate waited to carry Captain Verrin away unpunished, promoted by his own exposure. Somewhere up the hill, Tobias was walking home past the lanterns with an empty bottle's ghost in his pocket and a hymnal full of evidence that had done exactly one good thing and one only.

And here, in a chair by a dying man's bed, sat a dead man — nineteen years old, unregistered, unemployed, erased — watching the new year come in like fog off the water.

It should have broken him. For a moment, honestly, it nearly did. The bitterness rose in him like a dive too deep, pressure building behind the eyes, the taste of iron — *justice partial, survival incomplete,* the whole crooked sum of the year — and he let it rise and let it crest and let it pass, because his father was breathing, raggedly but breathing, and breathing was the whole job tonight.

Survival was not living. He understood that now, had paid dearly for the understanding. But survival was also not nothing. It was a berth on a ship for Briony. It was a ring on her hand. It was a name list growing on slate, and children who would need letters by spring, and a town that would need remembering, and a father who needed one more trip to the shallows.

One thing at a time. One tide at a time. That was how you surfaced from deep water — you didn't fight the whole ocean, you just made it to the next breath, and then the one after that.

Near dawn, his father woke briefly and gripped his hand again.

"Kes," he said. "The shallows. When it warms. Promise me."

"I promise," Kestrel said.

And meant it, though he did not yet know — could not yet know, lying to himself as gently as he'd lied to his father about the debt — that promises made to dying men in the dark have a way of becoming debts, and debts have a way of coming due, and the water, when it finally warmed, would ask something of Kestrel Dunmore that no ledger had ever thought to write down.
