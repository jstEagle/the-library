## 22. Letters from Across the Water

The letters came with the spring tides, the way they always did.

Kestrel knew the ship before the harbor did — he'd learned to read hulls in the dark years before he'd learned to read words — and on the morning the *Meridian* worked her way past the breakwater with the mail flag flying, he was already standing at the end of the pier with his hands in his pockets and his heart doing the thing it did, that slow hard knocking, as if the letter were a person coming home.

She wrote twice a year. Once after the harvest of the pearl season south, once before the winter crossings closed. The letters came in brown paper wrappers, sealed with cheap wax because Briony Dunmore had opinions about spending money on wax when there were mouths to feed, even an ocean away. Her hand had changed over the years. The first letter, seven years ago now, had been careful as a child's copybook, every letter formed like it was afraid of being wrong. Now her script ran fast and slanted across the page, confident, sometimes crowding into the margins, sometimes crossing out a word with one decisive stroke — *no, that's not true, let me say it better* — because she wrote to him the way she'd argued with him, all at once and without apology.

He didn't open it on the pier. That was ritual too. He carried it up through the town, past the company house with its new paint and its new name — Atlas Trading had been reorganized, restructured, absorbed; the ledger lived on in other hands, other cities, the way a disease lives on in clean blood — past the records house, which was still a burned shell because nobody in the harbor would sell stone to rebuild it. He carried it up the hill to the schoolroom, which was not a schoolroom in any way the colonial office would have recognized, being a converted net loft with a slate roof and forty-one salvaged slates and a stove that smoked if you looked at it wrong.

The children were already there. They were always already there, which had taken him years to stop finding miraculous.

"Letter," he said, holding it up, and the room went quiet the way it went quiet for this, only this — not for sums, not for the histories, but for the letter, because the letter meant the world outside, and the world outside was the thing most of them would never see and all of them wanted described.

"Read it out," said Maren Cobb, who was eleven and bossy and whose grandmother's name Kestrel had written on slate eleven years ago from memory, along with three hundred and six others.

He broke the seal. He read it first with his eyes, silently, the way he always did — because some parts were for him alone, and he needed to know which parts those were before he stood up in front of children and gave them the rest.

*Dear Kestrel.*

*I've sold the southern lease. All of it. Don't argue, I can see your face from here. The buyer is a syndicate out of Corvane and they paid more than it was worth because they don't know pearls and I do, and I'm done pretending the water owes anybody anything.*

*This means the money I've been sending will change. It will get smaller. I know what you do with it — don't think I don't, Tamsin Voss writes to her cousin and her cousin tells everyone — you buy slates and chalk and lamp oil and coal for that stove, and last winter you bought boots for the Pell children and told everyone they won them at raffle. There is no raffle, Kestrel. You are a terrible liar and always were.*

*So here is what the smaller money is for: paper. Real paper, bound into books, the kind that doesn't crumble. If you're going to write down a whole town you should write it on something that lasts longer than you do. I've thought about this a great deal and I've decided you're going to live a long time, so the books need to be very good indeed.*

*Mama's ring is on my hand as I write this. I wear it every day. Some mornings I look at it and I'm seventeen again, standing on a gangplank, watching you get smaller on the dock. I used to believe I'd come back when it was safe. Then I believed I'd come back when I could afford to. Now I understand there is no such day, that safe isn't a place you arrive at, it's a direction you walk, and I chose my direction, and my direction was away, and you chose yours, and yours was here, and neither of us has ever once been sorry, and both of us have been sorry every single day, and I think that's what family is.*

*Here is what else. I met a man.*

Kestrel stopped reading. The children waited, patient, trained by years of these pauses. He read the paragraph again. *I met a man.* His sister, thirty-one years old, writing it the way you'd write *it rained today*, daring him to make anything of it.

He made nothing of it. Out loud.

He cleared his throat. "All right," he said. "Who remembers where we left the harbor history? Tam?"

"The requisition," said Tam Pell, who was nine and could recite the names of the conscripted divers faster than he could recite his times tables, which Kestrel considered the correct order of importance.

"The requisition." Kestrel set the letter down on his desk, faceup, next to the slate that lived there permanently — the master slate, cracked across one corner, on which three hundred and eight names were written in his own hand in rows small enough to fit and large enough to read. Three hundred and eight, because two more had surfaced in the years since, men who had come back from the mainland work camps after the war collapsed and found their names missing from every record and themselves therefore missing from every memory until they'd walked up the hill and seen their own names on a slate in a net loft and sat down on the floor and wept.

That had happened twice. He had never gotten used to it. He hoped he never would.

"We left off with the requisition," he said. "Soldiers in the streets. The ledger gone. And a boy named Kestrel Dunmore discovering that a book could own a man more thoroughly than any chain." He looked around the room. Forty-one faces, some of them children of children he'd taught, the harbor turning over its generations the way the tide turned over the shallows. "Now. Before we go on, there's news from across the water. From Briony."

The name still landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well. They all knew it. *Briony's slate* was what they called the second board on the wall, the one with the names of the departed — not the dead, the *departed*, because Kestrel kept them separate, the dead got dates and the departed got destinations, and he refused to let the occupation blur that distinction the way it had blurred everything else.

"She sold her pearl lease," he told them. "All of it. She says she's done pretending the water owes anybody anything."

A ripple of laughter. The children knew that line; it had entered the harbor's speech years ago, worn smooth by use, applied to everything from bad fishing seasons to the tax assessor.

"And she's sent money for books. Real ones. Bound ones." He held up the wrapper. "Which means the history gets written properly. Every name. Every date. What the soldiers took, and what we took back."

"What we burned," corrected little Sef Aldous from the front row, age seven, ferocious.

"What we burned," Kestrel agreed, "and what we wrote down after, which was harder."

It was the lesson he returned to more than any other, the one he built the whole edifice on, though he'd never quite managed to say it plainly, even to himself: that the fire had freed them and the writing had saved them, and that the two acts were the same act, because a people who remember themselves cannot be owned by a book.

He taught the morning's lessons. Reading, for the little ones — the letters cut into wood blocks he'd carved himself, each one shaped to be felt in a pocket, in the dark, underwater if it came to that. Arithmetic for the middle ones, done in oyster-shell tallies because numbers should cost something to move. And for the older students, the history, always the history last, when the light came long through the east windows and the stove had warmed the room and the children were tired enough to be honest.

That afternoon he read them the rest of the letter, the parts for sharing.

Briony wrote about the south coast, about the pearl camps dissolving now that the war was over and the army had moved on to some other harbor, some other ledger, leaving behind the same arithmetic everywhere it went. She wrote that a woman named Odile Ferrant — and Kestrel's breath caught, because Odile Ferrant was on the slate, third row, taken in the raid of 1901, dragged away with no papers and never returned — Odile Ferrant had been seen alive in a prison quarry two provinces inland, alive in 1904, according to a mason who'd worked beside her, and Briony had hired a man to search the quarry records, and the search was ongoing, and *do not tell the family yet, I want to be sure, but Kestrel, if she is alive, then someone must be looking, and if no one else will look then we will look, because we are the ones who keep the names*.

He read that part aloud. His voice held. Mostly. The Pell girl, Maren, watched him with those clear gray eyes that missed nothing, and afterward she stayed behind while the others clattered out, and she said, "You knew her. Odile Ferrant."

"I knew her brother," Kestrel said. "I know everybody's brother. That's the whole point of this place."

Maren nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd suspected for years. "Someday I want to go across the water," she said. "Like Briony. And send money back."

"Someday," Kestrel said, "you'll do whatever the water asks of you. Just make sure you decide it yourself. That's the only thing anyone ever really owns."

She frowned at that, working it, and ran out into the light, and Kestrel stood alone in the net loft with the letter and the slates and the long afternoon coming down through the windows, and he allowed himself, as he almost never allowed himself, to feel the full weight of what the letter had said and what it hadn't.

*Resolution without restoration,* he thought, though he wouldn't have phrased it that way; he'd have said: the debt was paid but the money never comes back, the fire went out but the smoke never clears, she is safe and far and I am here and poor and free, and between us lies an ocean that we crossed in opposite directions and neither of us regrets and both of us mourns. That was the shape of it. That was the price, and the price did not convert into anything. It just was. You carried it. You built with it.

* * *

Father had died in the spring seven years ago, quietly, in his sleep, with the window open to the sound of the tide. Kestrel had written to Briony and she had written back within the month, one line that he had copied onto slate and kept above his desk ever since, and that he had never read aloud to the children because it was not history, it was the thing underneath history:

*You were never invisible to me.*

Seven words. She had claimed later, in another letter, that she barely remembered writing them, that they'd come out of her pen before she knew what she was saying. But Kestrel remembered the night she'd said them first, or something like them — the night of the raid, when she'd burned her registration in the stove and stood watching the flames and told him that if the ledger wouldn't hold her then she would have to hold herself, and hold him too, and hold the whole damned town, because somebody had to, because memory was the only property the army couldn't requisition.

He'd built the school on that sentence. He supposed she deserved to know that. He'd told her, in his replies. He suspected she'd never quite believed it, the way she'd never quite believed the boots-and-rafﬂe lie either, because she knew him, and knowing someone is the opposite of believing them.

He lit the lamp — the good lamp, whale oil, bought with Briony money — and took down the master slate, and began, as he did every evening, the work of copying. Slate to paper. Paper to book. The bound books now filled half a shelf: four volumes of harbor history, written in a hand that had grown steadier and slower over the years, because he had learned that haste was how things got lost. Names first. Then dates. Then, in the margins, in smaller script, the things that didn't fit anywhere official: what Odile Ferrant liked to sing while she mended nets. How Tobias Voss —

Tobias. Kestrel set down the pen.

Tobias Voss had died two winters ago, of the lung sickness, at thirty-four, in the company clerk's flat above the harbor office where he'd spent his whole life between the ledger and the door. Kestrel had sat with him the last week. They had not spoken much. There was nothing left to settle — Tobias's surviving copies had brought Verrin down years ago, recalled in disgrace, unpunished, promoted eventually to a desk in the colonial capital, because institutions forgive their instruments anything except exposure, and even exposure they mostly forget. Tobias had settled nothing and been forgiven everything and died owning one trunk of belongings and one request: that his name go on the slate not among the taken but among the teachers, because, he'd said, near the end, his voice a dry rattle like wind through rigging, *I taught you your letters, Kestrel. Whatever you built, I laid the first stone of it. Write that down. Don't soften it. Don't make me noble either. Just write it true.*

Kestrel had written it true. In volume three, in the margin, in small script: *Tobias Voss taught me to read using the entries in a ledger that owned us both. He betrayed me once, without malice, because the institution made liars of everyone including him. He gave me the means to bring down the man who broke him. He asked to be remembered exactly as he was. This paragraph is the remembering.*

He picked the pen back up. Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the harbor the way it had filled it before any ledger, before any company, before any century, and it would fill it long after, indifferent and honest, the only account that never lied because it never claimed to keep anything.

* * *

The letter from Briony lay on his desk, and at the bottom of it, beneath the signature, beneath the postscript about the man (his name was Alden, he built ships, he was steady and kind and Briony commanded him effortlessly and he seemed to love it) — beneath all of it, one more line, added late, crowded against the edge of the page as if she'd had to fight the margin for it:

*Come and visit. Not forever. I know you'll never leave. But come and see the water from the other side, once, so you can describe it to them. You're the only one who describes things truly. Come in the autumn. Bring Maren Cobb — her mother has already agreed and I've already written to her. I've arranged everything, because I know you, and left alone you would find forty reasons and call them duties.*

Kestrel read it three times.

Then he took down a fresh sheet of the good paper — Briony paper, bought with Briony money — and he sat for a long time with the pen poised, listening to the tide, thinking about the water from the other side, thinking about whether a man who had made himself the keeper of a town's memory could afford to leave it even for a season, and then thinking, with a slow spreading warmth that felt almost like fear, about what it would mean to bring something *back*. A description. A true one. Of the world across the water, written in his hand, added to the shelf.

The children would need to hear it. The books would need to hold it. And the harbor, which had given him everything worth remembering, might for once receive something back from beyond its fog.

He began to write.

*Dear Briony. Yes.*

Outside, the tide turned. Inside, the lamp burned. On the shelf, four volumes waited for a fifth, and on the wall, the slates held their three hundred and eight names in the lamplight, patient as stones, certain as water, waiting — as all true ledgers wait — to be read aloud.

And somewhere across the darkening water, in a house she had built with her own hands, his sister was already writing back.
