## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width, and Tamsin Thackeray did not replace it.

She knew the trick of it — Aldous Vane had taught her in her first winter, when she was nine and all elbows and impatience, grinding ink while he worked by a light so thin it seemed more memory than flame. *A map drawn in poor light is an honest map,* he'd said, trimming the wick with his thumbnail. *It can't flatter itself. Every line must be certain before you commit it, because you won't see your mistakes until morning, and by then they'll be true.* She had thought it a lesson about ink. It had taken her fifteen years to understand it was a lesson about everything.

Now she sat at his bench, in his chair, with his magnifier screwed into her right eye socket until the flesh around it ached, and she drew the last coastline of his atlas.

The paper was Dutch rag, heavy as linen, and it drank the lampblack-and-gum ink the way chalk drinks rain. Her nib — a quill from a gull Vane had shot himself one grey October, saying a seabird's feather understood water better than any goose ever would — moved along the estuary of the Wend in strokes no wider than a hair. North bank: the clay cliffs, the shingle spit that shifted after every flood tide, the drowned orchard at Marlow's Reach where the river had taken three fields and left their apple trees standing hip-deep like drowned men who refused to lie down. South bank: the marsh, the eel-stakes, the long mudflats where the tide went out farther than any honest map could swear to twice in one year.

Her hand did not shake. That was the strange thing. Fifteen years of apprenticeship had built her hands into instruments steadier than her heart, and tonight, with the atlas nearly finished and grief sitting on her chest like a wet coat, the line came out clean as a bell.

*There,* she thought, lifting the nib at the river mouth. *That's the last of it.*

Except it wasn't. Nothing was ever the last of it. The coastline was only the body of the map; the soul went into the margins, and the margins were where the danger lived.

She set down the quill and flexed her fingers, one by one, listening to the workshop settle around her. Rain on the slates. The river muttering under the town. Somewhere below, old Cobb's dog barking at nothing, or at soldiers, though there were no soldiers yet — only rumors of them, moving up the great road from the south like weather. Everyone in Marlow's Reach spoke of the requisition the way farmers speak of a late frost: not *if*, but *when*, and what will it take from us this time.

Tamsin looked at the sheet drying on the bench, then at the stack beneath it — forty-one leaves of the borderlands atlas, each one a small country of careful lies and careful truths — and felt the fear come up through the floorboards into the soles of her feet, cold as the river in March.

Vane had died for these pages. Not quickly, not cleanly. They had found him at the bottom of the mill race in November, two years past now, and the coroner wrote *misadventure*, and the men in red coats who had been asking questions in the Reach that week packed up their questions and went away satisfied. Fifteen-year-old Tamsin had stood at the funeral between neighbors who meant well, holding a sprig of rosemary she could not remember picking, and she had understood even then — with the terrible clarity children are granted exactly once and never again — that the water had not killed him. The water had only finished the work someone else started.

And now she was going to finish his atlas. Which meant finishing the thing he died protecting. Which meant, if anyone ever read it rightly, handing every name in those margins to whoever held the book.

*So don't let them read it rightly,* said Vane's voice in her head, dry as the gum arabic on her tongue. *Read it wrongly. That's what I taught you, girl. That's the whole trade.*

* * *

He had taught her the cartouches in her fourth year, when she was thirteen and had just ruined a beautiful chart of the upper Wend by labeling a ford too plainly.

She remembered the afternoon precisely: the smell of hot glue and cut vellum, Vane standing over her shoulder with his hands clasped behind his back — he never touched a student's work, never; he said a teacher's hand on a pupil's page was like a stranger's hand on a child's shoulder, it taught the child to lean instead of stand — and his voice going quiet in the way that meant the lesson had stopped being about maps.

"You've written *Ford, safe at low water*," he said. "Why?"

"Because it is."

"Is it? In August, yes. In February, with snowmelt, a man on horseback drowns there, and his widow reads your map and curses your name." He tapped the margin beside her label. "But suppose the ford were dangerous for another reason. Suppose the danger wore a uniform. What then?"

Tamsin had frowned at the page. "Then I'd write *unsafe*."

"Then you'd have told the wrong people the truth." He pulled a stool over and sat, which he almost never did during lessons; sitting meant the lesson would be long. "Come here. Look at my Wexford sheet."

He unrolled it across the bench. To her eye it was one of his loveliest — the river braiding silver through green, hills hatched in fine parallel strokes like combed wool, and in the lower corner a cartouche: an oval frame of scrollwork enclosing the title, *A True and Exact Survey of the Waters of Wex*, with flourishes, with little painted roses at the corners, with a ribbon banner carrying the scale of miles.

"Pretty, isn't it," Vane said. "Count the petals on the second rose."

She counted. Seven.

"Now the third."

Nine.

"The scrollwork. Where does the ribbon cross the frame?"

Left side over, right side under.

"And the little fish in the water by the title — how many?"

Three. She looked up at him, and he was watching her with an expression she would spend years learning to read: the look of a man setting down something fragile and hoping the person catching it understands its weight.

"A surveyor who works for me — a friend of mine — needs to know that the Wexford crossing has a keeper who asks no questions. Seven petals, nine petals, the ribbon's weave, three fish. Anyone else sees decoration. He sees a sentence." Vane rolled the sheet closed. "The Crown pays for maps that tell everyone everything. A good mapmaker tells each reader only what that reader needs. The rest is silence."

"What if the wrong reader learns to count?" she asked — thirteen years old, and already asking the question that would keep her awake for the rest of her life.

Vane had smiled then, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "Then the mapmaker changes the flowers," he said, "and lets the old counting be found, and be wrong. There's always another silence behind the silence, Tamsin. That's not dishonesty. That's mercy with a locked door."

*Useful silences,* he called them. Never lies — he was fierce about that distinction, fierce enough to frighten her once, when she was sixteen and had suggested, cleverly, that they might simply forge a distance here or shift a bend there to mislead the tax surveyors. He had put down his pen with a click that silenced the whole room.

"We do not draw false rivers," he said. "The land is God's honesty and we will not perjure ourselves before Him. But which truths we set beside each other, and which we leave in the dark — that is ours. That is the craft. Anyone can copy the world. Only a mapmaker decides what the world is *for*."

* * *

The candle guttered. Tamsin trimmed it, decided against her earlier resolve, and lit a fresh one from the stub. Some devotions could be kept in the dark; fine linework could not.

She turned to the final leaf but one — the estuary sheet, still warm from the drying sand — and considered the cartouche.

*A New and Compleat Atlas of the Marches and Borderlands of the Wend River,* the frame read, in letters she had copied from Vane's own hand until her version was indistinguishable even to her. Around the title, the scrollwork waited for its ornaments. And here was the work that mattered, the work that had kept her at this bench past midnight for two years while the town called her the mapmaker's odd girl and pitied her gently and left her alone, which was exactly as she wanted it.

She dipped the quill and began the marginalia.

To any eye — to Ashgrave's eye, when it came, and she had begun to think of it as *when* rather than *if* — the ornaments would be what ornaments always were: roses with five petals and six, ribbons woven in the fashionable manner, a pair of herons standing in the shallows by the title, a motto in Latin she had composed herself and aged with tea so it looked a century old. *Fluminaque et sidera testantur* — the rivers and the stars bear witness. Pretty. Harmless. The kind of thing a provincial craftsman puts on his masterwork to prove he read his classics.

But Berta Quill, selling eels off her boat at the town stairs, would see the six-petaled rose and know the Quill crossing at Gallow Ford ran three feet deep at half-tide. The ferryman's widow at Netherby would find her husband's name folded into the flourish of the letter *W* — not written, never written, but shaped, the way a face is shaped in smoke — and know that the atlas remembered him. Eleven families. Eleven names the assizes had struck from the parish rolls after the rising, eleven households scattered or dead or fled north, and every one of them living here, in the margins, in petals and ribbons and the angle of a heron's neck.

Vane had spent twenty years building this silent census. Tamsin had spent two years completing it, learning his cipher the way you learn a dead language — slowly, reverently, alone.

And now, with the last coastline inked and only the cartouche unfinished, she sat back and let the fear have her properly.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: a finished atlas was evidence. An unfinished atlas was merely an eccentricity. If the army came — and they were coming, the drums of it audible now in the way the whole valley seemed to hold its breath — then every petal she painted was a name she painted, and every name was a rope, and the ropes were not only around her own neck. Berta's. The widow's. The fisherman Tam Roke, who carried messages and pretended to be too simple to carry anything. Half the town, tied together with her brushwork like a bundle of kindling waiting for one spark.

She could stop. She could leave the cartouche blank, declare the atlas incomplete, let it be a curiosity — the dead man's pretty papers — and no one would ever count her flowers.

Her hand hovered over the frame. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the ill-fitting window, and the shadow of her own arm lay across the coast she had just drawn, darkening the drowned orchard, the shifting spit, the whole honest length of the Wend.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the ghost in the chair she wasn't sitting in. "You didn't spend twenty years so I could leave you a blank."

But her voice shook, and the word *blank* hung in the air like the smell of the ink, and she understood that she had just made a promise whose price she could not yet count.

* * *

She worked until the candle was a puddle and the window showed the grey coming up over the water meadows. The cartouche took shape under her hand: the ribbon crossed left-over-right, the herons placed, the roses counted — five, seven, five again, and in the innermost fold of the scrollwork, so small it required the magnifier, the letters *E.C.* tucked into a curl of vine that any eye would read as pure ornament and only two people alive would read as a promise.

Ewan Corrie. She had not let herself think the name in years, and even now she handled it the way she handled the gull-quill — carefully, at the point where it was strongest, knowing it could snap.

She did not know where Ewan was. She knew only what the town knew: that after the rising the Corries had gone, all of them, in one night, over the ford and north, and that the eldest boy — the quiet one who fished, who had once shown her how to tickle trout out of the reeds and sworn her to secrecy with the seriousness only ten-year-olds can afford — had not come back. Some said dead. Some said transported. Some said worse, and lowered their voices, because *worse*, in Marlow's Reach, meant riding with the raiders on the border, and saying that aloud in front of the wrong listener was a hanging offense.

She sealed the atlas leaves between boards, weighted them, and cleaned the pen the way Vane had drilled into her — three waters, never two, and wipe against the grain, always — and as she worked the ritual motions her mind drifted, as it always did at this hour, to the sound of his voice saying *again, girl, again*, patient as the tide.

The workshop door had no lock worth the name. She had replaced the latch herself with a bolt, and beyond that, beneath the floorboards under the drying rack, there was a space Vane had shown her only in the last month of his life — a space she had not opened since the funeral, because opening it would make real whatever was inside, and she was not sure she could survive the contents being real.

She stood over the boards now, in the grey light, the crowbar in her hand.

The drums of the army were not yet audible. But the rumor of them had reached the market yesterday, and Mrs. Hallett at the bakehouse had whispered it across her loaves like a secret too hot to hold: *redcoats, before the week's out, and they'll want the records, mark me, they always want the records.*

Records. Ledgers. Maps.

Tamsin looked down at the floorboards, and the floorboards looked back at her with fifteen years of her master's silences hidden underneath.

She knelt, fitted the crowbar to the seam, and paused — one breath, two — hearing Vane's voice one last time, clear as the day he said it: *There's always another silence behind the silence, Tamsin.*

"I know," she whispered. "I'm about to go looking for it."

The board came up with a groan of old nails, and the smell of the hidden dark rose to meet her — dust, and oilcloth, and something else beneath it, something that smelled faintly of the river.
