## 2. Requisition

She did not hear them come. She heard the river change first.

Tamsin was kneeling in the dark of Vane's workshop, one hand still buried in the cavity beneath the floorboards, when the sound reached her — not through her ears but through the soles of her knees, a low tremor in the ground that had nothing to do with water. The river's voice altered too, flattened by something large and orderly moving along its bank, and the gulls that roosted on the mill roof lifted all at once like a sheet pulled off furniture.

Dawn was coming up grey and thin over Marlow's Reach. She could see it through the salt-fogged window: the long street running down to the quay, the crooked chimneys beginning to bleed their morning smoke, and on the far road — the one that came around the shoulder of Harrow Hill — a column of red.

Not a line. Not a thread. A column.

Her hand closed around what lay beneath the oilcloth before she even understood she had done it, and she drew it into her lap and sat back on her heels in the half-dark: a roll of paper bound with waxed cord, heavy as a sleeping bird, and beneath it a sealed letter, the wax unbroken, stamped with Vane's own matrix — the crossed dividers he used for everything official and nothing else.

There was no time to read it. There would be no time for anything again, she understood, except by stealth.

Boots now. Audible, human, hundreds of them, keeping time on the frozen mud of the high road. A drum somewhere near the head of the column tapping out a rhythm like a patient heart. Tamsin knelt there another moment, floor open at her knees like a wound, and then she did what fifteen years of apprenticeship had trained her body to do without consulting her mind: she put things back.

The letter went inside her shirt first, flat against her ribs where her heartbeat could keep its secret company. The rolled survey went back into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into the cavity, and she pressed the board down until the nails found their old grievances and seated themselves, then swept dust across the seam with the side of her palm until the floor looked like nothing at all — like every other worn plank in a shabby workshop, fit only to be stepped on.

*There's always another silence behind the silence.* She left the crowbar lying among the tools where any workman might have dropped it, banked the candle with two fingers, and walked out into the front room as the first fist hammered on a door up the street.

* * *

They came into Marlow's Reach the way water comes into a hull — not all at once, but steadily, finding every gap, filling every space, until you realized the thing you were standing in had changed its nature entirely.

Tamsin stood in the doorway of the shop with her apron still dusted from last night's work and watched them pass. The column filled the street wall to wall: grenadiers with their caps glinting dull copper in the new light, dragoons leading horses whose breath steamed, wagons with canvas tilted against the rain that hadn't started yet but clearly intended to. The red of their coats was the red of poppies, of arterial blood, of the sealing wax on government letters — a color chosen, she thought numbly, by someone who had never needed to hide from anything.

The townsfolk gathered at their doors the way people do when a flood passes: silent, holding children by the collar, making the arithmetic of their own danger with their eyes. Old Maren Cobb stood at the bakehouse with flour to her elbows, forgotten mid-work. The ferryman Tam Roke had stopped hauling in his eel pots and stood hip-deep at his stairs, dripping.

At the market cross, soldiers broke formation to nail up notices, four strokes of the hammer each, and an officer on a grey horse read the proclamation aloud in a voice pitched to carry to the farthest door.

*"By order of Colonel Elias Ashgrave, commanding His Majesty's forces in this district: all maps, surveys, charts, ledgers, books of account, correspondence, and records of trade whatsoever within the town of Marlow's Reach and the hundred thereof are hereby requisitioned for the use of the Crown. Any person concealing such materials after this notice shall be deemed to aid His Majesty's enemies."*

Aid His Majesty's enemies.

Tamsin felt the words go through her like a splinter seeking the bone. Somewhere behind her, under her feet, under her shirt against her ribs, lay exactly what the proclamation described. Fifteen years of Vane's careful silences, and hers layered over them like coats of varnish — and now the Crown had arrived with its lamp and its pry-bar and its legal language for prying.

The officer finished reading. He had a dry, unhurried voice and a face like a folded document, and though she would not learn his name for days yet, some instinct filed him away: remember this one. He is not here to shout.

Then came the knock at her own door.

It was a private, almost apologetic knock, which somehow made it worse than if they had broken the thing down. Tamsin opened it to a corporal with a list and two men behind him, and the corporal consulted his paper with great seriousness before saying, "Mapmaker's premises?"

"Mapmaker's," she said. "Was."

"He's dead, then? The master?"

"Fifteen months."

The corporal made a small mark on his list, a tick that seemed obscene in its tidiness. "Notice says records. That'd include his working papers, his ledgers, anything in his hand. You'll step aside, miss, and we'll have a look."

They were polite. That was the unbearable part. They wiped their boots — one of them actually wiped his boots — and moved through the shop with the efficiency of men who had done this in a hundred towns, lifting ledgers from the shelf, thumbing them, passing them back out the door to more hands waiting in the street. Her hands wanted to follow each volume as it left, the way a mother's hands twitch after a child crossing a road.

"The survey drawers," the corporal said.

"They're just charts," Tamsin said. "River soundings. Field boundaries. Nothing—"

"All charts are something, miss." He said it without malice, almost kindly, and pulled the drawer open anyway.

She watched his fingers walk across the rolled papers, and she thought of each one as a person: the Hollis family folded into the margin of the north moor; the Corries written in a hand disguised as tide-tables; old Jonas Fenn, drowned these nine years, alive in ink because Vane had refused to let the rebellion take him twice. The corporal saw paper. She saw faces. It was the difference between them, and it was everything.

He took eleven rolls. He left the drawer open.

When they had gone, carrying her master's life down the steps like so much salvage, Tamsin stood in the emptied shop and listened to her own blood. Then she went to the back room, pressed her palm flat against the hidden floorboard — warm from the banked candle's heat below, or she imagined it so — and said, very quietly, "Hold on."

* * *

By noon the whole town knew where the requisitioned records went: the old grain mill on the east channel, which the army had claimed the same morning and was already transforming. Soldiers swarmed its three storeys like ants in a felled tree. Timber palisades went up around the yard. The great waterwheel, which had turned for sixty years grinding Marlow's Reach's bread, stood locked and dripping, and someone had painted a broad white arrow on the mill's flank pointing to a new-posted sentry box.

The garrison mill. People said the words the way you'd name a churchyard.

Tamsin went down to the quay in the afternoon on the excuse of buying candles — she did need candles, and the lie sat easily because it was mostly true — and she positioned herself where she could watch without appearing to watch, which was also a skill Vane had taught her, though he'd called it *situating yourself honestly in the landscape.*

They brought the crates out of the wagon yard at half past three.

There were nine of them, pine crates with rope handles, and Vane's ledgers went into the fourth and fifth — she knew them at forty yards, knew the particular sag of the second crate's corner where the wood had warped years before she was born. Two soldiers carried each crate, grunting at the weight, joking about the burden of other men's scribbling. One crate tipped on the ramp and was caught with a laugh, and the laugh went through Tamsin like a fish-hook.

Fifteen years.

Fifteen years of arriving before dawn to grind pigments. Of learning why a coastline drawn too confidently is a lie and a coastline drawn honestly must admit its own uncertainty. Of learning the names nobody wrote anymore, learning them by tracing them in margins disguised as decorative flourish, learning to make a rose-compass out of a memorial. Fifteen years of Vane's voice — *slower, girl, truth has a tempo* — and of silences agreed upon in the evenings when certain visitors came to the shop after dark and left before light.

All of it stacked in pine crates. All of it carried up a ramp into the garrison mill by boys who couldn't read half of what they held.

She bought her candles. Her hands did not shake until she was paying for them, and then they shook, and the chandler's wife pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

* * *

She was home before dusk, and dusk came early now, pressing its face against the windows. Tamsin lit one of the new candles — a small extravagance of defiance — and set it beside the sealed letter she had carried against her ribs all day, and finally, finally, allowed herself to look at it.

Vane's seal. The crossed dividers pressed deep into wax gone amber with age. The letter was dated — she turned it over — dated the week before he died. Before the fever they said killed him, before the fall from the ferry stairs they also said, before all the soft lies the town had told itself about how a robust old mapmaker simply stops being alive.

She held the letter over the flame's edge for one moment, considering burning it unread. If it was dangerous, burning was mercy. If it was innocent, burning was loss. The wax softened, began to give—

And she pulled it back. *No.* Vane had sealed it for a reason; Vane sealed nothing without a reason; and whatever promise or confession lay inside deserved to meet the eyes it was addressed to, even if those eyes were hers alone.

She broke the seal.

The hand inside was his late hand, the cramped beautiful script of his final year, and she read it twice before the meaning stopped sliding away from her:

*Tamsin — if you are reading this, I am beyond the reach of correction, which will not surprise you, as I was always beyond your reach of correction. Know then what I have kept even from you.*

*There is a boy, Ewan Corrie, whom you fished with as a child and do not remember fishing with, because I asked you to forget, and you obeyed me, being obedient in everything but ink. His family passed by Gallow Ford the spring of the troubles, over the drowned bridge, on my word and my chart. I promised him — I gave my word as a mapmaker, which is the only oath I ever kept — that passage would stand ready at that ford whenever he had need of it, so long as my apprentice lived to hold the line true.*

*The survey is under the floor where you found it. The atlas is unfinished. Finish it. Keep the ford. What is promised in ink is owed in flesh.*

*Your master, who was proud of you in the manner of masters — silently, and too late —*

*A.V.*

Tamsin read it a third time. Then a fourth. The candle guttered and she did not move.

Ewan Corrie. She reached for the memory and found, precisely as he had predicted, a smooth place, a scar of absence — two children at a broken bridge, she was nearly sure, a boy showing her how to gut a fish, laughter, and then nothing, a hole cut cleanly out of her own childhood with a surgeon's skill. He had mapped her forgetting the way he mapped coastlines.

*I asked you to forget, and you obeyed me.*

"Gallow Ford," she whispered to the empty room. The name of the crossing in the hidden survey, the drowned footbridge only she could chart, the promise recorded in a dead man's hand — a promise that now, by the terms of his letter, lived in her whether she consented or not.

Outside, down the hill, the garrison mill showed lights at every window, and she imagined the ledgers stacked in the loft, guarded, patient, waiting to be read by men who would know exactly what a marginal name was worth.

She had promised a boy she could not remember a bridge she barely believed in, in a town full of soldiers, with the evidence of the whole arrangement sitting in the enemy's strongroom.

Tamsin snuffed the candle, and in the dark, her hand was already moving toward the floorboards.
