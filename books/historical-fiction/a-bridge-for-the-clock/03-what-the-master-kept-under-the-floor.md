## 3. What the Master Kept Under the Floor

The soldiers had not bothered to lock the workshop. That was the first cruelty of the morning — that Aldous Vane's life could be entered so easily, by anyone, with nothing more than a push of the shoulder against a door that had once required two hands and a whispered word.

Tamsin stood in the lane for a long while before she touched it. The requisition cart had gone by an hour past, its wheels groaning under the weight of his ledgers, and she had watched from behind Berta Quill's eel stall as fifteen years of careful silence were stacked like cordwood between crates of army biscuit. Now the town had that hollowed, held-breath quality it took on whenever red coats moved through it in numbers — shutters half-closed, eyes down, everyone suddenly fascinated by their own doorsteps.

She pushed the door.

It swung inward on hinges Vane had greased every Michaelmas, and the smell of the place broke over her like water: linseed oil, cut paper, the iron tang of fresh ink, and beneath it all the ghost of pipe smoke he had never been allowed, being a man who feared fire near his work above nearly everything else. Dust lay over everything now in a fine grey bloom. The long table stood where it always had, weights still pinning a half-finished chart of the estuary shallows, and his stool sat pushed back at the angle of a man who meant to return.

They had searched it, of course. She could see the search in the way things were *wrong* — drawers pulled out and shoved back crooked, the pigment cabinet standing open on its rows of little pots, lapis and ochre and dragon's-blood red, none of them worth a soldier's glance but each one worth more to her than the ledgers they'd taken. A chair lay on its side in the corner. Someone had driven a bayonet point into the plaster of the east wall, probing, and left the scar there like a signature.

But the soldiers had searched the way soldiers search: loudly, quickly, looking for weapons and gold and papers with seals on them. They had not searched the way a mapmaker searches.

Tamsin closed the door behind her and shot the bolt, though a bolt would stop no one. It was for herself. It said: *inside this room, for this hour, the old law holds.*

"You kept something," she said aloud, to the dust and the empty stool. Her voice sounded strange in it, too loud. "You always kept something."

* * *

She began where he would have begun, which was with what was visible.

Vane had taught her that a workshop confessed itself, if you knew how to listen. Every craftsman arranged his tools according to some private logic, and the logic was a fingerprint. So she walked the room slowly, cataloguing the wrongnesses, letting her eye do what his eye had taught it to do — find the line that didn't belong, the measurement that lied.

The ink stones were wrong first. He had kept three: coarse, fine, and a third he called the widow, ground so black it drank candlelight. The widow sat on the shelf above the grinding slab, exactly where it always lived, except that it sat a half-finger too far back, aligned not with the other two but with the shadow-line of the shelf's lip. Vane had been a man who set objects true to the edge of things. She turned it over. Heavy. Too heavy.

Inside, packed in waxed thread, was a key. Not iron — brass, small, the teeth filed irregular, the kind of key made for a strongbox or a cabinet with a false front, and warm from the sun through the window as if it had been waiting for her hand specifically.

Her heart began doing something she did not authorize. She put the key in her pocket and went on.

The floor came next, because floors were Vane's oldest trick. In the counting house at the Reach he'd kept his cash under a hearthstone; in the old shop before the flood year, his best vellum had lived beneath a flagstone dressed with forty years of boot-polish to look walked-on. But the bayonet scars in the east wall told her they'd probed the walls, and the disturbed dust showed they'd knelt and tapped along the boards near the table — she could read their knee-prints in the bloom, two sets, methodical as far as the third row of floorboards from the window.

And there they stopped. Because why would anyone keep going? The boards beyond were ordinary, scuffed, unremarkable — except that Tamsin had swept this floor six mornings a week for fifteen years, and she knew that the fourth board past the knee-prints, the one under the north corner of the drafting table where the light never quite reached, had always canted slightly upward at its southern end. A board with a personality. A board you learned to step over without thinking.

She dragged Vane's stool across the room — his weight, his tool, fitting her hand the way his lessons fit her mind — and worked the claw of a chisel into the seam. The board came up grudgingly, nails singing, and underneath was not coin or vellum but a shallow cavity lined with oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth:

A roll. And a letter, sealed with plain black wax, no crest, pressed with the flat of a thumb.

She sat down on the cold floorboards with her skirts around her and did not touch either one for a full minute. Outside, a patrol passed in the lane, boots keeping time, and she listened to them fade the way you listen to thunder counting itself away from you.

Then she unrolled the survey.

* * *

It was Gallow Ford.

She knew the river's every mood there — the drowned footbridge whose stumps showed at low water like rotten teeth, the shingle bar, the alders leaning drunk on the far bank. She had fished those waters as a child. She had drawn them a dozen times in practice, and Vane had corrected her drawings with a patience that felt, at the time, like mere pedantry.

But this was not practice. This was the master at full strength, and it frightened her how much stronger he was than she would ever be.

He had surveyed the ford twice — once in summer, once, by inference, in winter flood — and laid the two versions side by side on a single sheet, the way he sometimes did with coastlines to show how a channel migrated. Between them, in his small fierce hand, ran figures: depths, timber lengths, load calculations. And then, across the lower margin, in letters she recognized because she had seen them only once before, on the night he came home too late and too quiet and burned something in the grate —

*The bridge can be made to stand again. Seven piers sound below the silt. Timber enough within a day's haul. If it must be done, it can be done in nine nights, working between tides.*

Nine nights. Between tides. Her eye went down the column of figures and found, annotated in the same ink but fresher, dated — she checked it twice against her memory of that autumn — the week before his death:

*Tide tables enclosed for Saint Cuthbert's. E.C. to cross on the eve. Passage promised.*

E.C.

The letters seemed to lift off the page. Ewan Corrie. The boy who had baited her hooks with stolen cheese and laughed at her when the pike took the cheese and the hook both. The boy whose family had been driven out after the rebellion, whose name existed nowhere now except — she understood it all at once, the way a dark room comes bright with one struck match — except in the margins of an atlas that was currently sitting in a guarded loft in Colonel Ashgrave's mill.

Vane hadn't just mapped their escape, all those years ago. He had *kept the door open*. Fifteen years, through flood and peace and the slow rotting of a bridge, he had maintained the knowledge of how to open it again, and then, sensing — what? His own death? The coming requisition? Some soldier's instinct older than either — he had written it down, sealed it, buried it under the one floorboard a searcher would have to know the house to find, and waited.

For whom?

For whoever swept the floor.

Tamsin pressed the survey flat against her knees and found that her hands were shaking. It was not fear, or not only fear. It was the specific vertigo of inheriting something you cannot refuse — a debt with your name already written on it in someone else's hand. He had not asked her. Asking would have endangered her; silence was his kindness. But silence, she saw now, was also a trap of its own kind. All these years she had believed she was finishing the atlas for love of the work, for grief, for the dead man's honor. And underneath, sealed in wax and oak, had been the true reason, patient as a seed: *someone will come asking at Gallow Ford, and someone must be able to answer.*

She picked up the letter. The black wax was soft under her thumbnail. She almost broke it — her nail was actually in it — before she stopped.

Because the letter wasn't addressed to her.

There was no name on it at all, which meant it was addressed to whoever found it, which meant it was hers. But whatever promise lay inside had been written for another hand, another life, a boy grown strange and far away who now, if the rumors that came downriver with the fish were anything, rode with men the Crown called traitors. To open it was to make herself the keeper of a promise to a declared enemy of the King. To leave it sealed was to keep a promise she hadn't read, to a person she hadn't seen in fifteen years, based on four letters and a dead man's tide tables.

Either way, she belonged to it now.

She thought about burning it. She genuinely considered it, kneeling there in the dust with the grate cold beside her — feed the survey and the letter both to the flames and be only a mapmaker's apprentice, grieving, harmless, poor. The thought lasted the length of one long breath. Then she saw Vane's face as clearly as if he sat in his own stool: the dry, disappointed arch of his eyebrow, and his voice saying what he had said a hundred times over a hundred flawed charts — *a map that lies to protect itself is worthless, Tamsin. A map exists to carry truth across the water. Everything else is decoration.*

He had carried this truth fifteen years. Across his own death, apparently.

She would not be the one who dropped it in the river.

* * *

She reburied nothing. There was no sense in it — the floorboard was compromised in her mind now, and a hiding place you've opened is a hiding place you'll flinch at forever. Instead she took the survey apart from its roll, folded it small, and sewed it into the hem of her spare skirt where it lay against her ankle like a second pulse. The tide tables for Saint Cuthbert's she committed to memory that same evening, sitting in her cold room above the apothecary's, tracing the dates on her knee until she could recite them backward. The letter stayed sealed. She slept with it under her pillow and woke twice convinced she could hear wax cracking, though it was only the house settling, only the river talking in its sleep.

On the second morning she went back to the workshop — openly now, head high, the grieving apprentice come to mourn — and let herself be seen weeping among the ransacked drawers, so that her presence there would have an innocent explanation stitched to it in the town's memory. An old habit. Vane's first lesson, the one beneath all the others: *the useful silence is the one nobody thinks to question.*

But as she bolted the door behind her that afternoon, her eye caught the drafting table, and the half-finished estuary chart still pinned under its weights, and she saw what she should have seen the first day: the corner of a second sheet beneath it, showing at the edge, in a hand she knew.

She lifted the weights with fingers that had gone very steady — steadier than she felt, steady the way ice is steady — and slid the sheet free.

It was a list. Not a map. Names, in columns, with sums beside them, and next to certain names a small mark she had never seen Vane use: a tiny bridge, drawn in three strokes, like a child's drawing of a crossing.

Halfway down the second column, in the middle of strangers, stood a name she had known since she was seven years old, and beside it, the mark.

Berta Quill. Three strokes. A bridge.

Outside, somewhere toward the mill, a drum was beating, calling the tradesmen of Marlow's Reach to their conscription. Tamsin folded the list into her bodice, snuffed the candle, and went to answer it.
