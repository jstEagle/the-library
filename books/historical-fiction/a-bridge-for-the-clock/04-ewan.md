## 4. Ewan

The river in memory ran wider than it did now. That was the first lie Tamsin caught her mind telling, every time she went back — and she went back more often than she admitted, in the grey hour before the candle died, when the atlas lay finished for the night and there was nothing left between her and the dark but her own thinking.

In memory the water at Gallow Ford was a bright, chattering thing, wide as a street and shallow enough to read the stones through it. In truth the river had narrowed since; the drowned footbridge had sunk another hand's depth into the mud, and the town had grown another row of chimneys, and fifteen years had passed. But the boy was always exactly as he had been: sleeves rolled to the elbow, barefoot on the flat rock they called the pulpit, holding a willow pole with the solemn patience of a much older man.

"Your float's asleep," Tamsin told him from the bank, where she sat with her heels in the shallows and Vane's discarded practice-sheet of river soundings spread on her knees.

"It's not asleep. It's *considering*."

"Floats don't consider."

"These do." He did not look away from the water. He was a year older than her, all elbows and watchfulness, with a shock of hair the color of wet oak that his mother despaired of combing and his father called Corrie hair, as though it were listed somewhere in the family deeds along with the land they no longer owned. "Everything considers, if you're quiet enough. That's what your master says."

"My master says rivers are liars," Tamsin said. "He says they look slow and they're fast, they look deep and they're shallow, and any man who trusts his eye over his chain will drown in three feet of honest water."

Ewan considered this — everything considered, if you were quiet enough — and then said, "He's right about people, too."

She was eleven. She did not yet know how to answer a sentence like that, so she copied instead the way the water moved around her ankles, letting it pass and carrying nothing, and baited her own hook, and fished beside him while the afternoon leaned west and the broken stumps of the old footbridge stood in midstream like the last teeth of some patient, drowned giant.

* * *

The bridge had been broken longer than either of them had been alive. The grown-ups said the flood of '31 took it, or the frost of '29, depending which grandparent was doing the saying, and after the rebellion nobody had wanted to spend good timber on a crossing that led mostly to reeds and the Corries' failing croft. So the stumps stayed, and the fish loved them, and the children of Marlow's Reach loved them better, because a broken thing is a country all its own if you are small enough to govern it.

They had governed it together for three summers by then. It was understood without ever being said that the pulpit was Ewan's rock and the second stump, the one with the flat top good for drying lines and spreading out bread-and-dripping, was hers, and that the deep pool under the third stump belonged to neither of them and to both, the way the river itself belonged to no one and everyone, whatever the lawyers said. She had learned to gut a perch with his grandmother's knife. He had learned to read the contour lines on Vane's practice sheets, tracing with one blunt finger the shape of the pool they'd swum in all July, delighted and faintly offended to discover their river could be folded up and put in a drawer.

"She draws it smaller than it is," he'd complained once.

"Smaller so you can carry it," Tamsin had said, with the prim certainty of an apprentice repeating doctrine. "A map that's the size of the place is no use to anyone except God."

He'd looked at her then with something she hadn't had a name for — she had the name for it now, though she still didn't say it, even inside her own head, even now. He'd looked at her like she'd handed him the whole river folded small.

* * *

The soldiers came to the Reach on a Thursday, which was market day, which meant everyone saw it, which Tamsin understood later was the whole point. She was thirteen that autumn, two years past the fishing afternoons, already tall and already careful, spending six days in seven bent over vellum in the workshop above the tidewalk while Aldous Vane moved around her like a slow grey heron, correcting her hatching with one long finger and saying almost nothing, which she had learned to understand as praise.

She heard the horses before the bells. Everyone did. And then the square filled with red coats the way a spilled inkwell fills the cracks in a floorboard, finding every low place, and the proclamation was nailed to the market cross and read aloud twice by a man with a voice like a slate being scraped.

*Whereas certain persons late in arms against His Majesty do yet lurk within these bounds...*

She did not know all the words then. She knew the ones that mattered. *Lurk.* It was a good word for the work. It found you wherever you were standing and made you guilty of the ground beneath your feet.

That evening the Corries' chimney smoke was gone from the far bank when she looked for it — she looked without deciding to look, the way you scratch without deciding to itch — and by Saturday the story had made its rounds of the market in the way stories do, growing details the way a river grows tributaries. The eldest Corrie boy had been seen at the rising, someone said. Arms had been found. A name had been given, or names had been given, and the family had been given a day and a night to be gone from the bounds, and their croft would go to the Crown, and the fire that had burned on their hearth for four generations would burn for no one.

"They didn't all rise," Tamsin said to Vane that evening, too loudly, over the tracing table. "Tam Corrie's got a bad leg. He couldn't have walked to the rising, let alone fought in it. And the little ones—"

"The little ones," Vane said, without looking up from his ruling pen, "will need to cross the ford at Gallow on Tuesday next, at the dark of the tide, and they will need the water to be lower than it looks, and they will need no one watching who does not wish them well."

Tamsin stared at him.

He ruled a line, blew the vellum dust from it, and met her eyes at last. There was nothing in his face at all, which was how she had come to know there was everything in it.

"You will finish the Marlow sheet tomorrow," he said. "You will not finish it well. You will leave the sounding at the third stump unmarked, and when I ask you why, you will say you forgot."

"I don't forget soundings."

"I know it," said Vane, and went back to his pen.

* * *

She forgot the sounding. She said she forgot it. And on Monday evening Vane sent her down to the tidewalk with a parcel for Berta Quill, the eel-woman, and told her to take the river path and note anything unusual, because a mapmaker should never walk anywhere without noting, and she had known even then — she had known with the whole of her skin — that the parcel and the path and the noting were all one instruction wearing three coats, and that she was being made useful in a way that must never be spoken of.

Berta Quill took the parcel with hands like knotted eel-weirs and looked at Tamsin a moment too long, and said, "Tell the old man the fog comes early this time of year. Tell him it comes thick enough to hide a congregation."

Tamsin carried that message back the way she carried everything now: carefully, silently, in the place where words went to stop being words.

And Tuesday — Tuesday was the last time.

She had gone to the ford at dusk on some errand she invented herself afterward, some fiction about checking the gauge-stone, and she had crouched in the reeds where the path met the water, and the fog had come early, thick enough to hide a congregation, just as promised. And out of the fog had come the Corries: old Tam leaning on his grandson's shoulder, and the daughters with their bundles, and the youngest one asleep across Ewan's back like a sack of meal, his chin bobbing with each step, and Ewan himself — fourteen now, and taller, and gone thin in the face — walking last, turning every fifty paces to look back the way hunted things do.

Vane walked ahead of them with a lantern hooded to a coal, reading the water. He stopped at the third stump. He sounded the ford with a boathook marked off in hands and fingers, and murmured numbers to Berta Quill, who repeated them to the Corries, who stepped where they were told. And Tamsin, crouched in the reeds with her heart going like a bird in a box, watched the whole escape pass before her close enough to touch, and understood that she was watching the very thing her master's maps were *for* — not the fair copies sold to gentlemen, not the coastlines for the admiralty men, but this: the true use of a map, which is to move the living safely across the lies of the ground.

Ewan reached the far bank. He set his sister down. He turned once more to look back — and the fog thinned just enough, just for a heartbeat, and his eyes found the reeds where she crouched, or seemed to find them, and held there.

He didn't raise a hand. He didn't call out. He looked for the length of one breath at the place where she was hidden, and then he turned and followed his family up the far slope into the dark, and the fog closed behind him like a door.

* * *

For years afterward she told herself he hadn't seen her. That it was coincidence, a boy looking back at a burning candle of a town one last time. That the look had landed nowhere.

But she knew. She had known it in the reeds, felt it the way you feel a hook set — not pain, exactly, but the sudden knowledge that something has taken hold of you from outside and will not let go. He had seen her. He had known she was there, known she was watching, known whose apprentice she was — and he had said nothing. Had turned away. Had carried her silence out of the Reach along with his sleeping sister and his lame grandfather and every name his family had ever answered to.

And here was the thing she only admitted to herself now, fifteen years later, sitting over the finished coastline with the candle guttering: she had kept that silence for him ever since. Not decided to — kept it, the way you keep a debt. She had never spoken his name aloud in the workshop. She had drawn the far shore at Gallow Ford a hundred times and had never once, in any sheet, in any margin, let her line linger where the Corries' croft had been. Her pen had skipped that ground the way her eye skipped his absence in the market crowd, smoothly, automatically, a silence worn so smooth with use it no longer felt like silence at all. It felt like the shape of the world.

Vane had taught her the rule of useful silences. What she understood tonight, with the letter under the floorboards and the promise sealed inside it, was that some silences were not rules you followed. They were people you had lost, waiting all these years for you to notice you were still keeping faith with them.

He would come back someday. Boys who look back over their shoulder at the last breath of a crossing come back. And when he came, he would come to the water, because that was where promises lived — and her master's last survey was of a half-drowned footbridge only she knew how to chart.

She pinched out the candle, and in the dark the river in her memory ran wide and shallow and full of stones, and a float sat considering on the glassy pool below the third stump, and would not be hurried.
