## 5. The Mill Becomes a Fortress

The notice went up on the market cross before the church bells had finished ringing seven, and by half past the whole town had read it twice and pretended not to have.

BY ORDER OF COLONEL E. ASHGRAVE, HIS MAJESTY'S FORCES: all maps, surveys, ledgers, charts, and records of every kind within the parish of Marlow's Reach are hereby claimed for the Crown. Tradesmen possessing skill in penmanship, drafting, or the copying of documents will present themselves at the old mill on Thursday morning at eight of the clock. Refusal will be treated as concealment. Concealment will be treated as treason.

Tamsin read it a third time because her eyes refused to move on, snagging on the same three words as a nail catches cloth. *Concealment will be treated.* Not *may be*. The colonel did not deal in maybes. She thought of Vane's atlas lying beneath the third floorboard in the workshop — the real one, the true one, the one with names in its margins like seeds pressed into wax — and felt the familiar cold slide down her spine, the one that had lived there since the day they carried his body through the streets with his hat over his face.

*They have not found it,* she told herself, as she told herself every morning when she lifted the board and looked down into the dark and saw the oilcloth bundle sleeping there. *They have not found it yet.*

But Thursday was coming whether she liked it or not, and refusal would be treated as concealment, and concealment would be treated as treason, and treason in the year of our Lord 1746 was settled at the end of a rope in front of an audience.

So on Thursday morning she put on her good apron — the one without ink stains at the cuff, though she knew from experience that ink finds its way to every garment eventually, the way water finds the sea — and she walked down Fore Street with the other conscripted tradespeople, and she tried to look like a woman going to do honest work under compulsion, which was not difficult, because that was exactly what she was pretending to be.

* * *

The old mill had been built in her grandfather's time, back when Marlow's Reach still ground its own flour and the wheel turned fat and brown with the river's patience. It had been shut these six years, its machinery sold south, its windows whitewashed. Now the whitewash was broken out in places where soldiers had scratched their names or sharpened knives against the sill, and the great wheel stood still and dripping in its race like something drowned but not yet dead.

They had made it a fortress in three days. Sandbags at the doors. A sentry on the loading platform with a musket held the way a farmer holds a rake — competently, without love. Lanterns burning behind every window even at nine in the morning, because soldiers fear the dark in occupied towns the way children fear it, and with better reason.

At the door a corporal checked them off against a list. "Trade?"

"Mapmaker," Tamsin said. "Journeyman. My master is dead."

"Name?"

"Thackeray. Tamsin Thackeray."

He wrote it down wrong — *Thackery*, one c — and she did not correct him. There was a small fierce pleasure in being misspelled by the army. Let them hunt a woman who did not quite exist.

Inside, the mill's great ground floor had been cleared and filled with trestle tables in long ranks, and at each table sat a townsperson with a candle stub, a pot of army ink, and a stack of paper stamped with the broad arrow. And everywhere — everywhere — documents. Crates of them. Bundles tied with government string. Parish registers pulled weeping from their churches. Deeds, leases, tithe books, letters taken from the post. The whole paper life of the Reach, seized and sorted like grain awaiting the millstone.

And there, against the far wall beneath the loft, stacked in six crates with the lids pried off and no cover at all —

Vane's ledgers.

She knew them the way you know your own heartbeat in a crowd. The water-stained spines. The brass corners Vane had soldered himself. The third crate had a cracked lid she'd mended with fish glue four years ago on a rainy Tuesday while the master hummed some border ballad off-key. Fifteen years of her own hand was in those crates, interleaved with his, page after page of coastlines and firths and the little marginal annotations that meant nothing to a soldier and everything to the families who had paid for silence.

A sentry stood beside them, bored, shifting his weight. Nobody had opened them beyond the top ledger; she could see the ribbon bookmark still sitting where she'd left it, marking the Solway coast.

*Not yet read.*

The words arrived in her mind with the weight of a physical thing, and underneath them came another sensation, so inappropriate she was ashamed of it even as it bloomed: not despair. Recognition. The army had requisitioned her prison, yes — and in doing so had stacked the keys inside arm's reach, guarded by one bored boy with a rake-hold musket.

Vane had taught her a saying, one of his mapmaker's proverbs: *The chart that shows you the reef also shows you the channel.* Every danger drawn honestly is also a way through. She had thought he meant maps. Standing in the mill doorway with her heart hammering and her face arranged into dull compliance, she understood he had never once meant maps.

* * *

The man running the copying floor was not a soldier by bearing. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with ink-stained cuffs of finer quality than his coat and a clerk's stoop worn into his shoulders like a groove worn into a stair. He moved down the ranks of tables checking work, and when he reached Tamsin's table he stopped, and looked at her blank first page, and then at her hands.

"You've held a pen before," he said.

"I'm a mapmaker's apprentice, sir. Fifteen years."

"Fifteen years." He said it the way another man might say *a life sentence*, and something flickered across his face — not sympathy exactly, but the recognition of a fellow inhabitant of the same country, the country of people whose lives were made of paper. "Sergeant Hale. You'll copy requisition inventories. Fair hand, no abbreviations, dates in full. If you can't read a word, mark it and ask me rather than guess." He paused. "Guessing has gotten men hanged in this county lately."

"Yes, Sergeant."

He set a bundle in front of her — requisition lists, grain tallies, the small brutal arithmetic of an army feeding itself off a town — and moved on. But at the next rank he stopped again, and again, and Tamsin watched him and understood that this was how he moved through his days: stopping, checking, looking at hands. A man who read people the way she read coastlines, for the hidden rocks.

* * *

By noon she had learned the rhythm of the room, and rhythms were the first thing Vane had ever taught her to steal.

*"Everything has a rhythm, girl. The tide, the post rider, the guard changing watch. Find the rhythm of any place and you own its silences — the moments when nobody is watching because everybody is used to not-watching."*

The mill's rhythm went thus: the sentries changed at ten and two, and during the change both old and new guards clustered at the north door for the counting, leaving the loft stairs watched only by whoever happened to be seated facing them, which today was a cooper's widow named Mrs. Renwick, half-blind, copying letters home for soldiers too proud of their spelling to admit it. The loft itself was loaded with the most sensitive seizures — she could see the shape of them from her seat: flat map-chests, ledger crates, a locked writing desk with brass fittings she'd have known anywhere, because she'd polished those fittings every Saturday for a decade. Vane's personal desk. From the workshop. Which meant they had searched the workshop.

Which meant the floorboards had been looked at.

Her pen did not stop moving. That was the discipline, the deepest one, older than any craft: *the hand does not tell what the face does not show.* But her mind went down into the dark beneath the workshop floor and lay flat against the oilcloth and listened. They had pried boards — she'd seen fresh nail-scars on the skirting when she'd passed the window that morning. Had they lifted the third one? Had fingers closed around the bundle? No. She had checked it Wednesday night; it slept where it always slept. So either they hadn't found it, or they'd found it and left it as bait, watching the workshop, waiting for the apprentice to come home.

Both possibilities ended at the same place if she moved wrong.

So she copied requisition lists in a fair hand with dates in full, and she let her eyes rest, once every quarter hour, on the loft stairs, and she measured the distance the way she'd measure a crossing: thirty-two feet from her table to the stair foot, fourteen steps up, the third crate eleven feet along the loft rail, lid cracked, top ledger unopened. Guarded by one man, or none, depending on the hour. A crossing of perhaps ninety seconds, if the rhythm held.

It was madness. It was suicide. It was a channel drawn on a chart full of reefs.

She kept copying.

* * *

In the afternoon they brought out the atlas ledgers themselves and set them on a separate table near the loft stairs, and Sergeant Hale stood over them with a manifest, and Tamsin's whole body turned to a single listening instrument.

"Not those," Hale said to the corporal who'd reached for them. "Those go up. Colonel's orders. Nothing from the Vane seizure gets handled below this level until he's reviewed it personally." He glanced down the tables, and his gaze crossed Tamsin's face the way light crosses water — touching, not landing. "You. Mapmaker. Your master was Vane?"

"Yes, Sergeant."

"He's dead, I'm told."

"Drowned. Last October. Crossing the Ford at night."

"Mm." Hale wrote something on his manifest. "Colonel Ashgrave will want the hand authenticated. The master's hand, distinguished from the apprentice's. Expect to be asked." He said it flatly, information given to a tradeswoman about her trade, and moved on, and Tamsin stared at her requisition list until the columns swam.

*Authenticated.* Which meant Ashgrave could not yet tell which lines were Vane's and which were hers. Fifteen years of learning to draw like him, of swallowing her own style into his — she had resented it once, bitterly, at seventeen, wanting a line of her own the way the young want everything. Now it might be the thing that saved twenty lives inked into the margins.

And its opposite was also true, arriving coldly, in the same breath: the moment she authenticated anything, she would be telling the colonel exactly which pages held the master's secrets. Her skill was the lockpick and it was also the confession.

*The chart that shows you the reef also shows you the channel.* And the channel, sometimes, runs between the reefs so close that the hull scrapes on both sides at once.

* * *

At six they were marched out, counted through the door like coins. The evening air hit her lungs with the sweetness of a first breath. Berta Quill was at her eel stall on the quay as Tamsin crossed the bridge, and called out something about fresh catch, and Tamsin shook her head and kept walking, because the quay had ears now, everyone had learned in three days that the town had ears.

She let herself into the workshop through the garden door, barred it, lit no lamp. Stood in the dark breathing the smell of size and glue and old paper, her one unfurnished room in all the world.

Then she knelt, and lifted the third floorboard, and took the bundle out, and held it against her chest in the dark like a child, and made herself think.

They had searched here. They would search again, more thoroughly, once Ashgrave finished with the ledgers in the mill — and the ledgers would teach him, page by page, what Vane's marginalia looked like, and then the empty-seeming spaces in the atlas under her floor would start speaking to him, the way blank coasts speak to a cartographer. The atlas was safer under the floor today than it would be under the floor next week. The mill loft, ironically, was safer than either — guarded, sealed, untouched, *not yet read* — a hiding place the Crown had built for her with sandbags and sentries.

Unless Ashgrave read fast. Unless Ashgrave was clever. And everything about the notices, the phrasing, the systematic seizure told her he was clever the way ice is clever: patient, total, indifferent to what it crushed.

She rebundled the atlas. She did not put it back under the floor.

Instead she wrapped it in the oilcloth, then in sacking, then bound it with the straps of her old field-satchel, the one Vane had carried on survey walks, and she stood in the dark holding fifteen years of other people's lives and understood what tomorrow's copying would really be. Not servitude. Access. Every day at that table was a day she could learn the mill's rhythms deeper, learn which sentry drank, learn the loft stairs' fourth step, which she already knew groaned because she'd heard it groan this afternoon when a soldier fetched his pipe. Every day was a day the atlas sat unread, gathering safety the way a tide gathers height.

There would come a morning when it would have to come out of the loft and go somewhere — to her hands, to the Ford, to fire — and she could not yet see that crossing, could not chart it, and the failure frightened her worse than the sentries did. But cartographers, Vane always said, do not need to see the whole road. Only the next true bearing.

Tomorrow's bearing: get assigned, somehow, to anything connected with the Vane seizure. Authenticate the master's hand. Stand in the same room as the atlas and be invisible.

She slid the bundled atlas into the false bottom of the coal scuttle — a new hiding place, the old one burned — banked the fire, and lay down on her pallet without undressing.

Sleep came slow, and when it came it brought the mill: rows of candles, crates stacked to the rafters, and herself walking the loft rail in bare feet with the atlas open in her arms while a voice from the ground floor, courteous and surgical, read out names she almost recognized, asking each one, *and is this hand yours?*

She woke before dawn with the answer already formed in her mouth, rehearsed and ready:

*No, Colonel. None of it is mine. I only copied what I was told.*

Which was, she thought grimly, lacing her boots in the grey light, the truest lie she had ever been trained to tell.

Thursday's work began at eight. She was at the mill door at five past seven, first of the conscripts, apron clean, hands steady, wearing the face of a woman with nothing in her head but wages.

Sergeant Hale unlocked the door and looked at her a moment longer than he needed to.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm keen to earn my shilling, Sergeant."

"Mm," he said again — that same reading-the-water sound — and stood aside to let her in, and did not write down, on any list, that she had come.
