## 6. A Ledger of Names

The mill loft smelled of dust and old oil and, beneath it, the river — a green breath seeping up through the floorboards from the wheel turning below. They had given Tamsin a trestle table by the north window, where the light came in grey and even through glass fogged with the morning's damp. A bottle of ink, army issue, thin as ditchwater. A dozen quills cut badly by someone who had never held one for anything but signatures. And in front of her, stacked to the height of her forearm: requisition ledgers.

*Copy all names, holdings, and stores into the master register,* the orders said, in a hand that belonged to some clerk three ranks above anyone she'd met. *Omissions to be treated as concealment. Concealment to be treated as sedition.*

Tamsin dipped a quill, blotted it on the rag they'd allowed her, and began.

It was work she could have done asleep. That was the cruelty of it — that the skill Vane had spent fifteen years hammering into her hands, the steady wrist, the unvarying line, the patience to render a thing true without loving it too much — was exactly what the army wanted, and wanted cheap. She was one more instrument in their machine, a pen with legs. She wrote *Marlow's Reach* in her best round hand and thought: *I am copying my own prison's inventory.*

The first ledger was grain. Barley, oats, rye, field by field, farm by farm, each entry ending in a tally and a signature. She copied them without reading them, the way you cross a street you've crossed ten thousand times, and it was only when she reached the third ledger, the one bound in scuffed calfskin with water damage along its spine, that the names began.

*Hollis.*

Her quill stopped above the word for half a heartbeat. Then it went on, because a stopped quill was an omission and an omission was sedition, and because the hand that wrote on must never be the hand that trembled. Vane's first rule, taught before she was ten: *the mapmaker's face is part of the map.* She wrote *Hollis, Josiah, farmer, 40 acres, 2 cows seized, 1 cow remaining,* and somewhere behind her ribs a door she had kept shut for fifteen years rattled on its hinges.

Because Hollis was a name in the margins. Not this ledger's margin — the atlas's. The atlas she had not seen in eleven days, stacked in crates somewhere beneath her feet or above her head, guarded by men who couldn't read what they were guarding. In the atlas, in Aldous Vane's small italic hand, curled into the cartouche of the north-sheet like a vine grown into a wall: *Hollis. True name: Hollachan.* A family that had been Hollachans since before the Reach had a church, who became Hollises in the winter after the rebellion because a name was the easiest thing a man could shed and the hardest thing he could survive shedding.

Josiah Hollis had been born Josiah Hollachan. He was sixty now if he was alive at all. He still owed two cows, apparently, to the Crown.

She wrote it down. She wrote every letter of it, clean and legible and correct, and felt something tear quietly in her chest, painless, like wet paper.

That was the thing about erasure that no one warned you about, she thought as the quill moved. Erasure didn't kill the name. It just made the name homeless. It wandered. It turned up in requisition ledgers, in parish rolls, on tombstones under the wrong spelling, and every time someone spoke it aloud without knowing what it had been, the erased family died a little further. Vane had understood that. That was why the margins existed — not as rebellion, exactly, or not only. As a lodging house for the dispossessed. A place where a name could sleep until morning came, if morning ever came.

And now the Crown had confiscated the lodging house, and Tamsin Thackeray sat at a soldier's table writing the names into a different book entirely, a book that would be used to find those families, tax them, quarter soldiers on them, take their last cow.

*Omissions to be treated as concealment.*

She wrote. Her line did not vary.

* * *

Sergeant Hale had been a clerk for nineteen years, which meant he had learned to read men the way other men read dispatches — quickly, and for what was between the lines.

He noticed the woman flinch at the third ledger. It was a small thing. Most officers wouldn't have seen it; most officers didn't see anything smaller than a regiment. But Hale had spent nineteen years watching hands across tables — hands signing away farms, hands taking receipts for sons conscripted onto transports, hands counting out coin that would never cover the debt — and hands told him everything a mouth was careful enough to hide.

The mapmaker's apprentice — Thackeray, the tall one with ink permanently in the creases of her knuckles — had been writing steadily all morning, machine-steady, better than any clerk he'd supervised in the regiment. Then page forty-one of the calfskin ledger, and her quill had paused. Not stopped. Paused, the length of one drawn breath, and resumed. And there had been nothing in her face at all, which was how he knew it had cost her.

He looked at the page. Grain. Cows. Names of farmers. Nothing a sensible woman would pause over.

Unless she knew the names.

Hale filed it away, the way he filed everything, in the great damp archive of his own discretion, and went back to his own table across the loft, where he was compiling the garrison's correspondence. His back ached. It always ached now; nineteen years of army chairs will do that. Below him the millwheel turned with its patient groan, and the whole loft shivered faintly with it, like a sleeping animal breathing.

He had signed the requisition order himself. That was the thing he chewed on at night, the stone in his boot. Not the marching — marching was marching, and he'd been marched most of his life. Not even the bayonets; Marlow's Reach had surrendered without blood, and God spare him from ever learning what Ashgrave would be like with blood available. It was the ledger. *Requisition of all maps, records, and papers pertaining to the district, for the security of the realm.* His own hand on the order. Nineteen years of copying other men's decisions had finally promoted him into making one, and the first decision of his seniority had been to steal a town's memory.

A clerk's conscience is a strange organ, Hale thought. It doesn't stop working just because the work has stopped being innocent. It simply files things away, and aches, and files.

From his table he could see the whole loft: six conscripted townsfolk at six trestles, four guards doing a bad job of looking alert, and the crates. The crates interested him more than anything else in the room. They'd come up from the mapmaker's shop on the second day, heavier than paper had any right to be, and the corporal in charge of the inventory had logged them as *books, misc., approx. 40 vol.* Approximate. Forty volumes of a dead man's life, and the man sent to catalogue them couldn't have found *approximate* in a dictionary.

The colonel knew better, though. Hale had watched Ashgrave go down into the mill's ground floor twice now, alone, and stay an hour, and come up with nothing visible in his hands, and that frightened Hale more than open reading would have. A man who read was a man you could predict. A man who circled a locked crate for days, saying nothing —

Hale sharpened his pen, and copied a letter about fodder, and watched the woman write.

* * *

By afternoon they gave her the parish rolls, and that was worse than the cows.

Names were different in a parish roll. In a requisition ledger a name was attached to property — you could tell yourself it was the land being counted, the barley, the beasts, the Crown's due. In a parish roll a name was attached to baptisms. To marriages. To burials. *Maren, daughter of Tam and Effie Corrie, baptized the third Sunday after Trinity.* She had known Maren Corrie once. Had shared stolen plums with her on the towpath the same summer the plums were green and everyone was laughing anyway, the summer before the family was driven out and the name became dangerous and Maren became nobody's daughter in official ink.

Tamsin copied the baptismal entries in her round hand and let the names pass through her like fish through a weir — caught, counted, released downstream into the army's register. *Corrie* appeared eleven times in the roll. She did not linger over any of them. She did not let herself think of Ewan, because thinking of Ewan at an army table with a guard six feet away was the kind of luxury that got people killed, and she had a promise now, a folded survey hidden in the false seam of her bodice, and promises required a living promiser.

But the eleventh Corrie entry was a burial, dated the week of the rebellion's aftermath. *Elspeth Corrie, widow, aged 71.* Elspeth had taught her to gut an eel on Berta Quill's dock when Tamsin was seven, laughing at her squeamishness, saying *girl, everything worth having fights you on the way out of its skin.* There was no cause of death recorded. There rarely was, that year.

Tamsin's quill paused. One drawn breath. She resumed.

Across the loft, Sergeant Hale looked up from his letters.

He didn't say anything. He didn't look long — a glance timed to be invisible, the glance of a man checking whether rain was coming. But Tamsin felt it land, the way you feel a moth touch your shoulder in the dark, and she understood two things at once, both of them dangerous. First: that he had seen her pause before, at the Hollis entry, and had been waiting to see it again. Second: that a man who saw and said nothing was not the same as a man who saw and approved — and not the same, either, as a man who saw and reported.

He was perhaps fifty, this sergeant, with a clerk's stoop and a soldier's cropped hair going grey at the temples and a face like a ledger itself — ruled lines, entries, everything balanced. His uniform was immaculate in the way of men for whom neatness is the last territory they still govern. When he caught her eye — one heartbeat, no more — his expression did not change at all.

Then he returned to his letters, and so did she, and between them, across twenty feet of dusty lamplit air, something was agreed that neither of them would ever put into words. Not an alliance. Alliances had oaths and risks and names on lists. This was smaller and stranger: two people doing the same terrible job, recognizing the ache in each other's wrists, and choosing — consciously, deliberately, in the only language either trusted — to keep writing.

*Omissions to be treated as concealment.* Fine. Neither of them would omit anything. But there were silences that lived between entries, in the pauses, in the not-asking, and those silences belonged to no register the Crown could requisition.

Vane would have called it a useful silence. Tamsin, copying the twelfth Corrie entry, called it the first thing in eleven days that had felt like company.

* * *

The interlude broke at dusk, when the guards changed and Corporal Fenner — young, loud, proud of his inventory — climbed to the loft with a lantern and a clipboard and announced that the copying detail would continue tomorrow, and that the woman Thackeray alone was summoned to remain another hour, as the colonel had "a small matter of authentication."

Authentication. The word sat in Tamsin's stomach like swallowed ice.

Fenner read it off his clipboard with relish, pleased to deliver anything with a colonel's seal on it, then lingered, as young men do, over the crates. "Forty volumes of old rubbish," he said, kicking one affectionately. "Half of it water-damaged. You'd think the colonel would just burn—"

"Corporal." Hale's voice was not loud. It didn't need to be; nineteen years of clerking had taught him exactly how flat a voice needed to be to close a subject. "The detail dismisses at the bell. Except Thackeray."

Fenner went, wounded. The other conscripts filed out — the cooper's son, the two Widow Marsh girls, old Penn the ropemaker, all of them careful not to look at her, because whatever she was summoned for might be catching. The guards changed below. The millwheel groaned. The light in the loft went from grey to amber to blue.

Hale banked the fire in the little stove, set a lamp on her table without being asked, and stood a moment, looking not at her but at the calfskin ledger, open to page forty-one. Hollis. Two cows.

"My grandmother's name was Halloran," he said, to the ledger rather than to her. "Before it was Hale. Nobody living spells the old one right." He straightened the ledger's corner, minutely, a clerk's gesture, squaring the world's edges. "I'll be at my table till the bell. I see poorly after dark."

Then he went to his table, and took up his pen, and copied a letter about fodder, and saw nothing at all.

Tamsin sat alone in the amber circle of the lamp with forty volumes of her dead master's life around her and the survey crackling softly against her ribs with every breath, and waited for the colonel.

She thought about running. The window, the roof of the lean-to, the dark — she'd mapped the drop herself, once, out of habit, the way Vane had taught her always to map exits. But the summons wasn't a trap; traps weren't announced on sealed paper. It was something worse. It was the hunter asking the quarry to hold still while he examined the scent.

*Authenticate the master's hand.* Which meant Ashgrave suspected the marginalia meant something. Which meant the atlas was no longer just confiscated paper — it was a puzzle he had decided to solve, and puzzles, in Tamsin's experience of the past eleven days, got solved by the only person in the room who understood them.

Which meant she was about to sit across a table from the man who held her atlas, and teach him, with her own hands, how to read it — and somehow teach him everything except the truth.

She dipped the army's thin ink. She squared the army's bad paper. Above her the crates sat silent in the dark like coffins, or like seeds.

When boots sounded on the stairs — unhurried, measured, a man who had never once needed to hurry anywhere — Tamsin Thackeray did not look up until the footsteps stopped. Whatever her face showed then, she made sure of one thing first, the way Vane had taught her before she was ten:

She made sure it was part of the map.
