## 7. The Woman Who Sells Eels

The eel woman kept her traps at the far end of the Reach, past the salt sheds where the town's smell turned from bread to brine, and Tamsin had been sent there exactly twice in fifteen years — once for Aldous Vane, to deliver a rolled chart wrapped in oilcloth and to ask no questions about it, and once for herself, when she was nine and foolish enough to want to see what an eel trap looked like at dawn.

Both times, Berta Quill had been awake before the light.

Tamsin came down the shingle path now with the fog still thick on the river, boots wet through, a basket over one arm disguised as an errand. The disguise was for the soldiers on the quay, two of them stamping warmth into their feet by the customs house brazier, watching everything that moved with the particular boredom of men who had not yet been given anything worth shooting. She did not look at them. Vane's first lesson, older even than the pen-work: *walk like your errand is duller than you are.*

The salt sheds gave way to osier beds, and the osier beds to a lean-to of driftwood and tarpaulin canted against the wind off the water, smoke leaking from its seams. Eel traps hung in rows along one wall, willow-woven, dripping. A punt was hauled up on the mud, black with tar, its belly scarred from years of gravel. And in the doorway of the lean-to, silhouetted against her own fire, stood a woman built like a capstan — short, wide through the shoulders, forearms bare despite the cold, gutting a fish with a knife that looked old enough to have opinions.

"You've come at a bad hour," Berta Quill said, without looking up.

"Fish don't keep business hours."

"No." The knife paused. "But women who sell eels do, and mine opened an hour ago and closed ten minutes after I saw a red coat on the quay. So either you're here for eels, which you're not — you walked past my best stock twice and didn't glance at it — or you're here for something else." Now she looked up. Her eyes were pale grey, the colour of the river in February, and they went through Tamsin like a sounding line finding bottom. "You're Vane's girl."

"Tamsin."

"I know your name. Knew your master better." She flicked the offal into a bucket and wiped the knife on her apron. "Sit, if you can find a dry spot. There's tea if you'll drink it, and there's honesty if you'd rather that instead. Most people take the tea first and work up to the rest."

Tamsin found an upturned crate near the fire and sat. The heat found her bones and she realized, with some embarrassment, how cold she had been — not just this morning but for days, since the crates of ledgers had gone up into the mill loft and the door had been locked behind them.

Berta poured tea from a blackened kettle into two tin cups and pushed one across. Then she sat down opposite, elbows on knees, and waited.

"I need to rebuild Gallow Ford," Tamsin said.

She had rehearsed other openings on the walk down — careful ones, sideways ones, the kind of approach Vane would have called *sounding the channel*. All of them died in her throat, because Berta Quill was already watching her with the expression of a woman who had been expecting this sentence for twenty years and was mildly annoyed it had taken so long.

"The drowned footbridge," Berta said.

"The footbridge. Yes."

"That fell in the flood of 'thirty-one. That nobody has crossed since, because nobody alive knows where the pilings stand under the water except me, and I'm not telling." She sipped her tea. "Go on."

"How do you know where the pilings—"

"Because I laid half of them, girl. Before it was a ruin it was a route, and before it was a route it was a favour I did for people whose names I never asked." Berta set her cup down on a nail-head, precise as a compass point. "Now. You tell me why a mapmaker's apprentice comes through the fog before light to ask an old smuggler about a bridge that's been under the river since before she could hold a pen. And you tell me true, because I'll know if you don't. It's a trade habit. I can feel a lie the way I feel a snagged net."

So Tamsin told her true. Not all of it — not yet — but the shape of it. She told her about the survey hidden under the workshop floor, the sheets in Vane's hand showing every piling of the old footbridge, soundings taken at low water, the tide tables pencilled in his cramped leftward script. She told her about the sealed letter with *E.C.* on the fold, and the promise dated six days before the man who made it died on the mill stairs with his skull broken by a musket butt. She told her that the army had the ledgers now, stacked in the loft, unread, and that Colonel Ashgrave was a man who read things.

Berta listened without moving. When Tamsin finished, the fire popped once, and out on the water a heron lifted, unhurried, as though the world were a reasonable place.

"Ewan Corrie," Berta said.

Tamsin's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "You knew him."

"I ferried his mother across that ford twice before he was born and once after, carrying him in a market basket with a cloth over the top so he wouldn't cry at the spray." Something moved behind the grey eyes, quick and then gone, like a fish turning under ice. "I remember the weight of that basket. You don't forget the weight."

"He rides with the raiders now. Upstream. If the army finds him—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud gave it weight, and it was heavy enough already.

"If the army finds him, they hang him from the mill beam and leave him there through market day, to be instructive." Berta said it flatly, the way she might report the price of eels. "Yes. I know how it goes. I've watched it go."

"And Vane promised him a crossing. A safe one, on a night of his choosing. That promise is sitting on my floor now with nobody left alive to keep it except me." Tamsin leaned forward. "I can read the survey. I can chart it, I can measure it, I can probably even rebuild it — Vane taught me how structures carry load before he taught me letters. But timber doesn't move itself, and neither does a boy who needs to be on the far bank before the patrol changes watch. I need boats. I need hands that know the water. I need someone Ashgrave's soldiers don't look at twice."

"They look at everyone twice now."

"Then someone they look at three times and trust anyway, because she sells them eels."

For the first time, Berta laughed. It was a short sound, more breath than voice, and it warmed the lean-to more than the fire did. "Oh, you're his, right enough. He used to talk to me the same way. Polite as church and sharp as a gutting knife underneath." She rose, took her cup to the kettle, refilled it, and stood with her back to Tamsin, looking out through the tarpaulin flap at the fog-bound river.

"Here's what you should understand," she said. "Gallow Ford isn't a place. It's a memory. Half the families between here and the hills got out over that crossing or ones like it, after the rising, when the courts were burning tenancy rolls and drawing new lines through living people. I carried some of them myself. Farmers. Priests. Two girls not much older than you, and a fiddler who played the whole way across because he said if he stopped playing he'd start screaming. Some of them lived. Some came back later, when it was safe, and bought eels from me on a Thursday and never let their eyes say thank you, because thanks leaves a trail." She turned around. "And then the flood took the bridge, and I let the river have it, because a route that stands is evidence, and a route that's drowned is only a story. Safer as a story. I've kept it that way for fifteen years."

"And now?"

"Now there's a boy in a basket grown into a man upstream with redcoats combing the water meadows for him." She came back and sat, and the set of her shoulders had changed — squared, deliberate, the posture of a decision being put away somewhere load-bearing. "And now there's a dead man's promise in a living woman's hands, which is the most dangerous kind of paper there is. And now you've come to me in the fog, which means you've already decided, and what you're really asking isn't whether I'll help. You're asking what I'll cost."

Tamsin held her gaze. "What will you cost?"

"That's not settled yet." Berta drank, slowly. "I'll help. That part's done — don't ask me why; the answer's twenty years long and mostly made of names I won't give you. But I'm not a charity and I'm not a rebel. I'm a trader. And I'll name my price when I know the full shape of what we're doing, and not one hour sooner, because a price named too early is a price named blind."

"I can't promise what I can't—"

"You can't promise anything. Good. Neither can I. We understand each other perfectly." She set the cup down and rubbed her hands together briskly, and just like that she became practical, a woman planning a job. "Right. The survey — the real one, under the floorboards. Can you get it out of the workshop?"

"It's already out. I moved it the night after the requisition, before they sealed the doors. It's in pieces, wrapped in waxed linen, in three places no soldier would think to search."

"Where?"

"I'd rather not say."

"Good girl." Berta almost smiled. "First rule of the trade: never keep all your cargo in one hull. Second rule: never tell anyone your hulls. You'll notice I haven't asked where you sleep."

"The bridge itself. Can it stand? Honestly?"

Berta was quiet for a moment, and Tamsin watched her count something behind her eyes — tides, timbers, years.

"Honestly? The footings are sound. River-scoured but sound — Vane would have known that; his soundings would show it, the way the depths run clean under the old pier positions. The decking rotted, the rails went, but the bones are stone and oak, and oak forgives more than people think. Give me salvaged timber, tar, two nights of low water, and three pairs of hands that can keep quiet, and I'll give you a crossing a man could take at a walk." She looked up sharply. "A walk, mind. Not a gallop, not a parade. And only at the tide window. Miss it, and the ford shows its teeth."

"There'll be a tide window on the eve of Saint Cuthbert's."

"I know when Saint Cuthbert's tide falls. I've landed eels on it for thirty years." A pause, and then, more quietly: "Vane always did pick his nights well. Low water, dark of the moon, and the whole town drunk on the saint's ale so nobody counts boats." She shook her head slowly. "He planned this before he died. Maybe before he finished the atlas. Maybe the atlas *is* the plan, and the rest is just ink around it."

The thought landed in Tamsin's chest like a dropped anchor. She had spent weeks thinking of the atlas as a thing she was rescuing — a duty, a debt, a grief with a spine and pages. She had not let herself think it might also be an instrument, tuned and waiting, with her hands the last piece missing.

* * *

They talked until the fog began to lift and the risk of being seen together outweighed the value of talking. By the end, the plan had a skeleton: Berta would find the timber — "there's a barn upriver full of planking that fell off a legitimate barge, entirely by accident, repeatedly" — and Tamsin would bring the survey sheets, one at a time, memorized and then redrawn rather than carried whole. They would meet at night, at the osier beds, never twice at the same hour. Nothing written between them. Nothing spoken in front of the water, because Berta claimed, with a completely straight face, that rivers kept accounts too.

"One more thing," Berta said as Tamsin rose to go. "The boy. Corrie. Do you know him yourself, or do you only know the letter?"

The question caught her off guard. "I — knew him. When we were children. Before his family went north. We fished off the broken bridge. He pushed me in the river once and I never forgave him properly."

"Fifteen years, and that's what you kept."

"It's what there was room for." Tamsin pulled her cloak tight. "Why does it matter?"

"Because promises made for strangers are business," Berta said, "and business can be renegotiated when the price gets high. Promises made for people we've fished with are something else." She picked up her gutting knife again and turned back to her table, and her voice came over her shoulder, matter-of-fact and devastating. "I'll know which kind yours is when the night comes and it costs you something. So will you."

Tamsin walked back along the shingle with the fog thinning around her and the town assembling itself out of the greyness — roofs first, then masts, then the mill with its new flag, then the soldiers at the quay, one of whom glanced at her basket and saw exactly what she intended him to see: a woman who had been out early and come back with nothing worth reporting.

Her basket was not empty, though. Under the false bottom, wrapped in waxed linen no bigger than a prayer book, lay a single tracing Berta had pressed on her at the last minute — a rough sketch of the ford's western approach, drawn from memory, marked with a cross where the mud gave way to channel. *So you believe me,* Berta had said. *And so if I drown before we finish, you'll know where to dig.*

Hope, Tamsin thought, climbing the path toward the town. It arrived looking like a bargain. It always did. Vane's books were full of travellers who had traded something at a ferry crossing and only learned afterward what they'd paid for — and every one of those stories ended with the traveller standing on the far bank, alive, counting the cost and finding it strange that they'd have paid twice as much.

She was halfway up the high street, composing in her head the lie she'd tell at the workshop door — *checking my eel seller's ledger, sir, the army wants trade records too* — when she saw the soldiers outside it.

Two of them, at her own door, and a third coming down the steps with the collar of his coat wet with river fog and an order in his hand. As she slowed, the door opened from inside and a staff officer stepped out into the grey morning, folding a paper with the unhurried precision of a man who had found what he was looking for.

Inside, through the gap, Tamsin could see the workshop floor — and the place where the floorboards had been.

"Apprentice Thackeray?" the officer said, and his voice was mild as milk. "Colonel Ashgrave would like a word about the master's hand."
