## 9. Forging the Master's Line

They gave me Vane's own desk.

That was the first cruelty of the morning — that and the sunlight. The garrison had cleared a corner of the mill's ground floor for my work: a trestle table set beneath the eastern window so the light would fall honest across the paper, two soldiers at the door, and Aldous Vane's drawing desk hauled from his ruined workshop on a handcart like so much seized furniture. Someone had wiped the ink stains from its surface. Fifteen years of his working life, scrubbed away with river sand until the oak showed pale as bone.

I stood over it a long moment before I touched it.

"You'll work here," Sergeant Hale said behind me. He sounded tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "Colonel's orders. You demonstrate the master's methods, sheet by sheet, so we know what we're holding. Anything you can't account for gets flagged."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you stand in the yard while they burn the lot, and you watch." He set down my tool roll — my own tools, taken from my lodgings, unrolled now on Vane's desk with a delicacy I hadn't expected of him. "He said to tell you it's not a punishment. It's an examination."

Vane used to say something similar every quarter-day of my apprenticeship, usually before making me redraw an entire coastline from memory. *It's not cruelty, girl, it's examination.* The echo landed somewhere beneath my ribs and sat there.

"Then let's be examined," I said.

* * *

The technique they wanted first was his coasting line — the double-stroke shore that looked, to any eye but a trained one, like simple flourish. Vane had built it from a habit of the old Dutch engravers: a heavy stroke for the true waterline, then a hair-fine ghost stroke above it, close as a whisper, that marked where the land went soft at spring tide. Marsh ground. Ground that could drown a cart or carry a man, depending on the month and the moon.

To Ashgrave's clerks it was ornament. To anyone who'd ever run cargo at night, it was a language.

I pinned the first sheet — a survey of the lower reach, copied from the seized ledgers onto army paper — and took up my finest nib. My hands were steady. I noticed that about myself with something like surprise, the way you notice your own breathing in a sickroom. Under the eyes of two bored soldiers I drew Vane's coasting line eleven times, each stroke loaded at the heel and lifted clean at the toe, and the ghost strokes came out fine and faint and true.

"She's good," one of the soldiers said to the other.

"She's supposed to be," Hale said. He hadn't left. He stood at the window with a ledger under his arm, watching me the way he'd watched me flinch over the requisition lists — with attention he pretended was boredom.

The second demonstration was the hill-hatching, and the third was Vane's lettering, which no living hand could counterfeit perfectly because no living hand had been shaped by forty years of holding a graver wrong on purpose, cutting letters that leaned into the wind of the page. But I had fifteen years instead of forty, and I had been, though neither of us ever said it aloud, the closest thing to a continuation of him that existed. The letters came out leaning. Hale's mouth tightened when he saw them — recognition, maybe, or unease.

By noon I had demonstrated six techniques and the colonel's clerk had filled four pages of notes describing them in dry military prose, as if craft could be captured by inventory. *The subject applies a secondary stroke indicating tidal margins.* Yes. And a prayer could be described as the movement of a mouth.

Which is when I understood what I would do. Not in a flash of cleverness — nothing so clean. It arrived the way Vane said real knowledge always arrives: slowly, and all at once.

They wanted me to prove the master's hand. Very well. A hand that could prove itself could lie with the same authority. And a forgery examined by men who couldn't read the language would pass as truth precisely because it carried the master's signature strokes.

I could give Ashgrave maps that were accurate in everything except the one thing that mattered. I could build him a false country, drawn beautifully, honestly, in every line but one.

I just had to survive long enough to place it.

* * *

They brought me sheets through the afternoon. Working copies, the clerk called them — fair reproductions of Vane's surveys that the army intended to use for patrol planning, once authenticated. The atlas ledgers themselves stayed stacked in the loft above, guarded, unread, waiting for the colonel's slow cipher-work to crack their margins open. Every sheet that passed across my desk was another piece of the borderlands translated into a document the Crown would trust.

Trust. There was the fulcrum. Everything I meant to do depended on Ashgrave trusting my lines more than he trusted me — and he was not a fool. Chapter eight of his campaign, whatever he wrote in his own private book, would be doubt. So the lies I planted could not be lies of commission, not yet. They had to be lies of silence, of weight, of emphasis — the kind of falsehood that looks like diligence.

The opportunity came at the fourth sheet. Gallow Ford.

My hands did not stop moving. That was the whole art of it, Vane drummed into me by the second year: *the page must never feel you thinking.* I drew the Ford as Vane's original had it — the drowned footbridge rendered in his broken-pier symbols, the crossing marked with the small circled cross he used for usable fords, and beside it, in the margin, the annotation in his cramped italic: *passable at low water, St C— tide, hard going, foot only.*

All true. All already in his hand, already in the ledgers upstairs, already half-cracked by a patient colonel. I could not remove what the enemy had already read. But I could tell the truth in a way that made it sound like a lie — or better, tell it twice, differently, and let the difference do the damage.

So I added the ghost stroke.

Along the near bank below the Ford, where the marsh began, I laid Vane's hair-fine tidal line — and then, with the smallest adjustment of pressure, a second line, barely distinguishable, a hand's width higher. To any eye it read as ordinary draftsmanship. To a man studying the master's conventions — and Ashgrave had been studying nothing else for days — it would read as information. Ground that firmed at low tide. A route around the marsh's flank. Dry-ish going for infantry, if the timing held.

None of it existed. The ground there was bottom-suck and reed-bed, drowning ground, the kind of country that takes a soldier's boots and then his legs and then, given enough of them, his patience. Vane had mapped that stretch three times in his life and each time written the same word in his field book: *unusable.*

I inked the false line with the same breath-weight I'd used on every honest stroke that day, blotted it, sanded it, and passed the sheet to the clerk.

"The margin's doubled there," Hale said.

My heart performed one enormous, silent maneuver. "Tidal margin and firm ground," I said, keeping my voice at the flat pitch of a tradeswoman reciting prices. "Master's convention. Where the lines converge, the marsh carries weight at low water. Where they part—" I shrugged. "Where they part, it doesn't."

That much was even true. It was the location I had moved.

Hale looked at the sheet a moment longer. Then at me. Then back at the sheet. "And the Colonel will know this convention?"

"It's in his notes," I said. "Your clerk has three pages on it."

He did not answer, and I bent again to the work, and for a full minute the only sounds in the mill were the scratch of nibs and the groan of the millwheel outside, turning its slow confiscated circles.

Betraying my training — that was how it felt, laying that line. Vane had beaten falsity out of me year by year, held my wrist over the vellum and said *a map that lies kills people, girl, and the people it kills are the ones who trusted it most,* and here I lay with a steady hand and a quiet heart, building a lie designed to kill the trust of armed men. I searched myself for guilt and found something colder and more practical underneath, and I did not know whether that was grief still doing its work, or the person grief had made.

*A map that lies kills people.* Yes. I had chosen whose.

* * *

They let me break at dusk. Two hours' rest in the yard, supervised, before the evening session under lamplight. I walked the yard's trampled mud with a soldier ten paces behind, and I let my eyes do what eyes are permitted to do: count.

Barrels of bridging timber by the north wall. Powder magazine — the old tithe barn, roof newly repaired. The mill loft's loading door, facing the water, chained but with a padlock a pick could argue with. Boats: two, hauled up, oars locked in the guardhouse. The garrison's strength was arithmetic, and arithmetic could be carried in the head the way Vane carried coastlines — not written anywhere, just known, the way you know the weight of your own arm.

And the ford road. The track that ran from the town's edge down toward Gallow Ford was walked by milk-carts at dawn, and no one had thought to close it, because in the army's new maps — my maps, the ones I was building for them — the Ford was a broken bridge and a footnote.

For now.

"Thackeray." Hale, at the yard door. "Evening session. Colonel wants the marginalia conventions demonstrated tonight, live. He'll attend himself."

The cold came up through the soles of my boots.

"He'll want you to explain the cipher-work as you go," Hale went on, his voice carefully level, a man reading orders he didn't relish. "He's matched some entries against parish records already. Renamed holdings. He says the pattern proves intent." A pause, the length of a man deciding something. "He also says forgery of the Crown's documents is punishable by hanging, and asks that you keep that in mind regarding the accuracy of your working copies."

There it was. Not a warning from the colonel — a warning about the colonel, wrapped in the colonel's own words, delivered by a clerk who had watched a woman flinch at a dead family's name and chosen to remember it.

"I keep everything in mind, Sergeant," I said. "It's the trade I was trained in."

Something crossed his face — not quite a smile, more the place a smile had been excavated from. "Mind it thoroughly, then."

* * *

The evening light came slantwise through the east window, gold on the pale scrubbed wood of Vane's desk, and I sat down to demonstrate cipher-conventions for Colonel Ashgrave while inside my skull I walked the Ford road in the dark, beam by beam.

While I talked — the circled cross, the renamed places, the way Vane marked safe houses with nothing but a well-drawn church spire — I made my inventory. Which sheets upstairs could be altered without notice. Which should simply vanish, miscounted among the fair copies, blamed on the chaos of requisition. Which pages of marginalia I might, given one unsupervised minute and the loft's dry August air, persuade a candle to visit.

And I thought about the working copy of the lower reach, the one with my beautiful false line drying in the clerk's basket, already stamped, already entered in the army's index. Somewhere downstream of me, a staff officer would spread that sheet on a table and plan a patrol along ground that did not exist, and men would march into the marsh whistling, and the marsh would do what marshes do.

Vane's voice, in the old workshop, in the smell of ink and cut quill: *You'll be tempted, someday, to make the map say what you need it to say. Every mapmaker is. The ones who survive it are the ones who choose the lie the way they'd choose a wound — knowing exactly where, and why, and what it will cost to carry.*

I chose the wound. Below the near bank. A hand's width high.

Ashgrave arrived as the bells rang nine, and the room straightened around him like iron filings finding a magnet. He stood over the desk a long moment looking at my demonstration sheet — at Vane's leaning letters coming off my pen, faithful as sin — and I felt his attention pass over me like a lantern beam over a wall, pausing at every crack.

"The master's line," he said softly. "Convincing."

"He taught me nothing else."

"Mm." He picked up the Gallow Ford copy from the basket, held it to the lamp, and studied the doubled margin for what felt like the length of a tide. "Firm ground here, your convention says."

"At low water. Saint Cuthbert's tide especially." Let him have it plainly; a gift freely given disarms suspicion better than any concealment. "Though I wouldn't march men on it, Colonel. The master's annotations call it hard going for a reason."

"No," Ashgrave agreed pleasantly, setting the sheet down. "One wouldn't march men on annotated ground. Annotated ground is exactly where one expects the enemy to look." He smiled — the first smile I had seen on him, and it was worse than his scrutiny. "We'll continue tomorrow. Do sleep well, Mistress Thackeray. Your hands are worth more to this campaign than you know."

He left. The soldiers left. Hale doused the lamps, all but one, and stood a moment in the doorway with the ledger under his arm.

"Saint Cuthbert's tide," he said quietly, not looking at me. "That's eleven nights off."

"Is it," I said.

"It is." He paused. "The eel-woman was asking after bridge timber at the yards today. Salvage rates. For her punt repairs, she said." His face gave away nothing at all. "Thought you'd want to know what's being sold in this town, since you're minding everyone's maps."

Then he was gone, and I sat alone in the lamplight with Vane's desk under my hands and my heart going like the millwheel, and I understood that the sergeant had just handed me, deliberately, in the colonel's own garrison, the thread that could either hang us all or pull the whole net loose.

Eleven nights. Berta Quill was already buying timber.

I blew out the lamp, and in the dark I began, silently, to memorize the room.
