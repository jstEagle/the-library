## 10. Riders at Dusk

Sergeant Hale had copied requisition lists for eleven years and had never once been sent to count the dead.

The order came at mid-afternoon, delivered by a breathless drummer boy who had run the whole way from the garrison mill: riders had struck the supply column two miles upstream, near the old drover's road where it bent along the river. Three wagons burned. Four men dead, maybe five — the lieutenant hadn't stopped to be precise. Hale was to take a handcart, a canvas sheet, and Corporal Dunn, and bring back everything that could be brought back: bodies, papers, the dead men's effects for the widow's allotments. The colonel wanted an inventory by nightfall.

Hale looked at the boy's mud-spattered face and felt the familiar weight settle into his shoulders, the one he'd carried since Breda, since every campaign that ended in paperwork. War was mostly cartage. Someone always had to fetch what was left.

"Take the mule too," he said. "We'll never shift a wagon axle on our backs."

Dunn was twenty and eager in the way that made Hale tired just to look at him. The corporal walked fast, musket squared, scanning the hedgerows as though the raiders might still be crouched behind them, and Hale let him scan. It kept the boy from talking. Hale needed quiet for the walk, needed to get his mind arranged before he arrived at whatever the river road held, because he had learned long ago that the difference between a man who could do this work twice and a man who could only do it once was the arrangement of the mind beforehand.

The afternoon was going gold and grey at once, October pressing down on the water meadows. Herons stood in the shallows like clerks waiting for a signature. The river ran low and clear over its gravel beds, and Hale found himself reading it the way he'd begun reading everything lately — as a document. Where it ran shallow, men could cross. Where it ran deep and slow under the alders, boats could lie hidden. He had spent three weeks copying maps of this country and now the country itself seemed annotated, every bend a marginal note, every ford a line of text. It was an uncomfortable literacy. He had not asked for it.

* * *

They smelled the wagons before they saw them — wet char and burnt tallow, the sweetish reek underneath that Hale knew and hated and would not name even inside his own head.

The column had been strung out along the drover's road when it happened; you could read the whole engagement in the ground the way you could read a ledger. First wagon halted where the road narrowed between two ditches. Second and third bunched behind it, wheels locked. Scorch marks fanned out from the first wagon's bed — fired, not exploded, which meant torches thrown from cover, which meant the raiders had come close enough to throw them. Close enough to see faces.

Hale did not want to think about faces yet.

The dead lay where the fighting had left them. Two privates in the ditch, tangled together, one still holding a carbine he had never fired — the pan was dry, the charge unspoiled. A driver slumped across his bench with the reins wrapped twice around his fist, as if he had decided, in the last moment available to him, that his job was to hold the horses and he would go on doing it. The fourth was ten yards off in the grass, face down, and Dunn turned him over with the particular gentleness soldiers reserve for men who can no longer object to being handled.

Hale knelt beside each one in turn and did the work. Name from the collar tag or the paybook. Effects into the canvas: coins, a pipe, a letter from a mother in York that he read without meaning to because it was open and half-burnt and said *come home at Michaelmas if you can*. He folded it small and put it in the effects bag anyway. Somewhere a woman would want the ashes of that sentence.

It was while he was going through the driver's wagon — the third one, the one that had mostly survived, its canvas scorched but its cargo intact — that he found what he was not looking for.

The wagon carried oats, salt pork, and a strongbox of regimental correspondence, and the strongbox had been forced. Not looted — forced and then shut again, carefully, the hasp pried up and pressed back down so that a casual inspection would pass it. Hale had done inspections for eleven years. He knew what casual passed and what it didn't. Whoever had opened this box had wanted something specific and had taken nothing bulky, because the bulk was all still there: dispatches, muster rolls, the colonel's sealed orders, all accounted for against the manifest in Hale's own handwriting, copied three weeks ago in the mill loft.

What was missing was thin. Paper-thin. He counted the packets twice, then a third time, running his thumb down the edges like a man counting cards.

One item gone. A single folded sheet, listed in the manifest as *Survey extract, Gallow Ford approaches, Vane (decd.), copy*.

Hale sat back on his heels in the straw and felt the afternoon tilt.

He knew that sheet. He had inked the fair copy of it himself, or rather he had watched Tamsin Thackeray ink it, her hand moving in the dead mapmaker's line so exactly that he had signed the certification without a flicker of doubt, and had thought about that flickerless doubt ever since. Gallow Ford. The drowned footbridge. The crossing that appeared on no working chart because the working charts were his to make, and he had made them without it, because the master copy showed it drowned and ruined and good for nothing but eels.

Someone had ridden two miles through hostile country, opened a guarded strongbox under the noses of a burning convoy, and stolen a map of a ruined bridge.

Dunn came around the wagon. "Anything, Sergeant?"

"Inventory's short one sheet," Hale said. "Survey paper. Probably blew out when they cracked the box — we'll log it lost in the action." He heard himself say it and understood, with a clarity that frightened him more than the raiders had, that he had just committed his first falsification. Not on paper yet. In the mouth. The paper would come later, tonight, in the loft, in his own neat clerk's hand. All sins in this army began as sentences spoken calmly over inventory.

"Lost in the action," Dunn repeated, satisfied, and went off to help load the mule.

Hale stayed kneeling in the straw a moment longer. There was something else. Under the false floor of the driver's bench — he found it because the board sat wrong, a hair proud of its neighbours, and eleven years of inspecting crates taught your fingertips things your eyes missed — there was a scrap of cloth, coarse-woven, undyed, the kind of wool Highland crofters wove because dye cost money. And caught in the weave, dried to brown stiffness, blood. Too much of it to be the wearer's alone, unless the wearer had bled out entirely, and if he had bled out entirely he'd have been lying somewhere on this road.

The raiders had carried their wounded away. One of them had bled onto this bench, hiding, while redcoats walked past him in the smoke.

Hale put the cloth in his pocket instead of the evidence bag. He could not have said why. He told himself it was because logging it would raise questions about the strongbox, and questions about the strongbox would unravel the sentence he'd already spoken to Dunn, and that was true, and it was also not the truth.

* * *

They got the bodies loaded by dusk. The mule complained the whole way downriver, hauling its terrible freight, and Dunn sang to it, tunelessly, some marching song about a girl in Portsmouth, and Hale let him sing because the alternative was silence and silence on that road, with the herons standing in the shallows and the light dying gold behind Marlow's Reach, would have been unbearable.

At the top of the rise above the town, the garrison mill showed its windows lit against the coming dark. Hale saw the sentries on the loading dock, the lanterns, the cart tracks, and beyond it all the town itself spilling down to the water — chimney smoke, a church bell ringing the hour, lamplight beginning in the lower streets. An occupied town looks almost peaceful at dusk. That was the obscenity of it. Occupation, done thoroughly, looks like order.

He handed the dead over at the dock, signed the transfer, watched Dunn carry the effects bags inside with the exaggerated care of the young. Then, instead of going straight to the loft to make his report and begin the evening's copying, Hale stood a moment in the yard and looked at the river.

Gallow Ford was a mile downstream. He couldn't see it from here — the bend hid it — but he could see the line of alders that marked it, black against the last light, and he found his eye went there the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

A stolen survey. Bloodied Highland wool. Raiders who struck convoys on the drover's road and vanished toward the border country. And a promise, written in a dead man's hand, that Hale had never read but whose existence Tamsin Thackeray had gone white as bone over, three weeks ago, in a workshop full of splintered floorboards. He had not known then what the letter promised or to whom. He was beginning to assemble it now, piece by piece, the way he assembled everything: from lists, from discrepancies, from the small honest arithmetic of what was present and what was absent.

E.C. The initials had been on the seal. He had seen them upside down across the workshop and had the decency not to read them closely, and had read them anyway, the way men do.

There was a Corrie family in the old parish rolls. He'd copied them often enough — erased after the rebellion, struck from the tax lists, land forfeit, names scattered. If one of them had lived, and ridden, and bled on a wagon bench two miles upstream while stealing a map of the one crossing the army didn't watch —

Then the army was about to start watching it.

That was the thought that got Hale moving. Not loyalty, not conscience in any grand sense — those came later, and quieter. What moved him was simpler and colder: he had seen Colonel Ashgrave work. He had watched the man take a misfiled dispatch and unfold from it, over four days, an entire supply network, courteous as a surgeon, until six men swung. Ashgrave did not need proof. He needed a direction, and a stolen map of Gallow Ford was a direction drawn in letters of fire. Within a week there would be pickets at the ford, patrols on the drover's road, and every civilian who so much as gathered driftwood downstream would be logged, questioned, and remembered. Including — especially — the mapmaker's apprentice, who had certified the stolen sheet with her own perfect imitation of a dead man's hand.

She didn't know yet. She was in the loft right now, probably, bent over her copying in the lamplight, careful as ever, believing the day had been an ordinary day.

* * *

He found her at the long table under the north window, as he'd known he would. The loft smelled of tallow and cold iron and the dust of old paper, and she sat with her sleeves pinned back, ruling a margin with a straightedge, her lamp turned so the shadow fell away from her work. She looked up when his boots hit the stairs — she always heard him before anyone else did, another thing he'd noticed and filed and tried not to examine.

"Sergeant." She set down the straightedge. "You've been out."

"Upstream. Convoy was hit at the drover's bend." He kept his voice level, the voice he used for dictation. "Four dead. Wagons burned. I've made my report."

"I'm sorry for it." And she meant it, he thought. That was the strange, unfathomable thing about her — she was sorry for the dead redcoats, actually sorry, the way she was sorry for everything that bled.

"The raiders took nothing of value," he said. "Except—" and here he paused, and the pause was the whole message, packed tight as powder, "—one sheet of survey paper. Old stock. A copy of the drowned-bridge survey. Gallow Ford approaches."

Her hands went still on the table. Not frozen — he'd seen frozen, and this wasn't it. This was deliberate stillness, the stillness of a woman setting down something breakable before she turns around. Fifteen years of a master's training lived in that pause, and Hale, who had spent fifteen years watching men fail to hide what they felt, recognized craftsmanship when he saw it.

"Gallow Ford," she said. "But the ford is drowned. Vane marked it impassable years ago. There's nothing there to steal."

"No," Hale agreed. "Nothing there at all." He let that sit. "The box was forced and closed again. Carefully. Whoever did it knew manifests. Knew what a casual inspection passes." He watched her face and gave her, deliberately, the next piece, because she would need it and because no one else would give it to her straight: "The colonel will have the loss reported within the hour. He'll want to know why a dead mapmaker's survey of a ruined bridge is worth killing for. He'll ask you. You're the only hand alive that can authenticate the original."

"Yes," she said quietly. "He'll ask me."

"And Mrs. Quill sells eels," Hale said, "down by the water steps. I'm told the season's turning and the fog comes early off the ford this time of year. Fog makes poor visibility for sentries. A man might stand at the water steps of an evening and see nothing at all, however much was in front of him."

For a moment neither of them moved. The lamp flame leaned in a draught from the ill-fitted window and righted itself. Down in the yard a sentry called the hour, his voice flat against the stone.

"That will be all, Sergeant?" she said.

"That will be all." He turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the stair rail, because there was one more thing and he had carried it two miles in his pocket and it was burning through the wool of his coat. He did not turn around. He looked at the wall, at the pin-maps of the border country with their neat red flags marking rebel activity, and he said it to the wall:

"The raiders carried their wounded off. There was blood on the wagon bench. Coarse wool, undyed — border weaving. Whoever rode with them today grew up close to this river." A breath. "Whoever it is rides well enough to take a map out of a guarded box and live. But the colonel reads losses the way I read ledgers, miss, and by tomorrow morning he'll know there's a living name attached to that ford. Names are how he hunts. You'd do well to remember which names in this building are written where."

He heard her breath catch — small, quickly mastered, but he heard it.

"Goodnight, Sergeant Hale," she said, and her voice was steady, and he admired her for it and pitied her for needing it, both at once, which was becoming a habit with her and one he could not afford.

He went down the stairs into the dark of the yard, and behind him the loft lamp burned on, and somewhere downstream the alders at Gallow Ford stood black against the water, keeping, for one more night, the only secret the river had left.

By morning, he knew, there would be pickets on the drover's road. By tomorrow night, Ashgrave would have the name Ewan Corrie out of the parish rolls and pinned to his wall like a moth. Whatever Tamsin Thackeray meant to do about the bridge, about the promise, about the boy who had apparently grown into a rider — she had perhaps two days to do it in.

Hale climbed to his own narrow room, took the scrap of bloody wool from his pocket, and held it a long time over the candle flame, deciding.

He did not burn it. Instead he folded it into the back of his paybook, beneath the record of eleven years of faithful service, and blew out the light, and lay listening to the river move in the dark, carrying its secrets down past the mill, past the town, toward the sea.
