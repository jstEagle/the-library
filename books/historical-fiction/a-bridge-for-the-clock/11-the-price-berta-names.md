## 11. The Price Berta Names

The fog had not lifted by noon, and neither had Berta Quill.

Tamsin found her at the eel traps below the weir, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands wrist-deep in the cold green water where the wicker cylinders sat half-drowned. She worked with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done the same thing ten thousand mornings and expected ten thousand more, and she did not look up when Tamsin's boots crunched the shingle.

"You walk like your master," Berta said. "Heel first. Like you're afraid of waking the river."

"He taught me that."

"He taught you wrong. The river hears everything anyway." She hauled a trap up, tipped three eels into the pail at her hip, and dropped the trap back. Only then did she turn, wiping her hands on her apron, and study Tamsin with eyes the color of shallow water over slate. "You've been circling my stall for four days like a gull after offal. Say what you came to say."

"I came to say it away from the market," Tamsin said. "Away from ears."

Berta snorted. "There are no ears here but mine and the weir's, and the weir keeps better counsel than any priest in Marlow's Reach." She jerked her chin upstream, toward the bend where Gallow Ford lay hidden under its skin of mist. "Walk, then. If we're going to speak of drowned things, we may as well stand over them."

They walked. The river ran low and quiet between its alders, brown-gold in the thin light, and Tamsin carried the weight of the sealed letter against her ribs the way she had carried it for eleven days now — *passage promised to E.C., safe and unseen, by the hand that made this survey* — Vane's cramped signature beneath, dated six days before the soldiers took him from his own doorstep and he did not come back.

At the ford she stopped. There was almost nothing to see. A few blackened stumps broke the water midstream, the knuckles of the old footbridge, and beyond them the far bank slid into reeds and the long marsh that had eaten the rest. Fifteen years ago there had been planks under a boy's bare feet. Now there was only water doing its patient arithmetic.

Berta stood beside her and said nothing for a long while. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — stripped of the market-bawdy edge, down to something older and flatter.

"My son crossed here."

Tamsin went still.

"Tam Corrie's year. After. When they were burning crofts on the north side and asking names at every ferry." Berta's hands hung loose at her sides, but Tamsin saw the tendons standing in them like bridge cables. "I ferried eleven across this ford that autumn. Fishermen, a priest, two girls not older than you are now. And my own boy, Jem, who was seventeen and thought a boat and his mother's nerve were the same thing as safety." She turned her head. "You'll have heard I lost him."

"I heard you lost him to the fever. In the winter after."

"I let them hear that." Berta looked back at the water. "It was kinder than the truth, which is that the last crossing I ever rowed him on, the army was already waiting on the far bank, because someone had sold the route. Sold it cheap. A name written down where it shouldn't have been, passed hand to hand until it reached the wrong desk." Her jaw worked. "Jem saw the lanterns before I did. He jumped for the reeds to draw them off. They shot him in the shallows like an eel in a barrel, and I rowed home alone, and I told Marlow's Reach my boy died of fever so that no one would ever ask me again why I stopped selling passage."

The river filled the silence. Tamsin felt the letter pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"That's why you stopped," she said quietly. "Not fear. Grief."

"Fear came later, when grief got tired." Berta crouched suddenly, plunged her hand into the shallows, and came up with a handful of river mud, letting it run through her fingers back into the current. "You know what this is? This is what's left of my son. Silt over the ford. Every spring the flood brings more of him down and lays him here. The river remembers him better than the town does, and the river can't even talk." She straightened, and when she faced Tamsin fully, there was something in her face that made Tamsin think of Vane's finest work — the maps where every line was calm and every line was load-bearing. "So. You want my boats, my timber knowledge, these old hands that built crossings before you were whelped. You want the bridge rebuilt by Saint Cuthbert's tide so a Corrie boy can come home over it. Ask me plainly, girl, and then hear what it costs."

"I'm asking," Tamsin said. "What does it cost?"

Berta did not answer at once. She walked out onto the near bank, to the flat stone where fishermen gutted their catch, and pressed her palm against it as if testing whether the world still held.

"Vane kept a book," she said. "An atlas. Everyone who mattered knew he kept it, and no one knew what was in it, and that was the whole trick of Aldous Vane — he hid true things inside beautiful ones, and the beautiful ones were so lovely the soldiers never thought to read past the coastlines." She glanced back. "Don't look at me like that. I've known about the atlas since before you could hold a pen. Who do you think ferried his paper north all those years? Who do you think carried his surveys to the families whose names he inked into the margins? I was his river, girl. He was my map. We never spoke of it above a whisper and never once wrote each other's names down."

Tamsin's throat tightened. All those years she had believed herself the only keeper of the secret — the sole inheritor. It had never occurred to her that Vane's silence had been a chorus, and she had simply been too young to hear the other voices.

"The atlas is in the mill loft now," Berta went on. "Under Ashgrave's lock. I know. I watch the mill the way I watch my traps." She turned to face Tamsin squarely. "Here is my price, and I'll only say it once, so mark it well. When the atlas leaves that loft — however you manage it, and you will manage it, because you're his and stubbornness runs in teaching — when it goes north to do its work, it will not carry Gallow Ford as a secret. No hidden survey. No marginal cipher. No clever little silences."

"Berta—"

"It will record the crossing openly." Her voice did not rise; it grew heavier, like a keel settling into water. "Named. Charted. Drawn plain as a church door, with the depths and the banks and the safe line through the marsh, so that any soul fleeing the redcoats for the next hundred years can open that page and find their way across. The way my Jem never could, because the only map that knew the ford was a secret, and secrets get sold." She stepped closer, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "You want to keep hiding the route that killed my son. I want it carved into the world so large no one can ever sell it again. That's the price. That's the whole of it."

For a moment Tamsin could not speak. The argument rose in her fully formed, Vane's voice wearing her throat: *a published crossing is a hanged crossing, Berta — the moment Ashgrave reads that page, he posts a picket on the ford and the bridge becomes a gallows.* She knew it was true. She knew it the way she knew how to hold a pen — bone-deep, trained, fifteen years of the rule of useful silences.

And yet.

She looked at the stumps in the water. At the silt. At the woman standing over it with her dead son's name worn smooth as a river stone from fifteen years of carrying it alone.

"If Ashgrave finds that page," Tamsin said carefully, "he doesn't just close the ford. He closes every route in the atlas. One openly charted crossing tells him exactly what kind of book he's holding. It unmasks all the others. Your openness would drown everyone the margins protect."

"Then the margins were always drowning people," Berta said. "Slowly. Politely. One sold secret at a time." She shook her head. "I loved Aldous Vane, girl, and I'll say the rest of it gently because he's dead: his way kept families alive by keeping them nameless. My Jem had a name. He had a laugh you could hear across the weir. And the system that saved eleven souls the autumn he died couldn't save him because it was built on the same principle as a smuggler's blind — hide the channel, trust the dark." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, roughly, angrily. "Memory that lives only in hiding dies in hiding. I want his crossing in daylight. I want some clerk's clerk in fifty years to read that page and know a boy crossed here and it mattered. Is that so much to charge for a bridge?"

Tamsin turned away and looked at the water for a long time.

She thought of Vane's hands over hers, guiding the pen: *the margin is where mercy lives, Tamsin. Put nothing in the open field that you cannot defend in the open field.* She thought of the ledger crates on the requisition cart, fifteen years of careful silence rattling toward the mill. She thought of Ewan at nine years old, hauling a fish out of the broken bridge's shadow, laughing, before the soldiers came and the laughter left the north bank for good.

Two truths, both load-bearing, bearing opposite directions. She felt the atlas itself straining between them — every page a choice now, not a record.

"I can't answer you today," she said at last.

"You can. You just don't want to."

"No." Tamsin met her eyes. "I mean I genuinely cannot. Because the atlas isn't mine yet — it's locked in the loft, and I won't promise a page I haven't held. But I'll tell you what I can promise." She drew a breath. "When I hold that book in my hands, I will decide with it open in front of me, and I will weigh your Jem against every name in the margins, and whatever I choose, I will choose it looking at the cost. Not hiding from it. That's more than most bargains get."

Berta studied her for a long moment. Then something moved in her face — not softening, exactly. Recognition. The look of one craftswoman appraising another's joinery and finding it honest, if unproven.

"Aldous would have answered faster," she said. "But he'd have answered wrong, sooner or later. Maybe slow is the better flaw." She picked up her pail of eels and slung it over her shoulder. "Very well. Here's how it stands. I build your bridge. I lend my boats when the tide asks. And the question of the page stays open between us until the night the atlas leaves the loft — at which point, Tamsin Thackeray, you will give me your answer face to face, and I will abide it, whatever it is. Even if it breaks me." She paused at the top of the bank. "That's what memory owes the living, girl. Not agreement. Witness. Can you witness me?"

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I can witness you."

"Then we're contracted." Berta spat ceremonially into the river — an old boatwoman's seal — and turned to go. Then she stopped, half-turned, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle. "One thing more, since you'll be deciding with the book open. Ask yourself who the atlas is *for*. Vane made it for the dead and the fled. Fine work. Noble work. But a map that only serves ghosts is a gravestone with lines on it." She tapped her own chest. "The living need channels they can find in the dark. Remember that when you're holding the pen."

She went down the path toward her boats, and the fog took her the way it took everything at Gallow Ford — gradually, then completely.

Tamsin stayed a while longer. The stumps of the old bridge stood in the current, patient as questions. Somewhere upstream, faint on the wind, came the mill's new sounds — hammering, the tramp of boots, the rattle of Ashgrave's occupation building itself plank by plank. And beneath it all, the river, keeping its own ledger, entering every name without ink.

*Ask yourself who the atlas is for.*

She thought of the blank sheet waiting in her room at home, the final coastline half-inked, the cartouche where a name should go. She thought of Hale's tired eyes across the copying table, and the way he had looked away when she flinched at the requisition lists — a small mercy, filed away like a document.

Then, as she turned for home along the towpath, she saw the thing that stopped her breath.

A rider on the far bank. Dismounting at the reeds. A young man, tall, moving with a soldier's economy, leading his horse down to the water to drink — and even across the wide brown river, even in the failing light, she knew the set of his shoulders before she knew anything else, because she had watched those shoulders hunch over fishing rods fifteen years ago at the edge of this same water.

Ewan Corrie raised his head. Across the whole width of the river, across the fog and the years and the war between them, his eyes found hers and held.

He did not wave. He did not call out. He simply looked at her — the way a man looks at a shore he has been steering toward in the dark without daring to believe it — and then he touched two fingers to his brow, mounted, and was gone into the trees.

Tamsin stood on the towpath with her heart hammering out the only message it knew: *the bridge, the bridge, the bridge must stand.*

And tomorrow morning, she remembered with a lurch, she was due at the mill loft — summoned by name, under guard, to sit at Colonel Ashgrave's own table and authenticate the master's hand.
