## 12. Interrogation by Lamplight

The summons said nine o'clock. The soldier who delivered it said eight, and Tamsin understood that Ashgrave had told her nine so she would spend an extra hour in the knowledge of it.

She spent that hour badly. She sat at Vane's ruined workbench with a pen in her hand and wrote nothing, because anything she wrote might be read, and anything she thought showed too plainly on her face for even an empty room. Twice she rose and went to the corner where the loose floorboard lay beneath a scatter of shavings and a cracked inkstone, and twice she knelt and pressed her palm flat against the wood as if taking its pulse. The survey of Gallow Ford was no longer there — it lived now rolled in oilcloth inside her bodice, against her ribs, where she could feel it shift when she breathed — but the hiding place itself was evidence. A man who hides things once will hide them again. Ashgrave would not need to be told that.

At half past seven she dressed with care, which is to say she dressed to look as though she had not dressed with care. Good wool, mended hem, hair pinned plainly. A woman who looked prosperous would be suspected of something worth taking; a woman who looked desperate would be pressed for something worth squeezing. She aimed for the narrow country between: tired, competent, unremarkable. A mapmaker's hand, not a mapmaker's heir.

The mill loomed against the grey morning like a crouched animal, its new sentries posted where the grain-cribs used to stand. Inside, the smell of the place had changed — old flour and river-damp giving way to tallow, boot-leather, the cold iron reek of muskets racked along the wall that had once held hoes and billhooks. A corporal walked her up the stairs without a word, which she found worse than speech. Words could be weighed. Silence had to be carried whole.

Ashgrave's room occupied what had been the miller's parlour, and he had made it over entirely into himself: charts squared to the table's edge, pens laid parallel, the single window scrubbed so fiercely the light came through like a blade. He was standing when she entered, and he stood until she was seated, and both courtesies were performed with the precision of drill.

"Miss Thackeray." He pronounced it correctly, which unsettled her more than mispronunciation would have. "Thank you for coming early."

"You asked me for nine, sir. Your soldier said eight."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the socket of one. "Did he. I'll have him flogged for initiative later." He gestured at the table. Two lamps burned there despite the daylight, and between them lay a spread of papers she knew better than her own hands: leaves from Vane's atlas, the borderland sheets, the estuary charts, three of the cartouches turned face-up like cards in a suspicious game.

"Sit, please. May I offer you tea? The army's tea is an insult, but it is a warm insult."

"No thank you, sir."

"Then we shall insult each other cold." He sat opposite her, folded his hands, and did not touch the papers. That was the first thing she marked about being interrogated by Ashgrave: he began nowhere near the point, the way a good surveyor begins miles outside the territory he intends to measure.

"You apprenticed under Aldous Vane for how long?"

"Fifteen years, sir. Since I was nine."

"And before that?"

"I was born in Marlow's Reach, sir. Before that I was mostly fish."

This time the smile arrived properly, stayed two seconds, and left. "He was fortunate in you. Or you in him. Which, I wonder." He turned one of the sheets with one finger, rotating it precisely ninety degrees, so that the coastline ran east-west before her like a sentence waiting to be read aloud. "Your master drowned in April. Tell me about that."

The question was simple. Simple questions were the knives with the plainest handles. "There was rain for six days," she said. "The river came up over the towpath. He went out to check his sounding marks at the weir — he kept them himself, wouldn't trust the marks to anyone — and the bank gave way. They found him at Saltash bend two days after."

"You identified the body."

"Yes, sir."

"Were there papers on him? A satchel, a wallet, anything he'd carried out with him?"

"No satchel. His coat was gone — the river took it. There was nothing in his pockets but a pencil stub and a piece of biscuit gone to mush."

Ashgrave listened to all this with his head tilted slightly, eyes on her mouth rather than her eyes, and she understood he was reading her lips the way she read contour lines: not the shape itself but the pressure behind it. She made herself tell the truth, because the truth was safe here. Vane really had died stupidly, wetly, without documents. The lie of her life lived elsewhere, under other floorboards.

"Mm." He drew a paper toward him at last — the sheet of the upper reaches, the one with the winding of the Corrie water — and set it before her. "Now. I am a soldier, Miss Thackeray, and soldiers are accused of being blunt instruments, so allow me to be blunt in the most careful way I know." He touched the cartouche in the lower corner, the ornamental scrollwork that framed the title. "Who drew these flourishes?"

"The master did. Mostly. He let me do the ruling lines and the hatching. The cartouches were always his."

"Always?"

"After the first year or two, yes."

"So if I showed you a cartouche, you could tell me whether it was his hand."

"Yes."

"Good." He laid out four more sheets in a row, and she saw with a cold slide of the stomach that they were all cartouche corners, cropped close, magnified by some trick of his cropping so that the scrollwork filled the frame like jungle. "Then tell me about these."

She looked. She made herself look slowly, the way a woman looks who has nothing to hide and all the time in the world to hide it in. Three of the five were pure Vane — the double-flicked volutes, the little fish tucked into the terminal curls where he always hid them, one swimming upstream, one down, depending on whether the water depicted was tidal. The fourth was also Vane but early, before his line had learned confidence. The fifth—

The fifth was hers.

It was from the sheet of the eastern marshes, finished only weeks ago, in the stolen hours after his death, and she had drawn the cartouche herself because a map without its frame was a body without clothes, and because — God help her, because he had taught her, because fifteen years of watching his hand had taught her hand without asking her leave. It was very good. It was almost perfect. And it was wrong in exactly one particular that only two people alive could have named, and one of them was dead.

"That one," she said, touching the fifth sheet. "Not his."

Ashgrave went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has set a trap and felt it spring shut on something unexpected, and Tamsin realized, with a lurch like the riverbank giving way, that she had just stepped onto ground he had not known existed. He had expected her to authenticate everything — to claim the master's hand for all five, to be either ignorant or complicit. He had not expected her to give him a sixth thing.

"Explain," he said softly.

"He taught me fifteen years, sir. Some of it stuck." She kept her voice level and dry as blotting paper. "That cartouche is mine. I finished the eastern marshes after he died. It seemed wrong to leave the sheet naked, and wronger to forge him, so I signed it honestly by leaving it mine."

"Leaving it yours. Without a signature."

"A hand is a signature, sir. You've just proved it."

Something moved behind his eyes — reassessment, the recalculation of a sum. She watched him decide, visibly, not to pursue it. Not yet. He gathered the cropped corners back into their folder, squaring the edges, and when he spoke again his voice had returned to its earlier courtesy, which she now understood was not a mood but a uniform.

"You have been more useful than you know. Now. To the matter that actually brings us to table." He produced a single page, written in his own small square hand, and read from it rather than reciting, as though the act of reading lent it legal weight. "In going through your master's effects, my clerks have catalogued — among the ledgers, journals, and miscellaneous papers requisitioned under notice of the twelfth — a series of marginal annotations occurring across no fewer than thirty-one sheets of the borderland atlas. Annotations in a ciphered or abbreviated form. Place-names rendered as initials. Dates rendered as regnal years inverted. Family names appearing in margins adjacent to parishes where, per the parish registers, those families are recorded as having departed, died out, or never settled at all." He laid the page down and looked at her over it. "Does any of this surprise you?"

*Everything,* said the blood in her ears. *You have described the atlas exactly. You have read it more carefully than anyone but me.*

"No, sir," she said.

"No?"

"Vane talked to himself in the margins. Every mapmaker does. Corrections, reminders, things that didn't belong on the fair copy. He called it the conversation with the next draft." This much was true. Truth again, then — truth was holding. "If the margins contradict the registers, sir, it's because he was correcting the registers. Half the parish books in the borderlands were rewritten after the troubles. Vicars with loyalist handwriting. He trusted fieldwork more than vestries."

Silence. Ashgrave regarded her for a long moment, and she had the sensation of standing on a bridge of her own construction while a heavy cart rolled onto it, beam by beam testing, and she could not look down.

"Vicars," he repeated. "Vestries. Fieldwork." He leaned back. "Miss Thackeray, I have spent eleven days with your master's atlas, and I will confess to you — since confession costs me nothing and may cost you less — that I believe it contains a second atlas. One drawn in negation. Names absent from parishes present in margins. Fords unnamed where fords plainly exist. It is a map of what was removed, and removal, done deliberately, is the loudest mark a man can make." He steepled his fingers. "What I cannot yet prove is intent. A cranky old draughtsman talking to himself is a nuisance. A deliberate index of disaffected families, keyed to routes and crossings, is — well. It is the kind of document that gets people hanged. Both men look identical from the outside. The difference lives entirely in the hand that held the pen." He tilted his head. "You knew that hand. Better than anyone living. So I will ask you the only question that matters, and I will ask it plainly, and I will know if you answer it plainly. Was Aldous Vane recording the borderlands — or recording against them?"

The lamps hissed. Somewhere below, a crate dropped and a soldier swore.

Tamsin looked at the spread of maps between them and thought, absurdly, of Ewan at nine years old on the broken bridge, holding up a fish with both hands as though presenting a crown. She thought of Berta Quill saying *everything floats, girl, it's only a question of what you tie it to.* She thought of Vane's own rule, spoken over the drafting table a hundred times, his hand never pausing: *a useful silence is a fact left out, never a fact invented. Invention has a smell. Absence has none.*

She could say he was loyal. That word would satisfy Ashgrave's suspicion and hang every name in the margins the moment it was tested. She could say he was innocent, a mere pedant — but Ashgrave had already smelled invention, and innocence claimed too eagerly smells the same.

So she gave him neither. She gave him the absence.

"Sir," she said, "I can't answer that question honestly, and you've said you'll know if I don't answer it honestly. So I'll tell you what I can. I never heard him say a treasonous word in fifteen years. I never saw him carry a message, meet a stranger at night, or keep company beyond the town. What I saw him do was walk rivers in the rain and argue with vicars about parish boundaries and drink one glass of cider too many and complain that nobody appreciated the difficulty of drawing a tidal estuary. If there's a second atlas in those pages, sir, then it's the atlas of a man who hated bad information more than he hated anybody. Whether that makes him loyal or dangerous, I genuinely don't know. He didn't leave instructions. He left a workshop full of paper and a girl who could rule a straight line."

Ashgrave did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Then he exhaled through his nose, once — the sound of a man conceding a point in a game whose stakes the other player cannot see.

"That," he said, "is the most honest evasion I have been offered in this war, and I include my correspondence with Horse Guards." He rose, and she rose with him, and he came around the table slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and stopped beside her — close enough that she could smell the lamp-oil on his cuffs and nothing else, no scent of person at all, as though he had been distilled. "You understand that courtesy is not belief. I will keep thinking. Thinking is my trade, as pen-work is yours."

"Yes, sir."

"One more matter, and then Corporal Wrenn will see you home." He paused at the window, looking down at the yard, at the carts, at the river beyond, silvering under a wind. "Your master's workshop stands empty on Ferry Lane. Empty buildings accumulate damp, vermin, and secrets. Under the standing orders for occupied towns, structures of evidentiary value are to be secured for Crown use." He glanced back at her, mild as milk. "I am given to understand the flooring is old oak. Good timber. My engineers will want it."

The words took a moment to arrive in her body, and when they arrived they arrived all together, a cold flood from boots to scalp. *The floorboards. He wants the floorboards.* Not the papers — the papers he had. The *floor*. The hiding places themselves, pried up board by board, examined for what their concealment proved. He could not catch her keeping a secret. So he would take away the places a secret could live, and watch what surfaced.

"The floor's rotten in places, sir," she heard herself say. "Master Vane spilled ink like a farmer spreads muck."

"Then the engineers will enjoy the challenge." He opened the door and inclined his head, courteous to the last inch. "Thank you for your candour, Miss Thackeray. I find I believe roughly half of it, which in my experience is the maximum honest people achieve. Report to the loft tomorrow as usual. Your copying is valued."

* * *

Corporal Wrenn walked her down through the garrison yard, past the mill race where the wheel had been chained still, past a wagon stacked with Vane's ledger crates under sacking. At the gate he paused, and she braced for some final order, but he only shifted his musket and said, quietly, not looking at her:

"Rotten floor, is it. Shame. My gran's cottage had oak floors. Held a lot." A beat. "Held it right up till they rebuilt after the flood, and then nobody could say what was under it and nobody needed to."

She looked at him. His face was a wall, but walls have mortar, and mortar can be read. Sergeant Hale's friend, she thought — Hale had mentioned a Wrenn who'd been clerk before him, before the commission. A man who knew what floors held.

"Thank you, Corporal," she said, and meant the words to weigh exactly what they weighed.

* * *

She reached Ferry Lane as the church bells rang noon, and the soldiers were already there.

Two of them, with pry-bars and a handcart, working methodically from the street end of the workshop inward, and the sound — God, the sound. Fifteen years of her life came up out of the joists nail by nail. The board where she'd first hidden a copied name and been sick with fear for a week. The board Vane replaced the autumn after the rebellion, whistling, saying nothing, letting the silence explain itself. The loose one, her loose one, lifted now by a bored private who turned it over, scraped at the underside with a thumbnail, found nothing but old wax and worm-track, and tossed it onto the cart with the rest like a dead man's shirt.

She stood in the doorway and did not let her face do anything. This, too, was craft: to watch your own history loaded onto a barrow wearing the expression of a landlady annoyed about the mess.

The private with the pry-bar wiped his forehead. "Beg pardon, miss. Colonel's orders. We're to take the lot and sweep underneath."

"Take the lot," she said. "Mind the skirting by the north wall — it's load-bearing."

He blinked at her, nonplussed by the cooperation, and she almost pitied him. He had expected tears or fury, some human payment, and she had nothing to pay with because she had spent everything at Ashgrave's table. The oilcloth roll against her ribs shifted as she breathed. The survey was safe. The letter was safe. Everything else — every hollow, every cavity, every dark architecture of patience that Vane had built into the bones of the building — was coming up onto a handcart in the noon light.

And then the private pried the last board by the hearth, the deep one, the one Vane had double-nailed, and the nail screamed out of the oak, and beneath it there was a shallow void perhaps a hand's width square —

— empty. Empty. She had forgotten it was empty. She had moved the last thing from it months ago, and she watched the private stare into the little dark rectangle, and she felt the almost pass over her like the shadow of a bird. An inch of depth. A different week, a slower memory, and the bird would have landed.

"Nothing here," the private said, disappointed.

"There's never anything anywhere," said the other one, older, stacking boards. "That's the whole bleeding job."

They trundled the cart away down Ferry Lane, wheels complaining, and Tamsin stood alone in the gutted workshop, in a building that was suddenly all ceiling and no floor, all question and no answer. Dust hung in the light like something deciding whether to settle.

She thought: *he knows the hiding places exist even if he never finds what they held. He has taken the grammar of secrecy and left me the alphabet.* And she thought, colder and clearer, the way the river runs clear in frost: *then secrecy must move. Out of wood. Into flesh.*

Tonight there would be fog on the ford. Tonight Berta would be waiting with the first salvaged timbers, and the bridge would rise beam by honest beam, and every hour she spent under lamplight being questioned was an hour the tide was running out.

She gathered her tools — compass, pens, the one knife she trusted — and rolled them into her apron, and paused once at the doorway to look back at the bare earth floor, scored with the ghosts of joists, lying open as a page.

Ashgrave wanted the places a secret could live.

Very well. From tonight, the secret would live somewhere he could never pry up, confiscate, or catalogue. It would live in calloused hands and a rising bridge and a woman's stubborn, unforgivable heart.

She pulled the door shut behind her and went down toward the water, where the fog was already gathering like an answered prayer.
