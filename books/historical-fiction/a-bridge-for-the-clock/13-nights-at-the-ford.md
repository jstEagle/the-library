## 13. Nights at the Ford

The fog came off the river the way grief came off a house where someone had died slowly — not all at once, but in layers, each one thicker than the last. Tamsin stood at the lip of Gallow Ford with a coil of tarred rope over her shoulder and counted her heartbeats until her eyes adjusted to nothing at all. There was nothing to adjust to. The world had been folded closed, bank and water and sky pressed into one grey felt, and she might have been standing inside Vane's workshop with the shutters drawn, inside the blank space at the edge of a chart where the cartographer writes *here be nothing* because he has not yet earned the right to write what is.

But she had earned it. That was the difference. Fifteen years of earning it, and now the nothing was hers to fill.

She heard Berta Quill before she saw her — the particular knock of a flat-bottomed boat against stone, a sound like a knuckle rapped politely on a door. Then the fog opened a seam and Berta was there, waist-deep in the shallows, steadying the bow while two salvaged beams slid off it into the water with a groan.

"You're late," Berta said.

"The patrol turns at the churchyard at half past eleven. I waited until they turned."

"You waited until they turned and then you stood on the bank like a heron deciding whether the fish are worth getting wet for." Berta waded toward her, and the fog gave her up in pieces: the oilskin, the grey braid, the hands like tree roots. "Get in the water, girl. The river won't come to you."

Tamsin got in the water. The cold climbed her legs like something with intent, and she gasped once and then made herself stop, because Vane had taught her that the first sound a surveyor makes in the field is the sound she'll hear in her memory every time she returns to the place, and she did not want her memory of Gallow Ford to begin with her own whimper.

"Here." Berta pressed a beam-end into her hands. Old oak, black with forty years of river, but sound when Tamsin ran her thumb along the grain — she checked the way Vane had taught her, thumb and eye, reading timber the way she read a coastline. "From the old mill race. I pulled three the night after Ashgrave's men stripped the granary. They were lying in the mud like drowned men waiting for someone to say a word over them."

"You've been salvaging since before I came to you."

Berta's teeth showed white in the fog. "I've been salvaging since before you were born. It's only lately that anyone's asked me to salvage toward something." She spat into the water, which Tamsin understood to be a kind of prayer. "The old footbridge ran from that stone—" she nodded at the mossed stump of a pier upstream, "—to the shelf on the far bank where the alders grow. Nine bays. My son crossed it in the dark more times than I can count, and I crossed it carrying him when his legs were too short. The river took the middle three bays in the flood of '39, and the Crown took the rest by decree the year after, and nobody rebuilt it because nobody was meant to cross."

"And now?"

"Now you're going to tell me where the piers go, mapmaker, and I'm going to tell you whether your map remembers what my body remembers. Between the two of us we might build something that holds a man's weight." Berta turned her face up to the fog, to whatever stars were working behind it. "Vane's star-tables. You have them?"

Tamsin touched the oilskin packet strapped inside her coat. "Memorized."

"Good. Because if you'd brought the paper, I'd have made you swim home."

They worked. The fog was their wall and their roof, thick enough to swallow the sound of a beam being levered upright, thick enough that the garrison's lantern on the mill hill was only a smudge of amber, a distant infection of light. Tamsin took her bearings the way Vane had taught her to take bearings when no instrument was safe to carry: by the stars when the fog thinned, and by the river's own voice when it didn't. The ford had a sound. Over the drowned shelf of rock where the first pier had stood, the water spoke in a low continuous syllable, and two yards downstream it broke its sentence, and that break was the edge of the shelf, and the edge of the shelf was where the oak had to land.

"Left," she said. "Half a span left. The stone's there — I can hear it."

"You can *hear* it."

"Vane made me stand in the shallows at Marlow's Reach for a whole week with my eyes shut, learning the bottom by ear. He said a map drawn only from what the eye sees is a map of the surface of things." She said it and then heard it, the way his voice had said it, patient and dry as old vellum, *the surface of things, Tamsin, and the Crown is very interested in surfaces* — and her throat closed for a moment, and she was glad of the fog, and glad of the cold, which gave her something else to feel.

Berta grunted. "Your master talked like a man pricing his own funeral. Get the rope around the beam."

They walked the first beam down into the dark water, feet finding the shelf of stone under the current, and set it into the socket where the old pier had been — and it fit. Not loosely, not with the apology of a makeshift thing, but *fit*, the way a word fits a sentence, and Tamsin felt something in her chest turn over. Fifteen years of drawing lines that stood for things, and here was a line that *was* the thing, that a man would stand on with his whole weight, that would hold or fail with no ink in the argument at all.

"Second bay is the deep one," Berta said. "Four feet at low water, six at the tide. My son could stand on the old beam there with his chin just above the surface and swear he was a river god."

"What was his name?"

The fog held very still. "Colm," Berta said at last. "He was called Colm. You'll want that for your survey, I suppose. A name for the deep bay."

"No," Tamsin said. "I want it because you gave it to me."

Berta looked at her a long moment, and the fog moved between them like a curtain stirring in a doorway, and then she turned back to the rope and said, "Third beam's still in the boat. Stop standing about."

* * *

By the third night they had three bays standing, and Tamsin had learned the ford the way she had once learned the workshop floor — by the soles of her feet and the skin of her hands. Her palms were striped with rope-burn. Her shoulders had stopped complaining, which she understood to be the body's version of surrender, and she had never been so tired or so awake.

They measured by starlight. The fog thinned after midnight most nights, and when it did, Berta held the lantern shuttered to a coin of light while Tamsin lay on her back on the near bank with the star-tables unfolding in her memory, finding Arcturus, finding the pole, sighting along her own straightened arm the way Vane had shown her when she was nine and the sextant was bigger than her head.

"Again," Vane had said, that first summer. "You flinched. The star didn't move; you did. A surveyor who flinches makes maps of her own fear."

She did not flinch now. She lay on the cold shingle with the river talking under the bridge-to-be, and she took her sight, and she called the numbers to Berta in a whisper, and Berta — who could not have read a star-table if her life had depended on it, and who had once, Tamsin now understood, ferried men across this water by exactly the same stars without any tables at all — drove the stake where the numbers said.

"You know what I can't decide," Berta said, on the fourth night, hauling timber while Tamsin sighted. "Whether your master taught you all this so you could build, or so you could hide. A girl who can measure a river by the stars is a girl who can find her way anywhere and leave no trail. That's not a trade. That's a survival."

"He said a map is a promise that a place exists."

"And did he keep his promises?"

Tamsin sighted along her arm at Arcturus and did not answer for a long time. "He kept one I only just found," she said. "In the floor. A survey of this bridge. And a letter. Safe passage, dated the week before they killed him. For a boy called E.C."

Berta set the beam down. Even in the dark Tamsin felt the change in her, the stillness of a woman recalculating.

"Corrie," Berta said. "Ewan Corrie. The tanner's boy who used to fish off the third bay with a line made of hair ribbon." She laughed, low and without much humor. "I carried his father across this ford twice in '31. The whole family went north after the trials. I'd thought them all under the ground by now."

"He's not. He's upstream, and he rides with the raiders, and the army is going to hunt him with the very maps I'm copying in that mill unless this bridge stands and he's across it before they read what's under the floor of Vane's workshop." Tamsin heard her own voice go thin and made it stop. "Ashgrave has the ledgers. He hasn't cracked the marginalia yet. But he will. He's not a stupid man; he's worse, he's a patient one. Every night we build here is a night he's sitting in the map room with Vane's handwriting under a lamp, and one morning he's going to look up and see Gallow Ford the way I see it, and then this fog won't be thick enough."

"Then we'd best be quick." Berta shouldered the beam. "And girl — when your Corrie boy crosses, he crosses owing a debt to a dead mapmaker and a live eel-wife. Make sure he knows which of us he can repay."

"He can repay you by living."

"That," said Berta, "is the only coin I take."

* * *

The fifth night, the river gave them trouble.

The tide came up the Reach stronger than Berta had reckoned — the Saint Cuthbert's spring building early, the water shouldering into the ford with a low muscular push that made the standing bays groan like a ship. Tamsin stood in the shallows holding the second pier steady while Berta lashed the crossbeam, and the water climbed her thighs, her waist, and the whole structure leaned a finger's width downstream and her whole chest went cold.

"Hold it," Berta said. "Don't fight it sideways. Lean *into* it. The river respects weight honestly given."

Tamsin leaned into it. Her boots found the shelf of stone, her spine took the load, and she thought absurdly of the atlas, of the weight of all those names she carried the same way — not fighting them sideways, not pretending they weren't heavy, just leaning into the whole mass of them and refusing to be moved. The pier steadied. The tide kept coming, patient as Ashgrave, and they lashed and braced and drove a new stake into the gravel, and when the water finally slackened toward dawn, three bays and the bones of a fourth stood against the sky like the ribs of something deciding to live.

Tamsin sat down on the bank, shaking, laughing, or some animal sound between the two.

"You feel it now," Berta said. She lowered herself onto the shingle beside her, stiff, breathing hard, and for once not pretending she wasn't. "The difference between drawing a bridge and building one. Ink forgives everything. Oak forgives nothing."

"He used to say the river was the only honest client he ever had."

"He said that?" Berta snorted. "Cheeky old vellum-worm. The river's a liar like everything else. It just lies slowly, so you can watch it."

They sat while the fog began to lift off the water in rags, and the first grey came into the east, and Tamsin looked at what they had made — four bays of black oak, crooked as a healed bone, standing — and felt her master's hands over hers so plainly that she turned her own palms up to look for them. Rope-burn. Splits in two fingers. The callus on the right thumb where the stylus sat, fifteen years deep, and under it, newer, the rope's own callus forming, the mapmaker's hand becoming something that could also build.

"One honest beam at a time," she said aloud.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. Something he—" She stopped. Started again, because Berta had earned truth the way she'd earned everything, in the water and the dark. "He used to say there were two ways to make a lie and one way to make a true thing, and the true thing always took longer, and that was the whole of the craft."

Berta was quiet a moment. Then: "Your master and I drank together once. Did you know that? Year before he died. He came to buy eels and stayed to drink my plum brandy and asked me questions about this ford for two hours, and I thought he was mad, and I told him so, and he said—" she put on a dry, precise voice, a fair copy of a dead man, "—*madam, I am drawing a letter that will outlive us both, and I would like it to be legible.*"

Tamsin's eyes stung. She blamed the fog, which was mostly gone.

* * *

The sixth night, the fog failed them.

It came up thin and ragged, torn by a wind out of the northeast, and the stars burned hard and clear and merciless, and the lantern on the mill hill was no longer a smudge but a distinct point of light, and worse — a second light had joined it, down by the garrison's water-gate, moving. A patrol lantern, walking the river path.

Berta's hand closed on Tamsin's arm in the dark, hard as a manacle. "Down."

They went flat among the alder roots on the near bank, the four standing bays of the bridge a black lattice above the water, and Tamsin lay with her cheek in the wet shingle and watched the lantern come. It moved slowly. It stopped. It stood — she could see the silhouette now, one man, red coat gone grey in the starlight, at the exact place where the river path bent and the ford came into view — and it stood there a long, long time, long enough for Tamsin to count her own heartbeats past a hundred, long enough for her to think with terrible clarity: *he is looking at the bridge. Someone is looking at our bridge.*

The lantern turned. It went back the way it had come, unhurried, and the dark folded over it.

Berta didn't move for another hundred heartbeats. Then she rolled onto her back and let out a breath. "Sentry walking his post. Could be nothing."

"Could be," Tamsin said. But she was looking at the bridge, at the four honest bays standing in the starlight, and thinking about the fifth night's tide, and about how many more nights the work needed, and about a man named Hale in the mill loft who logged the comings and goings of civilians in a neat clerk's hand — and she thought: *if anyone asks where I have been at night, the record will either save me or hang me, and I do not yet know which way that man's heart falls.*

She rose and wrung out her skirt. "Tomorrow night we set the fifth bay. The tide will be at its lowest an hour past midnight."

"And the night after?"

"The night after, the sixth — and then we're done, and I send word, and the bridge stands ready on the eve of Saint Cuthbert's tide, exactly as a dead man promised." She looked upstream, into the dark where the raiders were, where Ewan was, where the promise was already traveling toward her faster than any boat. "Pray the fog comes back."

Berta gathered her rope, and paused, and said quietly, "Girl. That sentry stood at the bend four hundred heartbeats. A man walking his post stands four."

The fog did not come back that night, or the next, and far up the mill hill a lamp burned in the map room past two o'clock, and in the morning there were two extra soldiers at the water-gate, and Sergeant Hale looked at her across the copying desk a moment too long before he looked down at his ledger and said, in his tired flat voice, that the weather was turning fine.
