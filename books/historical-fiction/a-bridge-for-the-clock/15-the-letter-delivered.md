## 15. The Letter Delivered

The letter was small enough to swallow.

Tamsin did not mean to think of it that way, but the thought arrived anyway on the third night of composing it, as she sat by the banked fire in Vane's ruined workshop with a scrap of paper no larger than her palm and a stub of pencil worn down to a brown nub. Small enough to swallow. That was the standard now — not whether the words were true, or kind, or dangerous, but whether a man could tuck them behind his back teeth if redcoats stopped him on the towpath and turned out his pockets into the mud.

She had written nine drafts. The first had been a page long, full and flowing, the way she would have spoken to Ewan Corrie if they were children again at Gallow Ford with lines in the water and no names between them but their own. *The bridge will stand. Come home.* Four words, and every one of them a gallows.

The second draft was shorter. The third shorter still. By the ninth, the whole of it read:

*Gallow Ford. Eve of Cuthbert's tide. As he said.*

Three lines. No signature. No name for the sender, because the sender's name was already in too many ledgers, and no name for the recipient, because the recipient's name would hang him twice over if the paper were found. Even the phrase *as he said* was a risk — but Tamsin could not bring herself to cut it. It was the only sentence in the letter that belonged to Aldous Vane instead of to her, and she had decided, in the stubborn private country of her own heart, that the promise should travel under his hand even though his hand was fifteen years cold. She had copied the phrasing from the sealed letter she'd found beneath the floorboards, that brittle paper with its wax broken by her own thumb: *passage shall be found at the Ford when the tide serves, as I have said to him before.* Vane had written like a man laying stones across a river he knew he would never cross himself. Now she was setting the last stone, and her fingers shook doing it.

She held the scrap over the candle flame — not close enough to burn, just close enough to warm the ink dry — and watched the graphite settle grey and faint into the paper's grain. Graphite, not ink. Ink could be washed and read; graphite lifted off damp paper with a wet finger, leaving nothing but a ghost. Berta Quill had taught her that trick two nights ago at the ford, shouting it over the fog while they wrestled a salvaged beam into the water, and Tamsin had thought: *fifteen years a smuggler, and every habit of hers is a lesson in staying alive.* She was learning a second apprenticeship at forty-one years old, and its master was an eel-seller with rope burns on her palms.

* * *

Old Tam Pellow fished the Reach below the mill, and had fished it since before the rebellion, since before Vane came to town with his cases of instruments and his careful silences. He was seventy if he was a day, weathered to the color of his own nets, and he had the particular gift of men who work alone on water: he saw everything and repeated nothing, unless repeating it suited him.

Tamsin went down to find him at first light, when the mist still stood waist-deep on the river and the garrison mill behind her was only a black shape with one lit window — Sergeant Hale's window, she guessed, though she did not look long enough to confirm it. Looking long at lit windows was how people got remembered.

Tam was mending a net on the shingle, squatting with his back to her, and he heard her boots on the pebbles long before she reached him.

"If you've come about eels," he said without turning, "Quill's got the corner on them. Always has."

"I've come about a fish that swims upstream."

He turned then, slowly, and looked at her the way old men look at young women carrying something heavy — not with suspicion exactly, but with the weary recognition of a man who has been asked to carry things before. "Ay," he said. "There's always one."

"Up past the second weir," Tamsin said. "Where the water runs fast and the banks are willowed. There are folk there who take post, if the post is quiet."

"You mean rebels."

"I mean fishermen," she said, "who know other fishermen."

Tam studied her face for a long moment. The mist moved between them like something alive. Downriver, a sentry on the mill wall turned his lantern toward the water, and both of them went still until the light swung away again, bored, indifferent, blind.

"I knew your master," Tam said at last. "Before you were born, girl. He bought perch off me for his supper and never once asked what else was in the creel." His hands kept working the net as he talked, knot after knot, the motion older than thought. "He paid fair and he looked away proper. There aren't many like that left."

"No," Tamsin said. "There aren't."

"And now you want me to carry something upriver for you. Something that if it's found on me, they'll hang me off the mill beam and leave me there for the crows to teach the town a lesson."

"Yes."

"And you're asking because?"

"Because you looked away proper once," Tamsin said, "and I'm asking you to do it again. Not for me. For a promise that isn't mine."

Something shifted in the old man's face — not softening, exactly; weathering, the way stone weathers, grain falling away to show the harder shape beneath. He tied off his knot. He bit the line clean with teeth gone yellow.

"The eve of Saint Cuthbert's," he said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled on this river faster than any boat, and what passed between Berta Quill and the willowed banks above the second weir had evidently already passed through Tam Pellow's hearing too. "That's four days."

"Four days," Tamsin agreed.

"And you'll be at the ford."

"The ford will be standing. Whether I'm at it is another matter."

Tam snorted — a sound like a stone dropping in shallow water. "Best be at it," he said. "Promises don't swim on their own." He held out one gnarled hand, palm up, and waited.

Tamsin took the letter from inside her coat. She had sewn it that morning into a twist of oilcloth the size of a plum, weighted with a split shot from her own survey kit so it would sink if it had to, and she set it in his palm the way she imagined you'd set down a live coal — gently, quickly, and without letting it touch anything it might burn.

Tam weighed it, nodded once, and tucked it into the brim of his hat, under the sweat-stained leather, where no soldier searching a fisherman would ever think to look because no soldier had ever yet searched a hat for anything but lice.

"If I'm stopped," he said, "I never saw you. If I'm asked, I was mending nets. If I'm hanged—" he shrugged, and the shrug contained more courage than most men's speeches, "—well. I'm seventy-two. They'd be doing me a kindness, though I'd rather they didn't say so at the time."

"Thank you—"

"Don't thank me." He was already turning back to his net. "Thank him. He started this crossing thirty years ago. You're just the last plank in it."

* * *

She watched him push off.

It was a small thing, an old man in a leaky punt poling out into the morning mist with a hat full of secrets, and Tamsin stood on the shingle and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that she was watching the largest thing she had ever done leave her hands. Fifteen years of hiding. Fifteen years of finishing Vane's atlas line by line in secret, of learning which silences were load-bearing and which were cowardice dressed in caution's clothes — and all of it, all of it, had been preparation for this: a scrap of graphite on oilcloth, entrusted to a stranger, going upriver to a boy she had loved before either of them knew what the word cost.

Once it left her hands, it could not be recalled. If Tam was stopped, the letter would die with him or worse, and Ewan would ride toward a bridge that never stood, and the fault would be hers forever. If Tam succeeded, Ewan would come — and Ashgrave's soldiers might come behind him, because the colonel was not a stupid man, and stupid men did not hold towns. Either way, the choosing was done. That was the nature of a promise delivered: it stopped being yours the moment it left your mouth, and became fate's instead, and fate had never once shown Tamsin Thackeray its accounts in advance.

The punt dwindled. The mist took it. Somewhere beyond the second weir, a fish that swam upstream began to move.

* * *

The next three days were the longest of her life, and she spent them the way she suspected condemned men spend theirs: working, furiously, at things that must look ordinary.

By day she sat in the mill loft under Hale's mild supervision, copying requisition lists in a hand so deliberately dull that Ashgrave himself had glanced at it once and looked away. By dusk she walked the towpath like any woman taking air, counting sentries, noting which lanterns burned and which were dark, learning the garrison's rhythm the way she'd once learned the tide tables — not by being told, but by watching until the pattern confessed itself. And by night, when the fog came up off the water thick enough to drink, she went to Gallow Ford with Berta Quill and three of Berta's river-cousins, and they built.

The bridge was half-standing now. Nine beams of salvaged oak, mortised by lamplight, sunk into the old drowned footings that only Vane's hidden survey had ever charted. Tamsin worked with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back and her master's dividers hanging at her belt like a relic, and every time she drove a peg home she felt the strange doubled sensation she could not name — her own hands, and under them, faint as a watermark, his. *Measure twice. Cut once. Never let the timber tell you what it is; tell the timber.* She heard him in her skull so clearly some nights that she half-turned to speak to him, and the fog where he should have been was just fog, and she drove the next peg harder to keep from weeping in front of Berta, who would have pretended not to notice, which would have been worse.

On the third evening, hauling the tenth beam down the shingle, Berta paused beside her in the dark.

"Pellow came back through at noon," she said quietly. "Mended nets, sold six perch, complained about his knees. And dropped a word at my stall, casual as a coin into a cup."

Tamsin's arms went still around the timber. "And?"

"He's got it." Berta's eyes, sharp as an eel-gig in the dark, held hers. "Your letter's upriver, Tamsin. Past the weir, past the willows, into hands that have been waiting on it longer than you've been alive."

Waiting on it. The words went through her like cold water. Because that was the truth she had not let herself feel until now — that somewhere north of the Reach, a man called Ewan Corrie had carried Vane's promise for fifteen years the way you carry a splinter too deep to draw: always there, always aching, never quite fatal. And tonight the splinter had begun to move.

"Then it's done," Tamsin said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — flat, far away, a voice speaking from the far bank of something. "Whatever happens now, it's done."

"Ay." Berta spat thoughtfully into the water. "Done things have a weight to them. You'll feel it tomorrow."

She felt it within the hour.

* * *

She was walking home along the towpath near midnight, fog to her shoulders, when she saw the light.

Not a lantern — she knew the garrison's lanterns now, their color and cadence, the slow patrol-swing of them along the mill wall. This was different. This was a window in the map room, the long low room on the mill's ground floor where Colonel Ashgrave kept Vane's seized atlases under lock, and the light in it was candlelight, steady, unmoving, burning hours past the hour when honest officers slept.

Tamsin stopped in the shadow of a beached skiff and watched.

Through the fog-thick glass she could make out a shape bent over a table — Ashgrave's shape, unmistakable, the straight back and the stillness of a man reading something that mattered. On the table before him lay papers. Many papers. And beside the papers, catching the candlelight in a thin bright line, lay the object she had dreaded for weeks without letting herself name why: a magnifying glass, brass-mounted, the kind used for reading fine engraving.

The kind used for reading marginalia.

Her mouth went dry. She thought of the atlas margins — the tiny cartouches, the flourished initials, the little birds and anchors and broken bridges she had inked into Vane's unfinished sheets, each one a name disguised, each one a family the Crown had erased. She thought of the false ford she had planted in the working copy, the clever trap she had laid for the army's charts, and how cleverness, in her experience, was a knife that cut whichever hand held it carelessly. She thought of Tam Pellow's punt, four days gone upriver, and a letter that could not be recalled, and a tide that would come on schedule whether she was ready or not.

Ashgrave turned a page. The candle flame leaned. And Tamsin Thackeray stood in the fog on the towpath and understood that the clock she had been racing all her life had just begun to strike.

She turned for home, walking fast, not running — running drew the eye — and behind her the light in the map room burned on, patient as a hook, patient as the tide.
