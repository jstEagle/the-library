## 16. Ashgrave Closes the Net

Colonel Ashgrave had learned long ago that the truth of a country lived in its margins.

The maps themselves lied — every mapmaker's maps lied, that was the trade. A river widened to flatter a landowner. A forest thinned where a surveyor had grown tired and hungry and wanted his supper. The honest lines were the ones drawn for pay; the dishonest ones were the ones drawn for love, tucked into corners, curled around compass roses, hidden in the flourishes of a cartouche where no clerk would ever think to look. Aldous Vane had understood this better than any man Ashgrave had ever hanged or broken or bought. Which was why Vane was dead, and why the atlas lay open on the colonel's table at the garrison mill like a patient on an operating table, pinned at four corners with candlesticks, waiting to give up what it carried.

It was past midnight. The mill loomed over the black water of Marlow's Reach, and inside it the great wheel had been stopped since the requisition, so that the only sound was the tick of the case clock in the corner — a clock Ashgrave had ordered wound and kept running because he could not abide a room that did not tell him the hour. Time was the one enemy that never required decoding. Everything else in this town was cipher.

He sat alone except for Sergeant Hale, who copied documents three tables away under a guttering lamp, and who had the particular stillness of a man pretending not to see what was in front of him. Ashgrave had noticed that stillness weeks ago and had decided, provisionally, to find it useful rather than suspicious. A man who looked away from things could be relied upon to look away from other things, and there were nights when a commander needed witnesses who witnessed nothing.

Tonight he needed no witness at all. Tonight he needed only the atlas, the lamp, and the patience that had won him three commissions before his fortieth year.

* * *

The marginalia had defeated him for a month.

Not because it was invisible — Ashgrave had seen coded text before, in Flanders, in the papers of a Jesuit courier whose neck he had personally measured for the rope. No, Vane's work was worse than invisible. It was *innocent*. Little annotations in a fine brown hand: *herons nest here, third spring.* *The miller's boy lost a hat at this bend.* *Good clay for bricks.* *Eels taken by moonlight, ask B.Q.* Every note plausible. Every note useless. He had set two clerks to transcribing them all into a ledger, and the ledger read like a diary of a man who loved a river, which perhaps it was, and which told him precisely nothing about troop movements, arms caches, or the names of families who had backed the wrong king fifteen years ago.

The Crown's men had burned those names out of the parish rolls after the rebellion. Burned them out of the tithe books. Burned them off gravestones where they could reach the chisels. But Ashgrave knew — had always known, the way a surgeon knows the body keeps what the mind discards — that a mapmaker who walked every ford and field of the borderlands would have known those families by their land if not their names. And a man like Vane, a man who died rather than surrender his ledgers, did not die for herons.

So Ashgrave had done what he always did when force failed. He had waited, and watched, and let the thing come to him.

It came tonight through the eel-seller.

Not the woman herself — though he had a file on Berta Quill thick enough to stop a musket ball, and had read it twice since the raiders began striking upstream. It came through the *note*. *Eels taken by moonlight, ask B.Q.* On the surface, nothing. But Ashgrave had spent the evening cross-referencing the annotation against the working copies Tamsin Thackeray had authenticated for army use — copies made in this very loft, under guard, in the master's own hand, or something so close to it that Ashgrave had privately begun to wonder whether the apprentice's hand and the master's hand had ever been two hands at all.

On the working copy of the Reach itself, the same stretch of bank bore a different note. Not *eels taken by moonlight.* Something else. Something about a footbridge.

And here was the thought that had kept him from his bed, the thought that had finally, at half past eleven, cracked the whole structure open like a dropped plate: the notes did not match. The original said one thing. The copy said another. Two versions of the same margin, both in a hand too fine to be anything but deliberate, and between them a gap wide enough to march a company through.

Vane had not been writing a diary. He had been writing a *key* — and distributing it in pieces, one piece per map, so that no single sheet gave up the whole. The heron note marked a landmark. The eel note marked a contact. The miller's boy's hat — Ashgrave pulled the sheet closer, tilted it to the lamp — the miller's boy's hat had washed up, according to the margin, *against the old piles below the ford.*

The old piles. Below the ford.

There was no ford marked on any of Vane's finished maps within five miles of that bend. Ashgrave had checked. Three times. The river ran deep and unbroken there, according to every chart the Crown possessed, including the charts the army had paid good money to have verified by the dead man's own apprentice.

Which meant either that Vane had noted a drowned bridge beside a ford that did not exist —

— or that the ford existed everywhere except on paper, and the paper was the lie, and the lie had been maintained for fifteen years by everyone in this valley who had anything to lose by telling the truth.

Ashgrave set down his magnifying glass and allowed himself, for exactly ten seconds by the clock, to feel the cold pleasure of a man who has finally heard the lock turn.

* * *

He worked until three.

The method, once seen, was simple enough to be beautiful, and Ashgrave resented it for that. Take any annotation in the margins. Match it against the corresponding location on the working copies. Where the notes agreed, the ground was as mapped — safe, surveyed, true. Where they diverged — where the original said *clay* and the copy said *gravel*, where the original counted seven willows and the copy counted six — there lay the encoded difference, and the differences, plotted across eleven sheets, drew a line.

The line ran northwest out of the Reach, along the high bank, past the ruined chapel, down through alder scrub to a bend where the original margin read, in letters so small Ashgrave needed the glass and a steady hand and the whole of his patience: *G.F. — piles stand at low water. Cross at slack before the flood tide. Ask for the eel-woman.*

Gallow Ford.

He said the name aloud once, quietly, tasting it. It appeared in none of the current records. It appeared in one place only: a court-martial summary from '31, a single line among hundreds, noting that certain persons *fled by the old crossing at Gallow Ford and were not afterward found.* Certain persons. The Corries among them, though the summary had already been redacted of names by some clerk as frightened as any in this town. Ewan Corrie. The boy who had fished the broken bridge with the mapmaker's apprentice, if the schoolmaster's old registers and the tavern gossip could be believed, and Ashgrave had learned to believe tavern gossip over registers, because registers were written by men who wanted to keep their posts.

He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the river. Black water, black sky, one lantern burning at the mill race. Somewhere out there was a drowned footbridge with its piles still standing after fifteen years of floods, and somewhere beyond it a girl who had spent a month in his custody copying his documents while — he was now nearly certain — feeding his cartographers a landscape with holes in it, holes shaped exactly like the places she meant someone to go.

He should have been angry. He found, examining himself honestly, that he was not. Anger was for men surprised by treachery, and Ashgrave had never once been surprised by it. Treachery was simply competence pointed the wrong way, and he respected competence the way he respected weather: without taking it personally, and with a firm intention of using it.

Because here was the elegant part, the part he turned over like a coin of rare minting: he did not need to catch her altering the charts. He did not need proof. He did not need to break her, which would be wasteful, or hang her, which would be wasteful and loud. He needed only to accept her gift.

She had given the army false charts. Very well. The army would act upon the false charts — openly, visibly, stupidly — and she would know they were acting upon them, because she had drawn them, and she would do what any faithful conspirator must do when the enemy marches confidently into error. She would warn her people. She would move them along the true route, the route only she could chart, the route that ended at a drowned bridge that stood at low water and crossed at slack tide.

And the army, marching confidently into error, would be waiting there.

Her cleverness was not merely discoverable. It was *load-bearing*. Pull one stone out and the whole arch came down on the heads of the people it had been built to shelter. She had built her escape route out of his ignorance, and all he had to do was remain ignorant in the correct direction, loudly, on the night of her choosing — and be standing, instead, exactly where she had told herself he would never be.

* * *

There remained the matter of the tide.

Slack before the flood. Ashgrave sent for the harbormaster's tide tables at dawn — sent Hale, actually, because Hale asked fewer questions than the harbormaster's own clerk and wrote faster — and worked the dates against the almanac. Saint Cuthbert's eve fell nine days hence. The evening tide would turn at twenty minutes past eight, slack water holding perhaps half an hour in the narrow water above the bend. Half an hour. Enough time to cross a file of men over a rebuilt footbridge, if the footbridge were rebuilt, if the timber came by water, if the eel-woman's boats moved at night the way her file said they had moved for thirty years.

Nine days. It was almost generous of fate. Almost as if the promise had been made in advance — and Ashgrave paused over that thought, because it had the texture of evidence. A promise made in advance implied a letter. A letter implied a courier. A courier implied a fisherman, and there were eleven licensed fishermen on the Reach, and he intended by tomorrow noon to have reason to interview every one of them, gently, about their nets and their ledgers and their recent correspondence, without ever once saying the word *letter*, because the fastest way to stop a message was to make the sender believe the channel was watched, and he did not want the channel stopped. He wanted the channel *flowing*. He wanted the message delivered. He wanted young Corrie to ride for Gallow Ford on Saint Cuthbert's eve with the confidence of a man keeping an appointment kept for him by a dead mapmaker's word.

Let the appointment be kept. Let the bridge stand. Let the girl watch her promise walk across the water into the guns.

He felt no cruelty in it — or rather, he felt the cruelty and set it aside as one sets aside the cost of materials. War was arithmetic. He had learned that at Dettingen, watching cavalry go into a bog that a local man had sworn was firm ground, and he had learned it again in every year since: the side that knew the ground won, and the side that loved the ground lost, because love made men loyal to the terrain instead of to the outcome. Vane had loved this valley. The girl loved it. The eel-woman loved it. And Ashgrave intended to win it, which required only that he know it better than they loved it.

He drafted the orders himself, in his own hand, because some documents should exist in only one copy.

A patrol of forty, dressed as the routine sweep they had run twice before along the north road — visible, predictable, boring. Their route would take them, at dusk, toward the ruined chapel and then *away*, doubling back through the alder scrub under cover of dark, down to the bend below the ford. Two companies more held in reserve at the mill, unlit, muffled. Boats — he would take Berta Quill's own boats when the time came, or hire others; there were always boats — posted downstream to close the water. And at the center of it, the piece that made the trap a trap rather than a skirmish: the working charts. His officers would navigate by the sheets the girl had authenticated. They would trust the gravel where she had written gravel, the shallow where she had written shallow, the marsh that her careful, loving, lying lines declared passable on foot.

If she had planted a false route for the raiders' pursuers — and Ashgrave would have staked his commission that she had, because a craftsman that good never left the enemy a clean road — then half his patrol might drown in it, or founder, or wander. He accepted the cost. He wrote the acceptance into the orders in plain words, so that no subordinate could later claim surprise: *Some loss in the marsh is anticipated and authorized. The position at the ford is to be held regardless.*

Then he sat back and read the whole design again, and found it sound, and found it also — this was the part he lingered on — *hers*. Every element of the ambush was built from materials she had supplied: the charts, the timing, the route, even the psychology, her certainty that the army read maps the way clerks read them, flatly, without faith. He was going to defeat her with her own craftsmanship, and on the morning after, when he showed her the captured men and the mud-stained copies of her own linework, she would understand that she had not been outargued or outspent or outcrueltied. She had been *read*. Her margins had been read the way she had taught herself to read Vane's, and there was a justice in that which Ashgrave intended to savor slowly, the way he savored everything: alone, at night, by lamplight, with the clock ticking.

* * *

In the morning he summoned her.

He did it casually — a message sent down through Hale, no escort, no ceremony: *the Colonel requires the apprentice to verify three sheets before the survey party departs.* He watched her come across the yard from the loft window, a small figure with ink-stained fingers and her master's stoop already forming in her shoulders, carrying her tools the way a surgeon carries knives. She had grown thinner through the occupation. Careful. Watchful in the way of animals that have learned which shadows hold owls. Good. Fear sharpened craft, and he needed her craft at its finest for nine more days.

"Three sheets," he said, when she stood before his table, "for the north sweep. The party goes out Thursday week." He laid the charts before her and watched her eyes — watched, specifically, the half-second pause before they settled, the flicker that a less patient man would have missed, the small internal lurch of a gambler seeing her own cards dealt back to her face-up. "You'll confirm the ground as marked. Gravel at the chapel bend. Firm going through the alder scrub. The marsh east of the willows is passable on foot in dry weather."

"Passable in dry weather," she repeated. Her voice was level. Her hands, folding the charts, were level. Only her eyes stayed a moment too long on the marsh line, and Ashgrave catalogued the look and put it away.

"You've done faithful work," he said. "The army thanks you for it."

Something moved behind her face — he could not name it and did not try. Gratitude, perhaps, or grief, or the particular expression of a person watching a door close that she believes is closing on the other side. He dismissed her with a nod and returned to the atlas, to the tiny brown letters that had cost a good man his life and would now, within the fortnight, cost the rebellion its courier, its crossing, and its mapmaker's heir.

That afternoon he walked the rampart of the mill as the light went long and gold over the water, and saw, far downstream, a boat he did not recognize moving under sail toward the estuary, and a fisherman on the weir who watched it pass with an attention he would remember. Threads. All of it threads, and every thread leading back to the same knot at the same bend of the same river, on the same night, nine days from now.

Saint Cuthbert's tide. Slack water at twenty minutes past eight.

He would be there early.
