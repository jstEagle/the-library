## 17. Saint Cuthbert's Tide

The fog came up the river at dusk like something poured, thick and slow, filling the hollows of Marlow's Reach before it filled the air above them. Tamsin stood at the edge of Gallow Ford with her hand flat against the new rail of the bridge — her bridge, their bridge, eleven nights of Berta's timber and her own arithmetic — and listened to the water move beneath it.

Slack water at twenty minutes past eight. The tide had turned early, or she had counted wrong, or her heart was simply running ahead of the clock. She could not tell anymore which of her senses to trust. For a week she had lived inside a doubled world: by day, the obedient copyist bent over Ashgrave's charts in the mill loft; by night, this other woman, this builder of impossible bridges, who measured by starlight and lied by lamplight and was now standing in fog waiting for a boy she had not seen in fifteen years.

Behind her, upstream, came the sound she had been listening for all evening — not oars but paddles, muffled, unhurried. Berta's boats. Three of them, riding low.

Berta herself stepped out of the reeds at Tamsin's elbow, so quietly that Tamsin started despite knowing she was there. The eel-seller wore her old oilskin, dark as river mud, and her face beneath the hood was set in lines that might have been carved.

"Fourteen of them," Berta said. "Corrie's boy at the front. He has his father's shoulders."

"You've seen him?"

"Saw him come down the bank at Miller's Hollow with my own eyes." Berta's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "He asked if the bridge would hold a horse. I told him bridges built by Vane's girl hold whatever you ask of them."

Tamsin's throat tightened. She looked down the black spine of the footbridge, its posts fresh-cut and pale even in the fog, running out across the Ford toward the far marsh like a sentence written in someone else's hand.

*It will hold,* she told herself. *I charted every beam.*

But there was another chart in her mind tonight, the one she had not drawn and could not stop drawing: Ashgrave's face in the map room, courteous and surgical, saying *authenticate the master's hand*; the ambush he must have laid from her own forged coordinates, because she had come to understand, in the sleepless arithmetic of the last week, that the false ford she planted had been too clever — clever enough to be believed, clever enough to be used, clever enough that a man like Ashgrave would position his men exactly where her lie said the ground was safe.

She had built the trap out of her own betrayal of everything Vane taught her. Now she had to spring it before it closed on the wrong people.

"The signal," she said, and her voice was steadier than she deserved. "When they're on the water."

"Two lights on the weir post." Berta pressed something into Tamsin's palm — a boat hook, short-handled, cold. "And Tamsin." She waited until Tamsin met her eyes. "Whatever happens on that bank, you mind the arithmetic. Not every life weighs the same tonight, and you don't get to save them all. Choose first, before the shooting starts, or the choosing will choose you."

Then she was gone into the reeds, and Tamsin was alone with the fog and the bridge and the terrible ledger opening in her head.

* * *

The two lights bloomed on the weir post at half past eight, small and orange through the mist, and Tamsin's heart went with them.

They came down to the water in single file, shadows made solid — fourteen men and one boy grown into a man, moving with the economy of people who had learned to be quiet or die. Ewan Corrie crossed the bridge second-to-last, and Tamsin watched him from behind, unseen, and felt fifteen years fold up like wet paper.

She knew him by the way he tested each plank before trusting it. He had done that as a child on the broken span — *the bridge is lying, Tam, listen, it creaks different when it's lying* — and he did it now, head tilted, one hand skimming the rail, and when he reached the midpoint of the crossing he stopped, and she saw his shoulders change, and she knew he had understood what this bridge was. That someone had built it. That someone had remembered.

He said nothing. There was no time and too many ears. But at the far end he turned once, scanning the near bank, and though the fog erased her completely, Tamsin raised her hand anyway, an offering to no one, and then the raiders were past the bridge and into the boats, and the arithmetic began.

Because that was when the drums started.

Not drums — boots. Hundreds of boots, or thirty, it made no difference; the sound came off the downs behind the far marsh in a rolling cadence that Tamsin felt in her teeth. A lantern flared. Then another. Then the whole eastern bank seemed to catch light at once, lanterns unhooded along a line that had been laid there hours ago, waiting, patient as Ashgrave himself.

"Ambush," someone shouted in the boats, and the night tore open.

The first volley went high — firing at movement in fog, at ghosts — and the second found the water. Tamsin heard the slap of shot on the river, heard a man cry out, heard Berta's voice cut through the chaos with a string of oaths that would have shamed a dockyard, and then the boats were turning, oars biting, trying to reach the near bank while the red line advanced down the far slope in disciplined waves.

*They think the raiders are trapped between the line and the river.* Tamsin's mind moved cold and fast, the way it did over a chart. *Ashgrave closed the net on the far side. He doesn't know about the near bank yet — he thinks the bridge is ruined. He thinks no one can cross back.*

He thought that because her forged chart said so.

So the net had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped exactly like her lie.

"Berta!" she screamed into the reeds. "The false ford! Drive them onto the false ford!"

Whether Berta heard her or arrived at the same sum independently, Tamsin never learned. But a minute later one of Berta's boats broke away from the rest and ran downstream hard, oarsmen bending, presenting itself to the advancing line like bait — and the line took the bait, because soldiers are trained to cut off retreat, and the retreat on their maps ran through the shallows at the Ford's southern edge.

Through the ford that did not exist.

Tamsin watched it happen from the near bank, watched the flank company peel off and double-time along the marsh edge toward her forged coordinates, and she wanted to shout, wanted to warn them, some of them were hardly older than Ewan — and she did not shout. That was the arithmetic. Berta had told her to choose first and she had chosen, weeks ago, alone at a drafting table, when she drew a safe crossing where the ground would swallow a man to the chest. She had just never let herself feel the weight of the answer until now, watching forty redcoats march confidently into six feet of drowned peat and silt.

The marsh took the first rank to the thighs within ten paces. The second rank, following, collided with the first. Lanterns went into the water. Somewhere in the fog a sergeant was roaring orders that only made the tangle worse, and the raiders' remaining boats slid out through the channel Tamsin had charted true — the deep-water run, marked on no Crown map — and were away into the fog before the line could reform.

But not all of them. Two boats were still under the bank, pinned by lantern-light, and one of them was Berta's.

* * *

Tamsin ran the bridge.

She had never run it loaded before, and for three strides she did not know if it would take her — the timbers spoke, groaned, held — and then she was across and into the confusion on the far landing where the last raiders were fighting to launch. Ewan Corrie was there, hauling a wounded man upright by the collar, his face lit strobe-bright by the muzzle flashes, and there was no time for anything, no time for names or years or the fishing rod they'd lost in this same river as children.

"Tamsin?" His voice cracked on it, half recognition, half disbelief, and she shoved the pain of it down where she kept everything else.

"Boat. Now. Push before they reload." She got her shoulder under the wounded man's other arm — he weighed nothing, he was burning with fever — and together they ran the gunwale into the water. "Berta's boat is pinned at the southern channel. Someone has to draw the lantern line off her."

"I'll draw it."

"No." She caught his sleeve. It was the first time she had touched him in fifteen years and she allowed it exactly one heartbeat. "You cross. That's the promise. That's the whole promise, Ewan — your side of the water, alive. Go."

"And her?"

"She has boats and sense and thirty years of this river in her bones. And I have one more lie left to spend." She pushed him, hard, toward the gunwale. "Go. Please."

Something moved across his face that she would carry the rest of her life — and then he went, over the side, dragging the wounded man in after him, and the current took the boat, and Tamsin turned south along the bank and started spending her last lie.

* * *

What remained of the patrol's attention was fixed on Berta's grounded boat and the eel-woman standing in it like a figurehead, hands raised, cursing with impressive fluency in two languages. The lantern line had contracted around her. An officer was wading out demanding she identify herself, and Berta was identifying herself, loudly, as a poor widow selling eels, and the absurdity of it was buying seconds, precious seconds.

Tamsin reached the weir and pulled the rope she and Hale had rigged three nights ago, the one that dropped the weir gate and sent the mill race surging, and the southern channel woke up.

That was the lie: not a false depth but a false calm. Her forged chart showed slack water at the channel mouth at half past eight. The truth, which she had known since childhood and which no map in Ashgrave's possession recorded, was that Saint Cuthbert's tide backed up the mill race and turned the channel to whitewater twenty minutes later. Anyone reading her chart would stand on the bank at exactly the wrong moment, believing the river was holding still for them.

It was not holding still for anyone.

The surge came down the channel with a sound like a door slamming, and Berta — who knew that sound better than she knew her own name — was already casting off, letting the flood take her boat sideways through the gap in the lantern line before the soldiers understood what the river was doing. One lantern went into the water. Then two. Men who had been standing on what looked like firm shelving found the shelving leaving under them, the tide scouring the footing away, and the officer was shouting to fall back, fall back, and the channel that Tamsin's chart promised was passable became, for a quarter hour, a brown god deciding who it would keep.

Berta's boat shot through into open water and was gone north.

Tamsin stood on the weir in the roar and the fog and counted the boats into the dark. One. Two. Three. Ewan's, far out now, a smudge against the smudge of the estuary. Four — Berta's, riding low and fast and free.

The arithmetic balanced. Everyone she had promised. Everyone she had chosen.

Almost.

* * *

She was still standing there when the lantern found her.

It came up the bank path without hurry, carried at shoulder height, and behind it walked Colonel Ashgrave with his coat collar turned up against the spray and his hands clasped behind his back, an evening gentleman taking the air beside a battlefield he had lost four hundred pounds of powder and a night's reputation to.

"Miss Thackeray." He stopped an arm's length away and regarded her through the fog, and his face held no anger at all, which was infinitely worse than anger. "The mill race. Of course. I have your chart in my coat pocket at this moment. Slack water at half past eight, the channel passable at all states of the tide." He drew it out, damp at the corners, and looked at it almost fondly. "You know what the tragedy of forgery is? The forger must always know more than the original author. And you knew more than Vane ever did. Which means—" he folded the chart along its old creases, precisely, "—that everything in that atlas bearing his hand may bear yours underneath. Every margin. Every name."

Somewhere out on the black water, faint as breath, a paddle turned.

"So we will continue our conversation," Ashgrave said, "in the mill loft. Tonight, while the tide is still high enough to be instructive. Bring nothing."

He gestured up the path, and two soldiers detached from the darkness to walk behind her, and Tamsin climbed the bank with the river loud at her back — carrying Ewan out, carrying Berta away, carrying every name she had ever hidden — and ahead of her the mill windows burned yellow against the fog, and in the loft above them, stacked and guarded and waiting, lay the atlas.

Her atlas. Still unread.

Still, perhaps, readable.
