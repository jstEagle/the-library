## 18. What the River Takes

The fog came off the marsh in shreds, and through every tear in it I watched the river decide what it would keep.

From the alder copse above Gallow Ford I had seen it all — the ambush kindled out of my own false lines, the patrol wading into the drowned meadow with torches held high like men carrying fire into church, the ground opening its black throat beneath them. Berta's boats had come out of the reeds quiet as thought. And then the shout, the flare of a lantern on the mill weir, and Berta turning her punt back toward the bank where a boy in a red coat was floundering in the shallows, because Berta Quill could no more watch a man drown than she could sell a bad eel.

They took her at the water's edge. I heard her laugh — *laugh* — as they dragged her up onto the shingle, that low rolling laugh she used when a buyer tried to haggle her down. Then a rifle butt, then nothing, and the fog closed over the place where she had stood like a page turned too soon.

Ewan crossed. That much the river granted. I had seen him reach the far bank, seen him pause — one instant, his face a pale smudge in the dark, turned back toward this side, toward me or toward the bridge or toward fifteen years of everything neither of us had said — and then he was gone into the birch scrub, and the raiders' horses were gone after him upstream, and the shooting dwindled to nothing but the water talking to itself.

Now dawn was coming up grey and thin, and I was still crouched in the alders with mud to my knees and Vane's brass dividers digging into my hip through my coat pocket, and I had to choose.

I could run. The thought laid itself out before me like a clean sheet: north along the drover's road, west at the standing stone, the escape Vane had mapped for other people all those years ago. My legs knew it already. Fifteen years of drawing escape routes will teach your feet something even when your heart refuses the lesson.

Or I could walk down through the mist into Marlow's Reach, past the soldiers dragging their drowned comrades out of the marsh, past the mill where the atlas sat in its crates, and put myself in front of Colonel Ashgrave before he came looking for me. Because he would come looking. Every line of the trap had been drawn from charts forged under my hands. When a snare closes on empty air, the trapper does not blame the wire. He blames the hand that bent it.

* * *

I went down to the ford first. I do not know how else to say it — I needed to see what the night had cost, item by item, the way you inventory a workshop after a fire.

The bridge stood. That was the unbearable thing about it. We had raised it beam by beam across eleven nights of fog, Berta swearing at the timber and me swearing at the measurements, and now it stood there in the grey light, honest and whole, wet ropes still swaying, footprints of twenty men printed in the mud of both banks. A thing built for a promise, used once, and left holding nothing.

A soldier had drowned in the pool beneath the third pier. They were hauling him out with a grapple as I watched from the willow shadow — a young one, red-haired, his coat ballooning around him in the current like a flower deciding to bloom underwater. I made myself look. Vane had taught me that a mapmaker who cannot bear to look at land is no use to anyone; I suppose the same is true of a woman who cannot bear to look at what her cleverness has done.

Sergeant Hale was on the bank. He stood apart from the others, ledger under his arm, not writing in it. When the red-haired soldier came up out of the water and was laid on the shingle, Hale knelt and closed the boy's eyes with two fingers, gently, the way you'd straighten a crooked line. Then he looked up, straight at the willows where I stood, and did not point, did not call out, only gave the smallest movement of his head — not toward the garrison, but away from it, downstream, toward the eel-stakes.

Then he opened his ledger and began, deliberately, to write.

I understood him. I think I would have understood him in any language: *go*. And underneath it, in the part of me that had spent the night doing arithmetic in other people's blood: *you cannot go.*

Berta was already being marched up the mill road between two soldiers, her hands bound with the same tarred rope she'd used on the bridge beams. Even at eighty yards I could see she wasn't stooping. She walked like a woman delivering goods, head up, calling out something to her captors that made one of them go scarlet and the other look quickly away. She passed within thirty feet of my willow, and though she could not possibly have known I was there, she turned her head and spat an eel-bone neatly into the river, and I heard her say, clear as a bell across the water:

"Mind the stakes, girl. They're worth money."

It might have been nothing. It was everything. The eel-stakes downstream were hers, and half of Marlow's Reach knew it, and Ashgrave did not. There are messages a captured woman can send that a free one cannot.

I watched them take her into the mill yard, and the choice finished making itself, the way a flood finishes finding its level.

If I ran, Berta hanged for a smuggler's treason with no one to speak for her, no one who knew which of her stories were lies and which were maps. If I ran, the atlas went to London in Ashgrave's crates with whatever he could wring from it, and Ewan spent the rest of his life hunted by his own history written fair in ink. If I stayed — if I walked into that mill yard with my master's tools in my pocket and my name on the requisition lists — then at least the bargain would be struck by me and not merely endured.

Survival's price, I understood at last, does not come due in coin. It comes due in people. Berta had paid herself into my debt the moment she turned that punt back for a drowning redcoat, and debts of that kind are settled in kind, not in flight.

I wrung out my skirts as best I could, took the dividers from my pocket, and set them where a mapmaker should carry them: openly, in my hand.

* * *

Marlow's Reach on that morning was a town pretending to be a fortress and failing at both. Redcoats on the corners with their collars up against the drizzle. Doors shut, shutters cracked an inch. Mrs. Petchy's window with a single candle lit in daylight — old signal custom, meaning *someone is safe*, and I could not tell for whom it burned, and chose to believe it burned for everyone.

At the mill yard gate a corporal moved to bar my way, took in the mud to my knees, the dividers, the ink stains fifteen years deep in my fingers, and hesitated.

"Tamsin Thackeray," I said. "Vane's apprentice. The colonel will want me."

"The colonel," said a voice behind him, "will want a great many things."

Ashgrave stood in the mill doorway with a cup of coffee he was not drinking, and he looked worse than I had ever seen him — not worn down but *lit*, the way a man looks when a long proof has finally failed him and he must find where. His coat was buttoned wrong. He had been up all night with Vane's marginalia, and I knew it the way you know weather.

"You," he said, "were not at your lodgings last night."

"No, sir."

"Where were you?"

"At the ford," I said, "fishing." And I let him wait on it while I looked him in the eye, because Berta had taught me that audacity reads as innocence if you hold it steady enough.

Something moved behind his face — not belief, but the exhaustion of a man who has suspected everyone so long that suspicion itself has worn smooth. He set the cup on the doorpost.

"Do you know what I found, Miss Thackeray, at four o'clock this morning?" He held up a leaf of Vane's atlas, a coastal survey, and tapped the cartouche with one finger. "The border families. Not all of them. Enough. Old letters hidden in the flourishes — a *g* shaped like a gallows rope, an *e* that carries a second stroke when the family named was attainted. Your master wrote a rebellion into his decorations and called it ornament." His voice never rose. That was always the worst of him; he spoke of hanging men in the tone of a man reading a laundry list. "He was careful. You were his apprentice. I am told you have his hand."

"He trained mine," I said. "That isn't a confession, Colonel. It's a biography."

"It is both," said Ashgrave, "and we both know it." He stepped aside from the doorway. Behind him, in the loft, the crates stood open, sheets laid out on trestles like bodies in a field hospital. "Come inside. There is a woman in my custody who claims she has never seen Gallow Ford in her life, though her boats were moored beside it. There are eleven of my soldiers in the marsh, three of whom will not be coming out of it. And somewhere north of here rides a man whose passage across that ford was arranged — *arranged*, Miss Thackeray, with timbers cut to measure — by someone who knows this river better than any officer I command." He picked up the coffee and drank it at last, cold, without noticing. "You will authenticate every sheet in this atlas. You will tell me what the margins say. And you will begin with the pages concerning Gallow Ford."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I proceed without you, and I am forced to assume the worst about everyone whose name appears in these ledgers, beginning with the eel-woman and ending, alphabetically and otherwise, with Thackeray." He paused, and for one moment the surgical calm slipped enough to show the man beneath, and the man beneath looked almost tired enough to be honest. "You came here voluntarily, into my yard, on the morning after an ambush built from your own charts. That is either guilt or courage, and I confess I no longer care to distinguish them. Both give me what I need: you, at this table, working."

So I sat down at the trestle among my dead master's sheets, and drew the dividers from my pocket, and opened them over the coastline of the borderlands like a woman spreading her hands over a sleeping child.

And underneath the terror, quieter than the fear, steadier than the grief, something in me settled and went to work — the thing Vane had built in me across fifteen years of useful silences. Because Ashgrave thought he had cornered the atlas's keeper. He had not. He had let inside his walls the only person alive who knew every secret those pages kept, every name in every margin, every lie I had planted myself — including the false ford that had swallowed his soldiers, and three more like it that he had not yet found.

He wanted my hands. He was going to get the rest of me as well.

I squared the first sheet against the table edge, and outside, far down the river, I heard the eel-stakes knocking in the current — Berta's stakes, keeping their own stubborn count — and I began to draw.
