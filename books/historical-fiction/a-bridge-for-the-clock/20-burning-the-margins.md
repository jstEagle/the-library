## 20. Burning the Margins

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle of tallow by the time Tamsin laid the atlas open on her workbench and understood what she had to do.

The bargain was struck. Ashgrave would have his atlas by first light — the completed thing, bound in Vane's good calfskin, every sheet finished in the master's hand, a trophy worthy of a colonel's report and a clerk's promotion and whatever quiet ambitions moved men like him through the machinery of occupation. In exchange: Berta Quill, released from the garrison mill before the week was out. Marlow's Reach left unmolested through the winter. A town's worth of breathing room purchased with a book.

She had won. She kept telling herself she had won.

And yet here she sat at midnight with the atlas open before her like a body on a table, and she could not stop looking at the margins.

They were everywhere once you knew how to see them — and only she knew how to see them now. A flourish of sea-grass along the Essex coast that was not sea-grass at all but a family name rendered in Aldous Vane's smallest hand: *Corrie*, tucked into the curve of an estuary like a bird sheltering from weather. A compass rose whose decorative points carried letters in their shadows, spelling out the true surname of a miller's family who had been entered in the parish rolls under a false one since the year of the rebellion. The cartouche on the Gallow Ford survey, all scrollwork and cherubs by any ordinary eye, dense as a beehive underneath with names — thirty-one names, she had counted them years ago, inked so fine they might have been texture in the paper itself. Families the Crown had declared extinct. Families Vane had refused to let die.

Fifteen years of learning to hide things in plain sight, and now every hiding place had to be emptied and burned.

She rose, went to the hearth, and fed the fire until it stood up properly — not the sullen cooking embers she'd need for morning bread but a working flame, tall enough to read by, hot enough to do what was asked of it. Then she fetched the good lamp too, trimmed its wick, and set both lights on the bench so the pages lay between them like a patient between two physicians.

Because that was what this was. Not destruction. Surgery.

She turned to the first sheet — the northern coast, drawn when she was nine years old and allowed, for the first time, to grind the pigments — and she began to memorize.

Not skim. Not glance. *Memorize*, the way Vane had taught her to memorize coastlines before ever touching pen to paper: look, close the eyes, hold the shape against the dark, open the eyes and check. He had called it *carrying the country*. A mapmaker who could not carry the country in her skull, he said, was only a copyist, and copyists were ten a penny and died anonymous. She had thought he meant geography. She understood now that he had always meant this — that the truest atlas is the one no soldier can requisition, no colonel can study, no flame can reach.

*Corrie,* she said silently to the estuary grass, and closed her eyes, and held it. Opened them. Still there. Good.

She worked methodically, sheet by sheet, and the night arranged itself around the work. The wind came off the river and worried the shutter. Somewhere a dog gave up on barking. The candle sank; she replaced it. The fire wanted feeding; she fed it. And page after page she took the names into herself — not as ink, not as marks, but as people, which was what they had always been.

That was the trick of it, she realized somewhere past the second hour. You could not carry a name the way you carried a word. It slipped. But you could carry the person. The miller whose real name was Fenn — a broad man, quick to laugh, who had once paid Vane in apples so bruised they were mostly cider. The widow Harestane, entered in the rolls as Mrs. Grey, who had taught Tamsin to gut a trout on the same bank where Ewan taught her to catch one. The Corries themselves: Ewan's mother singing low at the mending, his father's hands cracked from net-work, the whole family gone upstream in one fog-bound night while Vane drew their escape route in water-soluble ink on a slate he wiped clean himself.

Tamsin carried them. All thirty-one, and the dozen more scattered through the coastal sheets, and the handful hidden in the town plan of Marlow's Reach itself — including, near the churchyard on sheet fourteen, in what appeared to be a shading of yew trees, the name *Thackeray*. Her own father's name, restored by Vane's hand to the place where her family had actually lived before the erasure. She had found it at eleven and wept into the fixative and been scolded for crying near documents.

*Carry me,* the yew-shading said now. And she did. She folded her father back into her chest where he had lived all along, ink or no ink.

* * *

Somewhere past three o'clock, her eyes began to fail her, and she understood the cruelest part of the task was arriving.

Memorizing was the mercy. Burning was the wound.

She had rehearsed it in her head as though rehearsal would soften it: hold each page over the flame, just long enough, the heat kissing the paper without catching it, the iron-gall ink — which held its darkness stubbornly even when the paper browned — needing actual contact, needing the flame to lick directly across the margin until the fine lines curled into nothing. Too little and the names survived, legible to anyone who knew to look, dangerous to everyone they named. Too much and the sheet caught, and fifteen years of honest cartography went up with the dishonest margins.

There was no tolerance built into the operation. There never was, in Vane's craft. Precision or ruin.

She tested on the least precious sheet first — a working copy of the river below the mill, useful only for practice. Held the lower corner to the flame. Watched the margin scorch amber, then umber, then black, the sea-grass flourish curling like a living thing retreating from the heat, and the smell rose up sharp and bitter — hot paper and burnt size and something older underneath, the smell of tannin and iron, the smell of the ink itself dying. It smelled, absurdly, like the workshop in winter. Like Aldous Vane's sleeves.

She had to sit down for a moment when that came to her.

*You taught me to keep them safe,* she said to the empty room, to the man fifteen years dead. *Now I'm unkeeping them.*

But even as she said it she knew he would have agreed. That was the lesson under every lesson he'd ever given her, the rule beneath the rules he'd called *useful silences*: a map serves the traveler, not the maker. If the atlas could save Berta and buy the town its winter only by going silent — then silence it, girl, and grieve on your own time. She could hear his voice saying it, dry as the dust in his ledger grooves: *Sentiment is for after the line dries.*

*After the line dries,* she answered aloud, and stood, and began.

She worked the way she had worked the memorization: sheet by sheet, north to south, in order, because order was safety and safety was speed. Left hand holding the page taut over a board angled toward the hearth, right hand bringing the candle-flame or a splinter of fire low across the margin in one steady pass — Vane's own motion, the long unbroken stroke he'd drilled into her wrist for a decade, applied now to erasure instead of creation. The same line that had drawn coasts unmade them.

The margins went amber. Went black. Curled. She brushed each cooled page with the soft hake brush, checked the scorched edges in the lamplight for any ghost of legibility, and stacked the finished sheets face-down on the far side of the bench.

The estuary grass that spelled *Corrie* burned in perhaps four seconds. She made herself watch every one of them.

The compass rose with the Fenn letters took longer — the lettering sat deeper in the paper, and she had to bring the flame twice across it, and the corner of the sheet began to smolder and she had to clap it out with her palm, and the burn left a small ragged hole through the decorative border of the rose, and she decided, staring at it with her heart slamming, that a hole could pass as age and damage. Maps got old. Old maps were trusted. Even damage could be made to serve.

The Gallow Ford cartouche was the hardest.

Of course it was. Thirty-one names under the scrollwork, the densest hiding Vane had ever built, ink laid down in layers over years — some of those names were among the first he'd ever hidden, others added within weeks of his death, and the inks differed in age and formula and stubbornness. The old layers surrendered quickly. The new ones fought. She had to hold the flame so close she singed the fine hairs on the backs of her fingers, and the smell of it — that workshop smell again, unbearable, homelike — filled the room until she could taste it.

Halfway through the cartouche she stopped. Her hand was shaking. The flame wavered with it, and a wavering flame on an irreplaceable document was death, and she made herself set the splinter down and stand at the window a moment, looking out at the dark river, breathing.

Gallow Ford lay invisible out there beyond the roofs, the drowned footbridge she and Berta had raised beam by beam in the fog, the crossing that had cost Berta her freedom and very nearly her life. Berta's price, remember: record the ford openly. Let the map say what happened there. Memory made public, not hidden.

And Tamsin had promised her — and then had bargained the promise away, because the arithmetic of the mill loft had allowed nothing else, because one woman's monument weighed less than a town's winter. Berta had heard her out, jaw tight, and finally nodded once and said, *Then you'll be my map, girl. Every year, tell somebody.* 

Every year, tell somebody.

So the names did not die tonight. They changed address. They moved from calfskin and iron-gall into flesh and breath, into the one archive no requisition notice could touch. Colonel Ashgrave was about to receive the finest atlas of the borderlands ever assembled, complete and authentic in the master's hand, and it would contain nothing — nothing — that could hang anyone.

She went back to the bench and finished the cartouche. When the last name curled to ash she brushed the page, held it to the light, and saw only a handsome decorative frame, elegantly aged, framing a ford marked plainly and honestly, as if it had always been ordinary. As if no one had ever needed to hide behind it.

She found she was crying, quietly, the way you cry when your hands are busy and cannot be spared, tears running freely while the brush kept moving. She did not wipe her face. There was nobody to perform dry-eyed composure for, and Vane had never minded her crying near documents so long as the documents stayed dry.

* * *

The sky over the river had gone from black to iron to the first thin wash of pearl by the time she reached the final sheet, and Tamsin sat back on her stool, wrists aching, throat raw from smoke and something else, and looked at what she had made.

The atlas lay stacked and cooling on the far side of the bench — forty sheets, every margin scorched clean, every hidden name translated out of ink and into her. She paged through it slowly in the grey light, checking her work one last time, and it was beautiful. It was beautiful the way a healed wound is beautiful: whole, and marked, and carrying its history invisibly.

Only one thing remained, and she had saved it deliberately, the way you save the last sip of something you don't want to finish.

The title page.

Vane had lettered it himself, years ago — *An Atlas of the Borderlands, Surveyed and Drawn by Aldous Vane, Mapmaker of Marlow's Reach* — and beneath, in smaller script, the dedication line that had always made her chest tighten when clients admired it without understanding:

*For those who are not written elsewhere.*

She had assumed she would have to burn that line. It was practically a confession. Any man clever enough to find the Corries in the estuary grass would read *not written elsewhere* and know exactly what kind of book he held.

But sitting there in the growing light with smoke in her hair and her master's names beating inside her ribs like a second heart, Tamsin found she could not do it. Some things should survive, even in ink. Let Ashgrave puzzle over it. Let him suspect forever and prove nothing — suspicion without proof was the air the man already breathed. The line was true. It had been true when Vane wrote it and it was truer now than he had lived to know, because now the ones it named truly existed nowhere else on earth except in her.

She dipped a pen instead, and beneath Vane's inscription, in her own careful hand — not disguised, not forged, her own, because she was done pretending she was only his echo — she added four words:

*and for those who crossed.*

She watched the ink dry. Sanded it. Closed the atlas.

Outside, the garrison drum beat the morning call, and boots began to move in the street, and somewhere down by the mill a cart creaked under weight. First light. First delivery. Colonel Ashgrave expected his trophy within the hour, and with it, Berta Quill's walking free.

Tamsin wrapped the atlas in oilcloth, tied it with the plain cord clients' parcels used, and lifted it. It was heavier than paper had any right to be — or lighter, depending on how you counted, depending whether you weighed the vellum or everything that was no longer on it. She settled it in the crook of her arm the way you carry something sleeping, took one long breath of her cold workshop with its smell of burnt size and tannin and home, and opened the door onto the occupied morning.

Down the hill, at the garrison gates, two soldiers were already waiting. And beside them, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, stood Colonel Ashgrave — early, watching her come, his face giving away nothing except the certainty of a man who has decided, rightly or wrongly, that the book in her arms is not the only thing she is carrying.
