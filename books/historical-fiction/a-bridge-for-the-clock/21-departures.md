## 21. Departures

### I.

Berta Quill had worn chains for eleven days, and the first thing she did when they came off was ask the time.

"Not that it matters," she said, flexing her wrists where the iron had left bracelets of broken skin. "Only I've been asking for eleven days and nobody would tell me. A woman likes to know how much of her life has been stolen. It's easier to invoice."

Colonel Ashgrave's clerk — a pale boy who looked like he'd rather be anywhere in the Empire than this room — mumbled something about the quarter-hour and slid the release papers across the table as if they might bite him. Berta signed with a flourish that took up half the page. *Quill*, she wrote, and beneath it, small and defiant, *of Gallow Ford*. Let them file that. Let some poor archivist in London spend his whole life wondering what a ford had to do with eels.

Outside the garrison mill the winter light lay flat on the river like a sheet of hammered pewter. The town smelled of woodsmoke and thawing mud, and Berta stood on the mill steps breathing it in the way other women breathed lavender — because it meant she was not in a cell, because it meant the river was ninety paces away and running south, running free, answering to no colonel's requisition notice.

Tamsin Thackeray was waiting at the bottom of the steps. She had a way of standing lately, Berta had noticed — straighter than before, but carefully straight, the way a mast stands after a storm has cracked something inside it that only the ship knows about.

"You look terrible," Tamsin said.

"I look *expensive*," Berta corrected. "Eleven days at His Majesty's pleasure, and all it cost was an atlas." She took Tamsin's arm and they walked down toward the water, matching each other's slow pace without discussing it. Her legs were unsteady. She pretended they weren't. "Tell me true, girl. Did you burn it? Did you burn the names?"

"Every one." Tamsin's voice did not waver, but her eyes went somewhere else for a moment — down into herself, where Berta supposed a person keeps a hundred names she can no longer write but will never lose. "I read them until I knew them better than ink could hold them. Then I scorched the margins clean and gave him his blank atlas, and he gave me you, and the town its winter."

"And your Ewan?"

"Across. Alive." A pause. "Gone."

Berta grunted. Gone was what living men were supposed to be, after crossings like that. Gone was the whole point. Still, she heard the ache under the word, the way you hear water under ice.

They reached the bank below the eel sheds, where her own boat sat low and patient on the brown tide — the *Magpie*, tarred and patched, older than both of them put together. Someone had bailed her out. Someone had coiled her lines neat. Berta looked at that coiled rope longer than she looked at anything else that morning.

"Hale," Tamsin said, following her gaze. "He kept her bailed all through your imprisonment. Every morning. He didn't say whose boat it was."

"He'd have been shot for it if anyone had asked."

"He'd have said it was requisitioned."

Berta laughed then — a real laugh, rough as gravel, the first since the night the patrol took her on the bank with her hands still wet from pushing boats off into the dark. It hurt her ribs and she let it hurt. Then she climbed aboard, and her body remembered everything at once: the give of the deck, the tiller's worn smoothness, the particular argument between current and keel at this bend of the river. Eleven days in a cell and the boat took her back without comment, as rivers do.

She worked the mooring line loose and looked up at Tamsin standing on the bank, thin as a reed pen against the grey sky.

"I'm going upriver," Berta said. "Past the Reach, past every place Ashgrave's name is known. There are people on my pages too, girl — people I ferried years ago who owe me and don't know yet that I've come to collect. A woman my age shouldn't keep debts outstanding." She paused, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which on Berta Quill was more startling than shouting. "You could come. There's room."

Tamsin shook her head, and Berta had known she would.

"My work's here. The bridge is here. And there's a boy in the loft above the shop who's got quick hands and no family, and somebody has to teach him which silences matter." Tamsin smiled — small, tired, real. "Go collect your debts, Berta. But leave a mark somewhere I can find. If ever the river brings you back."

Berta considered. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the little knife she'd kept hidden through eleven days of searching that somehow never found it, and cut a notch deep into the *Magpie*'s gunwale — one clean stroke, the old smuggler's sign for *safe landing here*.

"That's your mark," she said. "Cut fresh whenever I pass. You watch for it."

She pushed off. The current took the bow the way an old friend takes an elbow — familiar, unceremonious, glad. And Berta Quill stood at her tiller with the wind finding the scars at her wrists and the town shrinking behind her, and she did not look back, because looking back was for people who intended to return by the same road, and she had never once in her life used the same road twice.

But she did raise one hand, high over her head, held there until the bend of the river took her — so that if the girl on the bank was watching, and she knew the girl was watching, the last sight of her would not be a leaving at all, but a wave.

### II.

Sergeant Hale wrote his resignation the way he had written everything for thirty years: in a fair, obedient hand, with margins ruled true.

*I, Josiah Hale, Sergeant-Clerk, do hereby tender my discharge from His Majesty's service, being of failing health and diminished usefulness...*

Both statements were lies, or near enough. His health was sound. His usefulness had never been greater — Ashgrave had told him so not three weeks ago, in the map room, with something close to warmth: *Hale, you are the only man in this garrison who understands that paper is the true soldier of this army.* And that was precisely the trouble. Hale had spent the winter understanding paper. He understood the requisition ledgers, the movement logs, the interrogation transcripts. He understood, better than any man in Marlow's Reach, exactly what his fair and obedient hand had done — and the one entry he had falsified, a single line in December to cover a woman's absence from her bed, weighed on him now like all the honest ones combined.

That was the discovery that had undone him, quietly, over months: that conscience was not a thing a man possessed but a thing he was issued, and that the army had requisitioned his along with the town's boats and grain and maps. He wanted it back. Discharge was the only requisition form for that.

Ashgrave received the letter in the map room, standing over the great table where Vane's blank-margined atlas lay open under lamplight. The colonel had won his winter and lost his quarry, and Hale had watched the arithmetic of it hollow him out by degrees. A man who hunts shadows cannot retire on a blank page.

"The town," Ashgrave said, not looking up. "You mean to stay in the town."

"I do, sir."

"On what trade? Your clerk's hand is army property. It doesn't transfer."

Hale had thought about this. He had, in fact, thought of very little else. "There's a schoolmaster's post vacant since the requisitions emptied the parish. Letters and figures for the mill children. I'd keep my hand busy, sir. Only on different paper."

Now Ashgrave did look up, and for a long moment the two of them regarded each other across the atlas — the occupier and the clerk, the hunter and the man who had quietly, with one inked line, helped the quarry vanish. Did the colonel know? Hale had turned it over a thousand times. There had been no accusation, no arrest, no cold inquiry at lamplight. Either Hale's forgery had been perfect or Ashgrave had chosen, for reasons of his own, to log nothing. Some silences, Hale had learned, were not empty. They were full to the brim.

"The schoolmaster's post," Ashgrave said at last, "pays eight pounds a year."

"Yes, sir."

"I draw forty times that, and I am not certain I have ever slept as well as a village schoolmaster sleeps." He closed the atlas with two fingers, gently, as one closes a coffin. "Your resignation is accepted, Sergeant. Effective when your relief arrives. Which will be spring, given the roads." He returned to his papers, and Hale saluted, and was three steps from the door when the voice came again — quieter, stripped of rank, almost human.

"The woman who sold eels. She cut a notch in her gunwale as she left. My sentries reported it. Do you know what it means?"

Hale stopped. He could lie; lying had become easy and that too was part of the sickness. Instead he said: "It means safe landing, sir. For whoever needs it."

A long silence. Then, from behind him, in a voice Hale would remember the rest of his life — flat, tired, envious beyond admitting: "Dismissed."

He walked out of the mill into the late light and down through the town he had occupied all winter and never once seen until now: the crooked lanes, the smoke rising from chimneys where families cooked suppers he had inventoried but never tasted, the river running silver-brown under the rebuilt footbridge at Gallow Ford. At the bridge he stopped. Below, moored in the lee of the bank, sat the eel-woman's boat, empty, riding at single anchor, waiting out the winter the way boats do.

And there on the gunwale, plain as print to anyone who knew the language, the notch. Cut fresh.

He stood a long time watching it, a man learning to read all over again.

### III.

No letter came from the far bank. Tamsin had not expected one — letters could be opened, and opened letters killed people — but knowing a thing in her head and knowing it in the chest at lamplight were different countries, and all that winter she crossed between them nightly.

The atlas was gone to Ashgrave, blank and honest and emptied of everyone she loved. The bridge stood. The town slept uneasily but slept. And in the workshop above the street, with the orphan boy Pip grinding pigments in the next room and whistling tunelessly through the gap in his teeth, Tamsin Thackeray began the slow work of not waiting for news.

Then, on a raw morning in the last week of February, she came down to the Ford to check the winter's wear on the pilings and found the rowan.

It lay on the third plank of the bridge — the plank Berta called the threshold, the one you stepped onto coming home. A sprig of rowan, red berries still clinging despite the season, bound at the cut stem with a strip of tartan thread gone grey with river damp. No note. No seal. No hand she could name. Just rowan — which grew wild on no ground within thirty miles of Marlow's Reach, and which grew thick as prayer on the Corrie land beyond the hills, where Ewan's mother had planted it row upon row after the old rebellions, berries for the dead and branches for the doors.

She picked it up. The wood was freshly cut; the sap had barely dried at the stem. Someone had stood on this bridge within hours — had crossed it, in fact, coming from the far bank, walking the beams she had raised with Berta in the fog, and had left nothing behind but this and taken nothing away but the knowledge that the crossing held.

He had come himself. He had stood where she was standing. Perhaps in daylight; perhaps, more wisely, in the blue hour before dawn, with the mist on the water. Had he watched the workshop window? Had he seen her candle burning late and known whose it was, the way she had always known his whistle across a crowded market? She looked down the length of the bridge, plank by plank, reading it now the way she read any surface — for marks, for weight, for story. And there, pressed into the frost-slick timber of the far end, half-faded already: the print of a boot, narrow-heeled, highland-made, pointed toward the north shore.

One set of prints. Going away. That was the whole letter, and it said what letters must not: *I crossed. I live. I do not turn around.*

Tamsin stood on the bridge a long while, the rowan sprig cupped in both hands like something lit. She did not weep. She had spent fifteen years learning from Aldous Vane that a mapmaker's business was precision, and she found she could be precise about this too: the exact shape of the loss, the exact coordinates of the gratitude, the exact distance between *gone* and *safe*, which on the best charts of the heart were the same point marked in different inks.

She carried the rowan back up the hill. In the workshop she did not press it in a book — books could be seized, shelves requisitioned, floors pulled up; she had proven all of that at cost. Instead she threaded the stem through the clasp of the locket that held her mother's portrait, where it would ride against her skin, unrequisitionable, uncensorable, hers.

Pip looked up from his mortar. "What's the rowan for, miss?"

"It means someone made it home," she said.

"All that, off one twig?"

"All that off one twig." She sat down at her drafting table, where a new sheet of paper stretched clean as January snow, and pinned its corners flat. The new atlas. Blank margins. Honest lines. Somewhere out beyond the hills a man was carrying her silence in his chest the same way she carried his, and neither of them needed ink to keep it.

She dipped her pen. Outside, the clock above the market hall struck ten, and the river ran on under the bridge, taking the winter away south.

"For those who crossed," she said softly — not writing it yet, just trying the words against the air — and set the first line down.
