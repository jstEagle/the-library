## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea at Vantage Point was cold enough to make a woman honest, and Mirabel Montrose had learned to hold her breath against both.

She went down on the ebb, when the shallows off Gullrock ran four fathoms and the light came through in long gray columns. Down there the world was simple: shell and stone and the pale flash of an oyster's lip. The Meridian Trading Company paid by the pearl, weighed to the grain, and it did not pay a woman to think while she worked. Thinking used air.

So she kept her mind as empty as her lungs, and she brought up oysters in her net bag until the cold pressed its thumbs into her temples, and then she rose because staying under longer would kill her, not because she wanted what was above.

Above was fog. Fog and the company wharf, where Osei the tally-keeper waited with his ledger and his scale and his way of looking at divers like sacks of grain that had learned to swim.

"Light day," he said when she emptied her bag onto the boards.

"Dark water." She kept her voice flat, a laborer's voice, unlettered and incurious. "Oysters are sulking."

"Oysters don't sulk."

"Then they're hoarding." She wiped her hair back with one hand. "Everything hoards something, Mr. Osei. Even you."

The corner of his mouth moved, which for Osei amounted to laughter. He weighed the catch, called out the grain-count to his clerk, and marked Mirabel's column in the ledger — *M. Montrose, indent., no. 44*. Forty-four. She had been forty-four since she was sixteen years old, when her father signed them both over to the company to cover a debt he'd been fool enough to owe and honest enough to admit. Seven years now. Her hands had gone from girl's hands to diver's hands somewhere in the middle of it, knuckles like walrus ivory, and every season the contract renewed itself the way tide renews itself, without anyone's permission being asked.

She climbed the cliff path to the drying sheds as the fog thickened toward evening. The trading post sat between two empires the way a bone sits between two dogs — claimed by both, fed by neither, useful only so long as neither noticed how good it tasted. Company flags on the factor's house, imperial gunboats passing once a month in the strait, and between them the small economy of divers and salt-boilers and lighthouse men who pretended not to see which side of the water anything came from.

Her father was in the shed, mending nets, his fingers slow now with the rheumatism. He looked up when she came in, and looked down again quickly, and she knew why. There was a book under his knee. A cheap thing, sea-swollen, a book of poems someone had left in a barrel of salvaged goods, and he'd been trying to pick out words from it the way a man picks out fish bones — patiently, wrongly.

He hid it before she could speak. Neither of them said anything about it. That was the arrangement they'd had for seven years: she read everything and admitted nothing. She read the manifests posted on the wharf. She read the notices nailed to the company door — *wages adjusted*, *divers cautioned*, *by order of the Resident Factor* — and she read the letters other people got from home, reading their faces while she read their mail over their shoulders, and she kept all of it folded up inside herself like a chart of waters no one must know she could navigate. An indentured girl who could read was not an indentured girl anymore. She was a problem. Problems got sold south, or worse.

So she was illiterate, loudly, cheerfully, whenever anyone asked, and she made her mark with a cross on the wage sheets, and once, memorably, she had "misread" nothing at all and let a foreman laugh at her confusion until he stopped watching her entirely.

But her father's poems. Those she read at night, by feel, in the dark of their hut, her finger tracing the shapes while he slept. It was a small treason. She allowed herself small treasons. They were the only property she owned.

* * *

It was on the third night after, in the hour when the fog came right into the rooms and made lanterns useless, that the storm arrived.

It came off the strait with no manners, flattening the salt-grass, driving the fishing boats in shrieking. Mirabel was awake anyway — she slept badly in storms, some old instinct insisting that weather was a debt collector come calling — and she heard the wreck before anyone raised an alarm: timber on rock, a sound like a giant's knuckle cracking, then voices, thin and torn apart by wind.

She went out into it. She never decided to; her feet simply went, down the cliff path to the shingle below Gullrock, where the company boat *Providence* — no irony intended by anyone but God — was breaking itself into interesting pieces on the outer rocks.

Lanterns bobbed along the shore. Men from the post waded hip-deep, hauling survivors out of the surf. The coxswain was already dead; they laid him on the stones with his cap over his face and turned immediately back to the living, because that is what shore people do, grief is a luxury you schedule for later.

Mirabel took a line and went into the water twice. The second time, the sea handed her a man.

He was not from the *Providence* — wrong clothes, no company wool, a coat of some foreign cut gone black with brine. He was half drowned, lips blue, and he was clutching something to his chest with both arms the way a child clutches a stolen loaf, and when she and two others dragged him up the shingle he fought them, weakly, ferociously, making sounds that were not words yet.

"The packet," he managed at last, teeth chattering hard enough to break the word in half. "The packet — don't let — it goes to the light — the lighthouse keeper —"

"Hush," Mirabel said. "You're ashore. Nothing more wants you tonight."

His eyes found hers. They were fever-bright even through the salt and exhaustion, and they were frightened in a way that had nothing to do with drowning. Frightened of people. Of this shore full of helpful people with their lanterns.

"You understand," he whispered, gripping her wrist. "You understand me. The others — they'll take it to the factor — it can't go to the factor —"

She understood him perfectly. That was the first mistake of her life, or the continuation of an old one.

"I've got you," she said. "Both of you. The packet and the rest."

Then the factor's men arrived with a stretcher and authority, and the stranger's eyes rolled back, and he went limp as kelp, still holding on.

* * *

They put him in the infirmary hut at the end of the row, the one that smelled of carbolic and old storms, and by midnight he was burning with fever, which solved everyone's questions for the moment because a delirious man can't answer any. The factor's clerk came, looked at the stranger's soaked papers — ship's papers, water-blurred, names illegible — shrugged in the way of a man paid too little for curiosity, and left word that the factor would want a statement when the fellow could talk.

Mirabel volunteered for the night watch. No one questioned it; she was known as steady, and steady women get given watches nobody else wants.

Alone with the fevered man and the storm hammering the roof, she pried the oilskin packet gently from his arms.

She told herself she was only keeping it safe. She was a liar even alone, apparently.

Oilskin keeps out seawater but not intent. The knot gave. Inside: a bundle of letters, tied with tarred cord, wrapped around a small brass compass whose needle swung wild, as if it had been dropped, or as if the world it pointed at had been.

She held the letters to the lantern. And stopped breathing.

She knew that hand.

Seven years ago, in another port, in another life, a tall man with a captain's bearing and ink-stained fingers had written a receipt for eleven hundred pounds sterling — *debt of Tomas Montrose, discharged in full* — and had crouched to her level, a girl of sixteen holding the paper like it might bite, and had said: *Your father will tell you he's ashamed. Tell him shame is just interest paid on pride, and I waive it.* Then he'd smiled, and she had spent seven years not forgetting the smile, or the hand that had written above his signature a single flourish like a wave breaking.

Elias Thorne. Captain Elias Thorne, who had bought her father free of the moneylenders for reasons no one ever explained and who had gone to war three years later and who had been reported drowned — officially, in dispatches, in the gazette her father couldn't read and she pretended she couldn't either — dead these eighteen months.

Dead men do not date their correspondence.

The topmost letter was dated six weeks past. The handwriting staggered a little, as if written in a moving boat or a shaking hand, but it was his. She knew the way the T crossed like a spar, the way the h descended like an anchor line.

Mirabel sat very still in the lamplight while the stranger muttered and tossed, and outside the storm began, at last, to spend itself against the coast.

She should wake someone. She should put the packet back and forget the shape of the letters, fold herself back into illiteracy like a garment, be forty-four again tomorrow, safe and owned and blind.

She broke the seal instead.

*To the keeper of the light at Vantage Point — if you are still the man I trust, and if God has not finally collected on my many debts —*

The first lines swam. Not from tears; she did not permit tears. From the sheer vertigo of it, a drowned man writing to her coastline, fate reaching down through four fathoms of dark water and taking her by the ankle.

She read on, and the storm died, and dawn came up gray on the fog, and Mirabel Montrose — diver, daughter, indenture number forty-four, a woman who could not read — sat in the infirmary holding a traitor's letters in a drowned captain's hand, and understood that her quiet life had just ended, and that some treacherous chamber of her heart had already begun, quietly, terribly, to sing.
