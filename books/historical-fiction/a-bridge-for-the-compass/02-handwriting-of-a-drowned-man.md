## 2. Handwriting of a Drowned Man

The stranger burned through the night, and Mirabel sat up with him because no one else would.

She'd smuggled him into the drying shed behind the oyster mounds, wrapped in the sailcloth she was supposed to have washed and returned. His name, when he could give one, was Ansel Corve. He said it the way a man says a word he's practiced lying with. Everything else he said made less sense. He spoke of a ship called the *Vespergate*, of a storm that came sideways, of men in the water who stopped shouting too quickly. Then his fever climbed and the words dissolved into a language of their own.

Mirabel worked by feel — wet cloth to his brow, broth spooned careful against his cracked lips when he could swallow. Twice he seized her wrist with startling strength and pulled her close enough that she could smell brine and gun oil on him.

"The lighthouse keeper," he rasped. "You have to take them to the lighthouse keeper. Swear it. Swear it on whatever you've got."

"I don't know any lighthouse keeper."

"You do." His eyes opened, glassy and black in the lamplight. "Everyone here does. Old man. North point." Then he shuddered and went under again, and his grip loosened, and Mirabel sat back on her heels with her heart going like a diver's pulse.

There *was* a lighthouse at the north point. Everyone knew it, and everyone knew better than to speak of it. The company had decommissioned it years ago, but the light still turned some nights, patient as a held breath, and no one asked why. Asking questions at Meridian Post was how you ended up reassigned to the shark grounds.

Near dawn, the fever broke. Corve slept like a drowned thing come ashore, and beside him lay the oilskin packet he hadn't let go of even unconscious — she'd had to pry it from fingers stiff as coral.

She should have left it alone.

She told herself she only meant to check it for water damage.

The knot fought her. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, were letters — seven of them, bound with tarred cord — and a small brass compass with a hinged lid, its needle swimming drunk in its housing. She lifted the first letter into the lamplight and the world tilted.

She knew that hand.

She had seen it exactly once, six years ago, on a receipt written in her father's factor's office — the receipt that said *paid in full*, the paper that had kept her father out of the debt-yard and Mirabel out of the sorting cribs until she came of age and the company found other uses for her. Captain Elias Thorne had written it himself rather than trust a clerk, and he'd signed it with a small flourish under the *T*, like a wave breaking. She remembered staring at it, at the age of fourteen, understanding for the first time that writing was not just marks but a voice — that this man's hand sounded calm, and certain, and kind.

Seven letters, all in that hand. All dated after the *Vespergate* war dispatches had reported Captain Elias Thorne lost with all hands.

Her own heartbeat was suddenly the loudest thing in the shed.

Corve stirred. She slid the packet back beneath the sailcloth and put her hands in her lap like a good girl, like an illiterate girl, and waited.

But later, when the post was still gray and asleep, she took them out again. She read all seven by the light of a shuttered lamp, reading fast and then slow, then some passages a third time, because what they said refused to fit inside her skull on the first pass.

Thorne was alive. Thorne had survived whatever the war had done to his ship and his crew, and had been court-martialed in absentia for refusing an order — the word *order* underlined twice, the ink pressed hard enough to score the page. A garrison town whose name she'd only ever heard whispered. A civilian quarter. An order given, and Thorne's refusal, and what had followed anyway while he stood down his own marines at pistol-point. The letters were apologies, of a kind — to a wife in the second, a brother in the fourth, to someone called only *K.* in the sixth, whom he addressed with a tenderness that made Mirabel close the letter and breathe for a while before continuing. Not love letters. Confessions. He was writing to everyone he'd failed, arranging his soul before whatever came next.

The seventh letter was different. It was addressed to no one. It began: *To whoever is carrying these —*

*— you are risking more than I can repay. The keeper at the north light will know what to do. Do not let the company take these papers. They will hang me with them or hang you beside me. There is a bridge at low tide and a boat beyond it, and if I am dead by the time you read this, burn everything and forget my name, which the empire has already forgotten.*

Mirabel read the last line four times. Then she folded the letters along their original creases, retied the cord, and sat in the dark with her palms flat on her knees.

Dread was the wrong word for what moved in her chest, though dread was part of it. It was dread braided through with something older — the memory of a receipt, of a wave-breaking *T*, of her father weeping without shame in a doorway because a stranger had unchained their family for nothing but decency. She had promised herself, at fourteen, that if that man ever needed anything from her — anything — she would pay it without counting.

She had assumed the debt would never come due. Drowned men don't collect.

* * *

The factor came to the drying sheds two days later, which was two days sooner than anyone expected.

His name was Aldous Renwick, and he had the soft hands and hard eyes of a man promoted past his talents into his cruelty. He wore the company's gray like a skin he'd grown into. He walked the oyster mounds with a walking stick he didn't need, tapping the shells as he passed, and stopped where Mirabel was culling undersized catch with the other divers.

"Montrose." He never used first names; names were for people, contracts were for assets. "They're saying your skiff was first on the wreck."

"My sister-divers say so, sir."

"Don't preen. It was luck, not virtue." He tapped a shell off the mound into the mud. "The *Vespergate* was carrying mail. Official correspondence. The naval office at the strait wants a full accounting — every packet, every sack, every scrap of paper recovered. You understand how it is. Paper is expensive. Paper is dangerous."

"Yes, sir."

"A survivor was pulled from the water." He said it lightly, watching the mud. "A man. Some of the fishermen talk. Fishermen talk too much, in my experience. Did you see a man, Montrose?"

Mirabel kept her eyes on the shells. This was the moment — she knew it even as she lived it, the way you know a riptide by the cold spot in the current. One sentence about a fevered stranger in the drying shed and she was done; the company would take him, and the letters, and her, and probably her father's old bones out of the pension rolls besides.

"No, sir," she said. "Only bodies."

It was the first lie she had ever told a man in a gray coat, and it tasted strangely clean.

Renwick's stick paused mid-tap. He looked at her then, properly, the way he looked at ledgers. "Bodies wash up on their own schedule. Papers don't. If anything should... surface, you'll bring it to me before you bring it anywhere. Before God, before your father, before your own mother's ghost. To me. Am I understood?"

"Yes, sir."

He smiled without warmth and moved on down the mounds, tapping shells, and Mirabel went back to culling with hands that did not shake until she let them.

That night she checked on Corve. His fever was lower; he watched her with clearer eyes and said nothing, but when she set the oilskin packet on the crate beside his cot, his gaze went to it and then to her face, and something in his expression shifted — the assessment of a man recalculating odds.

"You read," he said quietly. Not a question.

Mirabel didn't deny it. Denying it felt, suddenly, like part of the old life — the crouched life, the smaller life, the life where she made herself worth owning by being worth less.

"The keeper at the north point," she said. "I'll take them tomorrow. When the fog sits low."

Corve exhaled, long and slow, as though he'd been holding that breath since the wreck. "He'll want to know one thing from you. Only one. He'll ask if you can be trusted." His eyes held hers. "What will you tell him?"

Mirabel thought of the wave-breaking *T*. Of a receipt that had bought a family its whole future for the price of one man's kindness. Of Renwick's stick tapping the shells, counting things that weren't his.

She thought: *the tide brought you to me twice now, Elias Thorne. Once as ink. Once as a promise.*

"Tell him," she said, rising, taking up the packet and tucking it beneath her coat against her heart, "that I'm the daughter of a man your captain freed, and that I pay what I owe."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the strait, thick as wool, and somewhere north a light would be turning.
