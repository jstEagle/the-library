## 3. What the Tide Owes

**MIRABEL**

The lighthouse stood on the far spit of rock, a white finger in the fog, and Mirabel had walked past it ten thousand times without once climbing to the door. Tonight she climbed it. The keeper, old Sable with his salt-cured eyes, asked no questions — he only stepped aside and let her pass, which told her he had already been paid in something heavier than coin.

Thorne was sitting up when she reached the lamp room.

He was thinner than the man in her memory, greyer at the temples, and there was a healing cut along his jaw that he'd tried to shave around and failed. But the eyes were the same — the steady, patient grey that had looked across her father's debt table seven years ago and seen her, not the debt. He had a blanket over his shoulders like a man wearing borrowed authority badly.

"You read them," he said.

Not *hello*. Not *thank God you came*. She should have known.

"I read them," she said.

"Then you know I'm dead."

"I know they say so." She kept her voice level, though her hands wanted to shake. "I also know the Meridian factor is asking questions about the wreck, and that the man who answered the courier's delirium was me, and that if anyone traces those letters to this tower, Sable hangs beside you and I hang between you."

He flinched at that. Good, she thought. Let him flinch.

"Mirabel." Her name in his mouth was slower than she remembered, as if he were tasting it for rot. "I didn't send for you. I told the boy to find the keeper and no one else."

"He washed up half-drowned on our rocks. The sea makes its own deliveries."

"Then the sea has made a mistake, and I'll correct it." He pushed himself straighter. "I don't want rescue, Mirabel. I want your silence. That's all. You never saw the letters. You never saw me. In a fortnight I'll be gone over the strait and the company will still own your contract, and none of it will have touched you."

She laughed — a short, ugly sound she didn't recognize as her own.

"Touched me. Captain Thorne, do you imagine anything hasn't touched me? I dive their oyster beds until my hands crack. I hide that I can read because an educated indentured girl draws men like flies to a wound. I have spent nine years being *touched* by everything the Meridian owns, and you stand there wrapped in Sable's blanket telling me silence will keep me safe?"

"That's precisely what I'm telling you."

"Then you're a coward."

The word landed. She watched it land, watched his face go carefully blank in the way of men who had learned that expression was ammunition.

"Perhaps," he said quietly. "I refused one order. One. And they branded me traitor and shot my crew on the beach at Saltao while I swam. I've been dead for three years, Mirabel, and dead men keep other people alive by staying quiet. If you speak my name aloud — if you so much as look at this tower twice — they will take you apart to get to me. Your father's freedom is a signature away from annulment. Don't ask me to spend you too."

"I'm not asking you to spend me." She crossed the small room, close enough now to see the fever still banked behind his eyes. "I'm telling you what I already am. I carried your letters through a company checkpoint sewn into my petticoat. I lied to a guard's face about where I was going. There is no version of me left that stays untouched, Elias. There's only the version that chooses."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below them the fog pressed against the glass, and the great lamp turned its slow wheel of light through it, sweeping the strait like a lighthouse searching for something it had lost.

"The bridge," he said finally. "The old causeway at low tide. It's the only way over that isn't watched — the company thinks it's drowned at high water and impassable at low. They're wrong on both counts, but barely." His hand found hers, rough palm against rough palm, two divers' hands. "If it goes wrong—"

"When it goes wrong," she said, "you'll want me on the far side of it anyway. So stop arguing and start planning."

* * *

**THORNE**

He remembered her father's kitchen: the smell of tar and boiled fish, the ledger open on the table, and the girl of fifteen standing behind her father's chair with her chin up, watching Thorne write off the debt with the expression of someone memorizing every letter. He'd thought then: *that one will not stay bought.* He'd thought it with fondness. He thought it now with fear.

Three years dead taught a man patience. He had lain in ditches in Saltao; he had held his breath in a cargo hold while soldiers walked the deck above; he had learned to wait the way stone waits. But nothing in the war had prepared him for the sound of Mirabel Montrose lying.

**MIRABEL**

It happened two mornings later, in the factor's office, with the fog thick enough to chew.

Crake was not a large man, but he had the company's gift for making rooms feel smaller than they were. His desk was immaculate. On it, when Mirabel was shown in, lay a manifest, a pair of iron-rimmed spectacles, and — her heart stopped — the courier's coat, dried stiff and spread open like a gutted bird.

"The pockets," Crake said, "were empty."

"Was there money in them?" she asked. "That's theft, certainly. Terrible."

"There was an oilskin packet." He removed his spectacles and polished them with terrible patience. "The survivors' statements mention it. The courier himself, before his fever took him under, mentioned it. And yet it did not wash ashore, did not turn up among the wreck salvage, and does not appear in any of my records." He set the spectacles back on. "You were first on the rocks, Montrose. First on the rocks, and the packet is gone."

"The sea took it."

"The sea," Crake said, "is remarkably selective. It takes oilskin packets and leaves boots. It takes letters and leaves men." He rose, came around the desk, and stood close enough that she could smell the ink on him. "Here is what I think. I think a girl who dives all day found a waterproof thing that floats. I think she hid it, or sold it, or gave it to someone she shouldn't have. And I think you're going to tell me which."

Every lesson of her nine years said: *lower your eyes. Stammer. Be small.* She had made herself small so long she knew the architecture of it like a diver knows the bottom.

She lifted her chin instead.

"If I'd found it," she said, "I'd have turned it in for the reward. Everyone knows the company pays for salvage. Only a fool hides salvage from Crake."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not belief. Interest. He circled her slowly, the way she imagined a man circles a net to check for holes.

"Your father's contract," he said. "Seven years commuted to four for good service. A signature from freedom. You know what happens to a contract when the holder is convicted of theft against the company? It doesn't end. It *transfers*. Debtor's kin assume the balance. Your father would die in these beds, Montrose, and you'd dive until your lungs filled, and the ledger would call it fair."

"I know how ledgers work."

"Do you?" He stopped in front of her. "Then tell me again. Where is the packet?"

"The sea took it," she said, "or whoever searched the bodies before the surf took *them* took it. Ask your own beach gang. Ask why your salvage master reported the courier's kit bag empty before the company seal was ever broken." She was inventing it as she spoke, building the lie plank by plank over black water, and hating how easily it bore her weight. "But you already know that, don't you. Otherwise you wouldn't need me. You'd have arrested me."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the tide was turning; she could feel it in the floor's old timbers, the way she felt it in her bones before a dive.

Crake smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all week.

"You lie well, Montrose," he said. "Better than an indentured girl should." He returned to his desk, opened the manifest, and made a small mark with his pen — she would think about that mark for days. "The post is being searched. Every bunk, every boat, every bed of kelp we can drag. If the packet surfaces anywhere near your hands, the contract transfers. Understand?"

"Perfectly," she said, and walked out on legs that did not tremble until the fog closed behind her and she was alone with what she'd done.

She had lied. Not the small lies of survival — the hidden book, the lowered eyes — but a lie faced into power, defended, doubled down on. And beneath the terror, unmistakable as pearl under nacre, was something else.

Relief.

They were searching the post. Which meant they hadn't found the lighthouse yet. Which meant she had perhaps two days — one tide, maybe less — before Crake's dragnets dragged the far spit of rock.

Low tide came at moonrise tomorrow.

She turned toward the chandlery to steal rope, and toward the factor's strongroom beyond it, where a certain folded paper with her father's name and her own lived in a locked drawer — the one document in the post she intended to see burn.
