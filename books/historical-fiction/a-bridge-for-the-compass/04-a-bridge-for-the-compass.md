## 4. A Bridge for the Compass

They came for her at dawn, or near enough to dawn that the fog still held the post in its grey fist. Mirabel heard the boots before she saw them—six men moving through the compound in the factor's tight, economical rhythm, checking the drying sheds, the shell heaps, the long barracks where the divers slept off their aches.

She was already awake. She had not truly slept since the lighthouse.

The factor's name was Grell, and he had spent three days asking his questions with the patience of a man who already knew the answers. The missing packet. The wreck on the rocks. The fevered stranger who had been carried, by whom no one could say, out of the infirmary before first light. Grell did not raise his voice. That was what made him dangerous. He stood in the doorway of her father's hut with his hands folded behind him and said, "Your father's debt stands at eleven years' service, Miss Montrose. Eleven years. A man can be freed from that, if circumstances recommend it."

"And if they don't?"

"Then he dives until the sea takes him, same as any of them." He smiled the way a ledger smiles. "You're an intelligent girl. Intelligent girls don't steal from the Meridian Trading Company."

"I haven't stolen anything."

"Of course not." He let the silence do its work and left.

Mirabel sat with her hands flat against the floorboards until she stopped shaking. Then she got up, wrapped a shawl over her hair, and went to find the factor's office.

* * *

It was theft now, whatever Grell chose to call it later. Her own papers—her contract, signed at fourteen by a father drowning in debt he had not incurred alone, the ink of her mark pressed into the page like a wound. They lived in the iron cabinet in Grell's office, third drawer, beneath the wage rolls and the shipping manifests, and Mirabel knew this because she had dusted that office for two years and never once let her eyes linger where lingering would cost her.

Tonight she lingered.

Grell took his supper at the mess at eight o'clock and drank after it, and the office stood dark and unlocked because Grell believed fear was a better lock than iron. Mirabel waited until the last lamp in the barracks guttered out. Then she crossed the yard with bare feet on cold boards, let herself in through the door she'd oiled weeks ago without knowing why—no, she thought, I knew why; I have known since I read his handwriting—and knelt at the cabinet in the dark.

The lock yielded to the hairpin she'd kept in her braid for three days. The drawer slid open with a whisper. Her fingers found the paper by touch: heavy stock, company watermark, her father's signature above her own small mark.

She took hers only. Her father's she left, though it cut her like leaving him behind, because taking both would tell Grell everything before she was ready, and because her father's freedom would have to be won another way—a way that began tonight, on a causeway, under a burning signal.

Outside, the fog had thinned. Through the office window she could see the old tower standing black against the water, and beyond it, far out where the tide was falling, the dark spine of the causeway beginning to surface stone by stone like something rising from a dream.

* * *

The tower's lamp room smelled of oil and salt and forty years of keepers. Mirabel climbed the spiral stair with her papers pressed against her chest, and at the top she found the paraffin, the striker, and a view of the whole strait: the post below, the water silvering under a half-hidden moon, and the far shore a smudge of darkness where no lanterns burned yet.

She thought of Thorne's face when she'd told him about the letters, there in the keeper's dim kitchen—the way he'd looked at her as if she were something risen from deep water, unexpected and shining. *"I asked you for silence,"* he'd said. *"Not for this."*

"You asked me for nothing," she'd answered. "That was your mistake."

Now she unfolded the indenture papers on the lamp room floor. Fourteen years of her life, itemized. Wages owed, wages deducted, clauses in language designed to be unreadable to someone who wasn't supposed to read at all. She had hidden her literacy for nine years so that no one would look too closely at her, so that an educated girl wouldn't draw the attention of men who collected people like cargo. And here was the proof of what that hiding had cost, written down and countersigned and stamped with the company seal.

She struck the match.

The paper caught reluctantly, then all at once, curling into orange bloom. Mirabel held it up to the lamp room's great seaward glass and moved it slowly, once, twice, three times—the signal they had agreed upon, three passes of flame, repeated—and watched the fire eat her name off the page.

Below, on the dark shore across the strait, a light answered. Once. Twice. Three times.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside her chest something was not steady at all—it was breaking open, the way ice breaks in spring, all at once and irreversibly.

* * *

Low tide came at half past one, and with it the causeway.

The old bridge was older than either empire that claimed these waters—fishermen's stones laid before anyone thought to tax a crossing, running from the rocks below the tower out toward the far shore in a long, broken arc that drowned at high water and surfaced, twice a day, like a confession. At its lowest ebb the crest stood a hand's width above the waves, slick with weed, treacherous as a lie.

Mirabel went down the tower stair and out onto the rocks, and Thorne was already there, waiting at the first stone, a canvas bag over his shoulder and the wind pulling at his coat. Up close, in the thin moonlight, she could see how the wound had marked him—he moved guarding his left side, and his face was thinner than the man in her memory—but his eyes were the same eyes that had stood in her father's doorway seven years ago and said, *The debt is paid. Tell your daughter she owes nothing.*

"Mirabel." He said her name like something he'd been given back. "You shouldn't have come. If they take you—"

"They won't take me." She stepped past him onto the first wet stone. "There's nothing left to take."

He understood. She watched him understand—watched his gaze go to the smoke-smell still clinging to her shawl, to the ash flecks caught in her sleeve—and something in his face eased and sharpened at the same time.

"The papers," he said quietly.

"Burned. All of it." She lifted her chin. "I'm not indentured, Captain. I'm not owned. Whatever happens next, I choose it."

Behind them, up the slope, a lantern bloomed in the compound. Then another. Voices carried thin across the water—Grell's men, spilling out of the barracks, fanning toward the shore.

"They've found the empty cabinet," Thorne said. "Move."

They moved. The crossing was everything the old fishermen warned it was—stones furred with weed that gave like rotten flesh, gaps where the tide sucked and gurgled in the dark seams, wind pushing crosswise with the indifference of weather. Halfway out, Mirabel's foot skated and Thorne's hand closed around her arm, hard, sure, and held.

"I've got you," he said.

"No," she said, finding her footing. "I've got me. But thank you."

And she meant it—that was the miracle of it, that she could mean it. Seven years she had dreamed of this man as a rescue, as the answer to a sum she couldn't solve alone. Now she stood on a drowning bridge between two empires with nothing to her name but a literate mind and a free hand, and she found she did not need rescuing. She needed passage. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

The lanterns swept the water behind them. She heard Grell's voice, cracked thin by distance and wind, shouting orders at the shoreline—*the causeway, the causeway, they'll take the causeway*—but the tide was turning, the water climbing the lower stones already, and the searchers would have to come out onto the bridge itself or watch their quarry walk into the dark. Two of them started out, boots slipping, lanterns swinging wild shadows across the foam.

"Faster," Thorne said.

They ran the last hundred yards with the sea grabbing at their heels, spray cold as judgment, and then the far shore rose up to meet them—shingle and scrub pine and, drawn up among the trees where no patrol would think to look, a boat. Small, patched, rigged for two. Thorne had prepared for himself alone; she could see that in its proportions, its single spare coat rolled beneath the thwart. He looked at her over it, and for a moment the old courtesy warred with something newer in his face.

"There's room," he said. "There's always room. But I want you to say it plainly, Mirabel. Not out of gratitude for the debt. Not out of anything you think you owe me. Do you come?"

Mirabel looked back once, across the strait. The lanterns were still sweeping the water, small and furious and far away. Somewhere over there her father slept in the barracks, owing eleven more years to men who measured lives in ledgers, and her heart broke for him and hardened for her in the same breath.

*I'll come back for you,* she promised silently. *With paper, with witnesses, with whatever it takes. You taught me to read the world, Papa. Now watch me write in it.*

"I come freely," she said aloud. "That's the whole point."

She took the tiller when he offered it—she knew boats better than he did, and he knew it—and they pushed off into the black water as the first searcher's lantern finally reached the end of the causeway behind them, too late, its light finding only rising tide where two people had been.

The current took them. The strait opened ahead, wide and star-scattered and going wherever she pointed them.

Thorne settled beside her in the stern, close enough that his shoulder touched hers. "Where to?" he asked. "I know ports that will take a branded man, if we're careful. And a woman with no papers at all is freer than a woman with the wrong ones—we can make you new ones. Good ones." A pause. "Ones that say whatever name you choose."

Mirabel turned the tiller and felt the boat answer, alive under her hand, obedient to nothing but the water and her will.

"New name," she said, tasting the words like clean air after years underwater. "New papers. New everything." Ahead, the horizon was beginning to show the first grey seam of morning. "But Captain—one thing we keep."

"What's that?"

She reached into her bodice and drew out the last thing she had saved from the fire—not her contract, but the letter, his letter, the one dated six months after the world said he was dead. The edges were scorched but the words were whole.

"This," she said. "Proof that drowned men come back. We'll need the practice."

Thorne laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the wreck—and the sound went out over the water like a light answering a light.

Far behind them, on the shore they'd left, a bell began to ring the alarm. It faded with every stroke of the oars, growing smaller, until it was only another sound the sea made, and then not even that.

The tide was coming in behind them. Ahead, the whole day waited, unowned.
