## 1. The Question on the Form

The census man arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day the silver was polished, and I have often thought since that he chose it deliberately. A man who wanted to see a house at its most exposed should come when every drawer is open and every surface bare, when the household's small economies are laid out on baize like organs on a table.

His name was Mr. Prewitt, and he had the look of a man who had learned early that questions were a form of currency. He stood on the front steps in a coat too heavy for April, holding his leather satchel against his hip the way a vicar holds a Bible, and he smiled at me with all the warmth of an invoice.

"Elodie Vane," he said, reading from a card before I could offer it. "Driver and gatekeeper. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"Lady Rosamund Hale, proprietor. And the household list — you have it prepared?"

"Mrs. Ashcombe has it in the kitchen. If you'll come round to the service entrance, sir, we keep the counting there. Her ladyship's orders."

His smile did not waver, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts when a stone is dropped in a place you thought was shallow. "Her ladyship's orders," he repeated, and wrote something down. I would learn, over the weeks that followed, to dread that little book of his more than any document in the house.

* * *

The census had been announced in the papers for a month, and Lady Rosamund had spoken of it the way she spoke of most instruments of the state — with a kind of amused contempt, as though Parliament were a particularly tiresome nephew. "They will count us like spoons," she said over breakfast, buttering her toast with the decisive strokes she used for everything. "And they will be no wiser about the country than the spoons would be. Fill in the form, Elodie, and let us hear no more about it."

But I had heard about it. I had heard about it in the set of Mrs. Ashcombe's shoulders, in the way the under-maids whispered in the passage, in the fact that Lady Rosamund had asked — casually, over the top of her newspaper — how many names were currently kept in the household ledger.

"Eleven," I had said. "Twelve with Mr. Tull the gardener, though he sleeps in the bothy."

"And the old ledgers?" she asked. "The ones in the estate room. Are they in order?"

I had been her driver and her gatekeeper for fifteen years, and I knew when a question was not a question. "They are in order, my lady."

"Good," she said, and turned the page of her newspaper, and that was the end of it — or so I believed.

* * *

Mr. Prewitt sat at the kitchen table with his forms fanned out before him like a card sharp's hand, and Mrs. Ashcombe stood over him with her arms folded, radiating the specific hostility of a cook whose territory has been invaded. The census itself was simple enough. Names, ages, occupations, places of birth. Eleven names, written in my own careful hand because Lady Rosamund had said, "You write a fairer line than I do, Elodie, and fairer lines make fewer questions."

I should have understood then what she was asking of me. Fairer lines make fewer questions. She was arming me before I knew there was a battle.

Mr. Prewitt worked through the household with the methodical pleasure of a man who enjoys other people's lives. He asked Mrs. Ashcombe her age and received a look that could have curdled cream. He asked the maids their places of birth and wrote them down without comment. He asked after Mr. Tull, and I told him the bothy counted as part of the household for census purposes, and he wrote that down too.

And then he said: "I'll want to see the household books. The ledgers. It's regulation — I'm to verify the resident list against the house records."

"There's the census form," Mrs. Ashcombe said. "Filled out proper."

"The form is a declaration, madam. The ledgers are evidence. If you'll fetch them — " he looked at me, and I understood that he had already decided I was the one who would fetch them, the servant who moved, the one whose obedience was a tool kept sharp by habit — "the estate room, I understand?"

I looked at Mrs. Ashcombe. Mrs. Ashcombe looked at the clock. Neither of us had any authority to refuse a man with a government satchel and a government manner, and he knew it, and I think he enjoyed it, in the small gray way such men enjoy things.

"I'll fetch them," I said.

* * *

The estate room smelled of leather and cold stone and the particular dust of paper that has not been disturbed in years. The ledgers lived on the third shelf of the oak case, going back to Lady Rosamund's father's time, each one a year of the house's life written in servants' wages and coal deliveries and the small, relentless arithmetic of keeping a great house alive.

I took down the current ledger and the two before it. And then, because I am who I am, because fifteen years of keeping silences had made a certain kind of thoroughness second nature, I opened the oldest of the three — 1899, the year I came to Hale House myself, a girl of fourteen with chilblains and a head full of other people's letters — and I checked.

The name was there.

*Greta Lindqvist*, written in Lady Rosamund's slanting hand, in the spring of 1902, entered under the heading of *Guest, long-term*. And struck through. One clean line, the kind of line you draw when you are erasing a person from a record while still wanting the record to show that a person was once there. Lady Rosamund was a careful woman. She did not blot things out; she cancelled them, so that the truth remained legible to anyone who knew how to read a cancellation.

I had read that ledger a hundred times. I had read every ledger in that room. I had never seen the name, because I had never been looking for it, and because the mind protects itself the way the body does — it does not see the thing that would wound it.

I stood in the cold of the estate room with three ledgers in my arms and felt the hairline crack open, thin as a wire, running through fifteen years of careful silence.

* * *

Mr. Prewitt took his time. That was the thing about him — he was never hurried, never greedy, never eager. He turned the pages of the current ledger with a licked thumb, checking names against his form, and Mrs. Ashcombe watched him the way one watches a fox near the henhouse, and I stood by the dresser with my hands folded and my heart going like a trapped bird.

"Eleven," he said at last. "Eleven on the form, eleven in the ledger. Very tidy." He closed the book and let his hand rest on it. "And the older volumes?"

"They're accounts," I said. "Wages and stores. The census is concerned with persons resident on the night of the second of April."

"The census is concerned," Mr. Prewitt said mildly, "with the truth." He held out his hand. "The older volumes, if you please, Miss Vane. Regulation fourteen. I'm to sample the records of households of this size. It takes no more than a moment."

Mrs. Ashcombe made a sound that in a lesser woman would have been a word. I put the ledgers on the table.

He went through 1910 and 1909 quickly, finding nothing, and I let myself believe — the way one lets oneself believe in fair weather — that it would be nothing, that it was all nothing. Then he opened 1902, and his finger went down the entries with the patience of a man counting another man's money, and stopped.

"Here's a name not on your form," he said.

"The ledgers record all persons who have ever lodged in the house," I said. My voice was steady. I remember being surprised by that. "Guests, tradesmen's clerks who stayed the night, her ladyship's sisters. The census records only those present on the census night."

"Quite right. Quite right." He did not look up. His finger remained on the page, on the struck-through line, and he bent his head over it the way a man bends over a lock he intends to learn. "*Greta Lindqvist.* Guest, long-term. Entered April 1902. Cancelled — " he turned the page, checking the back entries, checking the wage lists, checking the departure records that did not exist — "cancelled, but no date of leaving. No settlement of account. No note of destination." He raised his eyes to me at last, and they were pale and patient and quite without malice, which is the worst kind of eyes to be looked at by. "That's an unusual entry, Miss Vane."

"It was before my time, sir."

"Was it?" He turned the ledger toward me. "You're entered here yourself. 1899. *Elodie Vane, under-housemaid.* So you'd have been in the house in 1902. You'd have known a long-term guest."

I had been fourteen. I had been nobody. But I said, "I was in the kitchens, sir. Guests of her ladyship's own circle weren't known to the under-housemaids."

"Names travel in houses," Mr. Prewitt said. "Names are the one thing that travels fastest." He took out his little book — the one I would learn to dread — and copied the entry in a small precise hand: the name, the date, the cancellation, the absence of any record of leaving. Then he closed the ledgers and stacked them neatly, and thanked Mrs. Ashcombe for her hospitality in a tone that made it sound like a threat, and stood, and gathered his satchel.

"I'll want to confirm the entry with her ladyship," he said. "A signature, nothing more. A formality. When would be convenient?"

"I'll ask her ladyship's secretary," I said, which was a fiction, because there was no secretary, because for fifteen years the letters had been read at the kitchen table by a girl with chilblains who had grown into the woman standing at the dresser with her hands folded.

"Ask her ladyship," Mr. Prewitt said, "about Greta Lindqvist. I find it saves time when the question comes from me directly." He smiled his invoice smile, and wrote something else in the little book, and let himself out through the service door into the April light.

* * *

I carried the ledgers back to the estate room and put them on the shelf, and I stood there a long moment with my hand on the 1902 volume, feeling through the leather the weight of what I had and had not read.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of reading letters at the kitchen table — the ones that were safe, the ones that were dangerous, the ones that were dangerous in ways Lady Rosamund never explained and I never asked about. Fifteen years of learning that a household is a fortress and its walls are made of silence, and that the gatekeeper's whole duty is to know which silences hold the walls up. I had thought I knew them all. I had thought the map of the house's secrets was complete, and that I had drawn half of it myself.

Now there was a name I had never been shown, in a hand I knew better than my own, cancelled but not erased. And a man with a government satchel had copied it into his little gray book, and asked me, politely, to make sure the question reached the top of the house.

I went up through the service stairs to the morning room, where Lady Rosamund was at her desk with her spectacles down her nose, attacking a suffrage correspondence with the efficiency she brought to everything. She looked up when I came in, and she must have read something in my face, because she set down her pen before I said a word.

"Well, Elodie?"

"The census man has gone, my lady." I closed the door behind me. "But he found a name in the 1902 ledger. A guest, long-term, with no record of leaving. He's copied it out, and he'll be back, and he asked me to give you his question directly." I made myself say it plainly, the way she had taught me, all those years ago, that dangerous things should be said. "He asked about Greta Lindqvist."

Lady Rosamund did not move. She did not go pale, or start, or do any of the things that betray a person in the novels. She simply sat very still, the way a house sits still when the ground under it shifts, and after a moment she took off her spectacles and folded them, and looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in fifteen years of service.

It was fear. And underneath the fear, something that looked almost like relief — the look of a woman who has been waiting a long time for a debt to come due.

"Shut the door properly," she said, "and sit down, Elodie. It's time you knew what it is you've been keeping."
