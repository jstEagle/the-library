## 3. The Name in the Ledger

I had not opened the green ledger in eleven years, and I would have preferred never to open it again.

It lived in the bottom drawer of the writing desk in my morning room, beneath the household accounts and a bundle of receipts I kept for no reason except that throwing things away had begun to feel like an admission. The ledger itself was innocent enough to look at — a stationer's ordinary book, marbled boards gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked where the binding had given up somewhere around 1903. Inside it were the subscriptions, the dues, the collected shillings of the Harwich and District Women's Social and Political Union, entered in my own hand in violet ink that has faded to the color of weak tea.

And inside it, on the fourth page, was the name.

Elodie had brought it up to me the evening after the census man left, carrying it flat on both palms as though it were a wounded bird. She set it on my desk and did not go away, which was how I knew this was not a matter of ink or arithmetic.

"You saw," I said.

"Yes, my lady." Elodie Vane has stood at my elbow for fifteen years and I have watched her learn every dialect of silence we speak in this house, but there are moments when she abandons all of them and speaks plainly, and those are the moments I have learned to fear. "Page four. There's a name struck through, and Mr. Callow's eye went straight to it like a ferret down a hole. He asked me why a name had been removed from a record. I told him I couldn't say."

"Quite right." I reached for the ledger and did not open it. My hand rested on the cover the way one rests a hand on the lid of a box one already knows the contents of. "You may go, Elodie."

She did not go. She stood there with her hands folded and her face composed into that particular stillness she wears when she is waiting out a storm, and after a moment she said, quietly, "Shall I close the door?"

I have been a suffragist for twenty years. I have been shouted down in market squares, pelted with rotting fruit in Ipswich, held by two policemen while a third forced open my jaw to feed a feeding tube into a body I had made my own weapon. I am not easily frightened by men with questions. But I sat there with my hand on a stationer's ledger and could not make myself lift it, because Elodie's question — *shall I close the door* — was not about the door. It was her way of saying: *I will keep your secret, whatever it is; you need only tell me what it is.*

"Greta Lindqvist," I said, to the room rather than to her, because I found I could not say it to a person. "The name is Greta Lindqvist."

* * *

She came to me first in the spring of 1904, at a meeting in a draughty hall in Colchester where I had been invited to speak and had been warned beforehand that eggs might be thrown. Greta Lindqvist was twenty-six then, half Swedish through her mother and half German through her father, raised in Hull, educated in Heidelberg, and possessed of a voice that could carry over a hostile crowd the way a gull rides wind — not fighting the weather but using it. When the eggs came, and they came, she stepped forward before I could flinch and said, loudly enough for the back rows, "Ladies, if you waste good food on Lady Rosamund, you'll only prove her point that politics makes people foolish. Eat your breakfast and listen instead." The hall laughed. The tension broke like a dropped plate. And afterward she walked with me to the station and talked the whole way about organization, about pamphlets, about how the movement in Germany was doing things we might learn from, and by the time my train came in I had agreed to something I hadn't known I was being persuaded toward, which was the story of the next nine years of my life.

For nine years, Greta Lindqvist was the other half of my working mind. She organized the northern districts; I held the southern ones. She wrote the speeches that made crowds roar and I wrote the letters that made Members of Parliament flinch. She stayed in this house dozens of times — the blue room, always the blue room, she said the color suited her temper. My husband tolerated her with the amused resignation of a man watching weather he cannot control. Miles, who was ten when she first came and nineteen when she last left, called her Aunt Greta until he was old enough to be embarrassed by affection, and then called her nothing at all, which is its own kind of history.

She was not a citizen of anywhere, really, and that was part of her power. A woman without a nation can speak to all of them. Half-Swede, half-German, English by upbringing and marriage — she had married an Englishman, a printer named Lindqvist who died of consumption in 1908 — she moved through Europe's borders the way our arguments moved through drawing rooms: slipping under doors, arriving uninvited, refusing to be shown out.

"If it ever comes to war," she said to me once — it was August of 1911, and we were sitting in this very room, she in the chair by the window where Elodie stands now — "they will come for women like me first. Not because we've done anything. Because we're easy. A woman with three countries is a woman with none, and men with files love a woman with none." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, turning her teacup in her hands. "You'd vouch for me, Rosamund? If it ever came to that? One roof, and passage out, and money enough to start again somewhere the files don't reach?"

I said, "You know you needn't ask."

She smiled at me over the rim of her cup. "One always needs to ask. Promises made unasked-for are just wishes wearing a coat." And then she laughed at her own aphorism, drank her tea, and turned the conversation to the Lancashire mill strikes, and I let her, because the sentence had unsettled me and Greta Lindqvist could always tell when she had unsettled me and was merciful enough to look away.

That is why the promise exists in writing. That is why, in September 1911, I took the green ledger from its drawer, turned to page four where her subscription payments marched down the margin in violet ink, drew a single line through her name, and beneath it wrote: *G.L. — refuge at H., passage & £50, upon request, without condition. R.H.* It seemed to me then a romantic, almost theatrical gesture — sealing a vow in the very book that recorded everything we had built together. It seemed fitting. It seemed safe. Ledgers, I reasoned, are read by accountants, and accountants ask about money, and fifty pounds is a sum no accountant would ever interrogate.

Then came the summer of 1914, and the world obliged us both.

* * *

"She wrote in July," I told Elodie. I had opened the ledger at last, once the door was shut, and turned to the page, and looked at my own handwriting across three years of distance. "Before the ultimatum expired. Before any of it was certain. She wrote from Lowestoft — she'd taken work under another name, packing fish, of all things, a woman who addressed a crowd of four thousand in Manchester and now stands in herring brine twelve hours a day." I heard my own voice grow brittle and despised it. "She didn't ask for anything. She wrote only to tell me where she was, and to remind me — gently, oh, so gently, in that way of hers — of the date. As though I might have forgotten the date."

"And have you sent for her?"

"No." The word fell out of me like a coin down a well, and I listened for it to strike bottom and heard nothing. "The Aliens Restriction Act was passed before her letter reached me. By November they will be interned wholesale, Elodie — Germans and Austrians, and anyone the authorities decide looks like either, and Greta has spent fifteen years making speeches beside me under her own name. Her photograph has been in the papers. If she is taken, she will not simply be interned; she will be made an example of. The friend of Lady Rosamund Hale. They will want to know what else passed between us. Letters. Money. Names." I closed the ledger. "Every letter I ever wrote her is potentially a charge sheet. For her, and for everyone whose names appear in them."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. Through the window behind her I could see the last of the light going off the water, the harbor flattening to pewter, and I thought, absurdly, of the census form lying completed downstairs with its tidy columns, its insistence that every soul in England stand still long enough to be counted, sorted, and assigned. Counting is the beginning of it all, Greta used to say. First they count you. Then they count you out.

"There is a son," Elodie said at last, and I flinched, because I had been thinking the same thing and had hoped she would be merciful enough not to say it. "Mr. Miles. He's home, and he's watching, and the census man's questions were answered too smoothly today, if you take my meaning. Smooth answers invite second visits."

"I know what sort of man my son is, Elodie."

"Yes, my lady. I only meant—" She stopped herself, which she almost never does, and I understood that she had been about to say something neither of us could afford to have said aloud, in this house, with Miles Hale home on leave and hungry. She bent instead and gathered the cold tea things onto her tray with quick, economical movements. "Fifty pounds," she said, not looking at me. "Passage. A roof until the ship sails. Those were the terms?"

"Without condition," I said. "Those were my words."

She nodded once, set her chin the way she does when she has finished a piece of arithmetic the rest of us haven't started, and carried the tray to the door. Her hand was on the latch when I spoke again, and I did not plan what I said; it simply came up out of the place where the guilt had been sitting for three years, growing heavier.

"Elodie. If it comes to choosing between protecting me and keeping the promise — keep the promise. Do you understand me? I made a vow to a woman who is presently hiding among fish barrels because of what I am and what she was to me. If my son, or the Home Office, or God himself demands a price for it, the price is mine to pay. Not hers. Not yours. Mine." My voice had risen, and I made myself stop, and lower it, and finish. "Swear it to me. I'm an old woman who has broken one promise already by delay. I won't compound it by letting someone else pay for my cowardice."

Elodie stood in the doorway with the tray against her hip and looked at me for a long moment, and what was in her face was not pity, which I could have borne, but recognition — the look of one debtor acknowledging another.

"I'll swear what I can, my lady," she said. "But promises cost more than they used to. You should know that better than anyone." And then she was gone, and I sat alone with the ledger, and after a while I turned to page four and put my thumb over the struck-through name, as if pressure alone could hold the line together.

Three days later, a letter arrived for Miles Hale, postmarked London, written on the crested paper of the Census Office. He read it in the study with the door shut, and when he came out his face had the smooth, pleased blankness of a man who has just discovered that the world contains levers, and that one of them bears his family's name on it.
