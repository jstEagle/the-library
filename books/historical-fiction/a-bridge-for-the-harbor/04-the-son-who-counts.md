## 4. The Son Who Counts

Miles Hale had learned to count before he had learned to read, and he had never entirely stopped.

He counted the way other men breathed. He counted the panes of glass in the long gallery at Hale House — forty-two, three of them cracked and unreplaced since before his father died, because his mother said the house must keep its character. He counted the acres his mother controlled through the trust his grandfather had drawn up with the precision of a man building a fortress: eleven hundred and sixty, more or less, depending on which surveyor's figures one believed. He counted the years. That was the count that mattered. Fifteen years his mother had held the estate in trust, promising — always promising — that when he came of age, when he married, when he proved himself, the thing would pass to him as it had passed to every Hale since the first of them had grown rich on wool and the misery of other men.

He was thirty-one. He had proved himself at Sandhurst, in India, in the dull grey corridors of the War Office where he now spent his leave doing paperwork that mattered to no one. And still the estate sat in his mother's hands like a jewel she had forgotten she was wearing.

He counted that, too. Every day of it.

He had been home four days when he found the letter.

* * *

It was the rain that drove him into the study. His mother's study, though she would have said the house had no rooms that belonged to anyone; that was her way of owning everything while appearing to own nothing. The rain came in off the Channel in long grey sheets, and the shoot was cancelled, and Miles found himself with nothing to do but walk the corridors of a house that was not yet his and feel the walls taking his measure and finding him wanting.

The study smelled of ink and cold fire. His mother's desk sat beneath the window, immaculate, every paper squared to the edge of the blotter. She was a woman who filed her life. He had inherited his orderliness from her and his hunger from his father, and the combination had made him, he suspected, a man people found useful and never warm.

The letter was in the tray marked *Pending* — a small mahogany tray she had kept since his childhood, into which went everything that required thought before action. Miles had been taught, at seven years old, never to touch it. He was thirty-one now, and the house was not his, and he touched it.

The envelope was cheap. Official. *Census of the Population, 1911 — Office of the Registrar-General.* His mother had answered the census in April; he remembered the household fuss of it, the forms, the servants lined up to be counted like stock. What followed it, he had not known. He slit the envelope with his thumb — a habit his mother deplored — and read.

The registrar's office wished to draw attention to a discrepancy. The household schedule for Hale House, delivered in April, had been returned complete. However, the district enumerator, in reconciling his books, had noted that the *household service ledger* — the old bound book in which the estate recorded names of resident staff across the years, a book the enumerator had been shown in the course of his rounds — contained a name struck through in a later hand, with no corresponding entry on the schedule. The office requested clarification. Was the individual in question a resident on the night of the census? If so, the schedule must be amended under penalty of the Act. If not, a brief written explanation of the ledger entry would suffice for the district's records. They looked forward to Lady Hale's prompt reply.

Miles read it twice.

The name struck through. The letter did not give it — of course it did not; bureaucrats never gave away the one thing that mattered. But it did not need to. What it gave him was better than a name. It gave him a fact: that his mother had something in her past that a government clerk had noticed, and that she had not yet answered, and that the letter had been sitting in the *Pending* tray for — he turned it over, checked the postmark — eleven days.

Eleven days. His mother answered letters the day they arrived. He had seen her do it through his whole boyhood, pen scratching before the ink on the envelope had dried. Eleven days meant the letter frightened her. And a frightened Rosamund Hale was a thing Miles had seen perhaps three times in his life, and never once turned to his advantage.

Until now.

* * *

He did not go to her. That was the first discipline of it, and he was surprised how much discipline it cost. The old Miles — the boy who had once marched into her study at fifteen and demanded to know why the tenants' rents went to the suffrage societies while his school fees were questioned — would have laid the letter on her desk and demanded an accounting. But he had learned things since. In India he had learned that the man who shows his hand first owns the table only until the next deal. In the War Office he had learned that information was not power; information *held back* was power, a blade kept sheathed, worth nothing drawn and everything promised.

So he put the letter in his breast pocket, squared the tray back to its angle, and went down to dinner and talked about the weather and the price of wool and his mother's health, and watched her across the candlelight for the small tells she would never believe she had.

She had them. He was almost disappointed. She refilled her glass once more than usual. She asked him twice about his leave — when he must return, whether the War Office might extend it — and Miles understood, with a cold little pleasure that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, that she wanted him gone. She wanted him out of the house while she dealt with whatever the letter contained. She was managing him, the way she managed everything, and she did not yet know that the thing she was managing had learned to count.

* * *

The ledger itself was the next question. The census man had seen it, which meant it had been fetched for him, which meant — Miles thought it through as he shaved the next morning, watching his own eyes in the glass — that it lived somewhere accessible. Not the study. His mother would not have handed a stranger her own records. It would be in the estate room, or the housekeeper's office, or the long cupboard at the end of the servants' passage where the old estate papers lived in tin boxes.

He found it on the third day, in the estate room, in a box marked *Household — Staff — 1894–1908*, while the housekeeper was at market and the under-maids were blacking grates.

The ledger was a fat, grimy book, its spine held with string. Miles turned pages of names and wages and dates of service, and the house rose up around him in ink: gardeners and cooks and scullery maids, coming and going, a river of the unimportant. His mother's handwriting marched down the columns, upright and merciless.

He found the entry in the spring of 1908. A name in the column for *household — resident*, written in his mother's hand and then struck through with a single hard line, the ink of the strike darker, later. No date of departure entered. No wages settled. The name sat there struck through like a small murder:

**G. Lindqvist.**

Miles stood in the cold estate room with the book open on his knees and let the name turn over in his mind. Lindqvist. Swedish, or German, or something of that northern stock. Not a servant — servants had wages; this entry had none. A guest, then. A guest recorded in the staff ledger, which his mother would do only if the woman had lived in the house long enough to be, for official purposes, household. A guest his mother had later wanted erased from the record — struck through, not ruled out, not annotated. Erased.

And now a government office wanted to know why.

He copied the name into his pocket-book in his own neat hand, and the dates around it, and the fact of the strike. Then he put the ledger back exactly as he had found it — he was good at exactly — and stood a moment in the dust-smelling quiet, listening to the rain on the estate room window, and felt something settle in him that he recognised from the trenches his brother-officers described: the moment before an attack went in, when fear and appetite became the same thing.

* * *

He worked the rest of it the way he had been trained to work anything: quietly, in order, without haste.

The London clubs gave him nothing directly, but a dinner with an old Sandhurst man now at the Home Office gave him the shape of the law. Enemy aliens. Registration. Internment camps on the Isle of Man. The country, his friend said over the port, was crawling with Germans, and half of them had been here twenty years, married to Englishmen, godmothers to English children, and none of it mattered now; a German was a German. "Half these society women don't know who their friends are anymore," the man said, laughing. "One bad marriage and your dearest friend is on a register."

Miles laughed with him and asked, idly, how a civilian might make enquiries. Official channels, his friend said. Or unofficial ones. There were men who did such work for a fee — ex-policemen, mostly — and he could give a name if it was for something respectable. "It always is," Miles said, "respectable," and took the name.

The name was a man named Crake, and Crake, for two guineas and an introduction, found in four days what the census office had found in eleven: that a Greta Lindqvist, born Hamburg to a Swedish mother, had been a lecturer and organiser for the suffrage movement in the years before the war, a close associate of Lady Rosamund Hale's, resident for some months at Hale House in 1907 and 1908, and unaccounted for in any record since. "Gone to ground," Crake said, in his office above a stationer's shop, wiping his glasses. "People who go to ground have usually been told to. Question is who told them, and what for."

"And if I wanted the second question answered?"

Crake smiled with the lower half of his face only. "Then we're not talking two guineas."

Miles named a figure that made the lower half of Crake's face improve. He could not afford it — that was the joke of his life, the heir who could not afford anything — but there was a man in the City, a lender who specialised in gentlemen between inheritances, and the interest was ruinous, and Miles had decided some time ago that ruin was a problem for a future he intended to make certain arrived. Debt was only another form of advance. He had learned that from his mother, though she would have died before admitting it: she had spent fifteen years advancing against the estate's future — on the movement, on the tenants, on her causes — and the whole edifice rested on the assumption that the future would arrive on her terms.

It would arrive on someone's terms. Miles intended them to be his.

* * *

The follow-up letter from the census office came while he was out — a second letter, firmer in tone, referring to the office's earlier enquiry and noting that no reply had been received. He was in the hall when the post came, and he had it in his hand before the footman could carry it in, and his mother, coming down the stairs with a letter of her own already sealed, stopped on the third step when she saw the envelope in his fingers.

For a moment — a full, delicious, terrible moment — neither of them moved. Rain against the tall hall windows. The clock in the gallery striking the quarter-hour. His mother's face doing the thing it did instead of blanching, going still and bright, like water about to freeze.

"That's for me, I think," she said.

"Is it." He turned the envelope over, read the postmark aloud, pleasantly. "The Registrar-General. How dull. More counting." He held it out to her, and she took it, and her fingers were perfectly steady, which told him more than trembling would have. "You know, Mother," he said, "when I was a boy you taught me that a household's books must always balance. Every name accounted for. Every entry explained." He smiled at her, the family smile, the one from the portraits. "I've been thinking about that a great deal lately."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide — he could see the decision happen, the weighing, the old general choosing her ground — and then she said only, "You'll be wanting to make an early start if you're back to London tomorrow," and went past him into the study and shut the door.

Miles stood in the hall and counted his heartbeats. Eleven, twelve, thirteen.

She had not asked what he knew. That meant she feared he knew something. That meant there was something to know — a woman, a promise, a name struck through in ink, hidden somewhere in a country that had just declared her kind the enemy. And somewhere out there, Greta Lindqvist was waiting, with no idea that the son of the house had begun to count her.

He went up to his room to write to Crake.
