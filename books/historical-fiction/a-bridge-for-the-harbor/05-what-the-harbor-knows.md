## 5. What the Harbor Knows

Elodie told Lady Rosamund she was going to visit her sister in Portsmouth. It was the first lie she had told her in fifteen years, and it came out of her mouth with a terrible smoothness, as though the lie had been waiting all that time behind her teeth for its turn.

"Give her my love," Lady Rosamund said, not looking up from her correspondence. The morning light through the breakfast room window caught the grey at her temples, and Elodie thought: *she knows.* But that was the trouble with women trained in silence — you could never tell whether they knew everything or nothing, and both looked identical from the outside.

The train south was crowded with men in new uniforms and old fears. Elodie sat with her hands folded on a leather case in her lap — inside it, three hundred pounds in notes drawn over months from the household account, small sums that would read as nothing to an auditor until you added them together. She had been adding them together herself for weeks now, at night, doing the arithmetic of the promise. *If it ever comes to war.* Well. It had come.

She had a name and a town and very little else. *Mrs. Anna Reed*, Lady Rosamund had said. *Brackwater. There is a packing shed on the quay — Corrigan's. She will be at the door when the boats come in; she has always liked doors.* And then Lady Rosamund had taken Elodie's hand, which she almost never did, and held it hard enough to leave marks. *Don't tell her I sent you until you're certain of her. And don't tell her about Miles.*

The harbor announced itself by smell before sight — brine and diesel and fish blood, thick enough to taste. Brackwater was smaller than Portsmouth, meaner, a street of slate roofs sliding down toward water the color of gunmetal. War had touched it lightly so far: a recruiting poster curling off the fishmonger's wall, a boy selling flags, nets mended by men too old to be ashamed of the work.

Corrigan's packing shed squatted at the end of the quay like something the tide had failed to take back. Through its open doors Elodie could see women in oilskins at long steel tables, their hands moving in quick, brutal rhythms — gut, pack, salt, stack. Herring came up the ramp in glistening loads, silver going dull in the air.

And there, at the door, a woman with a shawl over her head, directing the unloading in a voice pitched low but carrying.

Elodie did not approach. Fifteen years of reading letters had taught her this much: never approach anything directly. She bought tea at a stall, stood where she could watch without seeming to, and learned what watching could teach. That Mrs. Reed took her wages in an envelope she counted twice. That she ate alone. That when a customs man ambled past the shed she turned back to her work with a smoothness that was itself suspicious — ordinary women fumbled, glanced, worried. This woman became a painting of herself.

When the boats were in and the rush ebbed, Elodie crossed the quay.

"Mrs. Reed?"

The woman looked up. She was perhaps forty-five, broad-shouldered, with a face weathered into handsomeness and eyes a pale northern blue that went still the moment they landed on Elodie — not confused, not curious. Still. Like water deciding whether to freeze.

"You've the wrong shed," she said. Her accent was flawless coastal English. Almost flawless. One vowel, deep down, was not.

"I have a message from—" Elodie began.

"Not here." Not loud. Just final. "Tuesday market. Church of St. Alban's, after the ten o'clock bells. If you follow me now I'll call the constable and enjoy doing it."

Then she raised her voice and became ordinary again — *"You there, mind the third crate, it's split"* — and Elodie understood she had just been given a lesson in survival by someone who'd had to learn it faster.

* * *

St. Alban's on Tuesday smelled of candle wax and herring carried in on the coats of the market crowd. Elodie sat in a back pew beneath a memorial window to fishermen lost in '93 and waited while the bells finished their argument overhead. Greta Lindqvist — because that was who slid into the pew beside her, in a black coat and a widow's bonnet — did not look at her. She looked at the altar.

"You counted your money before you left home," Greta said quietly. "I watched you at the tea stall. Women who count money like that are either buying something illegal or leaving their husbands. You have honest hands, so I'll assume the first." A pause. "Rosamund's hands used to move like yours. Same training."

"You know me, then."

"I know the shape of her household. Driver, gatekeeper, reader of letters. Fifteen years, isn't it? She wrote to me about you before writing became dangerous." Greta's profile didn't move. "Say what you've come to say. Quickly. God is listening but He's discreet."

"The promise stands. Three hundred pounds, papers if we can manage them, passage out on a neutral ship. She wants you safe."

For the first time Greta turned, and Elodie saw her whole face, and understood that whatever softness had once lived there had been put away somewhere, carefully, the way one puts away a dress too fine for daily wear.

"The promise stands," Greta repeated. "How lovely. Does she remember which promise? Because she made me more than one, over twenty years. We chained ourselves to railings together. We shared a cell in Holloway — she talked in her sleep, did you know that? In German, sometimes. Her governess was a Hamburg girl. She never tells anyone that anymore." Something flickered across the stillness. "There was another promise too. Before you were born, probably. About her son."

Elodie kept her voice level. "Miles?"

"Miles." Greta said the name the way one sets down something heavy. "Ask yourself why a woman who trusts no one keeps a ledger with names struck through it. Ask why she paid, every quarter for eleven years, into an account under the name L. Hartmann — ask her what happened in the spring of 1903, and watch her face when she answers." She smoothed her glove. "I am not threatening her. I want that understood. I loved her. Perhaps I love her still, which is worse. But I am telling you what I know, because you are the one carrying the money, and people carrying money should know whose pockets they're standing near."

"What happened in 1903?"

"That," said Greta, "is the price of the next conversation."

* * *

Elodie walked back along the quay in a thinning rain and found her certainties rearranged like furniture after a burglary — same room, nothing where she'd left it.

She had come believing the shape of things: loyal servant, threatened mistress, hunted friend, promise to keep. Clean lines. Now there was an account named Hartmann, eleven years of payments, a spring thirteen years gone that Lady Rosamund had never once let surface in fifteen years of shared silences. And Miles — Miles, who had come home counting heartbeats, hunting the census taker's letter like a dog scenting blood. If Greta knew things, then Greta was not only cargo to be smuggled. Greta was ammunition. And whoever loaded her would decide where she fired.

At the harbor mouth the fog was coming in off the water, softening the boats, the sheds, the customs house light, turning the whole town into a rumor of itself. Elodie stopped at the sea wall and took out the envelope of banknotes and held it, feeling the weight of it, the weight of everything it was suddenly less simple than.

A fisherman nearby was coiling rope, watching her sidelong — a weathered man of sixty-odd with a navy tattoo gone green on his forearm. "Fog's thick tonight," he offered. "Bad for sailing unless you know the channel like your own teeth."

"Do you?" she asked.

He smiled without warmth. "Depends who's asking, and what for, and what the crossing's worth." He tied off the coil. "Everything's worth something here, miss. Only question is who sets the price."

She thought of Greta's still blue eyes. *That is the price of the next conversation.*

Two days ago she had believed the promise was the hardest thing in the world — keeping it, funding it, seeing it done. Now she understood the promise was only the beginning. Beneath it lay older debts, older springs, a ledger of what everyone knew about everyone else, and somewhere behind her, in a country house she could no longer quite see, Miles Hale was learning the same lesson from the other direction: that information was currency, and he intended to mint as much of it as he could.

Elodie put the money away and turned back toward the lodging house. Tomorrow she would go to St. Alban's again. Tomorrow she would pay Greta's price, whatever it was, because a promise half-understood was more dangerous than none at all — and because, though she would not say it aloud even to herself, some part of her needed to know what Lady Rosamund had done in the spring of 1903, needed it the way a crack needs to know how far it runs through the stone.

Behind her, out on the dark water, a ship's bell sounded once through the fog, and was answered by nothing at all.
