## 6. Papers and Prices

The forger kept shop above a chandler's on Cutter's Lane, where the smell of tar and hemp rose through the floorboards like something breathing. Elodie climbed the stairs with her collar up and her heart going hard against her ribs, and knocked twice, then once more — the pattern the fishmonger's boy had described, three shillings richer and sworn to silence he would not keep.

"Enter."

The room was small and lit by a single lamp turned low. A man sat at a sloped desk, fifty perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and the patient face of someone who had spent a lifetime making other people's words look true. He did not look up when she came in.

"You're the woman from Hale House," he said.

Elodie stopped halfway across the room. "I don't know how—"

"I know everyone who climbs these stairs, or I learn them fast enough." He set down his pen at last and looked at her. His eyes were pale, unhurried, the eyes of a man reading a document she hadn't yet written. "Sit down, Miss Vane. You've been standing in doorways of this town for two days asking careful questions. Careful questions are louder than careless ones."

She sat. The chair was hard; she was grateful for it, for the small discomfort that kept her sharp. "I need papers," she said. "Travel documents. A woman needs to leave England — a neutral ship, an American line if possible."

"A German woman."

Not a question. Elodie held his gaze. "A Swedish woman."

"Mm." He opened a drawer and drew out a blank sheet, holding it to the lamp so the watermark showed, faint as a ghost. "This is genuine stock. That is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that genuine stock in wartime is worth more than the paper it's printed on, and I am not a patriot, but neither am I a fool. If I make papers for a German-born woman while the country is hunting such women, and it goes wrong, they will hang me for treason before the tea goes cold." He laid the sheet flat. "So my price is not money."

"What is it?"

"Information." He folded his hands. "You serve Lady Rosamund Hale. Fifteen years, they say. She trusts you with her letters, her ledgers, her silences. I want to know what she knows. What the movement knows. Who in this town took German money before the war, which magistrates can be moved, which ships carry what the manifests don't say. Not for sale," he added, seeing her face. "For safety. A man in my trade survives by knowing where the knives are. Lady Rosamund knows where all of them are."

"She would never give me those things."

"No. But you could take them. You've taken things from that house for years, I expect. Letters read over candle flames. Names copied out. It's how gatekeepers survive — they trade in what passes through the gate." He smiled without warmth. "I'm only asking you to do openly what you've done in secret since you were a girl."

The truth of it went through her like cold water. She thought of the kitchen table in 1899, the steam off the kettle, Lady Rosamund's finger moving down a column of names. *Read me the ones that matter, Elodie.* She had been proud then. She had believed it was trust.

"One name," Elodie said. "One piece of information, verified, useful. And you make the papers. Full set."

He considered her longer than she liked. Then he nodded slowly. "One name. But understand what you're buying, Miss Vane. Everyone who comes up these stairs thinks they're paying once. Nobody pays once. The first price is always the smallest one." He pulled the blank sheet toward him and uncapped his pen. "Now. Tell me about the woman. Age, build, the name she's hiding under. Every detail you get wrong is a detail that gets her caught."

Elodie told him. Greta Lindqvist, forty-one now, grey coming in early at the temples, working as *Greta Lund* in the fish-packing shed by the coal yard. Hands scarred from the gutting knives. A habit of looking at doors.

"And you?" the forger asked, pen moving. "What's your habit?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"When the authorities come for you — and they will come, because they always come — what will you do? Run, or burn?"

She had no answer ready. He seemed satisfied by that.

"Papers in four days," he said. "Bring me the name, and bring it true."

* * *

Miles Hale had learned arithmetic from his father and patience from no one, so he conducted the census taker's business in the drawing room of the Royal Hotel, where the tea was overpriced and the waiters were discreet, and where a man could speak plainly without the walls having opinions.

Mr. Prewitt arrived at four o'clock precisely, a thin man with a ledger under his arm and the wary posture of a civil servant who had been summoned above his station and suspected a trap.

"Mr. Hale. You wished to see me about the enumeration at Hale House."

"Sit, please." Miles poured the tea himself, which put Prewitt further off balance; gentlemen did not pour. "You wrote to my mother regarding a discrepancy. An extra name in an old household ledger. Struck through, I understand."

"The matter is under review by the Registrar General's office—"

"The matter," Miles said pleasantly, "is under review by me. My mother is elderly and excitable and has a history of — enthusiasms. Suffrage, pacifism, correspondence with persons on the Continent. You'll have seen the newspapers. Women who chain themselves to railings rarely keep tidy records." He set a saucer before Prewitt. "I'd like to help your office arrive at the truth efficiently."

Prewitt did not touch the tea. "Efficiently, sir?"

"There are men in Whitehall whose whole occupation just now is finding irregularities. Enemy aliens unregistered. Sympathizers concealed. A household with a struck-through foreign name, a lady of known radical sympathies, and letters going abroad — well. A diligent man might make a great deal of that. Promotion, even. A transfer somewhere with better air than this coast." Miles let the silence do its work. "Or a diligent man might conclude that the ledger entry was a clerical error, corrected in due course, and nothing more. And enjoy a long and comfortable career doing exactly what he does now, with certain — improvements."

"That would be improper, Mr. Hale."

"It would be patriotic." Miles leaned forward. "There is a war on, Mr. Prewitt. Every man must decide what he owes his country. Some men owe blood. You owe accuracy. I am offering you the chance to be accurate in the direction of England." From his breast pocket he drew an envelope, thick and unmarked, and set it beside the teapot. "Consider it a contribution to the war effort. Discretion being a form of effort."

Prewitt stared at the envelope the way a starving man stares at bread he suspects is poisoned. Miles watched him calculate — the years of service, the wife with the weak chest, the cottage with the rising damp — and saw the moment the arithmetic finished.

"If there were irregularities," Prewitt said quietly, "and they were — overlooked — that would require regular oversight. Ongoing."

"Naturally. We'll correspond." Miles stood, ending the audience. "You have a good memory, Mr. Prewitt. I'm told the best civil servants are remembered for what they never wrote down."

After the little man had gone, Miles stood at the window watching the harbor grey and glittering below, and felt the peculiar clean satisfaction of a lever settling into its notch. His mother thought power lived in houses and titles and the old law of inheritance. But power lived here — in envelopes, in small men's fears, in the difference between what was written and what was reported. He had waited thirty years to be given his share of Hale House. He had stopped waiting.

There remained only the question of the woman herself. The German. His mother's promise, whatever it had been, was a debt against the estate — and debts against the estate were his to collect. He finished his tea, cold now, and rang for the porter.

"Find out which ships leave this harbor for neutral ports," he said. "And which of them have been taking unusual passengers lately."

* * *

That night Elodie sat in her narrow room above the stable loft with the lamp turned low and a sheet of paper before her, and understood at last what the forger had meant about prices.

She had gone back to the packing shed that afternoon and told Greta, in as few words as possible: *Papers are coming. Four days. There is a cost, and the cost is mine to pay.* Greta had looked at her with those door-watching eyes and said, "Nothing is free, and least of all kindness. What did you promise him?" And when Elodie wouldn't say, Greta had nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd known all along. "Lady Rosamund taught us both the same lesson, then. That other people's secrets are currency. She just never had to spend her own."

It wasn't true. It was cruel, and it wasn't true, and Elodie had walked back along the sea wall telling herself so with every step. But the words stayed, the way splinters stay.

Now she dipped her pen and began to write — not the name the forger wanted, not yet, but a list. Everything she knew that could be traded. Magistrates who owed Lady Rosamund favors. Shipping agents who had moved suffragettes out of town ahead of arrest warrants in the old days. One customs officer, retired now, who had looked away from certain crates in 1908 for reasons Elodie had read in a letter never meant for her eyes.

Fifteen years of silences, laid out in ink. She had thought she was the keeper of them. She saw now that she had merely been the vault, and every vault believes itself loyal until someone brings the right tools.

She thought of burning the list. She thought of Lady Rosamund's face when Elodie had promised, *I will keep her safe*, and how easily promises became debts, and debts became betrayals wearing borrowed clothes.

Outside, rain began on the slate roof, soft as footsteps. Somewhere down the hill, in the lit drawing room of Hale House, Miles was pouring whiskey and planning, and somewhere across town Mr. Prewitt was deciding whether patriotism weighed more than five pounds, and somewhere above a chandler's shop a man was sharpening his pens for work that would begin the moment she paid.

Everyone had a price. She had simply assumed hers was higher than this.

Elodie blotted the list, folded it into her bodice, and blew out the lamp. Four days. She would pay the forger tomorrow, and pray the name she gave him bought more time than it sold.

But when she closed her eyes, she saw the harbor at night — black water, fog coming in off the Channel, and a ship's lights moving steadily away — and she could not tell whether the figure watching from the quay was friend or son.
