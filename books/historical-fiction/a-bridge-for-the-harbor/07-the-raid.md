## 7. The Raid

The herring came in at dawn, and Greta Lindqvist had learned to read the sea's moods by its silver.

A good catch meant a good day — backs bent, knives flashing, the shed roaring with the wet slap of fish and the women's songs. A bad catch meant idle hands, and idle hands meant talk, and talk was the one thing she could not afford. She had been Frau Berg for eleven months now, a Swedish widow with a limp she exaggerated on market days and a dead husband she invented in finer detail each time someone asked. The lie had grown calluses. Some mornings she woke and could not feel the truth beneath them.

She stood at her station near the smokehouse door, hands red to the wrist, gutting with a speed that no longer required thought. Her thoughts went where they always went now — to the letter that had come three days ago, folded inside a parcel of needles and thread, in handwriting she would have known anywhere even after fifteen years.

*If it ever comes to war, you will have refuge and means. I do not break my promises, Greta. Not this one.*

Rosamund. Rosamund, who had held her hand at the funeral of a movement that never quite died; who had argued with her across kitchen tables until dawn about whether the vote would come by petition or by fire; who had kissed her cheek at Victoria Station in the spring of 1899 and said, *go safely, and write to me anyway.* Greta had kept every letter. That was her folly — she knew it was her folly — but when you have lost a country, a language, a name, you do not surrender the paper proof that you were once somebody else.

The woman beside her elbowed her gently. "Berg. You've missed six."

Greta looked down. Six fish stared up at her, glassy-eyed, waiting.

"Sorry, Mrs. Dunn." She bent back to her work.

It was the smell of coal smoke that warned her first, though she did not know it yet. Coal smoke, heavy and wrong, drifting down from the lane above the sheds where no engine came at this hour. Then the dogs stopped barking all at once, the way animals go quiet before weather turns, and Mrs. Dunn said, without looking up from her knife:

"Constables."

* * *

They came through both doors at once.

Greta saw it happen the way one sees an accident — in pieces, too fast and too slow together. The front doors banging open, boots, four men in dark coats and two constables in blue, a sergeant holding a paper aloft as though it were a lantern.

"By authority of the Defence of the Realm Act! Nobody leaves! Remain at your stations!"

The women did not remain at their stations. They surged backward against the far wall like a single frightened animal, and in the press of bodies Greta felt the packet against her ribs — papers, letters, the false Swedish passport with its careful forgery of a stamp she had paid half her savings to obtain — and understood, with a clarity that arrived colder than the fish-house air, that they had come for her.

Not for the shed. Not for smuggled brandy or evaded customs, though those excuses would serve. For *her.* Someone had written her name into a report, or something close enough to it. An anonymous tip, the sergeant would say later. A concerned citizen.

She thought, absurdly, of Miles Hale's face — the young man she had met twice, years ago, all his mother's stubbornness arranged around none of her mother's warmth. He had looked at her then the way boys look at their mothers' friends: as furniture. As things that belong to the house and can therefore be moved or sold. She had thought him harmless. Harmless men were the most dangerous kind, because they never doubted themselves.

"Sergeant." Mrs. Dunn planted herself in the middle of the floor, arms crossed over her apron. "These women have gutted four hundred stone of herring since five o'clock. If you're looking for contraband, the only thing we're hiding is a decent wage."

"I'm looking for a German, madam." The sergeant's voice was flat, administrative, terrible in its calm. "A woman of German parentage, residing under false pretenses. If such a person is among you, she will make herself known, and the rest of you may return to your work."

Silence. The kind of silence Greta had spent eleven months learning to live inside.

Then Mrs. Dunn said, "We're all Swedes and Scots here, Sergeant, and half of us can't spell either," and several women laughed, a brittle, defiant sound — and the sergeant began walking the line of them, examining faces, and Greta turned toward the smokehouse door as if drawn by nothing more than the natural instinct of a worker heading to her post.

No one stopped her. Why would they? She was Frau Berg, limping slightly, moving with the unhurried shuffle of a woman going about her lawful labor. Behind her she heard the sergeant say, "You there — take off your cap," and a girl begin to protest, and he said, "All of you, in time," and Greta pushed open the smokehouse door and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The smokehouse was a long low room hung with racks of herring, thick with a reek of oak smoke that had soaked into the beams over forty years. Light came through gaps in the boards in thin grey blades. At the far end was the flue — the great brick chimney channel where the smoke was drawn out over the fires — and beneath it, the ash pit.

Greta had cleaned that pit herself, in her first weeks, when the foreman gave the worst jobs to the newest woman. She knew the flue's mouth was wide enough for a thin body. She knew the ash pit below it opened onto the yard behind the building, because the sweep came through there every autumn.

She also knew what the papers in her bodice meant if they were found on her. Not prison merely. An internment camp, perhaps; deportation to a country that had disowned her mother's name and would not recognize her father's. Or worse, in these months — the law was a living thing now, growing teeth weekly, and nobody could say where it would stop biting.

Her hands shook so badly she could barely work the strings of her bodice. She drew out the packet: the passport, the letter from the forger's intermediary, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the letters from Rosamund — fifteen years of them, worn soft as cloth at the folds. *Dearest Greta. The bill failed again, and I am so angry I could bite nails. Dearest Greta, they arrested six of our girls at the gate and I have paid their fines and would pay them again. Dearest Greta, if it ever comes to war—*

Footsteps outside. Voices, drawing along the seaward wall.

Greta stood in the smoke-dark with everything she owned in her hands, and made the arithmetic of survival.

The passport she needed. Without it there was no ship, no neutral passage, no life beyond whatever Elodie Vane was arranging — the quiet driver-woman with the watchful eyes whom Rosamund trusted more than any living soul. Greta had met her only once, briefly, and had recognized in her at once the same quality she saw in mirrors: a person made almost entirely of discretion, which is another word for endurance.

But the letters —

The letters were evidence. Not of espionage, nothing so dramatic — only of love and loyalty and a promise made between two women in peacetime, which in wartime was treason enough. And they were not only hers. Every page carried Rosamund's hand, Rosamund's name, Rosamund's guilt. To keep them was to carry a fuse into the heart of Hale House.

To burn them was to burn fifteen years of being loved.

She heard the smokehouse door rattle behind her. A man's voice, muffled: "Check the outbuildings. She'll not have gone far with that limp."

There was no time for ceremony. Greta knelt at the smoldering edge of the fire bed, where the sawdust still glowed orange, and fed the letters to it one at a time — not tearing them, not crumpling them, but laying them flat upon the coals the way one lays flowers on a grave, watching each page brown and curl and lift into flame. She read what she could in the last seconds each offered. *I do not break my promises.* The words blackened first, before the paper took them, and she thought: *then prove it now, Rosamund, because I am burning my half of us to save yours.*

The last letter — the promise itself — she held a moment longer than the rest. Then she laid it down, and it went up in a bloom of light that showed her own face reflected in the brick, tear-streaked and soot-black, and she felt something tear loose in her chest that she suspected would never grow back.

Then she pulled herself up into the flue.

* * *

She came out of the ash pit into the grey morning like something exhumed, coughing, streaked head to foot in soot, the passport pressed flat and safe against her breastbone. Behind her, muffled through brick, she heard the search of the smokehouse — boots, the scrape of racks dragged aside, the sergeant's voice rising in frustration.

She limped — genuinely now, her ankle wrenched in the climb — through the fish-gutted lanes to the one place she had promised herself she would not go unless the world ended: the net loft above the chandlery, where a fisherman named Tam Brechin kept a cot and owed a debt he never spoke of.

He opened the door before she knocked. He had seen the raid from his window; everyone in the harbor had. He looked at her a long moment, at the soot, at the absence of the bundle she always carried.

"Gone?" he said.

"Burned." Her voice came out as ash. "All of it."

He nodded slowly and stepped aside to let her in. "Then you're nobody, Frau Berg. Just a poor Swedish widow with nowhere to be." He poured tea from a blackened kettle, and his hands were steadier than hers. "There's a woman been asking after you. Quiet sort. Drives a motorcar like she was born in it. Left word — said to tell you: *four days, and then the tide won't wait.*"

Greta closed her eyes. Four days. Somewhere inland, a house full of secrets was holding its breath; somewhere in it, a son was counting his inheritance in other people's ruins. She had burned her past to keep her friend's future, and now there was only forward — one forged passport, one fog-bound quay, one chance.

"Tell her," Greta said, opening her eyes, "that I'll be ready. And tell her—" her voice broke, and she let it, because there was no one left alive who remembered why it mattered— "tell her the letters are gone, but the promise isn't. It lives in me now."

Tam Brechin touched his cap, went out, and went down the stair whistling, a man carrying a message that weighed less than a feather and more than a body.

And in the loft above the harbor, Greta Lindqvist — widow of no one, daughter of two countries that had both let her fall — sat down to wait for the tide, listening to the gulls cry over water that had carried her name away and would, God willing, soon carry her too.
