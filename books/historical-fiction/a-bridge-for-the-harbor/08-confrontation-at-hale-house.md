## 8. Confrontation at Hale House

The train from the coast took four hours, and I spent every one of them rehearsing.

I would tell Lady Rosamund plainly. Greta was safe, or nearly safe. The papers were in hand. There was a fisherman named Cobb whose boat owed her a debt from the days when suffragists were chained to railings and Lady Rosamund's money had bought their freedom, and he would take a passenger out to the neutral ships on Thursday night. All of it could be arranged. All of it could be explained.

What I had not rehearsed was what I found instead.

Hale House rose against the grey afternoon the way it always did, its windows catching the last flat light, its gravel drive loud under my boots. But there was a car standing before the steps that did not belong — a low, expensive motorcar with mud on its fenders, and beside it, smoking, the census taker. Mr. Prewitt. He tipped his hat to me as if we were old friends.

"Miss Vane." He drew on his cigarette, unhurried. "They said you'd gone to the coast. Fishing villages are lovely this time of year."

My blood went cold and then colder. "You've been to the house."

"I've been invited," he said pleasantly. "By Mr. Hale himself. A remarkable young man — so devoted to his mother. So concerned about irregularities in the national record." He dropped the cigarette and ground it under his heel with something like ceremony. "Whatever happens next, Miss Vane, remember it was family who opened the door."

He walked past me toward the car. I stood in the drive long enough for my hands to stop shaking, and then I went inside.

* * *

I heard them before I saw them.

The morning room doors stood open, and Lady Rosamund's voice came through them — not raised, never raised, but stripped down to wire. I had heard that voice only twice in fifteen years: once when a constable put hands on her at a demonstration, and once when she read the casualty lists aloud to the servants' hall in August and her breath caught on a name.

"You will sit down, Miles, and you will hear yourself."

"I am sitting, Mother."

I moved along the corridor and stopped where the angle of the doorway hid me but gave me the room: the long table, the cold fire laid but unlit because coal was rationed now even here, and the two of them at either end of it like opposing counsel.

Miles Hale wore his uniform badly, the way some men do — not ill-fitting, but worn as a costume, one hand resting near the collar as though reminding the world of what he'd earned by surviving. His face was flushed and composed at once, a man enjoying his own cruelty and dressing it up as duty.

"The signature is a formality," he said. "Transfer the estate into trust now, with me as trustee, and none of this need go further. The Home Office takes a lenient view of families who report their own irregularities. It's practically patriotic."

"Blackmail is not patriotism. It has never once been patriotism, in any century, in any country, however fine the coat you put on it."

"It's arithmetic." He leaned forward. "There is a name struck through in your old household ledger. Greta Lindqvist. Half German. Your closest friend for eleven years — I remember her, Mother, I sat on her knee. And there is a promise in your own hand, dated 1909, offering her refuge and funds 'if it ever comes to war.' Well. It has come to war, and she is an enemy alien hiding somewhere on this coast under a false name, and you have been aiding her. That is a charge. That is prison. That is the end of this family's name, and I am the only one offering to save it — for a price."

Lady Rosamund did not flinch. She was seventy-one years old and she sat straighter than her son ever would. "And if I refuse?"

"Then I write the letter myself. Tomorrow." He smiled, small and terrible. "I'd rather not. Think of the scandal. Think of the girls at my club. Think of me, Mother — I'm asking you to think of me for once."

"I have thought of little else since the day you were born," she said quietly, "and it has taught me that thinking of someone is not the same as loving them, and that power handed early to an impatient man curdles before it pours."

She reached for the pen tray. My heart stopped — she would sign, she would buy his silence, she would give him everything rather than let Greta burn — and I stepped through the doorway before I could lose my nerve.

"No, ma'am. Don't touch that pen."

* * *

They both turned. Miles recovered first; he had the actor's gift of instant composure. "Miss Vane. You're back from your holiday."

"I wasn't on holiday, Mr. Hale. I went to fetch what you're selling." I walked to the table and stood between them, between the pen tray and the son. "You want to know what I know? I'll trade evenly, since that seems to be the currency of this house today."

"Elodie," Lady Rosamund said softly. A warning. Or a permission. With her they had always been the same word.

"Mr. Prewitt didn't find the ledger entry on his own," I said, looking at Miles. "He told me so in the drive ten minutes ago. He came back to this house *invited*. Someone sent him a letter drawing his attention to the struck-through name. Someone with access to the study, to the correspondence, to the follow-up inquiry the census office filed in June."

Something moved behind Miles's eyes — quick, swallowed. "That proves nothing."

"It proves everything, because I know about the letter you wrote him. I know because the man you paid is not the man you think he is. Mr. Prewitt keeps copies. He showed me one, in fact, before I left the coast — he wanted me to understand that whoever set the dog on us should know the dog bites any hand offered." I let that land. "Your letter is in his possession, Mr. Hale. Signed. Dated. Offering payment for confirmation of an enemy alien harboured at Hale House. Do you understand what that means?"

Miles stood. The flush had drained out of his face, leaving it the colour of tallow. "It means nothing. I was making inquiries as any loyal Englishman—"

"As a loyal Englishman who offered a civil servant fifty pounds to bury what he found, yes. That's not patriotism, that's the same blackmail you're holding over your mother, priced lower and aimed smaller. Only your blackmailer kept receipts." I turned to Lady Rosamund. "He didn't just threaten exposure, ma'am. He caused the inquiry. If there is a raid anywhere on that coast, if Greta is taken, it will be because your son sold her to save his inheritance."

The silence that followed had weight. I heard the clock in the hall counting through it, patient as tide.

Lady Rosamund looked at her son for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and final, the voice of a woman closing a ledger.

"When you were six years old you broke the conservatory glass and blamed the gardener's boy. I paid for the pane and thrashed the boy anyway, because I believed you. I have spent forty years believing you, Miles, because you are mine and belief is what mothers pay. But this—" she touched the unlit fire grate, the cold coal, the whole grey weight of the year "—this I will not believe was done for England. This was done for bricks."

"Mother—"

"Get out of my house."

"You can't disinherit—"

"I can do whatever a signature allows, and I have signed more documents in my life than you have read. Get out. Take your motorcar and your uniform and your arithmetic, and pray the war teaches you what I cannot."

He stood there a moment, and I watched something die in him and something worse get born in its place — I had seen that transmutation once before, in a magistrate's face the day the Cat and Mouse Act passed. Then he straightened his collar, looked at me last of all, and smiled the smile he must have practiced in mirrors.

"Thursday night," he said. "Cobb's boat. You think I don't listen in my own mother's kitchen?" And he walked out, and the front door closed, and the car ground away down the gravel.

* * *

Lady Rosamund sat very still. Then, without looking at me: "How much of that did you arrange, Elodie, and how much happened to you?"

"None of it was arranged. Some of it I made true on the way up the stairs." I set my handbag on the table, and inside it, the forged papers, Cobb's promise, and Prewitt's copied letter. "Ma'am — he knows about Thursday. He'll be at the quay, or he'll send Prewitt, or worse. We can't use the plan we had. We need a new harbour, a new boat, a new night."

She opened her handbag then — hers, not mine — and took out a small key on a faded blue ribbon. She pushed it across the table to me.

"Then you had better decide how we do it," she said. "Because after tonight, I am finished being the one who signs, and you are the one who knows the way."
