## 9. A Bridge for the Harbor

The fog came in off the water at four o'clock, the way it always did in November, and I had never been so grateful to see weather in my life.

I stood at the window of Mrs. Tredwell's back room above the chandlery and watched the harbor dissolve — masts first, then the breakwater, then the low shapes of the trawlers riding at anchor until there was nothing left but lamplight smeared yellow on wet air. Somewhere out in that grey blindness lay the *Vesta*, a Dutch freighter bound for Rotterdam, taking on cargo until midnight and sailing with the tide.

Somewhere in that same blindness was Miles Hale.

I turned from the window. Greta sat on the edge of the cot with her hands folded in her lap, the way she'd sat through every hour of the three days since the raid — still as furniture, patient as a woman who had learned that patience was the only property they couldn't confiscate. Her hair was pinned under a fishwife's cap. Her hands were red from the packing shed, from the brine, from fifteen years of work she had never been born to do.

"The papers," she said. Not a question. She had asked it every hour too.

I took them from inside my coat, where they had lived against my ribs since yesterday: a travel permit, an identity card, a letter of passage stamped with a shipping office's seal that did not exist until I invented it Tuesday night. I had learned the seal from a photograph in the harbor master's own office, held up to the light while he talked to me about the weather. I had learned the ink from the forger, before his price became more than I could pay. The rest I had learned from fifteen years of reading other people's letters — how officials write, how they hesitate over certain words, how they sign their names like men who are bored.

They were good papers. They would have to be.

"Elisabeth Vermeer," Greta read aloud, softly, testing the name the way you test ice. "Of Rotterdam."

"You're going home," I said. "Half of you is."

She looked up at me then, and for a moment the wariness dropped away and I saw the woman Lady Rosamund must have loved — the one who had marched beside her through the mud of 1908, who had held her arm when the constables came, who had laughed at dinner tables where such laughter was dangerous. Fifteen years older. Half a lifetime thinner. But underneath it, the same face.

"Tell her—" Greta began, and stopped. Started again. "Tell Rosamund I kept the promise too. All these years. I never wrote. I never said her name aloud. Not once."

"I'll tell her."

"And tell her—" She pressed her lips together. Whatever else it was, she swallowed it. Some debts are not meant to be spoken; they are meant to be carried. "Tell her the money bought bread for eleven people this winter. Tell her it was not wasted."

"It won't be me telling her," I said. "You'll write to her yourself. From Rotterdam. Under your own name, or another one — but you'll write."

Greta smiled then, small and crooked, and it was the first time I had seen her do it. "You sound like her."

"No," I said. "She sounds like what we ought to be. There's a difference."

* * *

We went down the back stairs at half past ten, into fog so thick it seemed to have weight, to press against the skin like cold wool. I had chosen the route the way I had chosen everything these past weeks — by walking it first, alone, at the same hour, until my feet knew it better than my eyes did. Down Rope Walk. Past the net-drying sheds where the tar smell covered any other smell. Along the quay wall where the stone was slick and the drop to the water was black and absolute.

Tobias Penhale was waiting where I had told him to wait, at the foot of the old slipway, with his boat lantern shuttered to a coin of light and his collar up against the damp.

He was sixty if he was a day, a crabbed, silent man who had fished the harbor for forty years and spoken perhaps forty words to me in all the time I'd driven Lady Rosamund along this coast. When I had come to him three days ago and put the matter to him plainly, he had listened without moving his face, and then he had said only: "In 'nine she paid my fine when they took my boat for blocking the road at the rally. Never asked why I was there." A pause. "Never asked nothing since, neither. That woman."

Now he looked at Greta — one long look, taking in the cap, the red hands, the fear she wore like the rest of us wear coats — and he nodded once, as if confirming a delivery of herring.

"Tide turns at one," he said. "I'll put you alongside the *Vesta*'s ladder at quarter past twelve. Fog holds till then, likely. After that, God and the Dutchman's lookout." He spat neatly over the quay wall. "Walk behind me. If anyone stops us, you're my sister's girl, come to help gut the morning catch, and you're simple. No offense meant."

"None taken," Greta said, and something in her voice made even Tobias's mouth twitch.

We moved into the fog.

It was the longest walk of my life, though it could not have taken more than twenty minutes. The fog ate sound as well as sight; our footsteps came back to us muffled, as if someone else were walking just behind, matching us step for step. Once a night watchman's lantern bloomed out of the white not six feet away, and Tobias stopped, unhurried, and called out a greeting about the weather, and the watchman grumbled about the weather, and the light passed on and dissolved. Greta's hand found my sleeve and gripped it. Through the wool I could feel her trembling, fine and constant, like a wire under strain.

Then the quay opened around us — I felt it rather than saw it, the sudden absence of walls — and ahead, faint as a memory, the *Vesta*'s riding lights burned twin smudges in the fog.

And between us and them, a figure stepped out of the white.

I knew him before I knew anything else about him. I knew the set of the shoulders, the particular way he carried his head, the silhouette I had watched from the driver's seat for two decades — through summers and funerals and Christmas homecomings. Miles Hale. In civilian clothes, collarless, as if he had dressed in a hurry, and holding a lantern that he made no attempt to hide.

"Well," he said. His voice carried strangely in the fog, close and far at once. "I wondered which door you'd use. The customs house is watching the main quay, you know. Has been since Wednesday. But Penhale's slipway—" He clicked his tongue. "Sentiment. It's always sentiment that undoes people."

"Miles." I put myself half in front of Greta, an instinct older than thought. "Go home."

"To Hale House?" Something moved in his voice that was almost a laugh. "To what, exactly? Mother has already signed the letter disinheriting me — oh yes, I've seen it, she keeps her solicitor's drafts in the library like a fool. You've seen to that, haven't you? You and your little confidences." He raised the lantern slightly, and its light found us: Tobias immovable as a bollard, Greta pale as the fog itself, me with my hand still on her sleeve. "A German spy smuggled out of England on a fishing boat, with forged papers, from my family's harbor. Do you know what that's worth? The authorities would give me anything I asked. And after tonight I wouldn't need it — I'd have the story itself. Leverage enough for a lifetime."

"Then call out," I said. My voice surprised me by being steady. "Call the customs house. Shout. Bring them all down on us."

"Don't tempt me."

"But you won't." I took a step toward him, into the edge of his lantern light, and made myself look at his face — the face of a boy I had once driven to the station with sweets in his pocket, grown into this. "Because if you shout, you have to explain how you knew. How you followed us. How you tipped off the raid on the packing shed last week and got nothing for it. You'd be the son who informed on his mother's friend for money, and men like you can't bear to be seen clearly, Miles. You'd rather lose everything than be *known*."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — I thought I had misjudged him. His jaw worked. The lantern trembled, just slightly, in his grip.

"You're nobody," he said at last, and the words came out cracked. "You're a servant. You don't get to stand there and—"

"She gets to stand wherever she likes," Tobias Penhale said, in the flat voice of a man stating the depth of a channel, "because I say so, and this is my harbor."

And then the foghorn sounded — one deep groan off the breakwater, the *Vesta* signaling her readiness — and in the half-second that Miles's head turned toward the sound, I moved. I pushed Greta toward the dark shape of the slipway and Tobias's waiting boat, and Tobias's hand closed on her arm and drew her down into the black, and I heard the soft knock of wood against wood, the creak of a thwart taking weight.

Miles lunged past me — I caught his coat, and he caught mine, and for a moment we grappled like children in the fog, breath ragged, his lantern spinning wild patterns across the wet stones. He was stronger. He was going to win. And then his heel found the slick weed at the slipway's edge and he went down hard onto one knee, and the lantern rolled free and guttered in a puddle, and I let go of him because there was no more time, none, the tide was turning and the fog was thinning at its edges and somewhere below us Tobias was pulling for the ship's ladder with a woman who had waited fifteen years for this night.

I stood over Miles Hale in the dying lantern light.

"You could have been your mother's son," I said. "That was the tragedy. Not the estate. Not the money. Just — that."

Then I ran.

* * *

I did not watch the boarding. Tobias had been clear about that, and Tobias knew harbors the way I knew letters. I stood instead at the top of the slipway, soaked to the knees, my chest heaving, and listened: the dip of oars fading into the fog, the faint foreign murmur of voices at the ladder, a pause — a long, terrible pause in which I aged a year — and then, faint and rising, the rattle of the chain being drawn up, and the bell of the *Vesta* ringing twice, and the foghorn answering from the breakwater.

Sailing signals. Cleared and going.

I walked back along the quay wall with the fog closing behind me like water behind a swimmer, and I did not look at the dark shape of the *Vesta*, and I did not look for Miles, and I did not let myself feel anything at all until I reached the chandlery steps and my legs simply refused to carry me further and I sat down on the cold stone and put my face in my hands.

The promise was kept. Fifteen years of silence, one week of terror, and it was done.

Which meant, I understood as I sat there in the salt-smelling dark, that there was nothing left between me and the reckoning. Lady Rosamund would want to know everything. The authorities would want to know more. And somewhere behind me on that quay, Miles Hale had picked himself up out of the wet with his leverage gone and his humiliation complete — and men like Miles did not forgive witnesses.

From the direction of the harbor road, through the thinning fog, came the sound of wheels. A carriage, moving fast, toward the chandlery.

I stood up, and wiped my face, and waited to see whose it was.
