## 10. Inheritance

The carriage belonged to Lady Rosamund, and she came alone.

She stepped down from it without waiting for the chandler's boy to bring the step, which was unlike her, and stood in the doorway of the chandlery with the fog curling at her skirts like something that had followed her from home. She had dressed quickly — I could see that, the hat pinned crooked, no gloves — and her face was the face of a woman who had spent a night doing arithmetic she did not like.

"Where is she?" she said.

"Gone. The *Valkyrien* sailed on the tide. Norwegian registry, calling at Bergen before any port that extradites." I heard my own voice, flat and steady, and marveled at it. "She'll write when she's settled. Under a new name. You'll never know what it is."

Lady Rosamund closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every year of her age, and more — looked, in fact, like the woman in the photograph I had once found in a drawer, laughing on a beach with another woman's arm through hers, fifteen years and a war ago.

"And you?" she said. "The constable at the quay — he'll have your description."

"He has Miles's description too. And Miles will not be making statements tonight." I let that sit. I would not tell her yet what her son had done — the tip to the authorities, the raid, the smokehouse, the terror in Greta's voice as the boards gave way behind her. She would learn it. It would be among the last things I ever gave her, and I wanted her to receive it sitting down, in daylight, with time to arrange her face. Even now I was managing her silences for her. Fifteen years of habit does not break in a week.

"Come," she said at last. "Get in the carriage."

"No, my lady." I said it gently. "Not yet. There are things that must be said first, and I won't say them in a moving box."

* * *

We sat in the chandlery office among coils of rope and ledgers that smelled of tar, and I told her all of it. The forger, and what his fee had been — not money; information, names, the geography of Lady Rosamund's own network laid out on a workbench like a fish to be gutted. The raid. The papers lost in the smokehouse. The letter I had burned in the grate of the packing-shed stove at four in the morning, my own correspondence with her, three years of instructions and reassurances gone up in a smell like burning hair, because paper is patient and people hang.

And then I told her about Miles.

I watched her take it the way one takes a diagnosis. Not surprise — confirmation. That was the worst part, watching her not be surprised.

"He told me you'd gone thieving," she said quietly. "He came to me two days ago with the census man's letters and said you were blackmailing me. He said it so smoothly. I almost—" She stopped. Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles white. "I signed nothing. But I was going to. I want you to know that. I was going to give him the estate rather than let him hand Greta to the authorities. So whatever else happens, Elodie, know that I would have paid."

"You paid anyway," I said. "You paid with me."

She flinched. Good, I thought, and was ashamed of thinking it.

Miles was gone within the month. Disinherited by deed and by announcement, cut off with a sum calculated to insult, gone to London to live off friends and grievance in equal measure. The solicitors called it irrevocable. Lady Rosamund called it overdue. The village called it, for a season, the only topic worth salt over, and then the war took even scandal's place at the table, because the war took everything's place eventually.

But that night, in the chandlery, none of it had happened yet. That night she only reached across the desk and took my hand, and hers was cold, and neither of us said anything for a long while, because there are silences that protect people and silences where people hide from themselves, and we sat in both at once.

"You could have run," she said finally. "When the raid came. You could have walked away and been nobody's driver, nobody's gatekeeper, nobody's accomplice. Why didn't you?"

I thought about lying to her, out of fifteen years of practice. Then I thought about Greta Lindqvist standing in the fish-smell dark of the smokehouse, holding out her hand to a stranger and saying, *You must be Rosamund's other promise.*

"Because someone made a promise," I said, "and everyone she'd have needed to keep it was busy being careful. Somebody has to stop being careful, my lady. It might as well be the person who was never promised anything."

* * *

A year later.

The house on the hill above the harbor town is smaller than Hale House by a factor one could measure in servants, and it suits me. Two rooms downstairs, two up, a garden that goes wild at the edges because I like it wild, and a view of the water that on fog mornings looks exactly the way it looked from the quay the night the *Valkyrien* took on her last passenger. I look at it every morning. I have decided this is not penance. Penance implies regret.

The town knows me as Mrs. Vane, widow, who keeps accounts for the fish merchants and writes letters for families whose men are at sea or in France, and who asks no questions because she answers none. It is remarkable, the respectability that attaches itself to a woman who can read and keeps her mouth shut. I learned that at seventeen over a kitchen table, sorting other people's post. Everything I am, I learned in service. I only changed whose.

A letter comes sometimes from Sweden. It is addressed to a name I invented myself, which pleases me more than I can say — that Greta Lindqvist writes confidently to a fiction of my manufacture, trusting my forgery with her life a second time. The letters are short. Herring prices. A room with a stove that works. Once, pressed between the pages, a pressed flower I did not recognize, and beneath it a line in her careful hand: *Tell her the arm healed crooked but the grip is fine.* I sent that line south in a letter of my own, and got back no reply at all, which from Lady Rosamund is the equivalent of weeping.

Because Lady Rosamund writes. Of course she writes. She has written eleven times since I left her service, and I have answered six.

The eleventh came yesterday, and it was different. Thicker paper. Her seal, unbroken until mine.

*Elodie,* it began, in the upright hand that had corrected my first attempts at formal correspondence twenty-five years ago, *the movement is not what it was and the women are scattered, but scattered is not finished. I have funds that Miles cannot touch and a house in the south of the town that sits empty, and I intend to make of them something useful — a place where women passing through trouble can pass further, quietly, with papers and money and a name that holds. I have done this badly before, alone, with promises instead of systems. You did it once, in fog, in a week, flawlessly.*

*I am offering you partnership. Half of everything — the house, the fund, the risk, the blame. Come and build it with me. I am sixty-eight years old and tired of gatekeepers. I want a keeper of bridges.*

*I will wait for your answer however long it takes. But I confess I hope it takes no time at all.*

*R.H.*

I sat with it for a long evening, the fire going down, the harbor lights coming on one by one across the water like a slow argument being won.

It was a generous offer. It was, I suspect, the most honest thing she had ever put on paper, and I do not doubt her sincerity, and that is precisely the difficulty. Because I know how her promises are made — magnificently, at kitchen tables, in drawing rooms, in the full flood of certainty — and I know how they are kept: deferred, delegated, struck through in ledgers and left for other hands to honor at gunpoint. She means everything she says. Meaning it has never once been the problem. The problem is that Lady Rosamund Hale has always believed the world would bend before her word did, and the war has taught the rest of us which bends first.

Partnership with her means her name over the door and her methods in the walls and, someday, another girl in service learning to read post and keep silence and call it safety. I would be the bridge, yes. Bridges exist to be walked on.

So tomorrow I will write my answer, and it will be the truest letter I have ever composed, because I owe her that much and she can afford it.

*I decline, with gratitude and love,* I will say. *But I am already doing the work, and here is proof: three women passed through this harbor this winter on papers no authority questioned, and none of them knew my name, which is how it should be. Keep your fund and your empty house and spend them well. What I ask instead is small. A letter of introduction — to the shipping agents in Hull, the solicitor in Rotterdam, the Quaker network in Amsterdam. Names, my lady. Addresses. The map you never let me see.*

*I will build my own bridge, and I will charge no toll except passage.*

*You taught me that the safest person in a dangerous house is the one nobody counts. I intend to remain uncounted, and to be very, very useful.*

*Yours in the work,*

*E. Vane*

I set the draft aside and banked the fire, and stood a moment at the window with the truth I carry — Greta safe in Bergen, the flower pressed flat, Miles gone to seed in some London boarding house, a promise kept by a woman nobody had ever thought to promise anything. It had cost me a household, a country of my own making, fifteen years of belonging. I would pay it again before breakfast.

Survival's price, paid in full, and worth every shilling.

Below in the town, a light still burned in the fish-packing shed where a girl I'd helped south last month used to work. Someone was moving inside it. I watched, and waited, and reached instinctively for the coat with the forged papers sewn into the lining.

Old habits. The best ones, I've found, are the ones you keep on purpose.
