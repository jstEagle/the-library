## 1. The Weight of Water

The water came in the way it always did — politely at first, then all at once.

I had learned to measure a leak by its manners. A polite leak was a seep, a whisper through a seam, something you could bail with a bucket and a curse. An impolite leak announced itself with a shove. This one shoved. The hold of the *Kestrel's Wing* tilted her cargo of tin and gutta-percha toward her ribs like a drunk leaning on a friend, and I stood calf-deep in the dark listening to the sea make its argument.

Most men would have shouted. Shouting is the first instinct of the drowning and the last refuge of the stupid. I did not shout. I put my ear to the hull the way you'd put your ear to a door, and I listened.

There are two sounds a ship makes when she's lying to you. One is the groan of timber flexing, which is honest work and nothing to fear. The other is a kind of wet breathing, arrhythmic, greedy — that's water finding a home it intends to keep. The *Kestrel's Wing* was doing both, but the second one was coming from forward, near the chain locker, which meant the caulking there had given up its religion.

I climbed out onto the deck and said nothing for a while, because the deckhand on watch, a Javanese boy named Yusuf who feared the bosun more than God, was watching me, and I wanted to see what his face did when it thought mine wasn't looking. Faces are honest for about half a second before they remember their employment. Yusuf's face did this: relief, quickly smothered; then a flicker of guilt, because he'd noticed the list an hour ago and said nothing; then a small, sour calculation about whether my noticing would become his trouble.

That last one told me everything. A man who calculates rather than confesses has already decided what he'll say under questioning. So when the bosun came stomping down the deck demanding to know why we rode like a market boat full of pigs, I simply looked at Yusuf, and Yusuf looked at the horizon, and I understood that whatever story got told tonight, I would not be in it. Fine. I'd survived five years by being no one's story.

"The hold," I said to the bosun. "Forward, by the locker."

He squinted at me. "How d'you know?"

"Water's where water isn't supposed to be," I said. "It usually comes from somewhere."

He stared at me a moment longer than the remark deserved, the way men do when they suspect a joke has been made at their expense but can't find the punchline. Then he bellowed for the pump crew, and I went back to my corner of the deck and let the ship be someone else's problem until morning.

You will want to know why a fifteen-year-old indentured clerk's apprentice was riding a leaky sloop up the straits instead of copying figures in a cool counting house. The answer is arithmetic, as most answers were, in those years. The Straits Trading Company had decided that its junior copyist could learn more about freight by seeing freight, and the company's factor, Mr. Edmund Harrow, had decided it personally, with a smile I have spent years trying to forget and will spend the rest of these pages failing to. So I went to sea for three weeks in the wet season, in a hull held together by optimism and pitch, because a man whose family owes money does not get asked whether he'd prefer otherwise.

But I'm ahead of myself. Water does that to a man — makes him tell things out of order. Let me start where the water started.

* * *

My father drowned on a Tuesday in March, four days before the monsoon turned, in sight of the lighthouse at Tanjung Rimau. I know it was Tuesday because my mother counted Tuesdays afterward the way other women count coins, hoarding them, turning them over, checking each one for wear.

His name was Eben Blackwood, and he was a pearl diver, which is to say he made his living holding his breath while the world tried to kill him. He was good at it. The old divers said he could stay down longer than any man in the kampong, that he'd once brought up a shell the size of a church bell from nine fathoms, and inside it a pearl so fine the Chinese trader who bought it paid in silver and apologized for the weight of the box.

What the stories left out was the advance. There was always an advance. The company lent him against the season's take — rice, oil, the repair of his boat, medicine for my grandmother Sefa's lungs — and the debt rolled over like a wave, gathering interest the way waves gather sand. My father signed with a thumbprint he couldn't read the words above. None of us could. We trusted the numbers would be fair because the man holding the book wore a clean shirt, and in our world a clean shirt stood for honesty the way a lighthouse stands for safety: visible from far off, useless when you're close enough to need it.

On the last Tuesday, his boat went down in weather the fishing fleet had already judged too foul to sail in. Three boats stayed ashore. His went out. I was eleven, and I watched from the seawall with my mother's hand crushing my shoulder, and I remember thinking, with the cold clarity children are cursed with, that the boat had gone out *on purpose*, that somebody had needed it gone, and I hated myself for the thought even as it arrived.

It took them two days to find him. The sea gives back what it owes eventually, my grandmother says, though she says it in a language older than the trading houses, and I didn't understand her then.

The company sent a man within the week. Not a mourner — a counter. His name was Prosser, and he had the particular gentleness of men who handle other people's ruin professionally. He sat in our house on the one stool, drank the tea my mother couldn't afford, and opened his ledger across his knees like a man unveiling an altar.

"Mrs. Blackwood," he said, "the account stands at one hundred and forty-two dollars."

My mother looked at the ledger upside down, which is how she saw it, being illiterate, and I saw her do the thing I have seen a thousand desperate people do since: she nodded. Nodding costs nothing. It buys time. It is the currency of people who aren't permitted to negotiate.

"There are provisions," Prosser continued, "for the settlement of debts of this character." And here he paused, and here is where I learned my trade, though I didn't know it yet. He paused *too long*. A pause before bad news is sympathy. A pause after it is theater. But a pause in the middle of a sentence, where no pause belongs — that is where a man hides the part of the sentence he hopes you won't hear.

"The company," he said, "is prepared to accept — " and there it was, the little hitch, the false bottom in his voice — "labor, in lieu of further payment."

My mother's hands were in her lap, around a net she was mending, and they went still. I have described those hands elsewhere in this account and I will describe them again before I'm done, because everything in our story lives or dies by what those hands chose to do. They were diver's hands, net-mender's hands, cracked at every knuckle, and they stopped moving the way birds stop singing when a hawk crosses the yard.

"I have three children," she said.

Prosser looked at us. I mean that literally — he looked at us the way a factor looks at freight, and I felt it happen, felt myself change in his eyes from a boy into a line item. His gaze moved across Mariel, six years old and chewing a strand of hair, across baby Dev asleep against my mother's back, and settled on me, because I was the oldest and the largest and therefore, in his arithmetic, the most solvent.

"The boy," Prosser said, "would be entered as an apprentice. Five years' service against the principal and interest. The remainder of the debt," and here the hitch again, smooth as a drawer sliding shut, "may be restructured."

My mother did not answer immediately, and in her silence I heard the whole conversation that never happened: *If I refuse, the debt remains and grows. If I consent, I keep two children and lose one. If I weep, he will wait, and waiting costs interest.* She was performing the same arithmetic I would perform for the rest of my life, and I hated that she was good at it, and I loved her for surviving it.

"Yes," she said finally, in a voice like a rope taking strain. "Yes."

I want to be fair to her, whoever reads this. She believed she was buying my future. Apprentice sounded like a word with a ladder in it. Nobody had told her that the company's ladders only went down.

* * *

They collected me on a Thursday. Of course they did — Tuesday for the drowning, Thursday for the collection; the company kept a tidy calendar.

A cart came to the kampong, and a clerk named Ambrose Fenn sat on it beside my single bundle of belongings. Fenn was older than the others, grey at the temples, with ink stains worked so deep into his fingers they looked like bruises. He did not smile at me, which I later understood was a courtesy. Men who smiled at me in those years were almost uniformly selling something.

"You're the Blackwood boy," he said.

I said nothing. Silence was free, and I had learned its price the hard way: my father had been a talker, warm and loud, the sort of man who narrated his own dives for the benefit of the shore, and the company had found his voice easy to quote, easy to twist, easy to drown out. I resolved early that no one would ever quote me.

Fenn waited. Most men fill silence like a leaking hull fills with water — inevitably, and to their own detriment. Fenn didn't. He studied me instead, with an expression I couldn't read then and can now: recognition. He was looking at a version of himself thirty years younger, though I lacked the equipment to know it.

"You'll do well," he said finally, "if you learn to be quiet in the right places."

"And loud in the wrong ones?" I asked, surprising myself.

Something moved behind his eyes — approval, quickly filed away. "No," he said. "Just quiet in the right places. That's rarer."

The cart rolled, and the kampong shrank behind us, and my mother stood at the seawall not waving, because waving is what you do when you believe the person is coming back, and she was too honest a woman to pretend. Mariel broke loose and ran along the sand shouting my name until the wind took it, and I kept my face forward and dry because the clerk was watching, and I had already learned that grief observed is grief taxed.

Five years. That was the term. Five years of quiet, in exchange for one hundred and forty-two dollars and the difference between my father's thumbprint and his grave.

* * *

Now it is night on the *Kestrel's Wing*, and the pumps have won their argument with the sea, for the moment. The bosun bought me a cup of arrack I didn't drink, and told me I had a sailor's eye, and asked how I knew where the leak was, and I gave him the answer about water being where water shouldn't be, and he laughed, satisfied, because men prefer simple answers from boys.

The true answer is uglier. The true answer is that I grew up in a flooded house after my father died — rain through the attap roof, tide through the floorboards at spring tides — and I learned every sound water makes when it's taking something from you. There is no version of that education I'd wish on anyone, and yet it has made me useful, and usefulness is the only armor the poor are issued.

I stand at the rail now, watching the lights of Singapore come up out of the dark — the harbor lamps, the counting houses, the warehouses where the company keeps its books. Somewhere in one of those buildings is a ledger with my name in it, written in a hand I am learning to imitate, recording a debt I was born into and cannot read my way out of.

Somewhere in one of those buildings is a man named Edmund Harrow who smiled at me three weeks ago and called this voyage an *education*, and I have spent three weeks wondering what the pause in the middle of his sentence was hiding.

Tomorrow we dock. Tomorrow I go back to the copying room, to the ink and the columns and the long arithmetic of other people's ruin.

But tonight, watching the water slide black beneath the bow, I think about my father's boat going out on a Tuesday it should have stayed ashore, and I think about Prosser's hitch, and Fenn's pauses, and Harrow's smile — all the silences I've learned to read — and I understand, with the patience of the drowned and the clarity of the indebted, that the company's books are not records.

They are weapons. And someday soon, someone is going to point one at my family.

The lamps of the harbor grow closer. I count them, because counting is what I have instead of prayer.

Forty-one. Forty-two.

One of them, I will learn, is lit for me.
