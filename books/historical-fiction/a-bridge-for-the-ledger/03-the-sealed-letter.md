## 3. The Sealed Letter

There is a particular way a man holds a letter he does not want read, and I had learned it the way other boys learn to swim — by nearly drowning in it.

Ambrose Fenn held his letters flat against his chest, as though the paper might hear something useful if pressed close enough to his heart. The factor, Harrow, held his at arm's length with two fingers, the way one handles a fish that has begun to smell. And the shipping master, Quill, folded his in half and half again until they were small enough to swallow, which I suspected was not far from what he did with them.

So when Fenn handed me a sealed packet on a Tuesday morning and said, "The factor's office, Silas, and mind you don't dawdle," I noticed three things at once.

First, it was heavy for its size. Wax and paper and perhaps two sheets inside, no more, but dense, the way a thing is dense when someone has pressed hard while writing.

Second, the seal was not Fenn's. Fenn used grey wax, cheap, the kind that crumbles if you look at it unkindly. This was black, stamped with the company's anchor-and-crown, and the stamp had been applied twice — once properly, once crooked over the first impression, as if a man's hand had shaken.

Third, Fenn would not look at me. In five years of copying his figures, I had catalogued every variety of Ambrose Fenn's gaze: the downward glance of a man checking sums, the sideways glance of a man checking me, the middle-distance glance of a man checking his conscience. This was none of them. This was a man looking at my collar because my collar could not accuse him of anything.

"Mind you don't dawdle," he said again, which was itself a curiosity, because Fenn had never in his life cared whether I dawdled. Dawdling was, in fact, one of my few sanctioned pleasures. A boy who walks slowly through Singapore is invisible. A boy who runs is either late or stealing, and both invite questions.

"I'll go directly," I said, and meant to.

* * *

Let me explain about the route from the counting house to the factor's office, because geography is a conspirator too, and on that Tuesday it conspired with me before I knew I wanted anything conspired upon.

The offices of the Straits Trading Company stood along Collyer Quay like teeth in a jaw, and between the counting house and the factor's wing there ran a covered walkway open to the harbour on one side. At midday the walkway filled with clerks going to their rice, porters going nowhere in particular, and the smell of salt and tar and frying fish that I have never since been able to separate from the feeling of being watched.

Halfway along this walkway stood a recessed doorway where the company kept its fire buckets. It was a shallow alcove, dark even at noon, and everyone walked past it without seeing it, because that is what people do with dark places they have decided contain nothing.

I had decided, some months earlier, that this alcove was where I would learn things, if ever I needed to learn them. I hadn't known what things. I am not a boy who plans crimes; I am a boy who keeps exits mapped. There is a difference, though I grant it is narrower than I once believed.

I went directly, as promised. I delivered the packet to Harrow's outer clerk, who logged it without reading the direction, because the outer clerk had the gift of seeing nothing, which is why he was the outer clerk. And then I walked back along the walkway, past the alcove, and stopped.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is otherwise scarce in this account. I did not stop because of some grand intuition. I stopped because the packet had been heavy, and heaviness had been sitting in my mind all morning like a stone in a shoe. Two sheets of paper should not weigh like that unless the writing was thick, and writing is only thick when a man has gone over his words more than once, and a man only goes over his words when he is afraid of how they will be read.

Fenn was afraid. Fenn, who had survived thirty years in that building by being afraid of nothing visible.

I stepped into the alcove and told myself I was only resting out of the sun.

* * *

Here is what I knew about seals at nineteen, which is more than most boys know and less than I would come to know within the week. Black wax takes an impression cleanly but releases it poorly; the stamp tends to grip. If a seal has been doubled — stamped, then restamped — the second impression sits proud of the first, and a thin blade laid flat against the paper can find the seam the way a diver's fingers find the seam of a closed oyster.

My father taught me oysters. He used to say the shell tells you everything if your hands are patient: the weight of the meat inside, whether the pearl has formed or the oyster has only made mud. *Patience in the hands,* he'd say, *and the sea can't lie to you.* Then the sea lied to him comprehensively, but that is a matter for another chapter, and mine, not yours.

I had no blade. What I had was the bone folder from my breast pocket — a copyist's tool, flat, thin, ivory-handled, the one luxury I owned and the one nobody questioned, because a copyist without a folder is a soldier without boots.

I will not pretend my hands were steady. They were not. But they were patient, and patience turned out to be enough. The black wax lifted along its crooked second seam with a sound like a small sigh, and the packet opened, and I read.

* * *

You must understand what reading meant to me in those days. I had been taught my letters by a mission schoolmaster who charged my mother two fish a week and gave up on me after four months, declaring I had "a memory but no imagination," which remains the finest compliment anyone has paid me. Everything else I had taught myself in the counting house, copying entries I was forbidden to understand, learning the grammar of commerce the way a servant learns the language of the house: thoroughly, secretly, and always slightly beneath the surface of politeness.

So I read fast. Faster than was safe. And the letter was short, which was merciful, because shortness meant I could hold all of it in my head at once, and holding it whole mattered, as you will see.

It began *Dear Sir* — Fenn wrote to Harrow as *Dear Sir*, thirty years of service compressed into two words — and then it came to the point with a directness Fenn never permitted himself aloud:

> *Regarding the accounts flagged last quarter. The following names have been entered on the conspiracy schedule for the Meridian's next sailing: TAN WEI MING, fisherman, Telok Ayer; RAHIM BIN SULAIMAN, boatman, Kampong Glam; BLACKWOOD, IDA, widow, and dependents (3), Tanjong Rhu.*

Dependents, three. My mother. My sister. My grandmother, who had not walked unaided since the year the monsoon took our roof.

I read the rest standing very still, which is a skill of mine. There were dates — the Meridian, six weeks out, berths allocated, costs itemized against the families' supposed debts. There was a paragraph about *evidence to be consolidated*, and another about *the factor's preference that matters conclude quietly*. And near the end, a sentence I have carried so long it has worn smooth as a pebble:

> *The Blackwood account requires settlement. Five years' maintenance against the estate of the deceased exceeds any honest recovery. The schedule provides the remedy.*

Five years' maintenance. That was me. That was the rice in my bowl and the mat on my floor and the shirt on my back, entered as debt against a dead pearl diver's estate, accumulating interest, until the arithmetic concluded that the only asset remaining in the Blackwood name was the labor of the three Blackwoods still breathing.

They had not caught my family smuggling. They had invented the smuggling so that the paperwork would have somewhere to go. A conspiracy needs conspirators the way a ledger needs columns, and we had been written in.

* * *

I would like to report that I felt terror. Everyone expects terror, and indeed terror arrived — but it arrived third, and I have always remembered the order, because the order told me something about myself I did not wish to know.

What came first was recognition. The cold, clerical pleasure of a pattern completing. *Of course,* said some dry voice in me that sounded unpleasantly like Fenn. *Of course it's arithmetic.* The debts inflated, the families transferred, the ships sailing on schedule — I had copied fragments of this machine for five years without seeing it whole, the way a man copying a map stroke by stroke may fail to notice he is drawing a coastline he knows. Now the strokes joined. The coast appeared. It was shaped like my mother's hands.

What came second was anger, and I mean anger in its oldest sense — not heat but wrongness, the way a diver knows a current has shifted before his body drifts. My father had drowned in water the company called an act of God. My mother had mended nets for five years under the eye of men who counted her children like cargo. And all of it — the drowning, the indenture, the hunger dressed up as maintenance — all of it resolved, in the end, into a line in a column: *exceeds any honest recovery.*

Honest. They had used the word *honest*. Somewhere a man had dipped his pen and written *honest* about my family's ruin, and his hand had not trembled.

Terror came third, when it came at all, and it came not as a flood but as a single precise thought: *they will sail her in six weeks, and she will pack willingly, because she trusts that obedience is protection.* That was the worst of it. Not the chains — the willingness. My mother had spent five years teaching herself that the company's patience was a wall between us and worse things. She would board that ship believing she was doing right.

I put the letter back together with hands that had stopped pretending to be steady and started simply being careful. Bone folder flat, wax warmed against my palm until it grew obedient, pressed down along its old seam. The double stamp settled crookedly, exactly as it had been. To any eye but mine, untouched.

Then I stood in the dark alcove beside the fire buckets and discovered the fourth thing, the thing that had not been in the letter at all.

Nobody was looking for me.

I had stolen perhaps four minutes from the company's day. Four minutes, and the world above the alcove had continued its noon traffic of clerks and porters and frying fish, entirely indifferent, because Silas Blackwood was a boy whose entire art was being forgettable. I had spent five years making myself into a hole in the air, and now the hole in the air had read a state secret, and no one had noticed, because no one ever noticed.

That was the taste of purpose. I will describe it accurately, because novelists lie about such things and I am trying not to. Purpose, at first, tastes like nothing. It tastes like the moment after a wave passes over you and you are still underwater and alive, and the roar recedes, and you think: *I am still here. The sea does not know it missed.*

I am still here. The company does not know it missed.

* * *

I returned to the counting house by the long way, past the fish market, because a boy carrying nothing should look like a boy who dawdled, and I had a reputation to maintain. I sat at my desk. I copied entries. My pen moved; my figures balanced; somewhere in the building a clock counted out the afternoon in the company's usual currency, which is other people's time.

Fenn passed behind my chair once, twice, a third time. On the third pass he paused — just paused, a man consulting his conscience or his sciatica — and said, without looking at me:

"The tide comes in early tomorrow."

"Yes, Mr. Fenn."

"Best not be on the flats when it does." And he moved on, an old man shuffling toward evening, leaving me to decide whether I had been warned, threatened, or merely spoken at.

I decided, that night, lying on my mat in the loft above the counting house, listening to the harbour breathe, that it did not yet matter which. What mattered was the letter's contents sitting in my head, whole and perfect, needing no paper — because a memory cannot be seized, cannot be burned, cannot be entered into evidence against the one who holds it. They had taken everything else from my family: the boat, the advance, the father, the years. They had not yet learned to take what a boy remembers.

*Tan Wei Ming, fisherman, Telok Ayer. Rahim bin Sulaiman, boatman, Kampong Glam. Blackwood, Ida, widow, and dependents, three.*

Three. Grandmother Sefa, who could not walk. Mariel, who was fourteen and scrubbed floors in the factor's own house. My mother, whose hands I could see even now, in the dark, working netting twine the way they had worked it every night of my life — patient, quick, and utterly unaware that in six weeks the company intended to close them around a ship's rail instead of a net.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I would find a reason to walk to Tanjong Rhu. Tomorrow I would stand on the shore where my father's boat had gone down, and look at the kampong, and look at her.

Tonight I lay still and practiced the only prayer I have ever trusted, which is repetition. Names, dates, figures. Again. Again. The sea gives nothing back for free — my father's saying, and he paid full price for it — but it keeps faithful accounts, and so, from that night forward, would I.

Somewhere below me, in the false bottom of a drawer, Ambrose Fenn slept badly beside his secrets. Somewhere across the town, Edmund Harrow slept well beside his. And somewhere in between, in a loft that smelled of tar and ink, a copyist's son began the slow work of becoming something the ledger had no column for.

The tide, Fenn had said, comes in early.

He had no idea how early.
