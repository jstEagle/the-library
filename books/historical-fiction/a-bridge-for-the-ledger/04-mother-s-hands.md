## 4. Mother's Hands

The nets tell you everything about a family if you know how to read them. Ours told of a woman who mended too carefully — every knot doubled, every tear patched with a patience that outlasted the tearing. The other wives in the kampong worked fast and loose and laughed about it. I worked slow because slow was the only speed at which my hands could think, and my hands had been doing my thinking for five years now, ever since the sea took my husband's body and the company took everything else.

A hole in the net this morning, third one from the left float, where the twine had rotted through. I drew it closed with a weaver's knot, which holds against strain better than any other kind, and I thought: *there is a lesson in that, Ida, if you cared to learn it.* But lessons cost, in my experience, and I had already paid more than the whole kampong put together.

"You'll blind yourself before noon," said old Chandra from two huts down, not looking up from her own work. Chandra was eighty if she was sixty, and had buried two husbands and one company clerk — the clerk by her own hand, though nobody ever proved it, and nobody ever tried very hard. She was the closest thing I had to a friend, which tells you something about the state of my life.

"The light is good," I said.

"The light has always been good. Your eyes have not."

I let that stand. Arguing with Chandra was like arguing with the tide; you might win for an hour, but the water would come back to its own opinion eventually.

* * *

My husband Ansel Blackwood was a pearl diver, and he was the best I ever saw, and I say that with the authority of a woman who grew up watching men hold their breath until their faces turned the color of dusk. He could go down on one lungful and come back with three oysters and his wits about him, which was two more oysters and considerably more wit than most. He used to say the sea keeps faithful accounts — that it gives nothing for free, but it never cheats you either. It pays what it owes and takes what it's owed, and a man who understands this can live beside it all his days without fear.

He understood it. That was the tragedy. He understood the sea perfectly.

It was the *company* he misjudged.

They came to him in the year the pearl beds thinned — came with soft voices and softer paper, an advance against future harvests, just enough coin to keep us fed through the monsoon. Ansel signed because Silas had fever and medicine costs money, and a drowning man will grab at anything, even a rope made of arithmetic. I remember standing in the doorway of our hut while the company man explained the terms, and I remember thinking the terms sounded like weather reports: currents, seasons, percentages. Nothing a person could hold onto. Everything a person could be held by.

"Your husband works it off in two seasons," the man said. "Three at the outside."

His name was Harrow. I did not know then that it would matter. Edmund Harrow, junior factor of the Straits Trading Company, a young man then, with a smile like a well-oiled latch — it moved smoothly and meant nothing. He sat in my house drinking my tea and counted my children with his eyes. I felt it happen. Most people look at children the way they look at flowers, with a general softness. Harrow looked at them the way a merchant looks at crates: estimating weight, volume, destination. His gaze went over Silas, who was nine and sharp-eyed as a gull; over Mariel, four, asleep against my hip; it paused, calculated, and moved on. I have been looked at by many kinds of men in my life, and I knew exactly what kind of arithmetic was running behind those pleasant eyes.

Two seasons, he said. Ansel dove three years trying to clear the debt, and each year the debt grew larger, the way a fire grows when you feed it the wrong wood. The advance became penalties. The penalties became interest. The interest became new advances to cover the old ones, each signature another plank removed from under our feet. Ansel began going deeper for longer, chasing beds the other divers had given up on, coming up with blood in his ears and less and less in his basket.

The night he died, the weather was nothing. That is the part nobody believes when I tell it — there was no storm, no great gray rage of ocean to blame. The water lay flat as poured tin. The company sloop was anchored off Tanjung Pagar with lights burning, loading cargo, and Ansel went down near her moorings after a bed he'd found the week before. Silas was aboard the family boat, eleven years old, tending the line.

What happened in those last minutes, only Silas knows, and he has never once spoken of it, not to me, not to anyone. When they pulled the boat in that evening, my son sat in it alone, soaked to the bone, holding a coil of rope with white knuckles, and his eyes had gone somewhere no eleven-year-old's eyes should go. The company men were already on the beach. They had come fast — faster than any rescue could have come, slower than nothing at all, arriving precisely in time to record the loss rather than prevent it.

"Accident," said Harrow, who had come himself, which struck me even then as strange. Factors do not come to beaches. Factors send people. But Harrow stood on the wet sand in his polished boots and pronounced the word like a magistrate: accident, regrettable, the sea being what it is. And then, in nearly the same breath, the true sentence: the debt, being a debt, did not drown. It survived my husband beautifully. It transferred. Debts are immortal, Mrs. Blackwood; it is their principal advantage over persons.

He said something like that. Not so plainly. Harrow was never plain. But I heard the shape of it, and I have spent five years turning it over in my mind, and I am certain now of what I only suspected then: the sea did not take Ansel Blackwood. The sea does not sink boats that are moored properly, does not fray lines that were inspected that morning. Old Sefa said it within a week, before her mind started wandering — said it flat out, that the company wanted the debt converted, and a dead debtor converts cheaper than a living one, because the debt falls to the family and the family can be collected.

I did not believe her then. Grief makes you stupid in one direction and wise in another; I was stupid toward hope. Now I mend nets and I believe everything.

They took Silas six weeks later. Indenture against the debt, seven years' service to the company in lieu of payment. There was a paper. There is always a paper. Harrow read it aloud in a voice like warm oil, and I refused to sign, and it did not matter in the smallest particular whether I signed or not, because the paper had already been signed by parties whose names I did not know, and my refusal was noted as an irregularity rather than an obstacle. My children stood in the room. Harrow counted them again with his eyes — one now, only one remaining in the ledger's reach, since Silas was the item under discussion — and I understood, finally and completely, that this man had never seen a family at all. He had seen stock. He had seen it from the beginning, from the tea and the advance and the smooth latch of his smile, and everything since — the inflated figures, the drowned father, the indentured son — was simply the working-out of sums begun long before Ansel ever touched that first handful of silver.

Silas went without crying. That frightened me more than tears would have. A child who cries is still fighting the world. A child who goes silent has decided to study it instead, and God help everyone when he finishes.

* * *

Five years. Say it quickly and it is nothing; say it slowly, the way the nights say it, and it is a country you can get lost in.

I have raised Mariel alone, or rather she has half-raised herself, because I was not always present in the way mothers ought to be. My body was here — hauling nets, gutting fish, carrying water — but some essential part of me stayed on that beach with Harrow's boots and never came home. Mariel learned to cook before she learned to read, learned to lie to the relief officers before she learned long division, and last year Harrow's household took her in as scullery maid, and I let it happen, and I will answer for that letting until I die. But the wages fed us, and the alternative was worse, and a mother's whole art is choosing between poisons by taste.

Grandmother Sefa lies in the back room now, her mind gone mostly to water, speaking sometimes to my dead husband as if he sat mending gear beside her bed. Uncle Tomas comes by when his own affairs allow, which is to say when his courage and his greed happen to blow from the same quarter. Cousin Dev sleeps on our floor more nights than not. We are a household of leaks bailing together, and somehow we have kept afloat, and I do not examine too closely why the company has let us. Boats that stay quiet do not attract guns.

This morning the tide brought in a piece of teak decking, barnacled, cut clean at one end where it had sheared away. I turned it over with my toe and did not pick it up. Some things you leave for the sea to keep.

Chandra watched me not pick it up. "Thinking about your man," she said. It was not a question. Chandra has earned the right to skip questions.

"I'm always thinking about my man."

"No," she said. "You're always thinking about the *company*. Different animal." She bit her thread and squinted at me across the space between us. "Your boy was on the hill last night. Standing there a long time, looking at the town."

"He comes and goes."

"Not like this. This was a boy deciding something." She tied off her knot with a snap. "When a man stands still that long, Ida, he is measuring the distance to something."

I said she was an old crow, and she agreed pleasantly, and we mended on. But the words went into me like a fishhook goes into a finger — small entry, deep travel.

* * *

I sensed him before I saw him. Mothers do; the body learns the specific weight of its children's attention the way it learns hunger. I was kneeling in the shallows rinsing the repaired net, the cold pulling at my knees, and there it was — a stillness on the shore behind me, a watching. Silas stood at the tideline in his company clothes, and the sight of him in that collar always does two things to me at once: pride, because my son survives among wolves and comes home with his face arranged properly; and grief, black and salt, because that collar is the leash, and every visit home is a furlough, not a freedom.

He didn't call out. He never calls out. He stood and watched me work, the way he has done since he was small — even as a boy he preferred watching to asking, hoarding knowledge the way other children hoarded shells — and I went on rinsing the net and let him watch, because I have learned that my son opens his mouth only when the silence has done its work.

But today the silence had a different grain. Today he was not studying me. He was rehearsing something, and failing to find the rehearsal's ending.

I rose, wrung the net, and walked up the sand to him. Up close I could see it: the tiredness around his eyes gone past ordinary fatigue into something harder, and his hands — his beautiful diver's hands, Ansel's hands — hanging at his sides like tools set down mid-task.

"You've grown thin," I said, which is what mothers say when what they mean is *something is wrong.*

"I've grown the same," he said. "Everything else grew around me."

We stood there. The tide worked patiently at our ankles. Behind him the town lay low and pale in the morning haze, and somewhere in it, in some drawer or office or sealed envelope, whatever had put that look on my son's face was waiting.

"There's a ship," he said at last. "The Meridian. She sails south in six weeks."

"Cargo ships sail every week."

"Not this cargo." And then my son, who has spent five years learning that questions are knives you fall on, asked me the only question that could have made the morning worse. He asked it quietly, evenly, the way he asks everything, and it was worse for the evenness. "Mother. If someone offered passage on that ship — one berth, only one — who should it be?"

I looked at him. I thought of Harrow counting children like crates. I thought of Ansel saying the sea keeps faithful accounts. I thought of Sefa muttering to the wall, of Mariel asleep against my hip ten years ago, of Tomas and Dev and the arithmetic of a family that has never once been permitted to forget what it owes.

And I heard, beneath the question, what he was actually telling me: that there was a way. That my silent son had found something in the ledgers, or the ledgers had found something in him, and that the price of it was already being counted somewhere in his head.

"Don't answer," I said. "Not yet." I took his face in my wet, net-burned hands, the same hands that had failed to sign a paper and failed to stop a drowning and failed at everything except enduring, and I made them a promise now. "You come home tonight. You bring whatever it is you're carrying. And we will count it together — all of us, by lamplight, the way this family counts everything."

Something moved behind his eyes — relief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"And Silas," I said, as he turned toward the town. "Whatever the company taught you about keeping quiet — unlearn it for one hour tonight. Do you understand me?"

He looked back once. My son, who had mastered silence the way his father mastered the deep, gave me a single nod — and walked up out of the tide with the look of a boy carrying a weight that was no longer entirely his own.

I stood in the shallows and watched him go, and the dread in my chest turned over, slowly, like a net being drawn — and settled at last into something with edges. Something that could be gripped.

That night I would gather them all. But first I went to the back room, and knelt by Sefa's bed, and waited, because lately, when the lucid hours came, my mother-in-law had begun to talk about ships.
