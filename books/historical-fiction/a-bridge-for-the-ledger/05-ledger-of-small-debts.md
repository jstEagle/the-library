## 5. Ledger of Small Debts

There is a particular hour in Singapore when the heat finally admits defeat. It comes somewhere past midnight, after the day's moisture has given up trying to rise and simply lies on everything like a damp shroud. The counting house at that hour is a different animal than it is by daylight — the desks no longer look like instruments of commerce but like coffins with paperwork inside, and the oil lamp I am not supposed to be burning turns every shadow into a witness.

I had been stealing into the counting house at night for three weeks before I touched anything that mattered.

Not theft. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the whole lesson. I never took so much as a blot of ink. What I took was knowledge, and the Straits Trading Company has never yet invented a column for that. They counted bolts of silk, tons of gambier, casks of arrack, and human beings — human beings most carefully of all, because people are the only cargo that can run away before delivery. But no clerk had ever been charged for reading. Reading was beneath suspicion. That was my invention, you might say: I stole with my eyes.

It began as survival, the way everything with me begins. A boy who copies ledgers for five years and asks no questions is a fixture, like the ceiling fan or the smell of camphor in Fenn's drawer. A boy who asks questions is a problem. So I became a fixture with a secret interior, and the interior needed feeding, and the ledgers were there.

The company kept its books in a long room behind the counting floor, behind a door that locked with a key Mr. Harrow wore on his watch chain. But the *working* ledgers — the ones the clerks actually bled over from eight until six — lived in the racks along the east wall, and nobody locked racks. Locking racks would imply the clerks were capable of dishonesty, and the company's entire theology held that clerks were incapable of anything at all.

Here is what nobody tells you about ledgers: they are honest. It is the only place honesty survives in a counting house. Men lie to factors, factors lie to directors, directors lie to God, but ink on ruled paper only knows how to say what it says. You just have to listen to it properly. My father taught me to read water — where the current lies, where the reef hides under a smooth surface. A ledger is water too. The surface tells you one story; the undertow tells you the true one.

So at night, while the house boys slept and the geckos ticked in the rafters like small impatient clocks, I sat with the lamp turned low and read the undertow.

* * *

I started with myself. That seemed fair. If you want to understand a machine, begin with the part of it that has already chewed you.

My father's account was easy to find because it had never been closed. Tomas Blackwood — no relation, though the coincidence of names used to make my mother cross herself — diver, entered into service of the Company in March of 1838 against an advance of forty-two dollars. Forty-two dollars. I have copied sums all day for five years and I still cannot make that number look like a life. There were subsequent entries: boat hire, gear depreciation, an "administrative fee" whose purpose was never administrative, interest calculated at a rate I could not find anywhere else in the books. By the month he died the debt stood at one hundred and eleven dollars, which meant that even if he had dived until his lungs gave out — which, in fairness, they did — he would have died owing more than when he started. The ledger did not record that he was dead. Ledgers do not record dead men; they record outstanding balances. Somewhere in the company's arithmetic my father is not a drowned man but a rounding error.

Then, one line, dated a week after the funeral: *Balance transferred — B. Blackwood & dependents — to estate recovery.*

*Dependents.* My mother. Mariel, who was nine. Me, who was twelve and standing right there on the pier while a man with clean fingernails counted us the way you count crates — touching each shoulder to check the weight.

I sat with that line for a long time. The lamp hissed. A gecko ate something with a crunch that sounded obscene in the quiet. And then, because grief is a poor companion for a working night, I made myself do the thing I do instead of weeping: I looked for the pattern.

Five years of copying entries will teach you things you don't know you know. When you write ten thousand numbers, your hand learns their habits before your head does. Certain figures sit differently on the page. A sum written honestly flows; a sum written to justify itself has a certain stiffness, like a man posing for a portrait he knows he doesn't deserve. I had felt that stiffness in the estate-recovery accounts for years without knowing what it meant. That night I stopped feeling it and started measuring it.

I began pulling the family accounts. Not mine — others'. The ones whose names I knew from the kampong, from the market, from the boats. Chan and dependents. Rahman bin Salleh and dependents. The widow Nurdjana and dependents. Old Kip, whose hands shook so badly he couldn't mend his own nets anymore, and his two daughters.

And here is what I found, laid out in the company's own immaculate hand, night after night, page after page:

Every one of them followed the same shape.

First, a debt that should have been closable but wasn't — inflated by fees, by interest rates that existed nowhere except on their pages, by charges for equipment the debtor had returned or never possessed. Then, a stretch of years in which payments were recorded faithfully — and the balance *rose anyway*. I checked the arithmetic four times on Rahman's account because I was sure I had gone mad. He paid. Every quarter, he paid. And the number owed grew, patiently, like a thing fed in the dark. Once you saw the trick you couldn't unsee it: the payments were being posted to interest first, and the interest was recalculated upward, and somewhere a clerk — I could even guess which clerk, from the slant of the fives — was adjusting the principal in years where no adjustment was authorized.

Then, always at the end, the same two lines. First: *Account referred for investigation — suspected collusion with smuggling interests.* And within weeks: *Debt discharged via labor transfer. Passage arranged, S.S. Meridian or similar.*

Discharged. As if the family were a fever that had broken.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about old Kip, since he is the one I will use as proof to myself, forever, that I am not inventing this.

Kip had never in his life been within shouting distance of a smuggler. Smuggling on this coast requires speed, silence, and connections; Kip had a bad back, a loud cough, and the social reach of a hermit crab. The idea that he was moving contraband up the straits was like accusing a church bell of pickpocketing. And yet there it was, in the company's own records: *suspected collusion,* dated fourteen months before his transfer.

But here was the detail that turned my blood from warm to something mineral. The referral dates. I lined them up across six families, and they fell in a sequence — Chan, then Rahman, then Nurdjana, then Kip — spaced almost evenly, a season apart, like crops. And I happened to know, from five years of copying the shipping manifests that passed across my desk, exactly what fell in those same seasons: berths. The plantation brokers across the strait wanted labor in batches, steady and predictable, and paid the company per head delivered. The demand came first. The smuggling accusations came second.

The crime was not discovered. It was scheduled.

I put the papers down and pressed my palms flat against the desk, because I genuinely did not trust my hands.

You expect, if you ever find a thing like this, to feel horror. I suppose I felt some, distantly, the way you feel weather through a wall. What arrived first, flooding in ahead of everything else, was clarity — cold and level and terribly quiet, like the moment a diver breaks through the thermocline and the water goes suddenly clear. For five years I had carried my father's death and my family's bondage like stones in each hand, wondering what kind of world required them, blaming God, blame the sea, blame my own ignorance. And the answer was none of those. The answer was arithmetic. Somebody had sat at a desk very much like the one I was gripping, done sums, and concluded that my father's drowning was cheaper than forgiving his advance, that my mother's servitude balanced better on the books than her freedom, that a family could be converted into revenue with two lines of copperplate and a passage booked on a ship with a name chosen by optimists.

Evil, I had always assumed, would have heat in it. Passion, greed with teeth, something you could hate cleanly. This had no heat at all. It was bookkeeping. It was patient, procedural, and punctual. The men who did it went home to their dinners. One of them — I was nearly sure now — hid letters in false-bottomed drawers and still opened doors for me with a nod.

And the smuggling plot — the conspiracy that had supposedly stained my family's name, that I had half expected to spend my whole life ashamed of — was a fiction with a function. It was the story told to the outside world, and to the families themselves, to explain why the arithmetic had come for them. You cannot sell a free man's family; you can seize a criminal's. The accusation wasn't evidence of guilt. It was the receipt.

I laughed. I will admit it — one short, ugly bark of laughter in an empty counting house at two in the morning, the kind of sound that makes even geckos stop eating. Because my mother had spent five years holding her head at that particular angle women hold when they have been called guilty in a town small enough to hear it. Mariel flinched at the word *smuggler* the way other children flinched at snakes. And all of it, every flinch, every lowered eye at the well, every prayer my grandmother mumbled against unnamed sins — purchased. Invoiced. A line item in someone's quarterly report.

* * *

I worked until the sky behind the shutters went the grey of old rope, and by then I had filled six pages of my own notes — written backwards, in the cramped mirror-hand my father taught me for leaving messages under water, because a diver's habit is that anything worth saying might need to survive being found by the wrong person. Names. Dates. Referral dates against berth dates. The interest trick, worked out in columns so clean that Harrow himself could audit them, if it were possible for a man to audit his own soul.

Six families, fully documented. And at the bottom of the sixth page, added last, in letters pressed hard enough to score the paper beneath:

B. Blackwood & dependents. Referral pending.

Because that was the thing I had known since the letter in Fenn's drawer, and now I knew it in ink: our name had not yet been transferred. The machinery had been built around us — the inflated debts were already there, going back years, the same patient arithmetic that had eaten everyone else — but the final lines, the accusation and the discharge, were still wet. Still being drafted. Somewhere upstairs a man was composing our ruin the way he composed his dinner invitations, and the pen was not yet dry.

Which meant there was still time. Not much. Time enough to count, and no more.

I folded my pages into the lining of my shirt, blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the building settle around me — the creak of timber, the tick of cooling metal, all the small honest sounds a house makes when no one is lying in it. Five years I had survived this place by mastering silence. I had thought silence was armor. Standing there in the dark with the pattern of it all burning in my shirt against my ribs, I understood at last that silence was never armor at all.

It was ammunition. I had simply been loading it slowly, all this time, without knowing what for.

Somewhere across the compound a door closed — soft, deliberate, the closing of a man who does not wish to announce himself. Footsteps crossed the veranda and stopped.

Someone else was awake in the counting house. And they were standing outside the door, listening to my silence — the way I had listened to so many men's.

Waiting to learn whether I had read enough.
