## 6. Ambrose Fenn's Kindness

I have kept the ledgers of the Straits Trading Company for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that a man's handwriting tells you less about him than the way he sharpens his pen. Silas Blackwood sharpened his the way a soldier cleans a rifle — not for the task at hand but for whatever might come after it. I noticed this on a Tuesday. It took me another three weeks to admit what it meant.

You will forgive an old clerk his arithmetic. Numbers are the only honest language I have ever spoken, and so I will tell you my story the way I tell everything: in sums.

Thirty-one years of service. Four thousand two hundred entries copied in my own hand, give or take the ones I was ordered to falsify, which I stopped counting at one hundred because counting past one hundred felt like an ambition rather than a confession. One wife, buried. No children, which I told myself was providence and knew was cowardice — a man who sells other people's families does not deserve the noise of his own. And one drawer, false-bottomed, in which I have hidden thirty years of duplicates: every entry as it should have been written, before Harrow's corrections arrived like weather.

Some men keep mistresses. I keep the truth in a drawer, and she is more expensive.

* * *

The boy came to the counting house in the monsoon season of '44, all elbows and silences, with his father's shoulders and his mother's habit of watching a room from its edges. I signed the indenture papers myself. I remember because his name was the only true figure on the page — the debt it discharged had been invented over three evenings by a man named Quill who is now dead and unmourned, and the wage promised was a fiction so old it had grown whiskers.

He sat at the third desk, by the window with the warped shutter, and he copied entries without asking questions. This marked him immediately. New clerks ask questions the way new sailors vomit — constantly, loudly, and to no useful effect. Silas asked nothing. He simply watched. And I began to understand, over the following months, that his silence was not emptiness. It was storage.

A boy who asks questions wants answers. A boy who stores them wants leverage. I have been both kinds of boy myself, though in my case the storage rotted into compliance somewhere around my thirtieth year, and I have been carrying the smell of it ever since.

The first thing he stored, I think, was the way Mr. Pryce's hand trembled when he wrote the word *advance*. The second was the difference between the official shipping manifests and the ones Harrow's man delivered on Thursdays. The third — and here I confess my own guilt in the matter, because I left the drawer open a half-inch longer than carelessness explains — was the sight of me slipping a sealed letter into the false bottom of that drawer, one grey morning in the month when the frangipani went mad along Beach Road.

I saw him see it. He knows I saw him see it. Neither of us has said a word, which in Singapore is practically a marriage proposal.

* * *

Let me tell you about Edmund Harrow, since the boy will need to understand him and I would rather teach him than bury him.

Harrow is not a monster. Monsters are rare and dramatic and mostly fictional, and it comforts people to believe in them, because a monster lets everyone else off the hook. Harrow is worse than a monster. Harrow is a tidy man. He balances accounts. When a debtor becomes inconvenient — when he dies, or drinks, or has the poor judgment to organize his fellows — Harrow does not rage. He recalculates. A smuggling accusation appears, properly documented, witnessed by men whose debts ensure their testimony. The family's account is closed. The family itself is transferred, at a modest profit, to the plantation ships that cross the strait, where the mortality rate is listed nowhere because listing it would be untidy.

I have written the transfers. My hand. My ink. Thirty-one years, and the sum of my life is that I write beautifully in a criminal's book.

Why did I stay? Everyone asks this eventually, usually in the tone of a man who already knows he would have stayed too. I stayed because staying is easy and leaving is a cliff edge. Because I was twenty-two once and owed eleven dollars to this same company and saw exactly how they treated men who owed twelve. Because each individual entry seemed survivable, and only the total was monstrous, and no one ever shows you the total until it is finished. That is the whole architecture of evil in the Straits, if you want it plainly: never present the bill all at once. Send it in installments. A man will sign away a stranger's children in monthly payments he can almost ignore.

I am aware this sounds like self-excuse. It is. I have had thirty years of practice and I still cannot make it sound like anything else.

* * *

It was the rain that finally broke us both open, I suppose. Rain does that in Singapore. The shutters swell, the ink dries slow, the counting house fills with a greenish light like being underwater, and men say things they would not say under the honest sun.

Silas stayed late. So did I — I always stay late; going home to my empty house is the one installment I refuse to pay. He was copying a manifest, and I was pretending to audit columns I had audited twice already, and the rain hammered the roof like a creditor, and then his pen stopped.

"Mr. Fenn," he said. First words addressed directly to me in weeks that were not "yes, sir" or "the ledger, sir." His voice had been used so rarely it sounded unsharpened. "The Meridian. Her manifest lists forty berths in the hold."

"Forty-two," I said, without thinking, and then thought better of it, which is to say too late.

"The company documents say forty."

"The company documents," I said, "are written by men who count what is convenient." And there it was. Out of my mouth, into the wet air, irretrievable. Thirty years of professional silence, undone by a drizzle.

He looked at me then — fully, directly, the way his father must have looked at the water coming into the hull — and I understood that he was not asking out of curiosity. Curiosity is a luxury. He was asking the way a drowning man asks where the rocks are.

"You know," I said. Not a question. There was no point pretending anymore; pretense between the two of us had been dissolving for months like sugar in tea. "About the transfers. About how the accounts get cleared."

"I know my family's name is in a letter you hid," he said quietly. "I know my father's boat didn't sink itself. I don't know the rest."

And God help me, instead of telling him to go home and copy his manifest and keep his head attached to his shoulders, I poured him a cup of the terrible coffee we keep for nights like this, and I sat down across from him, and I opened the drawer.

Not the false bottom. Not yet. But near enough.

* * *

Here is what I taught him that night, while the rain kept its rhythm against the shutters like something practicing for a funeral:

I taught him that the smuggling plot laid at his family's door was arithmetic wearing a costume. That when an account grows troublesome — when the debtor dies and the widow cannot pay, or the debtor wakes up to what he owes and makes noise about it — the company does not forgive the debt. Forgiveness is not a line item. Instead the account is *resolved*: a conspiracy invented, a transfer executed, the family priced per head and shipped across the strait to the nutmeg estates, where their labor pays down a number on a page. The debt does not disappear. It is converted. Flesh into figures. I have done the conversion myself, hundreds of times, in a copperplate hand I was once praised for at school.

I taught him the names. Not all of them — I am cautious even in confession, an old flaw — but enough. The Lim family, '39. The Osei brothers, '41. A Bugis diver named Rahim whose widow still leaves flowers on a jetty post for a man the company records insist died of fever, ashore, surrounded by loving family we had not yet sold.

And I taught him, when he finally asked the question underneath all his other questions — *my mother, my sister, my grandmother, when* — I taught him about the Meridian.

"She sails for the plantations with the spring tide," I said. "Six weeks, perhaps less. She carries the condemned accounts — that's the term, did you know? Condemned. Like cargo gone off. Your family's transfer is drafted already. I have seen it. Harrow signs these things himself, at the last hour, so that no witness exists beforehand."

"How many berths," he said, and his voice did not shake, which frightened me more than shaking would have.

"One free passage remains on her manifest. A clerical berth — a company man rides with the cargo to keep the tallies. It is filled by authorization slip, countersigned and sealed. If a name were to appear on such a slip, in the proper hand, under the proper seal..." I let the sentence die, because finishing it would make me guilty of more than memory.

"One," he repeated.

"One," I confirmed. "One name, among all the names you love."

He looked at his hands for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face was not gratitude and not hope but something colder and far more dangerous: calculation. The boy was doing sums. I recognized the exercise. I have spent my life doing the same sums and arriving at the same answer, which is that the numbers never balance and someone always pays the remainder.

"Thank you, Mr. Fenn," he said formally, as though I had lent him an umbrella.

"Do not thank me," I said, sharper than I intended. "Thanking me is like thanking the tide for coming in. I did not choose it and I cannot stop it and it will drown us both if you stand in the wrong place." I rubbed my eyes, which ache these days in damp weather, an old man's complaint I had earned the right to. "Listen to me, Blackwood, because I will say this once. What I have just given you is knowledge, and knowledge in this building is a debt. Harrow collects it. If he suspects you know what you know, your family sails early and you sail with them, chained. So you will copy your manifests, sharpen your pens, and remain the most boring young man in Singapore until you decide — and decide carefully, because there is no revising it — what you intend to do with what I have told you."

"And you, sir?" he asked. "What will you do?"

What a question. I laughed — an ugly sound, startled out of me — and looked at the drawer that held thirty years of my secret honesty, and said the only true thing I had said aloud in a decade:

"I will grow older. It is the one crime this company has never managed to stop me committing."

* * *

He left at the tenth bell, bowing correctly, walking slowly, a boring young man to the last visible detail. I sat alone with the lamp and the rain and the ledgers, and I took out the false bottom, and I read my own hand from 1839 — *Rahim, advance of $14, recovered in full, no further obligation* — and beneath it, added later in the same beautiful copperplate, the truth: *sold, with wife and daughter, to the estate at Penang, proceeds applied.*

No further obligation. I have thought about that phrase for fifteen years. I think I finally understand what I owe.

So: one drawer, thirty years heavy. One ship, six weeks out, one berth on her manifest. One boy with his father's shoulders and a talent for silence, who now knows everything I know and has not yet decided whether I am his teacher or merely the oldest rat on a familiar ship.

I have chosen to believe him. At my age, belief is the last extravagance available to a man of small means.

Tomorrow I will show him how Harrow seals his letters — the wax, the ring, the little turn of the wrist at the end, a trick I have watched ten thousand times and performed perhaps fifty. I do not know yet whether I will actually do it or merely intend to. With me, lately, the distinction has blurred.

But tonight, one last sum. Six weeks. One berth. And somewhere across the town, in a kampong that smells of salt and fish-gut and woodsmoke, a family sleeps who does not yet know that the ship has already been chosen for them, and that the only question left — the entire remaining arithmetic of their lives — is which single name will fit in the space provided.

The rain eased near dawn. I wrote nothing down. For the first time in thirty-one years, some things I intend to leave to memory.
