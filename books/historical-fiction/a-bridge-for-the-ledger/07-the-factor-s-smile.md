## 7. The Factor's Smile

There is a particular way a man walks when he has been summoned to the factor's office and does not know why. He walks as though the floorboards might be counting his steps, because they are — or rather, the men behind the doors are counting them, and every board that creaks under his heel is another small entry in some invisible ledger of suspicion.

I had learned to walk on those boards without sound five years ago. On Thursday morning, summoned by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes, I unlearned it. I made my steps ordinary. Heavy, even. A boy with nothing in his head walks heavy, because he doesn't know there is anything worth walking quietly about.

The factor's office sat at the top of the counting house like a brain atop a spine, all the clerks below it passing up their little signals of ink and paper, and everything deciding itself up there where the light came through louvred shutters in long amber blades. Edmund Harrow received me standing at the window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the harbor the way a farmer watches weather he intends to make use of.

"Blackwood," he said, without turning. Not a question. He knew my name the way I knew the shape of his signature — from copying it, hundreds of times, into the fair ledger under his instruction. *E. Harrow, Factor, per order.* My hand knew his name better than my own mouth did.

"Sir."

"Do you know why you're here?"

This was the first trap, and it was a fine old one, older than Singapore itself. Answer *no* and you admit you notice nothing in your master's house. Answer *yes* and you confess to noticing something. I let my face do what it was built to do: go pleasantly blank, the face of a boy whose whole mind is occupied by the price of copra and whether his boots will last the monsoon.

"No, sir. Was it the Raffles Street invoices? Mr. Fenn said I'd misdated one, but I checked them twice after—"

"It wasn't the invoices." He turned then, and gave me the smile.

I want to be honest, since honesty is a currency I rarely get to spend: Harrow's smile was a good smile. It was not the crooked grin of a stage villain, not the wet gleam of a moneylender. It was warm at the corners and level at the eyes, the smile of a man who has been told since childhood that he has a trustworthy face and has made excellent use of the report. Ships' captains trusted it. Magistrates trusted it. Mothers handing over sons against debts they could never read properly trusted it most of all.

That smile had put more souls across the strait than any storm.

"Sit down, Blackwood." He didn't offer me tea. Tea was for men. I sat on the hard chair set deliberately low, so that visitors sat looking slightly up at the desk like petitioners before an altar. Everything in this room was arranged, I understood, and everything arranged was information.

* * *

"You've been with us five years," Harrow said, settling himself. He picked up a letter knife and turned it over in his fingers as he spoke, not threateningly, just idly, the way a man handles anything sharp while thinking. "Came to us thin as a fishing line and half-drowned in your father's debts. And now look at you." He gestured at me with the knife, taking inventory. Ink-stained cuffs. Steady hands. A copyist's shoulders, slightly stooped. "You can write a fair hand. You know the ledgers. Fenn tells me you're careful."

*Fenn tells me.* The words dropped into my stomach like stones down a well, and I listened for the splash. Had Ambrose given me away? No — if Fenn had reported me, I would already be in chains, or already gone. This was something else. This was a man checking his arithmetic.

"He's a good teacher, sir. Very patient."

"Mm. Patient." Harrow examined the word as though it might be counterfeit. "Tell me about Tuesday evening."

And there it was. Tuesday evening: the delivery to Tanjong Pagar, the packet of documents, and the sealed letter I had held to the lamp in a doorway and read with my heart hammering so loud I was certain the whole street could hear it. *The Blackwood family. Conspiracy to defraud. Smuggling of opium and specie in collusion with parties at sea. Transfer recommended.*

Tuesday evening was the reason I had not slept since, and the reason I was now sitting in a low chair performing the role of a young man with no thoughts beyond his duties.

"Tuesday, sir?" I blinked. A real blink; I have found that genuine confusion is easiest to counterfeit by giving it one true ingredient. "I did the Tanjong Pagar run that day. Left around five, back by seven. Then Fenn had me recopying the pepper accounts until the lamps were trimmed."

"Did you deliver everything?"

"Yes, sir. Sealed packets to Mr. Rahman at the warehouse, and the letters to the factor's drop-box at—" I named the street, the door, the color of the shutters. Specificity is the skin ignorance wears. Vagueness is what guilt looks like.

"And you delivered them sealed?"

"Sealed as they were given to me, sir." I let a small pause happen, the kind a truthful boy produces when a question implies an accusation he hasn't understood yet. "Was something supposed to be added? Nobody told me—"

"No, no. Nothing of the kind." The smile again, soothing, and the knife laid flat on the desk, point toward me but resting, like a promise deferred. "It's only that things move through this office, Blackwood. Papers. Rumors. Money, of course. And a company like ours runs on trust the way a ship runs on caulking. Nobody notices the caulking until the water comes in." He leaned back. "You'd tell me, wouldn't you, if you noticed water."

For one absurd moment I nearly laughed. It was such a perfect sentence, so exactly the sort of thing he would say — the factor of the Straits Trading Company asking the son of a drowned pearl diver to report leaks, as though the whole edifice weren't standing in forty fathoms of them. I used the moment instead. I let my ears go red, which they do honestly enough when I am afraid, and looked at the floor.

"I wouldn't know water from caulking, sir. I'm just glad to have dry work. After my father—" I stopped, which was also part of the performance. Grief, deployed in fragments, is the most convincing substance known to man.

"Yes," Harrow said softly. "After your father." He studied me for three full breaths. I kept my eyes on the grain of the teak desk and thought about absolutely nothing, which is the hardest work I have ever done. "The company takes care of its people, Blackwood. That's what your family fails to understand."

I looked up too quickly. *Fails.* Present tense. As though understanding were still a thing under discussion, as though the failure had already been assessed and entered.

"Sir?"

"Nothing." He waved it away, and rose, and came around the desk, and did the thing I will remember at the hour of my death: he put his hand on my shoulder. It was not a heavy hand. It was light, almost paternal, and it weighed more than the anchor of any ship in the harbor. "You're a clever lad. Cleverness is a fine quality in a clerk. But there's clever and there's wise, and the difference is that wise men know which questions pay and which ones drown." He squeezed once. "Your mother mends nets, I'm told. Kampong Glam way. And there's a sister in service — good situation, here in town. Grandmother, uncle, a cousin." He recited them the way I recited invoice totals. Flatly. Completely. In order.

He wanted to see it hit my face. So I gave him something better than fear: I gave him a boy slowly realizing that the great man knows his family, and being flattered by it. I let my mouth open. I let hope come into my eyes, cheap and visible as tin.

"You know my family, sir?"

"I make it my business to know everyone who matters to this company." He removed his hand. "You matter, Blackwood. Your family matters. Whether they matter in the credit column or the debit column—" he returned to his chair, unhurried, a man closing a pleasant morning's business "—is largely up to circumstances. And to God, some say. Though in my experience it's mostly up to paperwork."

* * *

I stood to go, because he was clearly done with me, and a wise man leaves when the hand lifts from the shoulder.

At the door, his voice caught me between the shoulder blades like a thrown coin.

"The Meridian sails soon. Fine ship. Fast." A pause, measured out like laudanum. "She'll carry passengers this voyage. Some go willingly. Some go because the alternative is worse than the sea, and the sea is worse than almost anything." Papers rustled behind me; he was already reading one, already elsewhere, a man who had finished with a conversation hours before it ended. "Give my regards to your mother. Tell her the company remembers its obligations."

I walked down the stairs heavy, like a boy with nothing in his head. Down past the clerks' desks, past Fenn, whose pen did not stop moving and whose eyes did not lift, though I saw his knuckles whiten around the nib until the ink blotted. Out into the white heat of the forecourt, where the harbor lay glittering and innocent, and somewhere among the forest of masts the Meridian rode at her moorings, taking on water and stores, preparing her belly for cargo.

The mask had held. Every word, every blink, every cheap little flare of counterfeit hope — held. I had performed ignorance so perfectly that I believed it myself for whole seconds at a time.

But beneath the mask everything burned. It burned the way a kampong fire burns, quiet in the rafters for an hour and then suddenly everywhere at once, and I walked home through it, through streets full of people who didn't know that arithmetic had condemned them, past the fish stalls and the money-changers and the temple where my father's name was carved on a donor's plank by men who remembered him kindly, and every step of the way Harrow's sentence rang in me like a struck hull:

*Whether they matter in the credit column or the debit column.*

He had not asked me if I read the letter. He had asked me about Tuesday evening only to watch my face, and my face had passed, or he had decided it didn't matter whether it passed — because the transfer was already drafted, the conspiracy already written, the Blackwoods already guilty in every document that would ever reach a magistrate's desk. Guilt, I understood now, standing on the pier with the tide going out, is not a thing you do. It is a thing that is done to you, in triplicate, with seals.

They would sail whether they were guilty or not. Mother. Mariel. Sefa, who could not walk. Tomas. Dev. All of them, into the hold, across the strait, into the plantations where names wear off men like paint.

Unless the paperwork changed.

I stood there until the sun dropped behind the warehouses, doing sums in my head, and the sum always came out the same: one berth. Perhaps one, if a certain authorization could be made to appear in a certain file with a certain seal. One, against six.

When I finally turned for home, it was nearly dark, and the lamps of the kampong were coming out along the shore like a line of small, stubborn witnesses. And halfway up the beach path, I saw something that stopped my blood: Mariel, my sister, in her scullery dress, running. Not walking fast — running, skirts hauled up, bare feet throwing sand, straight toward our house, carrying something white clenched in her fist like a bird she had caught and dared not let go.

She had worked in Harrow's house for two years without ever once running anywhere.
