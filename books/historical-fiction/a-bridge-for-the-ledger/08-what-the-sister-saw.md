## 8. What the Sister Saw

There is an art to being invisible, and I had mastered it by my third week in the factor's house, which was fortunate, because invisibility was the entire job description.

Scullery maids are not meant to be seen. We are meant to be heard only as the scrape of a pot or the slosh of gray water thrown onto the herb bed, and even then, the household should be able to convince itself it imagined us. Mrs. Rennick, the cook, said it plainly on my first morning: "You've got hands and you haven't got a face, girl. Keep it that way and you'll do fine."

I have my mother's face, though. That's the trouble. Ida Blackwood's face is not one that hides easily — it's all cheekbones and stubbornness, a face that people look at twice and then feel vaguely accused by. So I learned instead the lesser art: the downcast eyes, the slightly open mouth of a girl who understands nothing and never will. A slack jaw is worth more in that house than honesty, industry, or a good character reference. I kept my jaw slack for eleven months.

Eleven months of hauling water before dawn so the kitchen would not hear the pump squeaking. Eleven months of fish guts and candle wax and the particular smell of Mr. Harrow's study, which was ink and bay rum and something older underneath, like a chest left shut too long. Eleven months of learning which floorboards sang underfoot — the fourth from the door in the back passage, the one beside the linen press — so that when I moved through the house, I moved like water finding its way around stones.

You learn things, being invisible. That is the joke nobody in the factor's house understood: they stripped me of a face so that I could watch them better.

I knew, for instance, that Mr. Harrow took his coffee at half past six and his temper at half past seven. I knew that Mrs. Rennick watered the soup and blamed the market. I knew that the second housemaid, Prue, met the Meridian's bosun behind the woodshed on Thursdays, and that if anyone ever asked me about it I would deny having eyes at all. And I knew — because the study door had a swollen hinge that wouldn't close flush, leaving a finger's width of darkness where sound came through like light under a door — I knew what Edmund Harrow sounded like when he was composing lies.

He had a particular voice for it. Not louder. Slower. When he dictated honest correspondence — invoices, orders, the small grammar of trade — he went along at a trot, words falling over each other. But when he was building a story, he paced himself like a man laying bricks. Pause. Settle the word. Tap the pen twice against the inkwell. Choose the next one. Lies, in Mr. Harrow's house, were laid with mortar.

I had heard it maybe four times in eleven months and thought nothing of it beyond a shiver, the way you shiver at a snake's rattle without knowing precisely what it promises. Then, on a Tuesday thick enough to swim in, I heard him lay the bricks with our name in them.

* * *

I had been sent up with the tray — coffee, cold tongue, bread — because the regular girl, Sariah, was down with a flux, and Mrs. Rennick didn't trust the tray to anyone who might spill it on the carpet, which apparently excluded me, though she'd never once used my name correctly. I balanced it on one hand, pressed my shoulder to the study door, and pushed.

The door opened inward and I stepped into the corner of the room where the tray table lived, eyes down, jaw slack, a piece of furniture among furniture.

"Set it there." He didn't look up. He was standing at the window with his back to the harbor, which I always thought was telling. Most men in this town stood facing the water. Harrow stood facing away from it, as if it owed him money.

The clerk from the office was there too — not Fenn; Fenn had been sent across town on some errand I would later thank God for — but a younger man, new, all collar and eagerness, sitting with a pad across his knee and his pen already wet. Ready to take dictation.

"You'll write it as I say it," Harrow told him, "and you will not improve upon it. Improvements are how men hang themselves."

The clerk nodded as though this were wisdom rather than threat. Perhaps it was both.

Harrow began to pace. I poured coffee nobody asked for, because pouring gave my hands something to do besides shake, and I had begun, by then, to need something for my hands to do.

"The deposition of one Ambrose Fenn," Harrow said, "clerk of accounts, Straits Trading Company, taken the fourteenth of October."

My ears did the thing they do when the sea drops suddenly out from under a boat — that lurch, that hollow.

"In the matter of certain irregularities at the western anchorage. Write: *It has come to my attention through the ordinary course of my duties.*"

Tap. Tap. The pen against the inkwell.

"*That a conspiracy exists among certain persons of the fishing kampong.*"

I want to tell you that I thought immediately of Mother, of Silas, of all of them, arranged in my mind like figures at a table. I don't remember that. What I remember is smaller and worse: I remember watching a drop of coffee tremble on the lip of the cup and willing it, with my whole soul, not to fall on the carpet. As if the fate of my family could be settled by whether I stained a rug in the factor's study. Fear does that. It shrinks the world down to whatever your hands are holding.

"*Persons known to the company as debtors,*" Harrow continued, in that patient bricklaying voice, "*and known to one another by long association, who have conspired to land goods untaxed at the western point, in concert with parties unnamed.*"

Parties unnamed. I had grown up on my father's knee hearing about parties named — every smuggler on that coast had a name, a boat, a cousin in every creek, and none of them had ever once knocked on Tomas Blackwood's door, because everyone on that stretch of shore knew two things about my uncle: he talked too much, and he owed too much. Smugglers have standards. They can't afford men who owe.

"*Among these persons,*" said Harrow, and then he stopped pacing, and turned, and looked directly at the place where I stood.

I did not stop breathing. I am proud of that to this day. I slowed it, made it shallow, let my eyes drift to the middle distance the way a cow looks at rain. Invisibility is not stillness — stillness is noticeable. Invisibility is being boring, thoroughly and convincingly boring, and no girl alive has ever been boring the way I was boring in that moment.

He looked at me the length of one breath. Then he dismissed me the way you wave off a fly whose legs tickle: "Done? Go on, then."

I went. The door closed behind me with its swollen, imperfect click, and I stood in the passage with the empty tray and listened through the gap.

"*Among these persons are numbered the family Blackwood,*" said the voice through the dark inch of the door, "*whose debts to this company are of long standing and whose means of payment are, by their own admission, doubtful.*"

By their own admission. My father had admitted nothing in his life except that he couldn't swim as far as he believed he could. My father had drowned owing a debt that the company itself had invented, inflated, and sold, and now the same arithmetic that killed him was being written out in fair copy upstairs by a boy with an eager pen.

"*Further,*" said Harrow, and here his voice changed — quickened almost, with something I did not at first recognize and then did, and it turned my stomach to warm lead. It was pleasure. Plain, unhurried pleasure, the satisfaction of a man doing the thing he is best at. "*Further, it has been represented to me that the said family has of late sought to dispose of goods and remove themselves from the jurisdiction before their accounts can be settled. Which circumstance makes necessary their immediate removal to company custody aboard the vessel Meridian, for transport, until such time as restitution—*"

I stopped listening to the words and started listening to the shape of them, because the words were going to make me cry and the shape was going to keep me alive. Removal. Custody. Transport. I knew what those words weighed. Every child in the kampong knew, the way children know about snakes without being taught. Transport meant the plantation ships across the strait. Transport meant a name on a manifest instead of a name in a prayer book. Transport meant that mothers stopped speaking your name aloud after a year or two, not from forgetting, but because saying it hurt less if it was only inside.

The fourth floorboard from the door sang when you stepped on it. I stepped on it anyway, hard, on purpose, so that my own body would jump and cover the sound of my knees going loose.

* * *

Here is what I did for the rest of that day, and I record it because it seems to me now like the longest performance of my life, and I never got applause:

I scrubbed the kitchen flags. I blackened the stove. I hauled two extra buckets of water and scalded the laundry copper and gutted enough fish to fill a barrel with eyes. I worked like a machine worked, steadily and stupidly, and inside the machine there was a girl screaming, and the screaming never once reached the air of the factor's kitchen, because Mrs. Rennick had told me I hadn't got a face, and by God I didn't have one. Faces are for feeling with. I had hands. Hands scrubbed.

But hands also think, it turns out. Or something in me thought while my hands worked, and by evening the screaming had settled into sentences, and the sentences were these:

One: they were writing testimony against us in the voice of Ambrose Fenn, and Fenn — old Fenn, who bought sweet oranges for the office boys at Christmas and once looked the other way when Silas came back late from an errand — Fenn was either party to it or about to be made the ghost of it, and neither possibility said anything good about the safety of anyone named Blackwood.

Two: the ship was the Meridian. Ships have schedules. Schedules mean days. Days mean time, and time is the only currency we had any of.

Three: someone in that house had to get the words off that clerk's pad and into hands that could use them, because a lie written down is a living thing — it grows, it gets countersigned, it becomes true the way silt becomes stone — and the only way to kill it is to catch it young.

I want to say something honest about fear here, since this chapter is nominally mine and I should tell the truth in it. People imagine fear as running, or screaming, or the weakness in the knees. Real fear, the useful kind, feels like clarity with teeth. It felt like the day my father's boat came back without my father, when I was nine, standing on the shingle watching the men carry in what they carried, and understanding — instantly, totally, without one wasted tear — that the world was divided into things that happen to you and things you do, and that I was now required to choose which side of that line I lived on. I chose. I was nine and I chose, and I have never unchosen it.

So on Tuesday night, when the house went dark, I did not run to the kampong. Running to the kampong is what they would expect of a frightened native girl, and frightened girls who run get followed, and families that get followed get transported ahead of schedule. No. I stayed in my narrow bed in the attic room with Prue snoring below me, and I waited for Thursday.

Because Thursdays, in the factor's house, were when the study was cleaned.

* * *

The study was cleaned on Thursdays after the factor rode out to the anchorage, which he did every week, weather permitting, to inspect what he called his interests. He left at eight and returned past noon, and for three of those hours the study belonged to the broom and the cloth and whoever held them, and this week, because Sariah was still fluxed and Mrs. Rennick wanted the girl with the strong arms, it belonged to me.

I cleaned that room the way I had cleaned it a dozen times before: slowly, thoroughly, and with my mind entirely elsewhere. I swept. I dusted the sill. I polished the little brass scale weights on the desk, each one to a shine it had never earned. I emptied the wastebasket into the fire pail, as instructed, because the factor was particular about his papers and particular-er about his ashes.

And the wastebasket — I want you to appreciate this, the sheer administrative stupidity of it — the wastebasket had not yet been emptied since Tuesday.

On top of the ash and the string and a broken pen nib lay a torn quarter-sheet of paper, written in a young clerk's careful hand, struck through twice in the factor's red ink, and crumpled with intent. A rejected draft. The first brick, pulled off the wall and thrown away — not because it was untrue, Mr. Harrow would never discard a lie for being untrue, but because it must have been insufficiently true-sounding, and he had made the boy start again.

I stood there holding the pail in one hand and my whole life in the other, and I did the fastest reading of my life, which is saying something, because I read the way kampong girls read — in scraps, in signs, in the margins of other people's documents.

*...the deposition of A. Fenn, clerk of accounts... irregularities at the western anchorage... conspiracy among persons of the fishing kampong... the family Blackwood, whose debts... sought to dispose of goods and depart the jurisdiction... necessitating removal aboard the Meridian...*

Struck through. Crumpled. Mine.

Now, a sensible girl would have put it back. A sensible girl would have reasoned: paper burns, evidence hangs witnesses, a scrap in a scullery maid's apron is a confession wearing a disguise. Every one of those thoughts went through my head in order, politely, like passengers disembarking. And then behind them came the last passenger, the one who pays for everyone's ticket, and it said: *they wrote our name down. They sat in a fine room with coffee and a carpet and wrote our name down like an entry in a ledger, and if it lives only in that room, then that room wins.*

I am my mother's daughter. I folded the scrap small — small as a coin, small as a seed — and I put it in the hem of my sleeve where I had carried stolen sugar for a year, and I finished cleaning that study until it gleamed, and I banked the fire, and I curtsied to an empty room.

Then I walked out through the kitchen, past Mrs. Rennick, who said "About time, girl," and past the gate, and down the hill, and along the shore road in my half-day, and I did not run, because running draws eyes, and I did not sing, though there was a hymn in my chest trying to get out, and I did not cry until the house was fully out of sight behind the nutmeg trees, and then only briefly, and walking.

* * *

The kampong smelled like home the way home smells — smoke and salt and fish drying on racks, the mudflat stink at low tide that visitors wrinkle their noses at and I could drink. Mother was at the nets, mending, her hands going through the pattern without her eyes' help, the way they had done my whole life. Grandmother Sefa was asleep in the shade of the house, breathing slow. Uncle Tomas was loudly absent, probably loudly present somewhere he shouldn't be.

Mother looked up when my shadow crossed her, and she saw my face before I had decided anything about it. That is the thing about Ida Blackwood — slack jaws do not work on her. She read me the way Silas reads silences, instantly and completely, and her hands stopped mending.

"Mariel. What's happened."

Not *what's wrong*. She didn't give me the option of nothing.

I knelt down in the sand beside her, close, so close our heads nearly touched, the way we used to sit over one story-book when I was small, and I unfolded the scrap from my sleeve, and I put it in her hands, and I watched her read it. I watched her lips move on the words *family Blackwood*. I watched her thumb press into the paper hard enough to dent it, as if she could push our name back out of the ink.

She didn't cry. I had known she wouldn't. My mother's tears are private citizens and rarely appear in public.

"They're writing it down," she said at last, very quietly. "Your father's debt wasn't enough for them. Now they're writing us into a crime."

"Yes, Ma."

"And the ship?"

"The Meridian."

Something crossed her face then that I will see when I'm old, God willing — a settling, like ballast shifting. She folded the scrap along its existing creases and tucked it inside her blouse, against her heart, which is where she keeps everything that matters and several things that frighten her.

"Then we count," she said. "Tonight. Everyone. Lamplight, all of us, and we count what we have and who can move and who cannot, and we do the sums before their sums finish us." She picked the net back up, because grief and terror notwithstanding, the nets still needed mending; that is the whole of my mother's philosophy in a single gesture. "Run and fetch your brother from the counting house road. Tell him—" She stopped herself. Reconsidered. Chose the words with care, the way you choose footing on wet rock. "Tell him his sister has brought something home from the factor's house, and he will know what kind."

I ran then. Down the sand, along the tideline, past the drying racks and the beached boats, with the wind full of salt and my heart going like a drum in a temple procession. Behind me the factor's house sat white and blind on its hill, believing it owned everything it surveyed, believing it had no faceless girl in it at all.

They had written us into their ledger as conspirators, as debtors, as cargo awaiting shipment.

Very well. I had written them into mine.
