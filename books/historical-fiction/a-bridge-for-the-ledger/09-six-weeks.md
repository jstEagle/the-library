## 9. Six Weeks

My mother's house smelled of salt, fish guts, and kerosene, which is to say it smelled like every house in the kampong, and like my entire childhood. I had forgotten how small it was. Five years in the counting house had stretched my sense of rooms; the company's offices were built to make a man feel like an insect, and my mother's single room was built to make him feel like a hand folded into another hand. That night it held seven of us and one lamp, and the lamp was the eighth presence, flickering on the wall like something that wanted to leave but couldn't.

I had come after dark, by the back way, past the drying racks, because I did not know yet whether Harrow had men watching the Blackwood door. He probably didn't. We were, on paper, merely a family of debtors among hundreds. But paper lies — I had learned that from the inside of it — and a man who has read enough false entries begins to see conspiracies in tide charts.

They were waiting for me. Word travels faster than ships in a kampong; someone had told Ida Blackwood that her son had been asking questions, and mothers hear everything before anyone decides to tell them.

"Sit," she said, though there was nowhere to sit that wasn't already occupied or holding mending. I sat on an upturned crate between Mariel and the wall, which was fitting. All my life I had been between my sister and a wall.

The lamp threw our shadows up onto the rafters, where they tangled with the hanging nets and looked, for a moment, like drowned men. My mother would not have appreciated the observation. My mother did not appreciate poetry unless it was practical.

"You've seen it," she said. Not a question.

"I've seen it."

"Say it out loud." She was working a net-mending needle through her fingers without looking at it, the way she'd done for thirty years, the way her hands did anything — automatically, relentlessly, as if stopping would let the sea notice her and come collect. "I won't have this family whispering around a thing like weather. Say what the paper says."

So I said it. Names, dates, the word *conspiracy* written in a clerk's tidy hand, the accusation of smuggling laid against a family that had never owned enough boat to smuggle with. I said the part about transfer across the straits, about the plantation account the names would be moved into, and I watched each face take the news the way different timber takes water — some swelling, some splitting, some going dark and silent.

Mariel heard it first. Of course she did. She was fourteen and worked in Harrow's own kitchen, scrubbing pots beneath the very mouth that had spoken us into ruin, and when I finished she said, flatly, "He dictated it on a Tuesday. I carried the ink."

Everyone turned to her.

"I carried the ink," she repeated, chin up, in exactly our mother's tone. "I didn't know then. I know now."

* * *

Uncle Tomas arrived late, which was itself information. Tomas was never late to anything involving his own interest; lateness meant he had stopped along the way to weigh the cost of coming at all.

He came in smelling of arrack and river mud, kissed my mother on both cheeks, ruffled Mariel's hair — she ducked it, sixteen years of practice — and settled onto the good stool like a man taking a chair at a table where cards were about to be dealt.

"So," he said. "How bad?"

"The Meridian," I said. "Six weeks. Maybe less. After that, the account closes and we're cargo."

Tomas nodded slowly, the way he nodded at prices he found offensive but intended to pay. "Then we have six weeks to be clever. Good. Cleverness is cheaper than money and I have neither." He rubbed his hands together. "Let's count what we have."

That was Tomas. Other men might have opened with grief, or rage, or prayer. Tomas opened with inventory. It made him easy to dismiss and impossible to beat, and I have spent years since deciding which of those mattered more.

Cousin Dev was already there, wedged into the corner with his long fisherman's legs folded up like a grasshopper's. Dev was nineteen, strong as mooring rope, and frightened in a way he thought nobody could see because he kept grinning. He grinned now. "Count me first," he said. "I'm worth the most and cost the least."

"You're worth nothing and cost everything," Tomas said fondly. "You can't hold your tongue longer than you can hold your breath."

"I can hold my breath four minutes."

"And your tongue four seconds. See? There's the arithmetic of this family."

But he was not wrong to start counting, my uncle. That was the horror of the evening, the thing I have never managed to write down plainly: we counted ourselves. We sat under a kerosene lamp and performed arithmetic upon the people we loved, assigning values and costs and probabilities as calmly as clerks, because that is what fear does when it has six weeks to work — it turns love into a column of figures.

* * *

My mother began it, actually. Tomas may have proposed the counting, but it was Ida Blackwood who took the needle out of her hands, laid it across the mending basket, and said, "Runners first."

She named them: Dev, who had a boat and no wife. Cousin Anli up the coast at Pengerang, three hours by prahu if the wind cooperated and eight if it sulked. Old Kassim who owed her a favor older than Mariel.

"That's three ways out," she said. "Dev goes by sea tonight, tomorrow, whenever the tide suits—"

"I'm not going anywhere until—" Dev started.

"—or Dev stays and hides in plain sight, because Harrow's list wants Blackwoods and Dev is a Salim by his father, on paper—"

"On paper," Tomas said, "we are all innocent. On paper is where they wrote us guilty. Let's not lean too hard on paper either direction."

My mother ignored him, which she had been doing since before I was born and would do after the sun burned out. "Mariel can run. Mariel should run — she's the one sleeping under his roof."

"No," said Mariel.

"Mariel—"

"They watch me *because* I'm closest. If I bolt, it tells them we know. And I know things, Ma." She said it quietly, but the room went still around it. She hadn't yet shown anyone the scrap she'd taken — the torn corner of draft testimony she'd smuggled out in her apron. I saw her hand go to her waist where she kept it, and I saw her decide, again, to keep it one more day. Fourteen years old, and she had already learned the counting-house virtue: hold what you hold until holding it profits.

I understood her. I had spent five years being silent for profit. But I looked at her sitting there with stolen evidence against her ribs and felt something I couldn't name yet, something between pride and seasickness.

* * *

And then there was the other end of the column. The figures that wouldn't move.

Grandmother Sefa lay behind the cloth partition at the far end of the room, where she had lain for a year now, her body slowly forgetting its arguments with gravity. The partition was old sailcloth, patched, and the lamp light came through it with her shadow printed on it, small and curled like a shell.

"She sleeps," my mother said, lowering her voice needlessly — Sefa's hearing had gone the way of her legs. "Mostly."

"Can she be moved?" Tomas asked, and there it was, the question underneath all the others, the one we'd all been not-asking while we talked about boats and aliases. Because everyone in that room knew what happened to the old ones and the sick ones on the plantation ships. Everyone had seen the berths for them, in the ledgers, priced at less than the able-bodied. Less per head. I had copied those figures myself, five hundred times, before I understood what the discount was for.

"In a cart, over the hill road, maybe to Uncle Anli's," my mother said. "If she survives the road. If she doesn't take the fever from the rain. If—" She stopped. Her hands had resumed the needlework without her permission, and she looked down at them as if catching servants stealing. "If wishes were keels."

"She stays," said a voice from behind the sailcloth.

We all turned. Sefa was awake. Sefa, apparently, had been awake.

"You should be sleeping," my mother said, crossing to the partition, but the old woman's hand came out and caught her wrist, and Sefa pushed the cloth aside herself, propped on one elbow, eyes bright and terrible in the lamplight.

"If the sea wants me, it knows where I live," she said. Her voice was thin but it carried, the way a knife carries. "I have lived in this house sixty-one years. I buried your father in its floor-dirt and your husband's luck in its walls. I will not be carried anywhere like a trunk." Her eyes went around the room, touching each of us. "Now. Someone put more oil in that lamp and tell me the true numbers. Not the kind ones. My grandson there has clerk's hands and a liar's face; he'll give me the real figures or none."

Everyone looked at me.

I gave her the real figures.

* * *

Here is what the arithmetic looked like, laid honest on the table:

Six weeks — possibly fewer; Harrow's schedules had a habit of accelerating when watched — until the *Meridian*. One family, nine souls counting Sefa and me, against a company that owned the harbormaster, the magistrate's patience, and most of the kampong's debts. Running meant the jungle, fever, and outlawry, and running only worked for the young and the unwatched. Hiding meant paperwork forged better than any of us could forge it. Staying meant the ship.

"We need papers," Tomas said, somewhere in the second hour. "Real-looking ones. A passage chit, a release-of-debt, something with the right seals. And those cost money, or a friend inside, or a miracle, and I've got coin enough to buy the lamp oil and no friends inside the company since Fenn stopped drinking with the likes of me."

"Fenn," I said, before I could stop myself.

Tomas's eyebrows went up. My mother's eyes narrowed. Even Dev stopped grinning.

"Ambrose Fenn," I said carefully, "is an old man who has begun, lately, to look at me like he remembers being young."

"What does that mean?" my mother asked.

"It means," I said, "that I don't know yet. It means I intend to find out. It means —" and here I paused, because the next part was the part I had carried alone from the counting house to this room, heavy as ballast, and saying it aloud would make it real and shared and no longer mine to control — "it means there may be a way. One way. Small. For one person."

The lamp fluttered. In the silence I heard the tide turning outside, the endless bookkeeping of the straits.

"One person," my mother repeated.

"For now. Possibly. If everything holds and nobody asks me how."

"Sila." My mother almost never used the short name. It landed on me like a hand on the shoulder of a drowning man — welcome, and an anchor dragging you down whether you want it or not. "You are twenty years old and you have learned to talk the way clerks write, in circles with a number hidden in the middle. So I will ask plainly, and you will answer plainly, and no one in this room will say another word until you do. Is my son telling me he can save one of us?"

The true answer was: I don't know. The truer answer was: I already know whose name I am afraid I'll write. I gave the answer that was neither.

"I'm telling you I'm going to try."

Sefa laughed from behind her partition — a dry rustle, like nets hauled in empty. "There it is," she said. "The sea gives back what is owed, child. It just likes to see what you'll pay first."

* * *

We broke up near midnight, in ones and twos, so as not to draw the eye of any neighbor who might later be asked questions. Dev left first, promising — seriously, for once — to keep his boat ready and his mouth shut. Tomas lingered over the last of the arrack, and I caught him watching me with an expression I couldn't read, an expression doing sums of its own. I was too tired, then, to be suspicious. That is the excuse I have settled on: I was too tired to be suspicious.

At the door, my mother pressed a bundle into my hands — rice wrapped in banana leaf, because it did not matter that the world was ending; her son was leaving her house and would eat on the road.

"Ma," I said. "Whatever happens—"

"Don't." She touched my cheek once, briefly, with the back of her net-scarred hand. "Whatever happens, you'll tell me after. You always tell me after. Go home the long way."

I went the long way, along the waterline, where the tide was out and the mud stank and the stars came down to double themselves in the shallows. Behind me the kampong lamps were going out one by one, each extinction a household choosing sleep over dread, and ahead the company wharf stood lit like a wound that wouldn't close, the *Meridian* already riding at anchor there, black hull, black masts, patient as arithmetic.

One berth. I had said *one person*, and watched hope and horror sit down at my mother's table and share the same plate.

What I had not said — what I had not admitted even to myself until the mud sucked at my boots and the ship's riding-lamp burned its small hole in the dark — was that I had lied by omission, there in the lamplight. Because I had already seen the authorization form in Harrow's cabinet. I already knew its shape, its seals, its single cruel blank line.

One berth. One name.

And six weeks to learn how to write it.
