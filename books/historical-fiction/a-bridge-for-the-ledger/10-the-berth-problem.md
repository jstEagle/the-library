## 10. The Berth Problem

There is a particular arithmetic to ruin, and I had learned it young. My father's boat was worth eleven dollars in salvage. His debt was counted at forty. The difference between those numbers was a boy in a counting house, and I had spent five years being the difference.

Now the arithmetic came again, smaller and crueler. One berth. One name on a forged authorization. The Meridian would carry away every Blackwood she could hold, and my pen could pluck exactly one of them back out of her belly.

I confirmed it three times, because the first two times I did not want to believe myself.

* * *

The confirmation was almost insultingly simple. Fenn had taught me the shape of the transfer schedules without ever teaching me — that is his gift, instruction by adjacency, knowledge acquired the way damp acquires mold. On Thursday I stayed late over the outbound manifests, copying figures I already knew, and when Ambrose went to fetch his evening rice I drew the Meridian's authorization template from the stack and read it as calmly as a man reading the weather.

Passage for one. Endorsed by the factor. Countersigned under company seal. There was no column for wives. No column for sisters, uncles, cousins, or grandmothers bedridden in a kampong hut that smelled of camphor and salt. The document did not contemplate a family. The company had never needed to; families were not passengers, they were cargo, and cargo does not require a berth. Cargo requires only tonnage.

One name. I set the paper down with the exaggerated care of a drunkard pretending sobriety, and I thought: well. At least the arithmetic is honest. Everything else about this business lies, but numbers keep faith. It is why I love them and why I trust no one who loves them as much as I do.

Fenn returned, saw my face, and said nothing, which from Ambrose Fenn is an entire conversation.

"It can only be one," I said, when the outer office had emptied.

"I did tell you the ships were small," he said, not unkindly.

"You told me the *berths* were small. You let me believe the problem was size."

"The problem is always size, boy. The company builds everything just large enough for its conscience, which is to say, barely at all." He gathered his sleeves, buttoned his cuffs with the fussy precision of a man who has nothing left to govern but himself. "You'll want to decide soon. Paper dries, ink changes color, and Harrow checks his seals more often now."

"How do I choose?"

He paused at the door. For a moment he looked every one of his sixty-odd years, and then some extra ones he kept in reserve. "In thirty years," he said, "I have watched nine men ask me that question. Eight of them never forgave themselves for any answer. The ninth didn't answer at all." He let that sit between us like a coin on a table. "Good night, Silas."

I sat alone in the counting house after he left, listening to the geckos ticking in the rafters, and understood that Fenn had just told me, in his oblique way, what he thought I should do. I was not ready to hear it. I filed it in the drawer where I kept things I was not ready to hear, and that drawer was getting full.

* * *

I told my mother first, because she would take it worst and best at once, and I wanted the worst over with while I still had daylight courage.

She was mending nets on the stoop, hands moving with the blind fluency of forty years, and when I finished speaking she did not stop moving. That frightened me more than weeping would have. Weeping means the wave has hit. Stillness means it is still coming.

"One name," she said.

"One name."

"Then it is Mariel."

"It is not Mariel."

"Silas—"

"She's sixteen. She'd be alone across the strait with no trade and no protector, and you know what they do with girls who have neither. Mother, I've seen the manifests. I've read what happens to unattached women on the plantations." I crouched so we were level, which is a thing you can do with a mother only when the news is bad enough. "It isn't Mariel."

Her hands worked the net. Knot, pull, knot. "Your father said something once, before the last voyage. He said if ever the sea gives us back less than it takes, we don't get to choose who pays. We only get to choose how we carry it." She looked at me then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You are choosing to carry it all. That is not the same as choosing nothing, my son. Remember that, whatever you decide."

"I haven't decided anything."

"No," she said, and went back to the net. "But your hands decided years ago. They've been reaching for doors your whole life, holding them open for everyone behind you." Knot. Pull. "It is a good quality. It will also kill you."

Mothers should be required by law to say less true things.

* * *

Mariel cornered me two days later in the shade of Uncle Tomas's boat, where I was pretending to inspect a hull that needed no inspection. My sister has inherited our mother's talent for ambush and improved upon it with youth's shamelessness. She simply appeared, folded her arms, and said:

"You've found a way to get one of us off the ship."

Word travels through a kampong faster than fire through dry grass, and it travels the same way — by every mouth, changed slightly each time. "Who told you?"

"Tomas told Dev, Dev told Auntie Rahel, Auntie Rahel told the fish-seller, and the fish-seller tells everyone because it's good for trade." She sat down on the gunwale with the gravity of a magistrate. "So. Me."

"Not you."

"Silas."

"Mariel."

"You're the one who knows the ledgers. You're the one who reads and writes and knows which fork in Singapore Town leads somewhere other than a debtors' cell. If anyone walks out of this with nothing but their own two hands, it should be the person who can use them." Her chin was up, her voice steady, and her hands — I noticed her hands were shaking, and I hated the world a little more for giving my sister cause to be brave. "Give it to me. I'll disappear up-country. Rahel has people in Klang. I'll change my name. I'll—"

"You'll be sixteen and alone."

"I'll be sixteen and *alive*, which is more than the Meridian promises."

"And what happens to Grandmother when you're gone? Who sits with her? She asks for you at night, Mariel. Half the time she thinks you're Father come home, and you're the only one she lets correct her gently. What happens to Mother, with both her children gone or worse?" I heard my own voice climbing and dragged it down. "This isn't refusal because I love you. Well. It is. But it's also refusal because it's stupid, and in this family I am permitted to call things stupid, it's my one prerogative as the literate one."

She glared at me. Then, horribly, her chin began to go, and I saw the sixteen-year-old surface beneath the magistrate. "I don't want to watch them take you," she whispered. "That's the real reason. I don't want to stand on a pier and watch."

The honest weapon. She had disarmed me with it entirely. "Then look at the sea instead," I said, which was a coward's answer, and we both knew it, and she let me keep it because she loved me.

* * *

Uncle Tomas chose the tavern approach, which with Tomas is a form of diplomacy. He bought me arrack I didn't drink and delivered his argument in the loose-legged cadence of a man performing drunkenness badly, which is to say, soberly.

"Give it to Dev," he said. "Boy's got no papers, no trade, no family line the company cares to track. A cousin vanishes, nobody asks questions. But a son of Blackwood? They'll hunt you, Silas, if only for the embarrassment."

"They'll hunt whoever's missing, Uncle. The paperwork doesn't distinguish."

"Dev's smarter at surviving than you think. Quiet like you. Knows boats."

"He's nineteen and he snores like a landslide. They'll find him in a week."

Tomas laughed, then stopped laughing, and put his hand flat on the table between us, and for a moment the tavern performance dropped entirely and I saw the man beneath, tired and frightened and fond. "Silas. I'm asking you as the eldest male of the family. Let it be Dev."

Something in his tone snagged on me — a wrongness, like a note played slightly flat. Tomas was too invested. Too quick. A man arguing for his son's life argues desperately; a man arguing for his own absolution argues *efficiently*. I filed it away, that flatness, next to Fenn's drawer of unready things. Later, much later, I would understand what I had heard in my uncle's voice that night. That night I only said: "I'll think on it, Uncle."

Which is Blackwood for *no*, and every Blackwood within a mile knew it.

* * *

Grandmother Sefa could not come to arguments, so arguments came to her, though hers arrived quietly, in the amber hour before sunset when the hut filled with that honeyed light that makes even poverty look briefly commissioned.

She was lucid that evening. These days came rarely now, weather systems crossing an interior map none of us could read, and when they came we all conspired to pretend they might stay. She held my hand in both of hers — her grip still strong, diver's grip, a grip that had hauled shell and stone and drowning men — and she said:

"They tell me there is one seat on the devil's boat, and everyone in this family has been whispering at you like chickens around corn."

"They tell you true."

"Hmph." She looked out at the water through the doorway, at the light going bronze on it. "Your grandfather believed the sea keeps accounts. Every debt, every payment, noted down in water. You can't cheat the tide, Silas. You can only delay it, and it charges interest." Her thumb moved over my knuckles, back and forth. "The others want you to spend the seat on them."

"Yes."

"And you want to spend it on yourself, and are ashamed."

I opened my mouth to deny it and found nothing there. That is the trouble with grandmothers: they have already read your ledger. "A little," I admitted. "I want to live. It seems a small shameful want, but it's mine."

"Wanting to live is not shameful. It is the first law. Even the oyster wants to live, and look how it manages — closed up tight around its own hurt, making pearls of it." She turned her clouded eyes on me. "But hear me, boy. Whatever name you write, write it with a whole heart. A half-hearted name on that paper will rot, like a half-hearted knot. And—" her voice drifted, and for a moment I feared the fog was taking her again, mid-sentence, mid-evening. Then it cleared, and what came out of it was so clear it cut. "And remember that the sea gives back what is owed. Always. Not when you want. Not how you want. But it pays its debts, my son. It has never once failed to pay."

I kissed her hands and left before she could see what her words had done to my face.

* * *

That night I laid it all out the only way I trusted — on paper, in pencil, in the dark, figures and names and arrows, the cold grammar of my trade. Mariel: protected by youth from the worst labor but exposed to the worst attention. Mother: forty-six, strong, but a woman of recorded debts; the company would work her like a mule and call it amortization. Tomas: fifty-one and insubordinate, a dangerous combination on a plantation. Dev: young, strong, anonymous — the rational choice, which was precisely why I couldn't make it, because ration is what the company used, and I would be damned before I ran their arithmetic inside my own family. Sefa: could not survive the passage; writing her name would be mercy dressed as rescue, and I flinched from how easily that argument assembled itself in my head.

And then the last figure, the one my pencil kept returning to without my permission. Silas Blackwood, aged nineteen, clerk, literate, indispensable to the Straits Trading Company in ways Harrow had not yet fully appreciated. The one name that, written on that authorization, would remove nothing from the ship and add nothing to it — because I was sailing regardless. The one choice that cost the ledger nothing.

Except everything.

I looked at that figure a long time. Around it, the other names stood in a ring like family at a graveside, each of them having privately pressed their claim into my hands — *take Mariel, take Dev, take Mother, take me* — and every single one of them refusing, in the same breath, to consider themselves. That was the thing I kept turning over, the splinter in the mind: five Blackwoods, five berths' worth of need, and not one of us willing to be saved. Love, in my family, expressed itself exclusively as refusal. We were a house of people shoving salvation at each other across a table, nobody's hands open, everybody's hearts breaking in the same direction.

Fenn's ninth man, I thought. The one who didn't answer. Perhaps Fenn hadn't meant him as a warning. Perhaps he'd meant him as a door.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the harbor sounds came through the wall — rigging ticking, water slapping pilings, far off, the low moan of a ship's horn feeling its way through the strait's evening mist. The Meridian lay at anchor past the point, black on blacker, filling her holds week by week with the arithmetic of other people's debts.

Six weeks, Fenn had said. Less now. And tomorrow, I knew — the way you know weather in your knees — I would have to begin practicing a dead man's handwriting, because whichever name went on that authorization, it would have to wear Edmund Harrow's hand to be believed.

I fell asleep with the pencil still in my fist, and dreamed of my father standing in the flooded hold of his boat, looking up through green water at the underside of the world, patient as arithmetic, waiting for the sea to finish paying what it owed.

At dawn there was a knock at the door, and Mariel's face in the gap of it, white as sailcloth.

"Silas," she said. "It's Tomas. Something's happened."
