## 11. Uncle Tomas's Bargain

There is a particular arithmetic that a man learns when he has buried a brother, raised half his dead brother's debts in argument, and watched the rest of the family's money evaporate like spray off a seawall. It is not the arithmetic of ledgers — I have seen enough of those to know they lie in neat columns — but the arithmetic of what a man can live with. Every choice subtracts something. The trick, I told myself for most of my fifty-one years, is to choose the subtraction you can carry.

I am Tomas Blackwood, and I have carried many things: my brother's boat hook, which hangs in my shed though no boat of ours has put out since; my mother-in-law's funeral debt, paid in installments over four years to a man who counted the coins twice out of spite; and now, apparently, the survival of an entire family on these two shoulders that have never once been mistaken for broad.

I want it understood — by whoever reads this, by God if He keeps minutes, by Silas with those quiet eyes that miss nothing and say less — that I did not go looking for Edmund Harrow.

That is a lie. I went looking for him the way a drowning man goes looking for water. But I want it understood anyway.

* * *

The factor kept offices above the counting house, and there was a time — before my brother drowned, before the company folded our lives into its books like a washerwoman folds linen — when I would not have been permitted past the second stair. Now I was permitted everywhere, because a man with nothing is no threat to anyone, and Harrow's clerk waved me up with the bored generosity of a man waving away a fly.

Harrow received me standing at his window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the harbor the way other men watch their children sleep. He was smaller than I expected. I don't know why I had imagined him large. Perhaps because ruin, when it comes to your door, always feels enormous, and I had assumed the bringer of it must match.

"Blackwood," he said, without turning. "The uncle. Fisherman."

"Formerly," I said. "Now mostly a mender of other men's nets."

"And other men's problems, I'm told." He turned then, and gave me the smile I had heard about from three separate people, each of whom had described it differently and all of whom had shuddered. It was a good smile. That was the trouble. It had all the parts of a smile arranged correctly, like a wax fruit — convincing at arm's length, nourishing never. "Sit down, Blackwood. Men who mend things are useful. What do you mend for me?"

I sat. My knees, which have complained for years about cold mornings, were silent as stones. Fear does that, sometimes. Freezes the joints into obedience.

"I'll come to it," I said, "because we're neither of us young enough for dancing. You've got papers moving against my family. Transfer orders. A story about smuggling."

Something moved behind Harrow's eyes — not surprise, I think, but interest, the way a fisherman is interested when a shark circles his lines. "I couldn't possibly discuss company business."

"No," I agreed. "You couldn't. So I'll discuss mine. And you can sit there looking like a monument to respectability while I do it."

He laughed. Short, genuine, and worse than the smile. "Go on."

* * *

Here is what I had rehearsed on the walk over, saying it under my breath so that passersby gave me the wide berth usually reserved for madmen and missionaries:

The Blackwoods never touched smuggling. Not one jar of arrack, not one bolt of untaxed cloth, not one coin that didn't come up wet from the sea or honest from a market stall. Harrow knew this. Whatever paper he was building against us, it was wallpaper over a wall with nothing behind it. And a man who builds false walls has enemies on the true side of them — men who move cargo through the straits without troubling themselves about the Straits Trading Company's fees, men whose names Harrow knew because a factor who sells families into plantation labor does not stop at selling families.

I would give him those names. Real names. Names with weight. In exchange: two berths on the Meridian. Two, not one. My sister-in-law Ida and my niece Mariel, the two most vulnerable, the two who could not run far or hide long.

Two lives for a handful of names. On any market in this port, that is a bargain a merchant would take before breakfast.

I laid it out for him in the office above the counting house, and I heard my own voice doing something terrible: it was steady. Reasonable. The voice of a man negotiating freight rates. Somewhere beneath that voice, a smaller one — mine, the real one — was saying, *Tomas, you know what you are doing. Say it plain, at least to yourself.*

But I didn't. Self-deception is a net, and I have spent thirty years mending nets. I know how they hold.

* * *

Let me tell you about the names, since God will ask me eventually and I might as well practice.

There is a man called Ong Kah Wee who runs cargo between here and the Riaus on boats so fast they seem ashamed of their cargo. There is a Malay shipping master named Rahman whose cousin married into a family that Harrow's own superiors in Calcutta would recognize. There are two Europeans whose names I will not write even here, because one of them dines with magistrates and the other owns the warehouse where my daughter buys her rice. I learned these names the way poor men learn everything — by being invisible in rooms where important things were said over our heads. Poverty is a strange education. It teaches you nothing useful and everything dangerous.

For five years I had sat on these names like a hen on stones, hatching nothing, telling myself that knowledge of wickedness is not participation in it. Then the letter came — the sealed letter, the conspiracy, the Blackwood name written where it had no business being written — and the stones cracked, and inside was not a chick but another stone, and inside that, more stones, all the way down.

A man can only sit on so many stones.

* * *

"You're offering to inform," Harrow said, when I finished. He said the word *inform* the way a priest says a name during excommunication. "On genuine smugglers. To save two of your people from a fate that, if I existed — which of course I do not admit to — they were headed toward regardless."

"That's the shape of it."

"And the others? The boy. The old woman. The uncle himself." He gestured at me with two fingers, a small courteous motion that included me among the goods. "Do they not sail?"

"The old woman can't walk to the end of her mat," I said. "And the boy—" I stopped.

Silas. There it was, the reef under my smooth water. Because the plan, my plan, the beautiful pragmatic plan I had built plank by plank on the walk over, required that nobody know about it except Harrow and me. Certainly not Silas, who read silences the way other men read weather, and who would look at me once and see the whole transaction laid bare, itemized, initialed. I could deceive Ida with effort. I could deceive Mariel easily; she trusted me the way children trust roofs. But Silas —

Silas would look at me, and I would know that he knew, and he would say nothing, because silence is his native tongue, and his silence would follow me the rest of my life like a debt I couldn't discharge.

"The boy works for you," I said instead. "He's your clerk. Your own paper says he's implicated. If he sails, you sail him as your property; if he stays, that's your affair. I've negotiated for the ones who can't negotiate for themselves. Someone in every family has to be the coward, Harrow, or there'd be no one left to remember why."

Harrow looked at me for a long moment, and I had the distinct sensation of being appraised as cargo — weight, volume, whether I'd spoil in transit.

"You misunderstand your position," he said pleasantly. "Which is common, in men of your station, and forgivable. You believe you are negotiating. You are not negotiating. You are applying." He came around the desk and sat on its edge, close enough that I could smell his hair oil, something expensive and faintly rancid. "I accept your names. They have some value — not as much as you imagine, but some. In exchange, I will ensure that the transfer manifest lists Ida Blackwood and Mariel Blackwood as *deceased*, which is a different document entirely from a berth, and considerably cheaper. Deceased persons require no provisions."

"Dead," I said. "You'll write them dead."

"On paper." He smiled the wax smile. "Paper deaths are very survivable, Blackwood. People vanish from manifests every week. Desertion, fever, fallen overboard. A death entry in a ledger is simply a door left unlocked. Whether anyone walks through it depends on the family's discretion." His eyes narrowed by a degree, the first honest thing on his face all morning. "Total discretion. No farewells. No explanations. They go where you say, when you say, and if the boy asks questions, the family knows nothing. Are we understood?"

Every instinct I have — and I have the instincts of a man who has survived fifty-one years around sharp hooks and sharper merchants — every instinct said: *this is how it begins.* First discretion, then complicity, then the day three years hence when Harrow sends for me again and the price is higher and the names are closer to home. A man who bargains with a leech should not be surprised to find the leech has opinions about his future.

"Yes," I said. "Understood."

Because two names on a manifest beat four. Because my mother Sefa would die within the year regardless, gently, in her bed, and Ida's hands and Mariel's future were worth my soul at any market rate. Because I told myself — I am still telling myself, hear me, I will be telling myself on my deathbed — that a man who sacrifices his honor to save his family has made a poor trade at the worst possible prices, but he has made the only trade on offer.

* * *

I walked home along the waterfront the long way, which is to say I walked past the kampong and kept walking, all the way to the breakwater, where I sat on the hot stones like a boy and watched the Meridian riding at anchor, gray and patient, a mouth waiting to be fed.

A gull landed near me and regarded my boots with professional interest. "Don't look at me," I told it. "I haven't got anything left."

It stayed anyway. Gulls are loyal to a fault.

I thought about my brother. Everyone in this family thinks about Daniel at odd hours; he had that kind of face, the kind that lodges. Daniel, who dove for pearls with his lungs full of prayer and his heart full of nonsense, who once told me that the sea gives back what is owed — his wife's proverb, actually, but he wore it like his own. The sea had given back Daniel's body on the third tide, which the old divers said meant something, though none of them would say what, and I have wondered for five years whether it was mercy or accusation.

If it was accusation, then I have just given the sea a great deal more to be owed on.

Here is the thing about betrayal that nobody tells you, because the poets are too busy with daggers in the dark: betrayal looks exactly like provision. It looks like a man walking home with lighter shoulders because he has set down something heavy. It looks like supper eaten with appetite. It looks, for the first few hours, like relief — and then the relief curdles, and you find you cannot remember which parts of your plan were sacrifice and which were convenience, and the not-knowing sits in your chest like swallowed seawater.

Ida met me at the door with rice and dried fish and the question she has been asking with her eyes for weeks: *what have you done, Tomas, and what will it cost?*

"Eat," I said, pushing the bowl toward her hands. "Everything's going to be fine."

She looked at me the way women look at men who have just lied badly, which is to say with a patience that is worse than anger. Across the room, little Dev was teaching Mariel a card trick he'd stolen from a sailor, and Mariel laughed at the wrong moment and ruined it, and Dev laughed too, and I sat there among them warm and fed and damned, thinking: *two of them. I have bought two of them. Let that be enough. Let that be enough.*

* * *

It was nearly dark when Silas found me at the shed.

I heard him before I saw him — or rather, I heard nothing before I saw him, which is how he arrives. One moment the yard was empty evening; the next, my nephew stood in the doorway with the last light behind him, and I thought, with the clarity of the recently condemned: *he knows.*

"Tomas." Just that. My name, in his flat careful voice.

"Boy." I kept my hands busy with a net I wasn't mending. "Your mother feed you? She cooks better than she used to. Marriage improved her, God rest him."

"Uncle Tomas." He came inside. He looked at the boat hook hanging on the wall, at the coils of rope, at anything except me, and I understood with a lurch that this was his version of courtesy: giving me time. "I went to the counting house today. Delivering documents. I saw you leaving Harrow's office."

The net slipped through my fingers. I made myself pick it up again, loop by loop, an honest motion to hang a lie on.

"He wanted advice," I said. "About nets."

"In Harrow's office. About nets."

"Fishing is a broad industry."

Silas turned and looked at me then, and I saw what five years in that counting house had built inside my quiet nephew: not hardness, exactly. Structure. A man who has learned to hold still while the world moves, and to notice everything the movement reveals. He looked at me the way an auditor looks at a column that doesn't sum.

"Whatever you traded him," he said quietly, "it won't buy what you think it will."

"There was no trade."

"Tomas." My own name, again, and this time it sounded like a verdict. He stepped closer, and lowered his voice further, until it was barely louder than the sea beyond the seawall. "I've seen the ledgers. All of them. The debts are invented. The ships are real. And there is one berth on the Meridian that can be bought with ink and nerve, and I am going to buy it, and you are standing between me and knowing who needs it most."

I stared at him. The net hung forgotten in my hands, and thirty loops of my alibi unraveled into the dark.

"One berth," I said, when I could speak. "One?"

"One." And then Silas Blackwood, who had mastered silence the way monks master prayer, did something I had never seen him do: he smiled, thin and terrible, and asked the question that cracked my beautiful arithmetic straight down the middle.

"So, Uncle. What exactly did you sell him?"
