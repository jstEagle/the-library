## 12. Night Copyist

The trick to stealing a man's hand is to first steal his vanity.

I had watched Edmund Harrow sign his name perhaps two hundred times in the five years I'd spent copying the company's ledgers — enough to know that he crossed his *t*s like a man fencing, quick and dismissive, and that he looped the tail of his *r* with a small flourish he likely believed no one noticed. He was proud of that flourish. You could see it in the way his pen hesitated a half-beat before it, gathering itself for the little performance. A man's signature is a confession he makes daily: *this is who I believe myself to be.*

Harrow believed himself to be a man of consequence whose time was too valuable for careful penmanship. That was the man I intended to become tonight, in wax and iron gall ink, at the bottom of a candle stub in the copying room where I had no lawful business after dark.

The company kept three keys to the counting house. Harrow wore one on a ribbon. Fenn wore one pinned inside his coat, which told you everything about how much trust Fenn inspired — none, but too little to bother watching. The third hung on a nail behind the door of the copying room itself, because Edmund Harrow had never once imagined that a boy who fetched his tea might also fetch a key off a nail. Contempt is a kind of gift, if you know how to unwrap it.

I had waited four nights for the house across the street to extinguish its last lamp before I came. Four nights of lying in the pallet behind the ink store, listening to my own heart conduct its vulgar arithmetic — *one berth, one name, one chance* — until the streets went quiet as held breath. Then the nail, then the lock, then the dark that smelled of tallow and vinegar and thirty years of other men's fortunes.

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery: it is mostly patience wearing a criminal's coat. You do not sit down and write, the way a guilty man writes. You sit down and copy, the way an honest clerk writes, and honesty is the whole disguise. I had a genuine authorization before me — a transfer docket Harrow had signed that very morning, sending the Ong family's eldest sons to the nutmeg estates, may God forgive me for copying from their ruin — and I studied it the way my father once studied the sea before a dive. Reading the water. Reading the weight of a stroke, the places where Harrow's hand grew lazy and the places where it woke up.

He began documents briskly. The opening line always leaned slightly downhill, as if even words were cargo he wanted moved quickly. By the middle of the page his discipline returned; he'd been schooled somewhere, some dreary academy where they beat slant into boys. And at the end, at the name, the flourish. The vanity. Every document of Edmund Harrow's ended with him introducing himself to posterity.

*E. Harrow, Factor, Straits Trading Company.*

I wrote it forty times on scrap paper cut from the margins of dead accounts. My first dozen looked like a schoolboy aping his father. My next dozen looked like a clerk mocking his master. Somewhere around the thirtieth, my wrist stopped arguing with my eye, and the flourish arrived without being summoned, and I felt something turn over in my chest that I did not like at all.

It was pride.

I sat back and looked at what my hands had learned to do, and understood that there was no version of this night afterward in which I remained the boy who only copied what he was told. The silence I had built my life on — five years of it, load-bearing silence, the silence of a man standing still on river ice — had finally cracked, and beneath it was not drowning but this: a candle, a stolen pen, and a name not mine, written beautifully.

*Silas Blackwood,* I thought, *you are now the most dangerous kind of debtor. The kind who owes nothing.*

That was when the floorboard sighed behind me.

* * *

I have startled before. I have been startled by morays, by falling cargo, by the sound a drowning man's family makes when the company man stops counting them aloud. But I had never felt my body leave the earth entirely, the way it did at that small, dry sigh of old wood.

I turned with the page already crumpling in my fist, some idiot instinct toward swallowing the evidence, and Ambrose Fenn stood in the doorway holding a shuttered lantern and a cup of something that steamed.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the practice sheets scattered on the desk — I had forgotten the practice sheets, of course I had, guilt is an incompetent accomplice — and at the genuine docket, and at my face, which I could feel doing something it had never done in his presence: expressing itself.

"You hold the pen too high," Fenn said. "Harrow chokes the shaft near the nib. That's why his downstrokes are so heavy. He's not writing, he's leaning."

I did not answer. There was a ledger of possible responses open before me — denial, defiance, weeping — and I found I could not afford any entry in it. So I did what I do best. I said nothing, and let the silence testify.

Fenn set the cup on the desk. Tea, I saw. Two cups. He had brought two cups.

He had known.

"Four nights," he said, easing himself onto the stool by the file cabinet with the groan of a man whose knees had been filing grievances for decades. "You wait for the factor's lamp across the street. Clever. He reads correspondence after supper — thinks it looks industrious if anyone sees his window lit." He nodded at the candle. "But your light shows through the same shutters as his does, boy, and I walk past this building every night because I sleep badly and my wife snores worse. Did you think the counting house kept its own secrets?"

"How long," I managed.

"Since the second night. I wanted to see whether you'd be caught." He unfolded his spectacles and put them on, and became suddenly, terribly official. "You won't be. Not by me. Sit down."

"I can explain—"

"You can't, and I don't require it." He drew a sheet of scrap toward himself and picked up a pen, and his voice dropped into the register of a man reciting scripture he was ashamed to know by heart. "In thirty years I have watched this company sell four hundred and eleven people. I know the number because I keep the number, because someone must, because numbers are the only souls this company believes in. Do you imagine I need your explanation? I need your hands steady, that's all. Yours are steadier than mine now." He looked up, and behind the spectacles his eyes were wet and furious and old. "Sit down, Silas. Let me teach you the part that will hang you if you get it wrong."

I sat.

* * *

What Fenn taught me over the next two hours would have been worth a flogging per lesson under ordinary law, and several sorts of hanging under the company's private arithmetic.

"The authorization isn't valid under the signature alone," he said, laying out the genuine docket flat and weighting its corners with ink pots. "Any fool can copy a hand. Harrow knows this — he copies hands himself when it suits him, though he calls it 'regularizing correspondence.' So the company uses countersign seals. Three of them." He touched each in turn without actually lifting the papers. "The factor's mark, here, bottom right — a griffin rampant, pressed crooked, always crooked, because the die slipped years ago and Harrow decided correcting it was beneath him. Bless his laziness; it's the easiest seal in Singapore to authenticate. Second, the counter-factor's ring seal, top left, small as a fingernail. Third—" he paused, and something moved through his face like weather, "—third is the witness seal. The senior clerk attests that the document passed through his office in the ordinary course of business."

"Whose is the witness seal?" I asked, though I already knew. His coat was heavier than other men's coats. I had noticed years ago and filed it among the thousand things I pretended not to see.

"Mine," Fenn said. "Thirty years of witnessing. Do you know what a witness is, boy, in this building? It is a man who certifies that the lie was properly formatted."

He took a small oilcloth packet from inside his coat — the coat was indeed heavy; I wondered now how many packets it had carried over the years — and unwrapped it with the tenderness of a man handling a relic. Inside lay brass dies, a stick of sealing wax the color of dried blood, and a stub of candle different from mine: black, tallow-foul, the kind used to darken a room while documents were burned.

"Wax first. Harrow uses cheap wax, brittle, and he skims the wick across it rather than letting the flame drip — impatience. Your forgery must be impatient too. Perfection will betray you faster than error. Error is honest; perfection is evidence." He demonstrated: a puddle of wax, uneven, a breath blown across it, then the press — firm, half a heartbeat, lift straight up. The crooked griffin bloomed in the ruddy wax, listing slightly east like a drunk sentry.

"Now you."

My hands, which had been steady all night, shook. Of course they shook. This was no longer practice. Practice happens in a country where consequences haven't been invented yet. This was Singapore. This was treason against a company that owned my debt, my mother's debt, my sister's labor and my grandmother's bed and my uncle's boat, all of it entered fair and square in ledgers I had helped copy true.

Fenn watched my hands tremble and did not insult me by pretending not to see.

"My first forgery," he said quietly, "was 1813. A bill of lading. I was seventeen, and my factor told me the cargo count was 'an estimate,' and I wrote the false number in a fine round schoolboy hand and told myself it was an estimate too." He pressed his own seal into fresh wax, absently, the way another man might worry a rosary. "The cargo was eleven people. I have been estimating ever since." He looked at me over the spectacles. "So when I say your soul survives this, understand the source. And when I say it doesn't survive intact, understand the same source."

I breathed. I pressed. The griffin rose crooked out of the wax, listing east, imperfect, honest in its dishonesty.

"There," Fenn said softly. "Now you're one of us. God forgive us both, and the devil keep the minutes."

We worked. He showed me the ring seal's placement — always overlapping the page edge, so that removal destroyed it — and taught me the wax blend Harrow favored and the particular cheapness of his blotting paper. He corrected my flourish twice ("Too elegant. He's vain, not skilled") and my spacing ("He crowds the date. Dates cost him nothing."). Somewhere in the middle of it he drank his tea cold without noticing, and I realized that the old clerk's hands were shaking worse than mine, and that teaching me this was either his salvation or his suicide and he had not yet decided which, and was proceeding regardless, the way men proceed when they have finally run out of ways to stand still.

"Why?" I asked him, near the end. It was the most dangerous question in the building and I asked it anyway. Five years of silence, and it turns out the habit breaks all at once or not at all. "Why teach me? You could report me and be safe forever. Harrow would promote you."

Fenn wrapped his seals away slowly, folding the oilcloth along creases worn soft by decades.

"Because safe is what I've been," he said, "and I have counted what it bought. A drawer with a false bottom, a wife who snores, and four hundred and eleven names I recite to myself on nights I can't sleep, which is every night." He closed the packet. "You are going to forge that berth, and it will save exactly one Blackwood and damn the rest unless something else changes, and I am too old and too frightened to change anything else myself. But I can change one thing." He looked at me, and for a moment the clerk vanished and something younger and more ruined looked out. "I can make sure the boy who does it isn't caught by anything so stupid as a crooked seal."

The candle had burned down to a coin of tallow. Across the street, the factor's window stood dark at last. In an hour the first carts would rumble past and the cooks would light their fires and Singapore would perform its daily resurrection of commerce, entirely unaware that somewhere in its midst a boy now possessed a factor's hand, a clerk's conscience, and three seals' worth of permission to commit one perfect crime.

I gathered my practice sheets to burn them. Fenn stopped my hand.

"Keep one," he said. "The best one. Fold it small."

"Why?"

"Because someday," he said, "you'll need to remember that you chose to be this. It wasn't done to you. You walked in that door four nights running." He pinched out the candle between wet fingers, and the dark came down gentle as a lid. "Now go, before the sweepers come. And Silas — come back tomorrow. There are things you don't yet know about how the Meridian's manifests are drawn up, and six weeks is shorter than you think."

I paused at the door, my stolen pages folded against my chest like contraband scripture.

"Shorter than I think," I repeated.

"Yes." Fenn's voice came quiet out of the dark. "Because Harrow has begun burning correspondence. And men who burn paper," he said, "are men who intend to sail early."
