## 13. The Kampong Remembers

There is a particular silence a woman learns when her husband drowns for a debt. It is not grief's silence, which at least has the decency to howl. It is the silence of arithmetic — the sound of a company clerk's pen moving across a page somewhere in town, crossing out one man and entering his children under *assets*. I lived inside that silence for five years. I mended nets in it. I buried my mourning under it like a seed I was too frightened to water.

My son Silas inherited it differently. He went quiet the way stone goes quiet — all its argument turned inward. Mariel went quiet the way a kettle does just before it screams. And me? I went quiet the way the sea goes quiet before it remembers what it has swallowed and decides to give some of it back.

It began, as most dangerous things in this kampong begin, with fish.

Old Rahman brought me a basket of kembong on a Tuesday, unasked, which meant he wanted something or he was sorry about something, and Rahman had never in his life been sorry about anything except once, when he'd bet his boat on a cockfight and lost it to a man with no legs. He set the basket down on my step and looked at the horizon as if it owed him an explanation.

"Your boy," he said, "has been asking questions."

"Which boy?"

"He has only the one, Ida."

He had me there. Silas had been coming to the water on his half-days, sitting with the old divers, saying almost nothing, which from Silas was practically a speech. He would listen while they talked of tides and prices and the new rules from the Trading Company — the ones that said a diver's catch now belonged first to the ledger and only second to the diver. He listened the way a man counts another man's money.

"What sort of questions?" I asked.

Rahman picked up his basket again, decided against it, put it down. "Not questions. Corrections." His mouth twisted. "He told Osman that the advance paid to Jumali in '38 was never entered in the kampong book. He told him the figure in the company's copy was twice what Jumali signed for. He knew the dates, Ida. He knew the amounts better than the men who took them."

I felt something turn over in my chest, slow and cold, like a boat shifting at anchor. "And Osman?"

"Osman laughed at him. Then stopped laughing. Then went home early." Rahman finally looked at me. "The boy reads silences, your Silas. Always did, even small. But lately—" he made a gesture, vague and enormous, taking in the sea, the sky, the whole rotten arrangement we called our lives — "lately it seems the silences are reading him back."

After Rahman left, I sat with the fish and thought about my husband's hands.

This is what I remember, when I let myself remember, which until that Tuesday I had mostly declined to do: Ah Blackwood — because everyone called him that, even the Chinese traders, even the priest, though his name was Joseph and his mother had been born Catholic in Malacca — Ah Blackwood had hands like rope, scarred where the coral had taken its tithe over twenty years of diving. He could hold his breath longer than any man on this coast, and he was proud of it in the modest way of a man who is proud of everything about himself without ever saying so. When he came up from the bottom with a shell in his fist he would open his hand slowly, like a conjurer, and watch your face for the reveal.

He took an advance from the company in the spring of '39. Forty dollars against the season's catch — forty dollars for a new pump, for rice, because Mariel was sick with the fever and the medicine cost more than dignity. He signed a paper he could not read, and the man who held the paper smiled at him with all the warmth of a door.

They told me after that he had defaulted. That his catch had fallen short. That the debt passed, by the terms of his indenture, to his family. They said it kindly, in the way of men who have said it so many times the kindness has worn smooth as river stone, revealing nothing underneath.

What they did not say — what nobody said aloud, then — was that his catch had not fallen short at all. That he had been bringing up pearl shell all that season, good quantities of it, and that every shell went into the company's hold and every entry into the company's book was written smaller than the last, so that by August my husband's labor had been shrunk on paper to almost nothing, and his forty-dollar debt had swollen to a hundred and ten, and the gap between those two numbers was a mouth.

I did not know all of it then. I knew some of it. I knew enough to keep my eyes down when the factor's man came through counting children.

But there were others who knew more, and they had kept their knowledge the way divers keep pearls — swallowed deep, past the reach of any hook.

* * *

I called them together on the Thursday, in the shade of the big tembusu behind Sefa's house, because Sefa could not come to anyone anymore and because I wanted her within hearing. My mother-in-law lay inside on her mat by the window, and though the doctors called her absence *the fading*, I have noticed that the faded hear more than the hale. She would want to hear this. She had married a diver too.

They came, seven of them, the old ones: Rahman; Osman, who was Rahman's cousin and his rival in everything including grievance; Jumali, whose lungs had been ruined by thirty years of pressure so that now he breathed like a man perpetually apologizing for the air; the brothers Halim and Yusof, who between them had fathered eleven children and buried four, three of those to the plantation ships across the strait; old Chong, the only Chinese diver the kampong ever trusted, retired; and Fatimah, who was not a diver at all but whose husband had died in the same season as mine and who had never once let me grieve alone, whether I wanted company or not.

I served tea because you serve tea. I waited until the cups were cool enough to hold.

"I'm going to speak of Ah Blackwood," I said. "And I'm going to ask you to speak of him too. Out loud. In front of witnesses."

Nobody moved. The tembusu dropped a leaf, the way trees will, adding their commentary.

"It's been five years," said Osman, not unkindly. "Grief gets heavy, Ida. Carrying it in public too — why?"

"Because it isn't only grief anymore." I set down my cup. "My son has found something in the company's books. Something about families being sold across the strait to pay debts that were made up out of thin air and bad handwriting. Our name is in those books. He says the smuggling charge against us is written in ink that hasn't dried yet, and that the ship that will carry us away leaves within weeks."

Jumali's breath caught, wheezed, caught again. Fatimah reached over and put her hand flat on his back, the way you steady a barrel.

"And you think," said Chong slowly, speaking the trade-language carefully, each word placed like a footing on wet rock, "that we can stop this. With talk. With old men's memories."

"With old men's memories," I said, "and paper, if we can get it. Yes."

Halim laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor anywhere in it. "The commission man comes, you tell him a story, the factor shows him a book full of proper figures, and then the story-tellers find themselves on a ship. I have seen this play, Ibu Blackwood. I know how the third act goes."

"So do I," I said. "I've been living in it for five years."

That silenced him, which was not my intention but served.

Then Jumali spoke, and what he said changed the shape of the evening entirely.

"The advance," he rasped. "You're speaking of the forty dollars. I was there when it was signed. I witnessed the mark." His ruined lungs gathered themselves. "I can witness more than the mark, if witnessing is suddenly wanted. I saw the hold entries that season. I helped load the shells. Your husband's catch was *good*, Ida. Better than good. I said so at the time, to the tally-man, and the tally-man wrote something else, and when I asked him why, he showed me a coin and said nothing at all, and I understood the coin was for me to stop asking, and I took it." His eyes were wet and furious. "I took it. For one Straits dollar I helped bury the truth of a good man. So ask me, Ida. Ask me in front of whoever you like."

The silence after that was a different silence from all the silences before it. It was the sound of a door that had been shut for five years beginning, joint by rusty joint, to open.

Rahman cleared his throat. "The night of the storm," he said. "You want to hear it said plainly? Fine. Plainly, then." He set his cup down with the deliberation of a man laying a stone. "Ah Blackwood went out that night because the company's man told him the season closed at moonrise and anything after was theft. There was no storm due — we all read the sky, we knew what the sky said, and the sky said clear. The squall came up out of nowhere, which squalls do not do. And his boat —" Rahman's voice hardened. "His boat was sound. I'd caulked her myself in June. Boats I caulk do not go down in a squall I have personally watched stand up and sit back down three times that monsoon."

"You're saying—" Fatimah began.

"I'm saying what we all said to each other afterward, quietly, over the funeral food, and then agreed never to say again. The seam opened. Caulked seams don't open. Someone opened one." He looked around the circle. "A forty-dollar debt became a hundred and ten dollars of invented shortfall, and a dead man's family became the property of the Straits Trading Company, and every one of us watched the sums work themselves out and kept our mouths full of teeth."

Yusof spoke for the first time, quietly, to his hands. "Three of my children are on plantations because of sums like that. I signed papers I couldn't read because the alternative was watching them starve slowly instead of quickly." He raised his head. "If there is paper now — if there is a way to make the numbers tell the truth for once — count me in it. Count all of them. Every name."

One by one they gave their pieces to the night, and I want to tell you it was noble and it was not entirely noble; grief makes poor rhetoric and fear keeps interrupting. Old Chong remembered the tally-house schedule, which boats carried shell on which days. Halim remembered — grudgingly, dragged up like an anchor from deep water — that he had once been offered an "advance" he never requested, forty dollars appearing in his account column out of nowhere, a gift that was really a leash, and that he'd had to dive two full seasons to swim out from under it. Fatimah, who missed nothing and forgot less, remembered the names of every family taken across the strait since '36, recited them like a rosary while we wrote them down by lamplight, and when she finished she said, "That's not all of them. That's just the ones from here. There were kampongs north of us. There were kampongs south."

By midnight we had nine pages of names, dates, amounts, and testimony, written in my hand because my father had the scandalous wisdom to teach his daughter her letters, and every page trembled a little, because lamplight flickers and so do hands that are writing down the crimes of powerful men.

Chong looked at the pages the way a man looks at a live coal he's been handed. "This needs to be copied. Hidden. More than one place, in case of fire — and there will be a fire, Ida, mark me, if Harrow smells this."

"There's a boy," I said, "who works in the counting house. Who found the pattern in the ledgers in the first place."

"Your Silas." Chong nodded slowly. "Good. Keep him careful. Careful boys grow into useful men. Careless ones grow into warnings."

Sefa's voice came through the window, thin and clear as a whistle off the reef, and every one of us froze. The fading woman, who had not spoken a full sentence in months, who we sometimes feared had already gone halfway across to the other shore.

"The sea gives back," she said, to no one and to everyone. "Cold and late, but it gives back. Tell them. Tell them my Joseph is coming back."

Fatimah went in to her. The rest of us sat a moment with our dead, who had, it seemed, been waiting all along for someone to say their names out loud where the living could hear.

* * *

I walked home along the tideline with the pages wrapped in oilcloth against my breast, and the sea beside me did its ancient indifferent breathing, in and out, in and out, and I thought: it isn't indifferent at all. It's patient. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

Five years I had kept my eyes down. Five years I had mended other people's nets and let mine tear. But a net, I have learned, is only rope arranged in the shape of a decision, and I had decided.

At the bend near the rocks I saw a lantern coming toward me, quick-moving, and my heart did what hearts do, and then I saw it was Mariel, running with her skirts hitched, barefoot, her face white above the dark sand.

"Mama." She grabbed my arms. She was breathing hard, and beneath the running-breath there was another kind of breath entirely. "Mama, I heard something at the factor's house tonight. Something about the ship. It's bad. It's worse than we thought — it's moving faster, and Uncle Tomas—"

She stopped, steadied herself against me, and I felt the oilcloth pages between us, nine pages of the dead insisting on their truth, and I looked out once at the black water keeping its patience, and I said:

"Start at the beginning, child. And don't leave out a single name."
