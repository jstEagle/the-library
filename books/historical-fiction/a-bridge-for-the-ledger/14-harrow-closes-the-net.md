## 14. Harrow Closes the Net

There is a particular smell to trouble in a counting house. It is not smoke, though smoke often follows. It is not ink, though ink is its accomplice. It is quieter than either — a stillness in the clerks' room that should not be there. A counting house at work sounds like rain on a tin roof: pens scratching, ledgers turning, the small wet coughs of men who have been sitting too long under a fan. When that rain stops — when the room goes dry and watchful, every man suddenly fascinated by his own column of figures — you may be sure that something has begun to move beneath the floor.

I smelled it on a Tuesday, over my morning coffee, which Mr. Rafferty's houseboy had brewed too strong because Rafferty was too cheap to buy proper beans. I had learned long ago that a factor who cannot taste bad coffee deserves everything else that happens to him, so I drank it down and let it tell me what it had to tell.

The trouble had a shape. It always does. In thirty years of managing other people's debts I have catalogued perhaps nine shapes of trouble, and this one — the shape where a room full of guilty men becomes polite — was the one I liked least, because politeness is expensive. Politeness means they have begun to think of themselves as witnesses rather than participants.

The occasion, I supposed, was the Blackwood transfer. It was coming along nicely, if one can call anything nice that involves moving seven souls across the strait to a nutmeg estate whose owner pays his invoices in rumour and promises. The boy Silas had come before me some weeks past and performed ignorance so beautifully that I had nearly applauded. Nearly. There was a moment — a half-second when I mentioned his mother's name and his hands went still on his knees, still as a man holding a door shut against wind — that told me everything the rest of his performance was built to conceal.

Stillness, in my experience, is the loudest thing a frightened person ever says. A man who is merely stupid fidgets. A man who is merely afraid trembles. But a man who has understood the whole of his situation, and has decided to do arithmetic with it, goes perfectly still. The Blackwood boy had gone still as a ledger line.

I had filed that observation where I file such things, in the drawer of facts that are true but not yet urgent. On Tuesday morning, listening to the too-quiet clerks' room, I took it back out.

* * *

Mr. Ambrose Fenn came to me at ten, as he did every day, carrying the day-book for signature. Fenn is sixty-one years old and has worked for the Straits Trading Company since before I could write my own name, and in all that time he has perfected the art of being furniture. He shuffles. He apologizes. He misplaces things just often enough to seem harmless and never often enough to be inconvenient. He is, in short, the finest piece of self-effacement I have ever employed, and I would trust him with nothing.

"Fenn," I said, while he stood with the day-book open and his eyes politely elsewhere. "How long have we known each other?"

"Thirty years, sir, near enough."

"And in thirty years, how many times have you lied to me?"

He looked up then, and his face did what his face does, which is nothing at all, beautifully. "I shouldn't know how to begin counting, sir, seeing as I've never been caught."

It was almost an honest answer, which from Fenn is a confession wearing its Sunday clothes. "Someone has been reading ahead," I said. "Someone has been in the correspondence drawers, or standing behind doors, or asking questions in the wrong quarters of town. Someone has made this office feel like a chapel. Do you know who?"

"I couldn't say, sir." A pause of exactly the right length. "Though if I were a guessing man, I'd say it's the season. People get restless before the ships go. Families talk. Kampongs gossip. Nothing a body can do about weather like that except sail through it quicker."

Sail through it quicker. I looked at him and wondered, not for the first time, whether Fenn was a fool who happened to be wise or a wise man performing folly so long that the performance had grown roots. Thirty years is long enough for either. It is certainly long enough for both.

"Thank you, Fenn," I said. "That will be all."

He shuffled out. And I sat with my terrible coffee and did the thing I do best, which is to say I thought about money.

* * *

Here is what I have come to understand about the trade I am in, the real trade, beneath the pepper and the tin and the respectable stationery. Every enterprise of any size rests upon a foundation of people who prefer not to know. Not villains — villains are rare and mostly useless, being prone to speeches. No: the foundation is ordinary men who have arranged their consciences the way a clerk arranges a desk, everything squared away, everything findable, nothing left out overnight. My own conscience sits on my own desk in exactly that fashion. I dust it weekly. It has never once inconvenienced me.

But consciences are like gunpowder: individually inert, collectively catastrophic, and dangerously responsive to damp. The damp in this case was a family named Blackwood, whose debts I had inflated by the standard methods — the advance for the boat that never got repaid, the interest compounded on interest, the funeral costs of a drowned man charged to his widow because someone must pay for rope and canvas even when the rope and canvas are at the bottom of the straits. Standard methods. Textbook. I have moved forty families this way and never lost a night's sleep, chiefly because I schedule my worrying between four and four-fifteen each afternoon and the Blackwoods fell outside the window.

What was different now? The boy. The boy who read silences. The sister in my own kitchen with her quick eyes and her scrubbed knuckles. An old clerk who spoke in proverbs about sailing quickly. And underneath it all, the oldest damp of all: the sense — irrational, unprovable, but as reliable as tide-tables — that somewhere a pen was moving that ought not to be.

I did not panic. Panic is for men whose affairs are disordered. Instead I made three decisions before lunch, which is the correct number of decisions for any crisis, two fewer and you drift, one more and you blunder.

* * *

The first decision concerned the ship.

The *Meridian* was scheduled to depart on the twenty-eighth with her cargo of transferred debtors, forty-one souls including the Blackwood contingent, bound for the estates across the strait. Twenty-eight was a civilized date. It allowed for manifests, inspections, the chaplain's visit, the various ceremonies by which we convert human beings into cargo while maintaining our eligibility for church pews.

I sent for the harbourmaster's runner and moved the departure to the nineteenth.

Nine days earlier. Let the gossips choke on it. A shortened schedule means shortened scrutiny; a manifest signed in haste is rarely read twice; and families who believe they have weeks to scheme discover, when the ship appears at anchor with her boats lowered, that scheming requires time the way drowning requires air. I have watched strong men reduced to bargaining for the privilege of packing their own shirts. Time is the only weapon the powerless ever hold, and so the first rule of my profession is simple: never let them hold it long.

The second decision concerned paper.

* * *

I burned correspondence on Wednesday evening, in the yard behind the counting house, in an iron basin I keep for the purpose, and I will tell you honestly that it is my favourite hour of any difficult month. There is something clean about it. Men imagine that evil delights in mess, but they have it backwards; evil is a housekeeper. Evil files, evil shreds, evil balances its books nightly. It is innocence that leaves papers lying about.

Into the fire went the draft testimony regarding the Blackwood conspiracy — the smuggling fiction, elegantly constructed, which named the father posthumously and the mother and uncle as receivers. Into the fire went three letters from the plantation concern quoting rates per head, letters written in the coy language gentlemen use when pricing things they will deny pricing. Into the fire went a memorandum in my own hand, six months old, headed *Re: surplus accounts, kampong district*, which said in cold arithmetic precisely what we were doing and why, because I am careful even with what I destroy and had kept only one copy.

The smoke went up straight in the still air. Somewhere beyond the compound wall a dog barked at it, or at the evening, or at nothing. Dogs always know. I have never trusted an animal that cannot be bribed.

While the basin cooled I reviewed what remained. One keeps, always: the official transfer orders bearing the resident councillor's rubber stamp, which is legitimate because I obtained the stamp legitimately, or legitimately adjacent. One keeps the original debt instruments, inflated but formally impeccable — every thumbprint genuine, every mark witnessed, because the beauty of debt is that the debtor signs his own condemnation and thanks you for the loan of the pen. What one burns is only the commentary. The crime itself lives in documents no court would ever call criminal. That is the entire art.

A boy passed the yard gate as the last pages curled — a company errand boy, one of the half-dozen we keep running messages through town. He glanced at the fire. I smiled at him and held up a sheet of plain wrapping paper, torn, and let him see me burn that, so that in his retelling — and boys always retell — the great factor burned rubbish after hours, eccentric but harmless. Witnesses are managed the same way as fires: fed the fuel you choose, starved of everything else.

His name was Pell, I remembered. Ong Pell. A thin, serious boy with excellent handwriting and no discernible sense of humour, which in a clerk is worth more than honesty.

Which brings me to the third decision.

* * *

On Thursday I promoted him.

Ong Pell, aged sixteen, junior copyist, elevated to assistant keeper of correspondence — a position with a stool, a raise of four dollars a month, and access to the letter-room, where every document entering or leaving this office passes under his young eyes. The clerks congratulated him with the sour generosity of men watching a favourite chosen. Fenn shook his hand and told him the drawer sticks in damp weather, lift it toward you, and Pell thanked him gravely, and I watched them both and felt the particular pleasure of a man placing a new lock on an old door.

Pell is mine. I chose him for three qualities. First, ambition: he wants to rise, and rising men report upward, eagerly and often. Second, poverty: his mother sells cakes outside the temple, and four dollars a month buys a loyalty that philosophy cannot purchase. Third — and here is the quality I prize above the others — he is incurious in the correct direction. Show Pell an interesting secret and he does not wonder about it; he weighs it. Everything is information about how to please the man who promotes him. Such boys make magnificent instruments. They also make terrible conspirators, because conspiracy requires believing that some allegiance outranks your own advancement, and Pell believes in nothing of the kind.

"Sir," he said, standing in my office that afternoon with his new keys already worn on a cord around his neck like a saint's medal, "the letter-room registers are in poor order. Entries missing dates. Some items logged without senders."

"Yes," I said. "Fenn is old. Old men log poorly."

"Shall I rebuild the register, sir?"

"You shall rebuild it," I said, "and you shall bring it to me weekly, and you shall tell me if any item appears in the register whose presence you cannot explain. That is all. That is the whole of your duty. Do it well and in five years you'll have Fenn's stool, and in fifteen perhaps my window."

He bowed. He was gone. Outside my window I could see him crossing the yard with his keys clinking, walking slightly taller, already a different animal. I gave him until Sunday before his first report. I estimated it would concern Fenn.

Was I certain of Fenn? Certainly enough. Thirty years of survival argues competence, and competence in a compromised man argues continued compromise — a man who has stayed bought stays bought, because the alternative is confessing that he was ever for sale. But the chapel-quiet in the clerks' room had started somewhere, and Fenn was the only soul in this building old enough to remember every transaction and quiet enough to survive knowing them. If a leak ran through my counting house, it ran through Ambrose Fenn the way water runs through limestone: invisibly, patiently, dissolving things.

And if I was wrong about Fenn — well. Then I would learn it from Pell's register, or from the boy Blackwood's face, or from whatever other small instrument the universe saw fit to hand me. Instruments always arrive. That is the consolation of power: you need never look for tools. You need only keep the workshop lit.

* * *

Thursday evening I dined with the magistrate's wife, which is a sentence I offer without comment, and Friday I attended to the ordinary business of the company — tin weights, freight rebates, an angry letter from a Calcutta house answered in my best conciliatory hand. Banality is the armour of my kind of work. A man seen doing dull things daily is presumed incapable of dramatic ones, and the presumption holds beautifully right up until the day the *Meridian* casts off with forty-one souls aboard and everyone remembers, afterward, how very normal Mr. Harrow always seemed.

Saturday I walked the wharf in the early heat, alone, as I do, because a factor who never looks at his own ships is a factor trusting other men's eyes. The *Meridian* lay at her moorings taking on water casks, and her boatswain touched his cap to me, and I counted the crew by eye out of habit. Nine days now. The Blackwood woman would be notified officially on Monday — notice of transfer, effective immediately, transport provided, debts discharged upon arrival, all in the company's most humane typeface. She would have eight days to pack a life she believed she'd have five more weeks to arrange. Grief needs a runway. I was shortening hers, and I felt about it precisely what I feel about all necessary expenditures: that sentiment is a cost, and costs belong in ledgers, and ledgers balance.

It was on the walk back, passing the fish stalls where the kampong women gutted the morning catch with knives they handled like extensions of their wrists, that I felt it — the sensation I get perhaps twice a year, the one that has kept me alive and solvent while cleverer men died bankrupt or worse. Eyes. Not hostile eyes, not yet. Attentive ones. Somewhere among the stalls and nets and drying racks, someone connected to my affairs was watching me walk, and measuring, and remembering.

I did not turn my head. Turning confirms. I bought a fish I did not want, overpaid magnificently, complimented the fishwife's daughter on her stitching, and strolled home like a man with nothing heavier on his mind than dinner. Let them report that. Let them report the fish. Reports of the harmless are the cheapest insurance a man can buy.

But in my study, with the shutters closed against the afternoon, I permitted myself the luxury of a single page of honest thinking, which I wrote and then fed to my lamp.

The threats, in order: the boy Silas, who could forge — copyists' sons can all forge, it is the hereditary disease of their caste. The old man Fenn, who knew everything and had lately developed the habit of meaning something by his silences. The girl in my kitchen, whom I had dismissed as furniture and furniture, as I should have remembered from Fenn, sometimes listens. And the uncle — Tomas, the brother-in-law, a pragmatic man with gambling debts of his own and no loyalty that a sufficiently precise offer could not purchase. Of the four, three were problems. One was an opportunity wearing a problem's clothes.

I wrote his name last, and I looked at it longest.

A man like Tomas Blackwood does not come to you asking favours; he comes offering goods, and the goods are always the same goods — other people. If the family was planning anything, and the chapel-quiet said it was, then Tomas was the loose plank in their deck, and planks can be pulled up, or nailed down, or walked across at one's convenience. All that remained was to arrange the meeting so that it appeared to be his idea. Men defend ideas of their own making with their lives. It is the single most economical fact I know.

I sealed nothing, sent nothing. I simply mentioned, to my steward, within earshot of the kitchen — within earshot, therefore, of a scullery maid with quick eyes — that I would be receiving a delegation of creditors at the Selangor Rooms on Tuesday evening, private, and that I disliked conducting delicate negotiations in my own house.

Let the information travel wherever it travels. Information always finds its market. And if a desperate uncle with debts and a weak deck-plank heart found his way to the Selangor Rooms on Tuesday — why, then I had merely gone there to dine.

Nine days until the *Meridian*. Four days until Tuesday. I trimmed my lamp, balanced my day-book, and slept the sleep of a man whose accounts, whatever else may be said of them, are current.

Somewhere across the town, in a kampong smelling of salt and fish-scale, a family believed they had weeks. I intended to enjoy the interval.
