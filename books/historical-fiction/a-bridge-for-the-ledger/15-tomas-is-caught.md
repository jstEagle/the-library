## 15. Tomas Is Caught

My uncle Tomas has a laugh for every occasion. I have catalogued them the way other men catalogue tides: the bark he uses at cards, the wheeze he deploys when a creditor grows tiresome, the low chuckle that means *I am about to do something foolish and would prefer you not watch.* It was that last one I heard on Tuesday last, as he clapped me on the shoulder outside the counting house and said, "Silas, boy, don't wait up for me tonight."

I have survived five years in this company by reading what men do not say. What Tomas did not say was: *I have done something already.* What his hands did not say — hands too still, too carefully folded over his belt — was anything reassuring at all.

I waited up anyway. He came home smelling of Harrow's cigars and gin, which was wrong in every particular, because my uncle cannot abide gin and cannot afford cigars. He went to bed without his usual sermon on the improvidence of merchants. That silence frightened me more than any speech could have.

But I did not yet know. Knowledge arrived three days later, folded into a scrap of paper no bigger than a hymnal page, smuggled out of the factor's house inside the heel of yesterday's bread — my sister Mariel's method, learned from watching how the kitchen maid smuggled out sugar. My family is not a family of thieves by nature. We have simply been given occasion.

The scrap read, in Mariel's careful schoolroom hand:

*H. met with T.B. Tuesday, the 14th, in the back office. H. dictating after. Words taken down: "the boy's arrangements — the berth — who else knows — the old man Fenn." T.B.'s answers not recorded. H. instructed me to copy the transfer papers early. New date on the manifest: the Meridian sails the 26th, not the 2nd.*

I sat with that paper in my lap for the better part of an hour, in the lean-to behind Mother's house where I go to think, while the monsoon tested the palm thatch above my head like a landlord checking for vacancies.

Let me be precise about what it said, because precision is the only virtue I have never been tempted to abandon.

It said my uncle had gone to Edmund Harrow. It said my uncle had offered something, because men do not meet twice with Harrow unless they bring tribute. And it said Harrow had asked about *the berth* — singular, definite, capitalized in the way of things that matter — which meant Tomas knew about the forged authorization, which meant someone had told him, and there were only three people alive who knew, and I had told exactly one of them.

I had told Uncle Tomas.

I want to describe my anger properly, because it deserves description. It was not the hot kind, the kind that breaks crockery. It was the cold kind, the kind my father must have felt the moment he understood the water coming into his boat was not weather but arithmetic — a debt being collected in the only currency the company respected. My anger was quiet enough to count things in. So I counted.

Count one: six weeks of planning, reduced to rubble in a single evening of gin and self-importance.

Count two: Fenn's name in Harrow's mouth. Ambrose Fenn, thirty years of gray obedience, now suspect because my uncle could not keep his mouth shut around a man who collects secrets the way other collectors collect bird eggs — with the bird still in them.

Count three: the twenty-sixth. Nine days. Nine days in which I had meant to perfect Harrow's hand, learn the countersign seals, and move like a man threading a needle in the dark. Now I would have to move like a man running through fire instead.

And then count four, which was the one that undid all the others: pity. Because I knew Tomas. I knew the ledger of that man the way I knew the company's ledgers, and both were full of inflated entries. Tomas had not sold us out of malice. He had gone to Harrow believing himself the cleverest Blackwood in the room, intending to trade the names of real smugglers — men he drank with, men who owed him money — for two berths on the Meridian. Two berths. One for Grandmother Sefa, who could not walk to the end of the pier unassisted, and one for whoever carried her. He would have stood in front of us afterward with his chin up and called it sacrifice. He might even have believed it. Men can believe almost anything if they rehearse it in front of a mirror long enough.

He had walked into Harrow's office carrying two berths' worth of hope, and Harrow had smelled it on him the way a shark smells the one bleeding fish in a school of ten thousand.

I folded Mariel's scrap small, buried it under a loose brick, and went to find my mother.

* * *

Ida Blackwood took the news the way she takes everything: standing up, hands busy. She was splicing rope when I told her, and she did not stop splicing rope, but her fingers slowed by half, which for her is the equivalent of another woman screaming.

"Tomas," she said at last. "That great ox. That dear, stupid ox."

"Mother—"

"He went to Harrow." She said the name the way you'd spit out a fishbone. "Your father's own brother went to that man's office and knocked on the door like it was a shop."

"He thought he was saving Sefa. Saving us."

"I know what he thought." She set down the rope. Her eyes, when they found mine, were the sea before weather: flat, deep, and entirely without mercy for anything floating on the surface. "That is what makes it unforgivable, Silas. If he'd done it out of greed we could hate him clean and be done with it. But he did it out of love, and love makes a worse mess than greed ever did. Greed knows its own size."

She was right, and I hated that she was right, because I had come to her wanting permission to hate him plainly, and mothers do not issue those permits.

"What will Harrow make him do?" she asked.

"The scrap doesn't say. But Harrow asked about the berth. About Fenn. About who else knows." I swallowed. "Harrow doesn't interrogate men he intends to punish, Mother. He interrogates men he intends to use. Punishment ends a thing. Use gets value from it first."

"And when the using is done?"

"You've seen what happens to tools at the end of a voyage."

She nodded slowly, and then did the thing I could not have predicted and should have: she began winding the good rope onto its spool, banking the lamp, putting her house in order. As if preparing for a storm. Which, in fairness, she was.

"Then we tell no one else," she said. "Not Dev, not the divers, no one. Whatever Tomas gave him, we assume Harrow has it all. We plan from tonight as though every word spoken in this kampong goes first to the factor's desk for his approval."

"That's not far from true."

"No," she said. "It very nearly isn't."

* * *

Fenn knew before I told him. Of course he did; Fenn knows things the way driftwood knows the tide, by long submersion. When I came to the counting house the next morning he had already moved my inkwell to the left side of my desk instead of the right — our signal, established in candle smoke weeks ago, meaning *we do not speak today.* But as I passed his desk he cleared his throat, a sound like a door deciding whether to open, and murmured, so quietly I nearly lost it under the scratch of forty pens:

"He came to me last night. Your uncle. Sat in my parlor and wept."

I kept walking. Counting houses are eavesdropping instruments; every word in them travels. But at midday, in the privy yard where even Harrow's spies draw the line, Fenn joined me and finished the account.

"He wanted to know if the countersign seals could be copied. I told him no such thing existed. I lied, Silas, and I want it noted somewhere that Ambrose Fenn lied to protect you, because it may be the only entry of its kind in thirty years." His laugh was a dry leaf. "Then he confessed. All of it. The meeting, the offer, Harrow's terms."

"Terms," I repeated. "So there are terms."

"There are always terms." Fenn looked at the wall, at nothing. "Harrow gave him until sailing day. Either Tomas delivers the family's whole scheme — the forgery, the helpers, the hour and manner of it — or Tomas boards the Meridian himself, in irons, listed as a debtor. And here is the cruelty of it, boy, the part I need you to hear before you decide what your heart is going to do: Harrow doesn't much care which Tomas chooses. If Tomas betrays you, Harrow catches the forgery before it happens and looks diligent to the directors. If Tomas stays loyal, Harrow loses one troublesome Blackwood and gains proof that the rest are dangerous. Either way the ship sails fuller and the factor's ledgers balance. Your uncle has been made into a coin, and coins have no vote on which pocket holds them."

I leaned against the wall and let that settle into me like silt.

"Do you know which he'll choose?" I asked.

Fenn was quiet a long time. "I've watched men for thirty years, in that building. I watched them sign away other people's children with steady hands and good penmanship. And I will tell you what I know about your uncle, which is the same thing I know about most men: he does not yet know which he will choose. He is waiting to see which choice hurts less, and he will not find out until the moment arrives, and by then it will be too late to be anyone else."

"Then the moment hasn't come."

"No. It comes on the twenty-sixth, at the pier." Fenn straightened his cuffs, an old man's ritual, armor made of habit. "Which means you have nine days to build a forgery that survives a man who now knows it exists. I suggest you stop practicing Harrow's hand, Silas, and start practicing his mind. Write what Harrow would write. Want what Harrow would want. Then put the paper where he will find it himself, and believe what he wishes to believe about it."

"That's not forgery. That's theater."

"It is the Straits Trading Company," Fenn said. "It has never been anything else."

* * *

I saw Tomas on the twentieth, at the fish market, haggling over ikan kembung he had no intention of buying — my uncle's way of thinking in public. I bought the fish out from under him just to have a reason to stand beside him. We walked toward the kampong together, and for a quarter mile neither of us spoke, which between the two of us was practically a confession.

"You know," he said finally.

"Mariel's scrap."

"Ah." He nodded as if I'd told him the weather. "Good girl. Better spy than her mother, God forgive me for saying it."

"Tomas." I stopped in the road, in the smell of salt and drying fish, and looked up at this enormous man who used to carry me on his shoulders through the shallows and pretend to be a warship under cannon fire. "Whatever you promised him. Whatever you're carrying. You don't have to carry it alone."

Something crossed his face then that I will remember until I die. It was gratitude and agony in the same expression, wearing each other's clothes.

"You don't understand what he asked of me, boy."

"I understand exactly what he asked of you. He asked you to sell us, or drown with us. And I'm telling you—" and here my voice betrayed me, cracking on the word like green wood, "—that neither one is yours to choose. Do you hear me? Neither one. You go home, you sit at Mother's table Sunday, you eat what she puts in front of you, and you touch none of it. No more offices. No more bargains. Let me carry the ledger-side of this. It's mine. I built it."

He looked at me a long time. And here is the thing that turned my fury into something worse: he looked *relieved*. A grown man, relieved to be told by a nineteen-year-old that he could put down the gun he had pointed at his own family.

"And if I can't?" he asked, very quietly. "If he comes for me before then? If he sends his new clerk with the constables? You've seen what they do to debtors in chains, Silas. I've seen it too."

"Then you run to the east coast and you stay there until this is over."

"A Blackwood doesn't run."

"A Blackwood," I said, "drowned. Running is newer and I recommend it."

That startled the laugh out of him — the real one, the card-table bark — and just for a moment he was my uncle again, the man who taught me to read currents by the color of the water. He squeezed my shoulder hard enough to bruise, which is how Blackwood men say certain things, and walked on ahead of me toward the kampong, a big man trying to make his shadow smaller.

I watched him go and understood, with terrible clarity, that I had just seen a man choose. Not once and forever — men like Tomas re-choose every morning, the way other men shave. But I had seen the direction of the blade fall. He would hold out. He would sit silent through whatever Harrow did to him. And he would do it not because he was brave but because I had asked, and my uncle's love, like his judgment, had always been largest when someone smaller was watching.

Which meant Harrow would get nothing from him.

Which meant Harrow would get everything from him anyway, because a factor who smells a forgery does not need the forger to confess. He needs only to change the locks.

* * *

That evening I went to the counting house to work late — legitimately, for once; the transfer manifests genuinely needed copying, and refusing the task would have raised more questions than fatigue ever has. Harrow's office stood open and lit. His new clerk, a pale precise young man named Quill whose gaze I had begun to think of as a hand on my collar, sat at the outer desk transcribing.

Through the open door I heard Harrow dictating. I did not mean to listen. I want that on the record alongside Fenn's lie, two small sins entered hopefully against a mountain of larger ones.

*"Item: the Blackwood transfer, amended. Passage confirmed for one, name to be supplied at sailing. Item: instruct the harbormaster that no berth on the Meridian is to be altered, assigned, or cancelled after the twenty-fourth without my countersign. Item: Mr. Fenn's drawer keys are to be inventoried Friday. All drawers."*

A pause. The sound of Harrow pouring something amber.

*"And item: if Mr. Tomas Blackwood comes to this office again, admit him at once and note the hour. He is a man who tells you what he wants you to hear, which is useful, and believes it himself within the week, which is more useful still."*

I copied my manifests with a steady hand. My hand has been steady since my father drowned; grief burned off everything volatile in me years ago. But beneath the steadiness, in the flooded hold where I keep the things that would sink me if I let them float, something turned over and began swimming.

Harrow was closing every door. Inventorying Fenn. Sealing the berths. Watching Tomas like a man watches a rat he has cornered but not yet killed, because a cornered rat can still lead you to the nest.

Nine days. Every lock changing, every key counted, my uncle a loaded question walking around in plain sight, and an old clerk whose thirty years of evidence sat in a drawer Harrow intended to open on Friday.

Friday. Three days.

I finished my last manifest, blotted it, capped the ink, and walked past Fenn's desk without looking at him. But as I passed, I moved his inkwell from the right side of his blotter to the left.

*We speak tonight,* it said. *About the drawer. Before Friday finds it first.*

Outside, the harbor lay black and patient, the Meridian riding at anchor with her lamps lit like a promise. Somewhere aboard her was a berth with one empty line on its manifest, waiting for a name.

Three days to Friday. Six to sailing.

And for the first time since the sealed letter came, I was not certain I had more time than Harrow did.
