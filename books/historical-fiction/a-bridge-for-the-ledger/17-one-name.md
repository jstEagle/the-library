## 17. One Name

The pen had been heavier all week.

I don't mean the weight of it — a goose quill weighs nothing, less than a feather's promise. I mean the way it sat in my hand like a small verdict waiting to be written down. For five years I had copied other men's words: debts, transfers, dates of sale dressed up as dates of passage. My hand knew how to make a ledger lie beautifully. It had never once been asked what it wanted to say.

Tomorrow at dawn, it would have to choose.

The berth authorization lay under my sleeping mat, folded twice, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp that got into everything in this town except, apparently, the consciences of the Straits Trading Company. Harrow's hand I could do now in my sleep. The tall ascenders with their little backward lean, the way he crossed his *t*'s low and impatient, like a man swatting flies. Six weeks of practice on scraps of paper I fed to the cookfire afterward, and Fenn standing behind me some nights saying nothing at all, which from Fenn was a full paragraph of approval.

One name. One berth. Five people I loved.

I had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones. Mother, who held the family together with fish glue and fury. Mariel, seventeen and already braver than any clerk I'd ever met, which is a low bar but she cleared it spectacularly. Uncle Tomas, who was currently somewhere between bargaining and betrayal, depending on which hour you asked him. Cousin Dev, strong enough to work a plantation and too proud to say he was afraid. And Grandmother Sefa, who had not known my name in four months.

Or so I believed.

* * *

I went to her at dusk, because the eve of a thing is for sitting with old women whether they know you or not. Her room smelled of camphor and dried pandanus, the smell that had meant safety my whole life, back when safety was something a kampong could actually provide instead of merely promising.

She was sitting up. That alone stopped me in the doorway.

For months she had been a shape under a blanket, drifting, calling my grandfather home from voyages he'd made forty years ago, arguing pleasantly with people who'd been dead since before I was born. But tonight her back was against the wall, her hands folded in her lap, and her eyes — my father's eyes, the ones I inherited and never thanked anyone for — were open and pointed directly at me.

"You walk like him," she said. "Your father. He walked like the water owed him money."

"Grandmother."

"I know." She said it before I could kneel. "You think I don't know things. The whole house thinks it. They speak over me like I'm a chest being packed." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "I heard them. The counting. Who runs, who hides, who stays with me. Your mother thinks I sleep through everything. I sleep through almost everything. There's a difference, boy, and it matters on nights like this one."

I knelt by the bed because my legs decided before I did. "Then you know about the ship."

"I know there's a ship, and I know there is one place on it, and I know every soul in this family has tried to give that place away to another one like it was a hot coal." She looked out the single window, where the last light was going copper over the strait. "Foolish, generous people. It's a wonder we've survived this long. It's probably exactly why we haven't survived better."

"There has to be a choice," I said. "There isn't room for more than one."

"The sea disagrees."

"Grandmother—"

"The sea," she said, with the patience of a woman correcting a child who has just confidently misidentified the moon as a lamp, "keeps accounts. Everyone in this family forgets that except me, because I'm the only one old enough to remember being paid back." She took my hand. Her grip was still stronger than mine expected; old women are full of these ambushes. "Your grandfather drowned off Batu Berhala. Forty-one years ago. The sea took him on a Tuesday and gave me back your father on a Sunday, nine months later, fat and furious and alive. That is a debt the water collected and repaid. Not kindly. Not quickly. But paid."

"That's not how ledgers work."

"No," she agreed. "It's how the sea works. Ledgers are just men pretending the water can be written down." She turned my hand over and studied the palm like she was reading an entry in it. "Ink-stained. You were always going to be ink-stained, even when you were small and smelling of salt. I told your mother once: this one will fight with paper. She laughed. Nobody laughs now."

Outside, a night bird started its idiotic complaint, and somewhere down the beach the tide was doing its slow arithmetic against the pilings.

"They'll take whoever sails," I said. "That's what the plantation ships do. They take people and they don't return them. There's no Tuesday and no Sunday. There's just gone."

"Yes," said Sefa. "And yet here I am, eighty-one years old, having buried two husbands, three children, one war, and every debt the company says we owe — and I am still here to tell you the sea gives back what is owed. Not what is wanted. What is *owed*." Her fingers tightened on mine. "So the question isn't which of you the ship takes. The question is which debt the sea owes this family. Think about that tonight instead of counting heads. Counting heads is your uncle's trade, and look how well it's going for him."

I flinched. "You know about Tomas?"

"I know Tomas went to town in his good shirt, which is worse than knowing anything else." She released my hand and settled back against the wall, suddenly tired in a way that frightened me more than her clarity had. "Whatever you write tomorrow, write it clean. A crooked line in a good cause is still crooked, and the sea reads straight lines only."

"Grandmother, if I choose wrong—"

"Then you'll be like every man who ever chose," she said, and closed her eyes, and for a moment I thought she'd gone back across whatever border she'd stepped over to reach me tonight. But her voice came again, smaller. "Silas."

My name. In four months, she hadn't once—

"When your father was born, I held him up to the water and told it: this one is borrowed, not given. Remember that. Whatever you decide — you were never theirs to keep, and you were never yours to throw away. Both things are true. Hold both. That's the whole trick of living."

Then she slept, truly slept, the lucidity folding itself away like a letter returned to its envelope, and I sat on the floor beside her bed until the candle burned down to a coin of light, holding both things, both untrue, both true.

* * *

I didn't go home right away. I walked the tideline instead, which is what Blackwood men do when thinking fails, on the theory that walking might succeed where sitting cannot.

The kampong was quiet in the way it gets before a bad day — not asleep but *braced*, dogs awake, shutters half-closed, everyone sensing weather that hadn't arrived yet. At the far end of the beach I found Mariel sitting on the hull of our father's old boat, the one the company left rotting as a lesson, her knees drawn up, turning something over in her fingers.

The scrap of paper. Harrow's draft testimony. She'd stolen it weeks ago and carried it like a talisman ever since, showing it to no one, needing it near.

"You should be asleep," I said.

"You should stop telling people what they should do. It makes you sound like Fenn." She scooted over to make room on the gunwale, which was damp and cold and which I sat on anyway. For a while neither of us said anything, which is the Blackwood dialect of love, spoken fluently on both sides.

"Do you remember," she said eventually, "when Papa taught us to hold our breath? He said the trick isn't keeping the air in. It's deciding you're allowed to let it out."

"I remember."

"He also said the sea takes back what it lends." She looked at me sideways, and I realized she'd talked to Grandmother Sefa too — or Sefa had talked to her, which amounted to the same thing. "Everyone keeps saying that tonight. Mama said it. Even Dev, who doesn't believe in anything he can't lift."

"It must be true then. Three Blackwoods and a proverb."

"Don't do that." Her voice cracked, just slightly, just once. "Don't joke tomorrow into happening. If you joke, it means you've decided, and if you've decided—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I stole that paper so someone would hang for what they're doing to us. I didn't steal it so it would be *you*."

"Mariel—"

"One name," she said. "You get one name, and there are five of us, and I've done the sums a hundred times and they come out wrong every time because the answer is always me or Mama or Dev or Tomas or *you*, and every version of it is a version where I lose somebody." She stood abruptly, the way she always did when feeling got too large for sitting. "So here's mine. Write *Mariel*. Write it and I will hate you forever, and I will live, and hating you forever will keep me alive better than grief would. That's my offer. Consider it seriously."

"It's a terrible offer."

"It's the best one on this beach." She jammed the scrap of paper deep into her pocket. "Grandmother says the sea gives back what's owed. Fine. Then it owes us you, because Papa went into the water and you came up out of it, and nobody in this family has ever explained that exchange to my satisfaction." She walked three steps toward the house, stopped, and didn't turn around. "Whatever you write — write it clean, Silas. Even Grandmother knows about the forging. This family keeps no secrets except the loving kind."

She left me alone with the boat and the tide and the enormous dark coming down over the water.

* * *

Back in my corner of the house, everyone else was asleep, or performing sleep, which in our family amounts to the same posture. Mother's breathing was deliberately even behind the curtain. Dev snored with the commitment of a man who wanted witnesses. Tomas's mat was empty. Still in town. Still in his good shirt. I filed that away for tomorrow's problems.

By candlelight, I unwrapped the authorization and read it through one final time. Every counterseal correct — Fenn had drilled me until my dreams featured wax stamps. Every date aligned. Harrow's hand flawless down to the impatient little crosses. The document was perfect. It waited, blank in the one place that mattered, for a name.

I dipped the pen.

I thought about my father, who went under the strait with a company advance unpaid and a company boat hole punched beneath him, and whose death the ledger recorded — I had found the entry myself, entry after entry, in Fenn's duplicates — as *loss of asset, negligible.* Negligible. My whole childhood weighed and found negligible, by a man in a good coat who signed with a backward-leaning hand.

I thought about the sea giving back what is owed.

I thought about Mariel's offer, and Mother's silence, and Dev's pride, and even Tomas, wretched, self-deceived Tomas, who had gone to sell smugglers to save us and would wake tomorrow to find himself saved by a boy instead, which would wound him more deeply than chains.

I thought about Grandmother Sefa, who could not run, could not hide, could not survive the hold of a ship even if I gave her the world's entire fleet — and who had climbed out of her long darkness for one evening specifically to tell me, in front of God and the tide, that I was borrowed, not given.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off, a ship's bell counted the hour — the Meridian riding at anchor, one berth emptying its lungs in the dark.

I set the nib to the line.

What I wrote was short. One word, really, in Harrow's neat, impatient hand. When it was done I blotted it, sealed it, and slid the paper back beneath the mat, and then I sat very still and listened to my family breathe around me, memorizing the sound, the way you memorize a shore you're about to leave.

Because here is what none of them understood, not Mother with her fury, not Mariel with her offers, not even Sefa with her sea-debts: they had each spent the week trying to hand the hot coal to someone else. And I had spent the week watching them do it — reading the silences, the way I read clerks, the way I read Harrow — and I finally understood that the coal could only belong to one person.

The debt the sea owed this family was not a daughter. Not a mother. Not a cousin, and not even an uncle who deserved drowning more than most.

It was the son of a pearl diver, who had learned to hold his breath by deciding when to let it out.

Tomorrow at dawn, the Meridian would sail, and I intended to be on it — among the condemned, carrying a true ledger hidden in my shirt, leaving behind a family that would curse my name and live.

I blew out the candle. Somewhere across the black water, the ship's bell rang once more, soft as a summons.

And in the grey hour before sunrise, my mother's curtain moved.
