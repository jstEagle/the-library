## 18. The Meridian Departs

There is a particular quality to dawn in Singapore that the counting house never taught me. The clerks speak of morning as a column of hours to be filled, but out here, on the water, dawn is an argument the sky makes with itself — grey refusing gold, gold conceding, the whole slow surrender repeated every day as if the heavens too were keeping a ledger and could never quite balance it.

I had not slept. I had sat all night at our table with the pen before me and the berth authorization beneath my hand, and I had learned something about ink that no clerk ever writes down: it dries slower when your hand shakes.

One name. That was the arithmetic Fenn had confirmed with his tired eyes six weeks ago, and it was the arithmetic Harrow's own schedule enforced now. The *Meridian* would take exactly one soul on papers that would survive scrutiny, because I had made them survive scrutiny — I had copied Harrow's hand until my wrist ached and his flourishes lived in my fingers like a second heartbeat. I had practiced his *H* until it looked less honest than his real one, which is the trick of forgery nobody tells you: you must make the lie more convincing than the truth, because the truth was written by a man who never expected anyone to look closely.

One name. And around our lamplit table, for six weeks, every person I loved had spent their breath trying to make me write someone else's.

Mother said: *You are the one who can read. They will need you here.* As if literacy were a reason to stay rather than a weapon to carry.

Mariel said nothing at all, which from Mariel was the loudest argument in the room.

Uncle Tomas, since his ruin and my strange mercy, had taken to sitting near the door of any room he was in, as if he expected to be shown out of life itself. He said: *The boy should go. The boy is young.* He could not meet my eyes when he said it. Shame sits strangely on a man who tried to sell his family and was saved by the nephew he'd have sold them to.

And Grandmother Sefa — Grandmother Sefa, who had surfaced from her long grey silence two nights ago like a diver coming up for air, lucid and terrible and gentle all at once — she had taken my hand in both of hers, hands thin as dried palm fronds, and she had said:

"The sea gives back what is owed, anak. It always has. Your father went down into it and it kept him, and now it must give something back. It does not choose what. Only that it balances."

I had thought she was raving. Then I understood she was the only one among us doing arithmetic honestly.

* * *

So at four o'clock in the morning, by the light of a candle burned nearly to its socket, I wrote my own name.

Silas Blackwood.

Three words' worth of letters, and my hand did not shake, and I want that recorded somewhere — if not in the company's ledger then in whatever truer book exists — that when the moment came, the boy who had survived five years by mastering silence wrote his own death warrant in a dead man's handwriting and his line was steady as a ruled margin.

It was the easiest thing I have ever done, and I do not say that for nobility. I say it because choosing was the hard part, and choosing had been done for weeks, done in lamplight and in refusals, done every time Mariel pushed her portion of rice onto my plate and every time Mother counted us at night like a woman counting coins she could not bear to spend. By the time the pen touched paper there was no decision left in it. There was only the writing.

Then I wrapped the true ledger in oilcloth, twice, and tied it with the knot my father used for mooring lines, because some inheritances come down through the hands and not the head.

* * *

I found Mother at the shore before the sun did.

She was standing where she always stood, at the tideline where the kampong's nets ended and the company's world began, and she heard me coming — mothers hear everything; it is their first language and they never lose it — but she did not turn until I stopped beside her.

"You're wearing your good shirt," she said.

"I'm going to the pier."

"Nobody wears their good shirt to watch a ship leave."

"Mother."

She turned then. She looked at the bundle under my arm and she knew. Mothers know. I think she had known since the night Sefa spoke, and had spent two days pretending otherwise, the way we had all pretended otherwise, the whole family performing a play whose ending everyone had memorized.

"No," she said. It came out very small. Then again, louder, with the sea behind it: "No. Silas. You give it to Dev. Dev is quick, Dev can work—"

"Dev is sixteen and has your brother's temper. He'd be in chains inside a month." I held out the ledger. "Take this."

She would not take it. Her arms stayed crossed over her chest like a woman barring a door.

"What is it?"

"The truth. All of it. Every family. Thirty years of names, dates, prices. Fenn kept duplicates of everything — he gave them to me last night." I pressed the bundle against her crossed arms until she had to either hold it or push it away, and I knew she would hold it, because Ida Blackwood has never once pushed away a weight that needed carrying. "They sold Papa's boat out from under him and called it debt. They are selling families across the strait and calling it transfer. This book says so. Every page. In Fenn's own hand, witnessed and dated."

"And what am I supposed to do with a book?"

"Everything," I said. "Copy it. Hide it. Give pieces of it to every priest, every magistrate's clerk, every harbor master's wife who gossips. Paper travels further than people, Mother. People get put on ships. Paper gets put in envelopes, and envelopes get answered."

She looked down at the bundle. When she looked up her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than weeping would have, because I have seen her weep at funerals and storms and empty nets, and dry eyes meant this grief was past the country where tears grow.

"If you board that ship," she said, "I will never see you again."

"You'll see me again."

"Do not lie to me. Not today. Today of all days, do not use that counting-house voice on your mother."

I wanted to. God help me, I wanted to wrap the lie in comfortable words the way Fenn wraps documents in oilcloth. But she had asked for truth, and truth was the only cargo I had left worth shipping.

"I don't know," I said. "I don't know if I'll come back. But I know that if I stay, they take all of us, and if I go, they take only me, and they believe they've won, and men who believe they've won stop watching their ledgers." I touched the bundle in her arms. "That is when paper travels."

For a long moment she said nothing. The tide made its small sounds against the sand. Somewhere behind us a rooster announced the morning to a kampong that had no use for its news.

Then Ida Blackwood, who once counted her children like cargo in front of a company man without letting her voice shake, pulled me against her chest and held me the way she held me when I was small enough to fit entirely inside her arms, and she said into my hair, so quietly I almost missed it:

"The sea gives back what is owed."

* * *

Mariel was waiting at the pier.

Of course she was. My sister has worked in Harrow's house long enough to learn the schedules of everyone in it, including — especially — the factor himself, and she had known the departure hour before I did. She stood at the end of the planks with her arms at her sides and her chin up, and she had put on her good dress too, and between us we must have emptied Mother's chest of good shirts entirely, as if dressing well were a form of resistance.

"You wrote it," she said. Not a question.

"I wrote it."

"Your own name."

"My own name."

Her jaw worked. I watched her assemble an argument and dismantle it and assemble another, and finally what came out was: "You didn't even ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Ask me if I could bear it."

"Mariel—"

"Because I can't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, and she lifted her chin higher to punish herself for it. "I can't bear it, Silas. You're the one who reads. You're the one who knows things. What am I supposed to be without the one who knows things?"

I took her face in my hands. She is fifteen and all elbows and fury and she let me, which is how I knew how frightened she was.

"You're going to be the one who remembers," I said. "You've already stolen more truth out of that house than I ever got out of the counting house. That scrap you took — keep it. Keep everything you steal. You and Mother and Fenn, you're going to drown them in paper. I'm just the diversion."

"You're not a diversion."

"I'm the best diversion this company has ever seen. They'll be so pleased with themselves." I smiled at her, and it cost me everything I had, and I paid it anyway, because that is what smiles are for at piers. "Besides. Grandmother says the sea gives back what is owed. You heard her. Are you going to argue with Sefa?"

"She argued with a typhoon once. The typhoon apologized."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out, and she grabbed me and held on with the desperate strength of a girl who has hauled scullery water since she was nine years old, and I felt her tears through my good shirt, ruining it, and I was glad. Let the shirt be ruined. Good shirts are for liars anyway.

"Write to me," she whispered.

"There's no post to a plantation, Mariel."

"Then remember out loud. I'll hear it somehow. I always hear things."

* * *

The *Meridian* rode at anchor like a fat black spider, and the condemned went up her gangplank in the grey light, and I went up among them.

That is the thing I want set down plainly, because history — if Fenn's ledger does its work — will record transfers and tonnages and signatures, and it may forget what the morning actually looked like. Forty-one souls on that gangplank. A fisherman named Osman who had once lent my father tackle and never asked for it back. A widow with twin boys who walked holding one of each of her hands, neither of them old enough to understand why the pier was full of weeping. A man still in the company's chains because he had run twice, and the chains sang against the planks with every step, a small bright idiot music.

None of them knew yet that the papers for one berth aboard this ship had been forged by a nineteen-year-old copyist to smuggle himself into their company. To Harrow's clerks I was simply the last entry reconciled, the Blackwood account closed neat as a sum. They checked my authorization, and the senior one — a dull-eyed man who had waved me through a hundred doors because I carried documents — glanced at Harrow's signature, glanced at me, and stamped the paper without a flicker.

Five years of mastering silence, spent on one final performance: the obedient indentured boy, reporting for transfer as instructed. I bowed. The bow was perfect. I despised it and I made it perfect.

At the rail I turned and looked back, because I am a novelist's conceit even in my own life and I wanted the picture: the kampong in the early light, smoke rising from cookfires, the nets hung out like the rigging of a fleet of ghosts. Mother at the tideline, small and straight-backed, the oilcloth bundle clutched to her chest. Uncle Tomas beside her, hat in his hands, a man learning at last to stand still and witness instead of bargain. Old Fenn had come down too — I saw him at the far end of the pier, keeping to the shadows as he had kept to them for thirty years, and he raised one hand, just slightly, the smallest gesture of his entire careful life, and it meant more than any speech.

And Mariel.

Mariel had climbed onto a bollard, above the crowd, where I could not miss her, and as the *Meridian*'s anchor came up and the ship began to lean toward the open strait, she screamed my name.

Not wept it. Screamed it. "SILAS!" — once, twice, three times, each one thrown across the water like something hurled, and heads turned along the deck, and the guards frowned, and I raised my hand and held it up, high, so she could see it, so the whole pier could see it, and I did not call anything back because if I opened my mouth the performance would fail and they would drag me off this ship and put someone else on it, someone who couldn't read, someone without a plan, and forty-one names would sink unrecorded.

So I stood at the rail with my hand raised while my sister's voice broke itself against the hull, and I committed her scream to memory the way I commit figures — precisely, permanently, in a hand that does not shake.

* * *

Edmund Harrow stood on the pier in his white suit, watching us go.

I found him there when I looked back a final time, apart from the crowd, hands clasped behind him, and I watched his face do the thing I had studied for five years across a counting-house desk: the small settling of satisfaction, the factor's smile, the expression of a man reviewing a column that finally sums. He believed the problem had solved itself. The troublesome Blackwood boy, the one who saw too much and said too little, had quietly shipped himself off to the plantations with the rest of the refuse. No scandal. No inquiry. No loose thread. He probably intended to note it in his private journal tonight as evidence of his own efficiency — *the matter resolved itself with minimal expense.*

Let him. A man who believes he has won stops checking his arithmetic, and somewhere behind that satisfied smile, a mother was already walking inland with thirty years of his crimes wrapped in oilcloth against her chest.

He thinks he is watching a problem sail away. He is actually watching the evidence leave in the safest hands in Singapore.

The *Meridian* caught the morning wind. The kampong shrank. The strait opened before us, wide and glittering and indifferent, and the chains of the runner two decks below me kept up their small bright music, and I stood at the rail and began, silently, to memorize names.

Osman. The widow Rahayu, and her twins, Jumil and Basir. The runner in chains — I would learn his name by nightfall; I learned everyone's name by nightfall; it was the one skill the counting house gave me that they could never confiscate.

Forty-one souls, plus one stowaway of the spirit: a true ledger's worth of memory, sailing east into captivity with its line steady as a ruled margin.

Behind us, Singapore dissolved into haze. Ahead, the plantation coast waited with its fences and its fever and its silence.

But the sea gives back what is owed.

Grandmother Sefa said so, and she has never once argued with a typhoon and lost.
