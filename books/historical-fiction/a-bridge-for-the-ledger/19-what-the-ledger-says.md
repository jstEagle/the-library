## 19. What the Ledger Says

The pier smelled of tar and fish and my son's absence, and I stood on it until the Meridian was a smudge and then not even that, because a woman who has already buried one man to the sea does not weep at the second departure. She counts. She counts the seconds she stood there, so that later she can say she gave him that many. I gave him nine hundred and forty.

Then I put my hand in Mariel's hair, and Ambrose Fenn put his hand on the ledger under his coat as though it were a heart he was carrying home to transplant, and we walked off the pier like three people who had errands. Which we did. The errand was memory, and it would take us eighteen months, and it would ruin four men's careers and one man's peace of mind, and I will tell you now what I told Fenn on that walk: grief is a poor workman if you let it stand idle.

"Mrs. Blackwood," he said, breathless, "I want you to understand what you're carrying before you carry it."

"Fenn," I said, "I carried four children through two fevers and a typhoon on fish heads and prayer. Carry the book."

* * *

We did not copy it in my house. That was the first thing we learned, and it cost us nothing but caution, which by then I had plenty of. Harrow's new clerk was watchful the way a cat is watchful — for movement, not for stillness — so we became still people. We met in the back room of the chandler's shop where my husband used to buy pitch, because the chandler owed my Tomas a debt of the ordinary kind, the kind paid in coin, and coin debts can be called in favors. There is an irony there about debt that I have chosen not to examine too closely.

The true ledger was not beautiful. People imagine ledgers as elegant things, ruled and gilded, but this one was thirty years of Fenn's secret hand on whatever paper he could spare: invoices' backs, wrapping paper, the flyleaves of ruined prayer books. Every family sold. Every debt inflated past honesty. The Tanjung Rhu fishermen, 1829. The widow Chandra and her sons, 1831. Page after page in a cramped clerk's script, each entry with the same terrible neatness, because Fenn had been a careful liar and could not stop being careful even when telling the truth.

"It should have been written louder," Fenn said the first night, spreading the pages like a man laying out a corpse for washing.

"It was written at all," I said. "That's the loud part."

Mariel copied. My daughter, who had spent a year scrubbing the factor's floors with her eyes down, sat with her spine straight and reproduced her letters in rows clean enough to make a schoolmaster cry. She had stolen a scrap of testimony out from under Harrow's own inkwell; next to that, transcription was rest. She copied the names, and beside each name she wrote what Fenn called the particulars and what I called the wound: how much owed, how much claimed, who sailed, who drowned, who was left standing on a pier counting seconds.

I wrote the letters.

That was my trade in this enterprise. A petition wants a woman's voice, Fenn explained, because petitions written in clerkly hands arrive pre-dismissed — the authorities see the flourishes and think *office grievance* and file it under dust. But a widow's hand, uneven, blotted, spelling the way the sea taught her? That they read twice. I resented it and used it. There is a particular fury in being underestimated on purpose.

* * *

You cannot imagine, unless you have done it, how slow truth travels when it must go by whisper. Ink is confiscated; whispers are not. So we built a chain of mouths.

The old divers came first — Ida gathers them, the kampong said, the way weather gathers, and they came. Pak Usman, who had held my husband's boat line the night the company sank it, sat with the ledger open on his knees for an hour without touching it, and then said only: "Rahayu's twins. Write that they went in the third year. She still sets out rice for them." So we wrote it. Rice set out. Grief unappeased. These are facts too, though no commission will ever audit them.

From the divers to the fish sellers, from the fish sellers to the boat crews, from the boat crews to every port town down the coast and up the other side. Malacca got it by September. Penang by the northeast monsoon, riding in the memory of a helmsman who had once been refused credit by Harrow and had never forgotten a face or a figure. Each place, someone memorized a page — not the paper, the page — and passed it on the way you pass a song. Fenn worried about errors creeping in, versions drifting. I told him what my mother told me about recipes: a thing remembered wrong in one mouth is corrected by ten others. Truth is a soup. Let the village stir it.

And always, always, the names. We sent names ahead of us like lanterns. To every family on every page, a letter: your mother, your brother, your children, entered here, sold here, on this date, by this man's hand, and the world is being made to read it. Some letters came back with money enclosed, which we returned, because this was not alms. Some came back with more names — *you missed my cousin, add my cousin* — and those we kept, and the ledger grew fat with corrections in my crooked widow's hand, and I confess I came to love that book the way you love a scar: proof the cutting happened, proof you closed.

Mariel asked me once, late, pen worn to a stub, whether hatred was keeping the lamp lit or love was. I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honest.

"Arithmetic," I said. "Your brother taught me. One name recovered, minus one name lost. You keep working while the sum is wrong."

She laughed, first time since the pier. It sounded like him.

* * *

Tomas came to us in the rain, and I nearly shut the door.

Understand: he had gone to Harrow offering to sell smugglers' names for berths. Whatever else that was, it was my family's danger dressed as my family's rescue, and sisters are permitted to hold grudges against brothers who gamble with nieces. But Silas had spent his berth to pull Tomas out of the fire — had written his own name where Tomas's treachery would otherwise have put him in chains — and a debt like that does not care how you feel about the debtor.

So I shut the door slowly, and opened it, and let him drip on the mat.

"They took my offer," he said, "and turned it into a leash. He had me sign things, Harrow. Papers saying I'd witnessed things I never saw. I was going to be his witness against my own blood." His hands were shaking around a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. "Silas bought that back. I don't know how a boy does arithmetic like that."

"He learned it holding his father's silence five years," I said. "Sit down. What's in the cloth?"

Harrow's correspondence. Not all of it — Harrow burned well, Fenn said later, professionally — but Tomas had been inside the bargain and inside the room, and a man signing away his family pays attention to what he signs. Copies. Instructions. A memorandum in which the phrase *irregular accounts resolved by transfer* appeared with dates that matched our ledger's pages like a key matches its shame.

Fenn looked at the dates for a long time. Then he took off his spectacles, cleaned them, put them back on, and became, visibly, a different clerk.

"This is admissible," he said. "This, with the ledger, with testimony — this goes past whispers. This reaches a commission."

"You'd testify?" I asked him. "Thirty years of it, Fenn. They'll ask why you stayed quiet."

He folded his hands, an old man arranging himself for judgment.

"I'll say I was a coward with excellent handwriting," he said, "and that I corrected the record in duplicate. Let them weigh the cowardice against the copies. It's fair accounting. I've always believed in fair accounting." And there was something in his face that I recognized from my mirror: mourning, converted. Burned for fuel.

* * *

Tomas turned crown witness in the spring.

I will not pretend I stood in the public gallery proud of my brother. I stood there with my arms crossed doing sums. But I watched him stand before the inquiry's men with Harrow's memoranda laid out like dissected fish, and I watched him answer every question plainly, including the ones meant to flay him — *did you offer to trade names for passage, Mr. Blackwood?* — and I watched him say yes, sir, I did, and I am answering for it now with everything I have. It takes a certain courage to confess your worst transaction in public. Tomas had spent his whole life avoiding courage; finding it at fifty was either redemption or theater, and I decided I would decide later. That is also a family trait.

Harrow fought, of course. Men whose crime is arithmetic fight hardest over the figures. He produced countersuits, character evidence, a petition asserting the Blackwoods were smugglers after all, signed with the confident flourish of a guilty conscience. Mariel read it in the inquiry office, walked out, and said only, "He spelled Sefa wrong." We put that in the file too. Small humiliations keep the soldiers fed between battles.

The commission was coming. Fenn heard it from the chandler, who heard it from a customs man, who heard it from God knows where: a reform inquiry, seated within the year, empowered to examine labor transfers across the strait. Eighteen months, we had told ourselves, for the paper to travel. It was traveling faster than paper travels, because it wasn't paper anymore by then. It was a hundred mouths in a dozen ports, all singing the same page, all correcting each other's verses.

Some nights I sat alone with the ledger after the others left and read it not as evidence but as scripture, which is what it had become: the account of my people's passage out of Egypt, kept by the most reluctant prophet alive. I would read until the candle guttered, and every time, on the last page, I would find the same thing waiting for me, because Mariel had copied it there without asking leave:

*Silas Blackwood. Berth taken voluntarily. Destination unknown. Debt: none owed. Owed to him: everything.*

I never argued with that entry. In our house we have learned not to argue with true figures.

* * *

The first reply from across the strait came in the nineteenth month, carried by a returning coolie ship's cook who had memorized it as instructed and delivered it aloud in my kitchen, word for word, with the solemnity of a man reciting another man's treasure:

*Mother — Forty-one present, forty-one remembering. We have organized by name. The fever took Basir's friend Osman in the second month; we recorded him. They cannot confiscate what we carry in our skulls, and I have made the skulls into a ledger. Do not mourn forward. Work forward. The sea gives back what is owed, and I intend to be standing on the shore when it does. — Your son, who signs his own name freely, wherever they put him.*

I sat with that a while. Then I called Mariel, and we lit three lamps, and we got back to work, because somewhere across the water a boy who owned nothing but forty-one names had just told his mother not to mourn, and it seemed disrespectful to weep instead of write.

Fenn arrived at midnight with news that made him look twenty years younger and ten years deader at once, both: the commission had convened, its first subpoena bore Harrow's name, and its second document requested — by title, in official ink —

*"The true ledger of the Straits Trading Company, custodian: Mrs. Ida Blackwood."*

Mariel looked at me. Tomas looked at me. Fenn polished his spectacles and waited, the way he had waited thirty years, to see what the record would finally be allowed to do.

I took the ledger down from its hiding place and weighed it in my two hands, all those pages, all those names, my crooked handwriting and my daughter's straight one and an old coward's careful duplicates, and I said the only thing the figures permitted:

"Then we had better make sure every copy is perfect. Because this book is going to town — and it doesn't come back until justice does."
