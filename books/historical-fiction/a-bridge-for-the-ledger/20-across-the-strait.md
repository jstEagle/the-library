## 20. Across the Strait

The sea between Singapore and the plantation coast takes three days to cross, and the company has calculated that a man can be kept alive on very little of anything in that time — a little water, a little rice, a little air. The hold of the *Meridian* was built for copra, not people, and the arithmetic shows. We are packed at the density of dried kernel, ninety-one souls between decks where the headroom is four feet and the lanterns swing like slow pendulums counting out a sentence.

I have survived five years by mastering silence. I intend to survive this crossing by breaking it.

But I get ahead of myself, as my mother always said I would. *You run downhill before you've tied your sandals, Silas.* She said it about everything — about the tide, about my father's debts, about the way I read men's faces in the counting house instead of mending nets. She was right, which is the worst thing a mother can be.

* * *

The first day, nobody speaks. That is the company's genius, if you want to call it that — they don't need chains below decks because they have something better: the arithmetic of despair. Every man and woman down here did the same sum I did. One berth. Ninety-some takers. And each of us chose, or was chosen, or simply failed to run fast enough, and now we lie shoulder to shoulder in the dark doing the accounting of who is to blame.

The answer, mostly, is everyone. Yourself first. Then the sea. Then God, if He's taking commissions.

There is a Javanese man two places down from me whose breathing sounds like my father's used to after a long dive — that wet patience, the lungs working through something. There is a Bugis woman with a child asleep against her chest, and she hums when she thinks no one is listening, a thin thread of sound she probably doesn't know she's making. There is old Rahman, whom I know — knew — from the fish market, who once sold my mother a shark so large she had to borrow a cart. He hasn't recognized me. The dark does that. In darkness we are all just breath and bone and debt.

Toward evening of the first day, the hatch opens and a face appears above us, backlit, holding a bucket of rice and a ladle.

"Two rows at a time," the face says. It belongs to a Malay sailor with the bored cruelty of a man who has done this run eleven times and will do it thirty more and die in his own bed. "No pushing. Pushing means less for everyone."

We go up in twos like penitents. The deck is gray with pre-dawn or post-dusk — I couldn't tell you which, and that frightened me more than the hold did. To lose track of the sky's hours on open water. My father navigated by stars he'd never seen named on any chart. Three days from land and I already couldn't say whether it was morning.

Rahman was in the row ahead of me. Up close, in the flat light, he finally saw me.

"Little Blackwood," he said. Not a question. Just the name, laid down like a coin on a counter.

"Hello, Rahman."

"They took you too."

"I took myself," I said. "It seemed polite."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he did the thing that old men do, which is laugh at exactly the wrong moment, quietly, into their hands. "Your father's boy," he said. "Same mouth."

They gave us our rice and put us back below, and I lay in the dark thinking about mouths, and names, and what survives a crossing.

* * *

Here is what I carried aboard the *Meridian* that Harrow doesn't know about, because Harrow believes the problem solved itself when I wrote my own name on that authorization. He watched me board among the condemned and thought: *the clever one removed himself; the ledger balances.* He is a man who reads accounts and not people, which is the single mercy of his otherwise merciless character.

What I carry is ninety-one names.

Not mine alone — all of them. On the last night, before dawn and the pier and Mariel screaming on the dock until her voice tore, I sat with Fenn's true ledger and I memorized. Page by page, family by family, every soul the Straits Trading Company had converted from person into entry over thirty years of quiet arithmetic. Fenn thought I was reading it for grief. I let him think it. Grief was part of it, God knows. But underneath the grief there was the counting house training, five years of copying entries until numbers ran in my blood like salt, and underneath even that there was my father's trade: a diver's memory. You learn it young, holding your breath. You learn to fix one thing in your mind — a rock, an anemone, the particular silver flash of a fish — and keep it whole and bright while your body screams for air. That is what memory is. A thing held bright while everything else goes dark.

So I know that the man with the diver's breathing is called Arif bin Salleh, of Batavia, entered 12 March 1843, debt recorded: forty-two dollars, original advance: nine. I know the humming woman is Sarimah, and the child against her chest — born after the ledger entry, therefore officially nonexistent to the company — is called Noor. I know Rahman's full account to the dollar, including the interest Harrow compounded quarterly on an advance for a boat the company itself sank.

Ninety-one names. Some of them I can't pronounce properly yet. Some of them belong to people who don't know their own figures — who were told round numbers in languages they barely spoke, and believed them, because what else does a drowning man believe but the voice telling him how deep the water is?

That is the weapon. It isn't much. It isn't iron or law or money. But consider: the company's whole machinery runs on the fiction that these people are entries. Entries can be moved, summed, cancelled. People — named, remembered, witnessed — are harder to sink. My father taught me that too, though he never said it in words. The company sank his boat over forty dollars and expected him to become a rumor, a loss, a line in a column. The kampong refused. They remembered him. Twenty years of remembering, and eventually it would come back across this same strait as evidence, as testimony, as Tomas turning crown witness with his shame worn openly like a borrowed coat.

Memory outlives men. Fenn said that when he handed me the ledger, and I thought it was an old man's consolation. It turns out it is instructions.

* * *

The second day, the weather turned.

The *Meridian* rolled in a long swell and the hold became a stomach, everything sloshing — bilgewater rising over ankles, over shins, rice bowls floating past like little funeral boats. The child Noor woke and cried, and her mother sang to her, and the singing broke something open in the dark. Men wept. Not loudly. Quietly, the way we do — tears being the one form of speech the company never managed to tax.

And I thought: enough silence. Five years I kept my mouth shut and it saved my skin and cost me everything else. Silence is how they farm us. A hold full of strangers is cargo; a hold full of neighbors is a problem.

So I began, in the dark, to speak.

"Listen," I said. My voice came out strange — rusty, too loud, mine and not mine. "My name is Silas Blackwood. Son of Ansel Blackwood, pearl diver, of the kampong at Tanjong Rhu. The company drowned him over a forty-dollar advance and called it weather."

The rolling made the lantern swing and shadows swung with it. Nobody answered. I hadn't expected them to. You cast a net and wait.

"The company has a book," I went on. "A true ledger, kept secretly for thirty years by a clerk named Ambrose Fenn, and in it is written every one of you — your real names, your real debts, and the lie beside each one. I have read it. I am carrying it in my head, and copies of it are already moving ashore into hands Harrow cannot reach."

That was only half true — the copies were just beginning their journey, in Ida's careful hand and Mariel's stubborn one — but a net needs some slack to catch fish.

"Arif bin Salleh," I said, into the dark. "Of Batavia. Forty-two dollars written against nine advanced. Your boat, the one they took in payment — it was worth sixty new."

Silence. Then, from two places down, the wet patient breathing stopped, and a voice like gravel in a gourd: "Who told you my name?"

"Your own book. The company's book of you. They wrote you down, Arif. Which means you can be read. Which means you can be read aloud."

Another long pause. Then, wondrously, dryly: "Forty-two dollars. They told me fifty-five."

"Interest," I said. "Compounded quarterly. It's how they make the numbers eat."

He laughed — short, disbelieving, angry. "The numbers eat," he repeated. "Yes. Yes, brother, the numbers ate my boat and my cousin's freedom and six years of my life." And I heard him turn in the dark, addressing the whole hold now. "Arif bin Salleh," he said. "Say it. If I drown on this crossing, say my name to whoever will listen."

And they said it. First a few voices, then more, awkward and echoing in that low wooden belly — *Arif bin Salleh, Arif bin Salleh* — until it stopped being a name and became a fact, a plank laid down in the dark.

I lay back against the hull with bilgewater in my boots and felt something I hadn't felt since the counting house, since before, since my father's shoulders in the shallows lifting me above the waves: not safety. Nothing like safety. But belonging — the fierce, improvised kind, forged under pressure, like charcoal pressed toward diamond.

* * *

By the third day it was a school, or a prayer meeting, or a conspiracy — the company wouldn't have known which word to use and it wouldn't have mattered, because all three were correct.

Word reached the deck above, of course. The bored sailor came down with his lantern around midday, suspicious, and found us not cowering but talking — trading names and figures across the dark like merchants in a wet bazaar.

"What is this?" he demanded. "Quiet down there."

"We're learning each other," Rahman called up, serene as Friday. "Is that against regulations?"

"Names don't feed you."

"No," Rahman agreed. "But hunger feeds on men without names. Ask any tiger."

The sailor looked at us a while — ninety-one faces turning toward his lantern, unhurried, unashamed — and whatever he saw made him climb back up without another word. I noticed, later, that the evening rice ration came with a few extra ladles scraped from somewhere. I choose to believe it was conscience. It might equally have been fear, and either will do.

All that day we worked. I gave them their own accounts, one by one, and asked each to give something back that no ledger could hold: a wife's name, a village, a craft, a joke their father used to tell. Sarimah's husband had been a kite-maker in Surabaya before the debt took him; she described his kites so precisely that half the hold could see them climbing. A Bugis man named Daeng taught us a work song for hauling nets, and we hauled nothing, but we sang it anyway, quietly, so the sound itself was a rope we pulled together. Old Rahman stood up — as much as anyone stands in a four-foot hold — and recited the names of eleven fishermen lost off Pulau Ubin in the storm of '36, from memory, because someone had to remember them and he had appointed himself.

"That's the trade," he said, sitting back down. "Everyone here learns it. We carry each other. It's the only cargo that gains weight by being carried."

Noor, the child, learned fastest of all. By nightfall she could say *Arif*, and Arif wept — big silent Javanese tears — because his daughter was across the strait and he had been afraid of arriving forgotten, and now a baby not two years old carried his name like a lamp.

* * *

On the third night, land. I smelled it before anyone announced it — mud and clove smoke and rain on hot earth, the smell of the other side of everything. The anchor chain went out with a sound like the world's largest debt being paid, and the hold settled into the particular quiet of arrival, which is worse than departure because now you must find out what you arrived as.

The hatch opened. Lantern light poured down the ladder like something liquid. And the factor's overseer stood at the top with his manifest, ready to call us up by number, by entry, by column — cargo to be weighed and assigned.

"Numbers!" he called out. "When I call your number, come up. Number fourteen—"

"Arif bin Salleh," said Arif, standing, saying it to the man's face the way you plant a flag.

The overseer blinked. "Fourteen—"

"Sarimah," said Sarimah, and behind her, clear as a bell, small Noor: "Sarimah! Arif!"

"Rahman of Pulau Ubin!"

"Daeng! Silas Blackwood! Ansel's son!"

Ninety-one names rose up the ladder and broke over that overseer like a wave over a seawall, and I watched him understand, slowly, the way men understand floods — too late and from a low place — that he had not loaded ninety-one entries onto this ship. He had loaded a kampong. A floating village with songs and grievances and grandmothers' jokes and dead fathers' trades, and villages are not divisible, and villages do not forget.

He retreated up the ladder calling numbers to nobody.

We came up in our own order, in our own names, into the last gray light, and stood swaying on deck looking at the plantation coast — the ranks of palms, the chimneys, the long low sheds — and it looked exactly like what it was, a machine for eating lives. But we looked at it together, and I heard Rahman begin the net-hauling song, low, and the others take it up, and I thought: they have crossed us to the far side of the strait, but they have also, without knowing it, crossed the only thing that could have destroyed us, which was our own silence.

Somewhere behind us, three days east, my mother was copying a dead clerk's ledger into petitions by lamplight. Somewhere a reform commission would open a package and find ninety-one reasons. Somewhere Mariel was refusing, with her whole obstinate heart, to let my name become an entry.

And somewhere ahead, in the barracks and the boiling houses of this coast, there were hundreds more like us — entered, numbered, erased — waiting for someone to arrive who knew how to read a ledger aloud.

I tightened my bundle, touched the memory of pages I no longer owned but carried anyway, and walked down the gangplank into captivity, teaching names as I went.

The sea gives back what is owed. Grandmother Sefa said so, lucid as noon on her last good day, and I intend to hold the sea to its account.
