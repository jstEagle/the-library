## 21. The Reckoning Tide

### Ida

Eighteen months is a long time to carry a ledger under a floorboard.

Mine lived beneath Sefa's bed, of all places — wrapped in oilcloth, wedged between a chamber pot and a bundle of my husband's old diving weights. There was a poetry to that which I did not miss: the truest account in Singapore sleeping beneath the oldest woman who could no longer walk to the sea she'd been born beside. If Harrow's men ever came rapping, they would search the men's rooms and the counting house and Mariel's trunk at the factor's house, because everyone forgets that grandmothers keep secrets better than clerks keep numbers.

Sefa knew what was under her bed. She never said so directly. She only grew fussy about the floor being swept, as if dust might somehow find its way into an oiled parcel and rot the truth.

The man who finally came for it did not rap. He knocked twice, gently, like a doctor, and when I opened the door there stood a thin Englishman in a coat too warm for the climate, sweating through the shoulders of it, holding a letter of introduction signed by a name I had copied out forty times by then: *Mr. Josiah Crane, Commissioner of Inquiry into Labour Conditions in the Straits Settlements.*

"You are Mrs. Blackwood," he said. Not a question. "Your son—"

"My daughter," I said, "has been writing your office since March. My daughter wrote you eleven letters before you answered one."

Crane had the grace to look ashamed, which is more than I can say for most of the Englishmen who have stood on my doorstep. "Letters are easily lost, Mrs. Blackwood. Ledgers are not. We received word last month from a correspondent in Malacca — a shipping clerk who had seen a partial copy of a document he described as—" he consulted a card — "*'the arithmetic of thirty years of theft.'* We understood then that the earlier letters deserved more than filing."

"Deserved," I said. "There is a fine word."

He accepted the rebuke the way a man accepts rain he cannot stop, and I let him into the house, and I knelt down beside Sefa's bed, and I drew out the oilcloth parcel, and my hands shook.

Not from fear. Fear had spent itself months ago, back in the weeks after Silas sailed, when Mariel and Ambrose Fenn and I sat up night after night at Fenn's cramped table above the chandlery, copying entries until our fingers went black. No — my hands shook because the parcel still smelled faintly of my son. He had carried it against his chest for a whole day before he gave it to me on the pier, and some part of him had soaked into the cloth the way salt soaks into everything here, permanently, patiently.

Sefa watched me from the bed. Her eyes had been clear for over a year now — clear the very night Silas came to say goodbye, as if her mind had been waiting for the moment it would be needed and had decided, finally, to attend.

"Tell the Englishman," she said loudly, "that the sea gives back what is owed."

Crane blinked. "Ma'am?"

"She means," I said, "that we have been waiting eighteen months for exactly this visit, and she does not intend to let you leave without tea, because whatever else the English have done to this coast, they do make proper tea, and she has opinions."

For the first time in my life I watched an official of the Crown laugh in my sitting room, and something in my chest unclenched that had been clenched since the day a company man counted my children like crates of nutmeg.

I made the tea. Then I put the ledger in his hands, and I watched a careful, cautious, career bureaucrat open it and go quiet in the way men go quiet when they understand they are holding the rest of their careers in their palms.

"Every name," he murmured. Page after page. Fenn's small precise duplicates: dates, debts inflated, families transferred, berths sold, sums paid to whom. Thirty years of the Straits Trading Company reduced to columns, and every column ending in a human being.

"There is a supplement," I said, and brought out Mariel's scrap — the stolen fragment of Harrow's draft testimony against my family, in Harrow's own hand — and the copies of the petitions, forty-two signatures from the kampong, divers and netmenders and widows, all of whom remembered the night the company sank my husband's boat over an unpaid advance of eleven dollars.

Crane looked at the signature count for a long moment. "You organized this."

"The dead organized it," I said. "We just held the pen."

### Fenn

I am sixty-three years old, and I testified before a commission of inquiry wearing my good coat, which fits me now the way my conscience used to fit me: badly, with visible straining at the seams.

They gave me water. They gave me a chair. They addressed me as *Mister Fenn, senior clerk (retired)*, which has the distinction of being the first thing the Company ever called me that was entirely true.

Commissioner Crane read aloud from the true ledger. "Entry of the ninth of March, 1839. Advance to Blackwood, diver, eleven dollars. Vessel forfeit. Do you recognize this entry, Mister Fenn?"

"I wrote it," I said. "Or rather, I copied the false version for the company books, and the true version for myself. The advance was repaid in kind within the month — four hundredweight of pearl shell, worth sixty dollars at then-current rates. The debt was extinguished. The boat was sunk anyway. The diver drowned. The company collected the insurance."

Silence in the chamber. Somewhere a fan creaked. I found I was not trembling. This surprised me; I had trembled for thirty years over far smaller things.

"Why did you keep duplicate records, Mister Fenn? A dangerous habit for a man in your position."

I had rehearsed a dozen answers to this question. Elegant ones. Noble ones. In the end I gave the commission the plain one, because after thirty years the plain one was all that was left.

"Because I could not sleep," I said. "And because somewhere in the back of my skull, where cowards store their better selves, I knew that someday a boy would come along who asked the right questions, and I wanted there to be an answer waiting for him." I looked at the commissioners, all five of them, English and Straits-born, and I said the sentence I had come to say. "Gentlemen, the ledger you hold is accurate. Every family named in it was sold across the strait on fabricated debts or fabricated crimes. I witnessed it, I recorded it, and I told no one. I am not here as a hero. I am here as evidence."

One of the younger commissioners — a Straits-born lawyer with excellent handwriting and no expression whatsoever — leaned forward. "And the boy. Silas Blackwood. He took a berth on the Meridian intended, by forged authorization, for another. Do you consider that act a sacrifice or a strategy?"

I considered my answer, because it deserved consideration. Eighteen months I had considered it.

"Both," I said at last. "That is the trouble with acts of real consequence — they refuse to be only one thing. He sacrificed himself and he outmaneuvered Harrow, because a ship full of witnesses who remember each other's names is a far greater threat than one runaway boy. But I will tell you what I told his mother. That berth was meant for any one of five people he loved, and he chose himself, and he chose it in about four seconds, and I have known men who spent forty years failing to arrive at decisions half as decent."

The questioning went on for three days. I testified to everything. Names I had not spoken aloud in decades came out of my mouth like stones from a riverbed, each one smoother and heavier than I remembered. Harrow's accounts. Harrow's arrangements with the plantation factors across the strait. Harrow's habit of burning correspondence quarterly, which had availed him nothing, because I had been the shadow copying his shadows since before he had hair on his chin.

On the third day, the seizure orders went out. I was told — though officials tell you these things with great ceremony, as if granting you a personal gift — that agents had entered the counting house at dawn and placed locks on Harrow's desks, his drawers, his false bottoms. Especially the false bottoms. Edmund Harrow, factor of the Straits Trading Company, architect of the tidy arithmetic by which troublesome families became profitable cargo, was to be detained pending formal charges.

They say he did not resist. They say he smiled at the agents and remarked that ledgers, like tides, always come back in.

I take no pleasure in the image of him in a locked room. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the only currency I have left. What I feel instead is something closer to vertigo — the sensation of a weight carried so long that its absence makes the ground seem untrustworthy. For thirty years I balanced two sets of books: the company's and my own conscience's. Both were fraudulent. Only one is now being corrected, and it is not mine. Mine I must correct the slow way, by testifying, and remembering, and teaching the new clerks — yes, they have asked me to teach the new clerks, God preserve them all — that behind every figure sits a face.

I returned home from the third day of testimony, ate a modest supper, and slept ten hours without dreaming. In the morning I felt lighter than I had since I was a young man with ink-stained fingers and a talent for looking elsewhere.

I mention this because the doctors tell me my heart is "tired," and one is advised, at my age, to put one's affairs in order while the light is good. My affairs consist of one chest, two coats, a complete set of true duplicates, and a letter to Silas Blackwood which I write a little of each evening. I do not expect him to receive it quickly. But the transfers are voided now; the commission's order went out under seal; and ships, as I have cause to know, sail both directions across the strait.

### Silas

The announcement came to the plantation the way everything came to us — late, distorted, and secondhand, shouted through a fence by a man who had heard it from a man who had read half a notice nailed to a wall in Malacca.

But even distorted, the shape of it was unmistakable. *Transfers voided.* The word traveled down the rows like fire down a fuse. Voided. Cancelled. Undone. A word with a door in it.

Eighteen months I had been on that plantation, and I will tell you what eighteen months of captivity teaches a man who once believed silence was his finest skill: silence is nothing. Silence is the raw material. Speech is the craft.

I had learned to talk in that place. Not clever talk — I had never been clever with words the way Fenn was, or Mariel. Plain talk. The kind you use at night between the barracks and the drying sheds, when a man from some village whose name I mangle confesses that he cannot remember his mother's face anymore, and you ask him her name, and he says it, and you say it back, and then you make three other men say it, so that the name now lives in four heads instead of one fading one.

Names. It was the only wealth the company could not confiscate, because I had swallowed it whole. Four hundred and eleven names from Fenn's ledger, memorized in the counting house during those last six weeks, recited nightly like a rosary. On the plantation I discovered that other men had their own lists — villages, wives, children, boats, gods — and that a list shared becomes a rope thrown across a current. We tied ourselves together with them. By the eighteenth month, I could walk from one end of that compound to the other and greet every man by his own name and the names of his people, and I watched what that did to them. Straightened spines. Men who had been cargo remembering, slowly, painfully, that they were crews.

So when the notice came — garbled, doubtful, half-believed — it was not I who doubted it loudest. It was old Rahim, who had been sold twice and trusted nothing that arrived by fence-shout.

"Papers," he kept saying. "Where are the papers? Since when does the company undo anything without papers?"

And I laughed, I confess, harder than the joke deserved, because I knew where the papers were. I had helped copy them. Somewhere across the strait my mother and my sister and a dying clerk had turned one stolen ledger into a hundred petitions, and the petitions into a commission, and the commission into seals and signatures and — yes, Rahim, papers. The most beautiful paperwork ever produced in the Straits, and I include in that assessment every pearl receipt my father ever brought home.

The first ship to carry freed laborers back was the *Cormorant*, a fat honest tramp steamer chartered by the commission, and we boarded her in a rain squall, four hundred and eleven souls minus the ninety-one who had died and would not be forgotten, plus the seventeen children born in captivity who owed that place nothing and would be told of it someday only as a lesson.

I stood at the rail as the plantation sank below the horizon. I expected weeping. Some wept. Mostly what I saw was men watching the water the way sailors watch water — professionally, appraisingly — and I understood that they were not looking backward at all. The sea runs both ways, my grandmother says. These men were reading the tide toward home.

The crossing took nine days. On the fourth day a commission agent aboard — a nervous young man with a strongbox chained to his wrist — sought me out.

"Mister Blackwood. I'm instructed to give you this." An envelope, worn soft at the corners, forwarded through three ports. "It came for you in March."

Fenn's hand. I knew it before I opened it, that small precise script, upright as a fence post.

*Boy — If this finds you, the ledger has found its audience, and you may consider the debt of my thirty years' silence partially discharged, though never fully; arithmetic does not forgive, it only balances.*

*Harrow is taken. Your mother is magnificent, which you knew. Your sister writes letters that should be classified as ordnance. Tomas has turned crown witness and looks, remarkably, like a man relieved of a stone.*

*I am tired, and my heart is tired, and I am content. Come home if the sea permits. Someone should inherit the drawer.*

*— Fenn*

I read it four times. Then I folded it away, and went and taught the evening names as usual, because grief and gratitude keep poor hours and the list does not recite itself. But that night, lying among four hundred sleeping men who were free and didn't fully believe it yet, I said one extra name into the dark. *Ambrose Fenn. Clerk. Keeper of the true book.* So that it would live in more heads than mine.

### Ida

The *Cormorant* was sighted off the eastern point on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday noon the whole kampong stood on the sand like a jury.

Mariel could not keep still. She had grown in eighteen months — taller, sharper, her letters now famous in certain reformist drawing rooms, though she pretended not to care and cared enormously. She gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

"He'll be thinner," she said. "They're always thinner. Don't cry before I do, Mama, you always cry first and then I can't see properly."

"I have not cried in a year," I lied.

Tomas stood a little apart, as he had earned the right to do and also had not. Testifying had cost him every friend he had made in thirty years of careful dealings, and bought back only our family's grudging tolerance, which he seemed — to his credit and everyone's astonishment — to find sufficient. Dev had come up from the fishing grounds. Even the old divers were there, the ones who had given their memories as evidence, standing in the surf like a delegation.

The ship came in slow. The freed men came ashore in ones and twos, blinking, and the crowd surged, and there was crying and shouting and a woman from two houses down collapsing into her brother's arms, and through all of it I watched the gangway the way you watch a door when you are waiting for one particular person to walk through it.

Then he did.

Thinner. Taller, or seeming so. Sunburnt, steady, scanning the beach the same way he used to scan the counting house — reading silences, reading faces, finding the one thing in the room that mattered.

He found us. And I will record honestly what happened next, since this family's history has suffered enough from dishonest records: my son, who survived five years of the company by perfect silence, crossed that sand at a dead run and did not manage a single word. Neither did Mariel, who talks like the tide. We simply collided, all three, and stood there being a fact again instead of an absence.

Behind him, the freed men were being greeted by their own, and the beach sounded like a festival and a funeral held simultaneously, which is, I have come to believe, what homecoming actually is.

Sefa was brought down in her chair to see it, of course. She waved him over afterward with imperial impatience, took his face in her hands, and studied him for a long moment with those clear old eyes.

"The sea gives back what is owed," she pronounced. "Though it deducts interest. You are too thin."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"And you will eat. And then you will tell me every name you saved, and you will speak up, because my hearing is not what it was."

"Yes, Grandmother."

Later — much later, after food, after the stories, after Mariel had extracted from him a full accounting of the plantation and fallen asleep mid-sentence against his shoulder — I walked with him to the churchyard at the edge of the kampong, where the sand gives way to grass, poor stubborn grass, and where a weathered stone marks the place we gave my husband when the sea finally sent him home in pieces, years ago now, in another life.

Silas crouched and touched the stone the way you touch a doorpost you grew up under.

"I brought the ledger's work home, Papa," he said quietly. "All four hundred and eleven. Minus ninety-one." His jaw worked. "Minus ninety-one."

"Ninety-one names," I said, "that will now be read aloud in places that matter. Mariel has promised me that."

He looked up at me then, and I saw it — the thing I had hoped against hope to see again and had feared, these eighteen months, I never would. Not the boy who survived by silence. The boy underneath him. The one who existed before the letter, before the ledger, before the berth. Still there. Sea gives back what is owed.

"Mama," he said, "there's something I have to ask you. Something Fenn wrote me. About a drawer."

I smiled, because I had been waiting for that question since the ship sighted land, and because I already knew the answer I intended to give, and because — for the first time in longer than I could remember — the future looked less like a tide coming in and more like a bridge going out. One plank at a time. Built by hand. Bearing weight.

"Come home first," I said. "Then we'll talk about inheritance."
