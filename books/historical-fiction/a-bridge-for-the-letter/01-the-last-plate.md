## 1. The Last Plate

The rain had been at the workshop roof since before dawn, working its slow arithmetic into the slates, and Wren Eastwick counted the drips the way other men counted prayers — one for the corner above the acid bath, one for the seam over the drying rack, one for the place where the old leak had stained the plaster in the shape of a coastline no surveyor would ever chart. Three drips. He had named them years ago without meaning to, the way a man names the creaks of a house he cannot afford to leave.

He warmed the ink between his palms first. Tobias Marsh had taught him that on his second day of apprenticeship, when Wren was fourteen and all elbows and hurry: *Cold ink lies, boy. It drags and it blots and it tells the Admiralty your hand shook when it didn't. Warm it like you'd warm a bird.* Seven years later the habit was so deep in him that he did it without thinking, cupping the little horn pot as though it were something alive, something with a heartbeat under the shell.

The plate lay on the sloped drawing board before him, copper bright as a coin fresh from the mint, and upon it — in the scribe's reversed mirror-writing that only engravers and saints could read forward — the last uninked stretch of the Norfolk coast. Blakeney Point. The shingle spit curling like a sleeping finger. The channel markers Marsh had sounded himself, in the spring of 1744, three months before the sea took him.

Three months before the sea *said* it took him. That was the sentence Wren kept in the locked room at the back of his mind, the one he never opened in daylight. He had learned to walk around that door for three years now, the way you learn to walk around a loose stair tread — automatically, without looking down, without letting your foot find the hollow.

He dipped the burin's ink pad, set the fine graver to the copper, and began.

The work itself was silence made visible. That was what he loved about it, what he had never been able to say aloud even to Bette: engraving was the only hour of his life when nothing was required of him but truth. A line was either true to the coast or it was not. A depth sounding was either sounded or guessed, and Marsh had beaten guessing out of him early, with a patience that had frightened young Wren more than shouting ever could have. *A false fathom drowns a man who trusted you. There is no sin in this trade greater than a pretty lie.*

Wren cut the line of the point, and the rain kept its time above him, and the little stove breathed its coal-smell into the room, and for forty minutes he was safe inside the geometry of the world.

Then the knock came.

* * *

It was not an unusual knock. That was the thing he would remember afterward — how ordinary it was, two soft raps, the knock of the post-boy or the oil merchant or Mrs. Quill from the chandlery come to ask whether they wanted sprats. Ordinary things were what killed you. Wren understood this now in his bones. It was never the storm that found a man out; it was the calm Tuesday, the familiar step on the stair, the letter that looked like every other letter until the seal broke.

He set down the graver. He wiped his hands on the leather apron, though they were not dirty — another inherited gesture, Marsh's gestures living in his body like tenants who had outlasted the landlord.

The letter lay on the wet flagstones where the boy had dropped it through the slot rather than wait. Cheap paper, greyish, rain-spotted at one corner. The direction was written in a clerk's round hand, not Marsh's. Wren noted that first, the way he noted everything now, cataloguing threats the way his master had catalogued soundings. Safe. A bill, then. A circular from some stationer's. He carried it to the bench and slit it with the bone folder and stood there a moment longer than he needed to, because there was no reason to hurry, because hurrying was itself a kind of confession.

The paper inside was better than the cover. Good rag paper, cream, faintly ruled by hand.

And the writing on it —

Wren did not read it. Not at first. He couldn't. His eyes went to the hand itself the way a man's eyes go to a face across a crowded church, and every thought he owned fell out of him at once, the way tools fall from an apron when the strap gives.

He knew that hand. He had spent seven years learning it, copying it, being corrected in it. The long descenders that leaned east like grass in wind. The peculiar crossed *t*, the crossbar struck twice, quick, as if the writer resented spending even that much time on formality. The numerals with their flat feet. He had inked over that hand a thousand times, transferred it to copper, burnished proofs of it. He knew it the way he knew his own knuckles.

*Tobias Marsh* had died on the ninth of November, 1744, drowned off Blakeney with his boatman, both of them given to the sea, neither body recovered. Wren had stood at the funeral — such funeral as there was, an empty coffin and a curate who had never met the man — and had listened to Bette weep beside him while he himself felt nothing but a vast ringing silence, grief and guilt fused into something too heavy to feel. He had rehearsed his answers since. To the magistrate's men, to the curious, to himself in the dark hours: *I was not there. I know nothing of that night. My master sailed to sound the point and the weather turned.* Three years of silences, each one polished smooth as a burnisher passes over copper, each one fitting into the next so that the whole surface of his life looked, to any eye, continuous and true.

The letter said:

*Mr. Eastwick — You will know the hand if not the name. Do not show this to anyone. The atlas must be finished. When it is finished, further word will come. Burn this after reading. — T.M.*

Wren read it four times. The words themselves were almost nothing; it was the hand that shouted. And beneath the signature, small, tucked into the tail of the final stroke the way Marsh always tucked a private mark into his finished plates — a tiny anchor, drawn in one unbroken line.

No forger would have known about the anchor. No forger *could* have known; Wren had never seen it on anything but proofs, never on a published plate, never on a letter. It was a joke between master and apprentice, from the first year: *Every honest chart carries an anchor somewhere, Wren. Find it and you've found the maker's conscience.*

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to an apprentice he had once been, and he set the letter down on the bench beside the half-inked plate of Blakeney Point, and the two things lay together — the coast Marsh had sounded the spring before he died, and the writing of a dead man asking whether the atlas was done.

The rain worked on above. One drip, two, three.

* * *

He did not burn it. That was the first order of his own nature the letter overturned, and he noticed the disobedience with something like wonder. Three years of careful obedience — to the magistrate's office, to the story, to the grave — and his hands refused this smallest command as if the paper were hot iron.

Instead he went to the back of the workshop, to the cedar chest where Marsh's surviving drafts lived wrapped in oilcloth, and he knelt and lifted out the topmost sheaf and laid it open on the floor among the smell of cedar and old linseed, and he compared.

There was no doubt. That was the terrible simplicity of it. He held the letter above a draft of the Wash soundings and the two hands matched stroke for stroke, the double-crossed *t*, the leaning descenders, the flat-footed numerals, the anchor hidden in the signature's tail. Whoever had written this letter had either learned a dead man's writing to a perfection no forger would trouble himself to reach — for what profit was there in imitating a drowned cartographer? — or the hand was the hand, and the man was the man, and the sea had lied.

Or — and here the third thought came, the one he had been walking around for three years, the loose tread, the door in the dark — or the letter was bait, and someone who knew Marsh's habits intimately had written it, someone who had access to the drafts, someone who wanted Wren to believe, and the believing itself was the trap.

Who benefited? He asked it the way Marsh had taught him to ask it of any chart offered for sale: *Who profits from this line being believed?* A smuggler wanting a guide through the channels. The magistrate wanting — what? Wren's stomach turned cold. Magistrate Halloway had taken an interest in the atlas already, twice, courteously, the way a cat takes an interest in a hole where something small is breathing. If the atlas contained more than coastlines — and Wren had begun, lately, in the margins of his memory, to suspect that it might — then a letter in a dead man's hand was a very elegant hook.

But the anchor. The anchor argued against the trap. Only two people alive had ever known about the anchor, and one of them had gone into the sea.

Unless the other one had talked, at the end, to someone. Unless the boatman — the boatman whose name Wren had also learned never to say, whose widow still sold winkles on the quay and would not meet his eye — unless the boatman had known too, and had told his killer before he drowned, or told someone after, or —

Wren sat back on his heels among the drafts and let the cold of the flagstones climb into him. Above, the rain thickened, drumming now, and the three drips quickened their count, and the light through the salt-fogged windows went the colour of pewter.

Bette would be home within the hour. She would come in shaking the wet from her shawl, smelling of the fish market and the cheap soap she stretched with lye, and she would read his face in one glance, because she always did, because she had been reading him since their father's ruin taught them both that faces were documents that could be seized. She would say, *What's happened,* and he would say, *Nothing, a bill,* and she would let it lie, the way they let everything lie, the beautiful exhausted diplomacy of siblings who have survived something together.

He gathered the drafts, wrapped them, closed the chest. He took the letter from the bench and held it over the stove grate, over the breathing coals, and again his hands refused, and this time he understood why. Because if it was truly Marsh — if somewhere in the world that voice still existed, that dry correcting voice, *warm your ink, boy, there is no sin greater than a pretty lie* — then burning the letter was a second drowning, and Wren would be the sea that did it.

He folded it instead along its original creases and slid it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay like a coal that would not cool.

* * *

The last of the daylight came grey through the windows as he returned to the plate. He should stop; the light was failing; Marsh had rules about failing light. But his hands needed the work, needed the small absolute country of the engraving where a line was only a line, so he lit the lamp, trimmed the wick, and bent again over Blakeney Point.

The graver sang its thin note against the copper. Cut, turn, clear the swarf with the brush, cut again. The shingle spit grew under his hand, faithful to the survey, faithful to the man who had walked it with his chain and staff in a spring that now seemed to belong to another century, another life. Wren inked the channel marks. Six fathoms. Four and a half. The dangerous shoal off the far point that Marsh had insisted be marked in the plainest possible style, no ornament, no elegance — *a danger is not decorated, Wren. Decorate the harbours. Tell the truth about the rocks.*

He was so deep in the work that he did not at first hear the difference in the rain — the way it had changed its weight, softening, the storm moving off toward the sea and taking its noise with it, leaving behind that enormous coastal quiet in which a man can hear his own blood. In that quiet, the workshop felt suddenly, precisely occupied. Not haunted. Watched. The feeling was specific and physical, a pressure between the shoulder blades, the certainty — unreasonable, total — that someone stood beyond the fogged glass of the street window, close enough that breath would have bloomed the pane.

Wren did not look up. Marsh had taught him that too, though not in words: *When you are unsure of your position, do not move. Take your sounding first.* He finished the line he was cutting. He cleared the swarf. He set the graver down parallel to the rule, exactly, deliberately, a craftsman ending a day's work and nothing more.

Then he raised the lamp and turned.

The window showed him only his own reflection, ghostly on the black glass, and behind it the wet street running silver under the lamp's spill, empty in both directions. A cart track filling with water. The gable of the chandlery opposite. Nothing. No one.

But on the inside of the sill, where the old paint had cracked away to bare wood, something pale caught the lamplight — a scrap of paper, folded small, pushed under the door's gap sometime in the last hour while he worked with his head bowed and the rain for company.

Wren crossed the room and picked it up, and his fingers were steady now, steadier than they had been all day, the steadiness that comes when dread finally stops pretending to be routine and shows its face.

He unfolded it. One line, in the same unmistakable hand, the double-crossed *t*, the anchor curled into the final stroke like a heart inside a ribcage:

*You kept the letter. Good. We have much to discuss, and less time than you think. — T.M.*

Outside, far off down the quay, a bell rang the hour — eight strokes, muffled by the wet — and somewhere between the sixth and the seventh, Wren understood that the three years of ordered silence were over, and that whatever came next had already begun, and that the door in the back of his mind, the one he had walked around for a thousand days, had just swung open on its own.
