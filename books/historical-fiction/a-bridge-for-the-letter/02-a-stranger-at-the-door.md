## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Wren Eastwick knew the rhythms of his street the way he knew the tides he drew — the milk cart's iron-rimmed wheels at six, Mrs. Quill's dog complaining at her gate a quarter after, the fishwives' voices rising and falling like gulls over the shingle by eight. No one knocked on the workshop door at half past seven. The men who wanted charts came in the afternoon, when the light through the north window made the vellum honest. The men who came early were the ones who did not want to be seen coming.

He set down his pen. The last plate of the atlas lay beneath his hand, the ink of its final coastline still wet enough to shine, and for one absurd moment he considered simply continuing to work — letting the knock go unanswered as though no one were home, as though the house had been empty these three years instead of merely quiet.

The knock came again. Three blows, evenly spaced, patient as a man who had all morning and knew it.

"I'm coming," Wren said, and heard how young his own voice sounded in the empty room.

* * *

The stranger filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon — not large, exactly, but total. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been used hard: a scar through the left eyebrow, knuckles swollen with old breaks, a coat of good wool gone salt-stiff at the cuffs. His eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of shallow water over sand, and they went past Wren immediately, cataloguing the workshop behind him with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory.

"Eastwick." Not a question. "The apprentice."

"The late master's apprentice," Wren said. "I'm finishing his atlas. Can I help you?"

"You can let me out of this wind, for a start." The man stepped forward without waiting to be invited, and Wren found himself giving ground, backing into the workshop as the stranger shut the door behind him with a click that sounded final as a lock turning. "Callum Voss. I sailed with Tobias Marsh eleven years. Boatswain, last three of them."

The name landed in the room like a dropped weight. Wren kept his face still because he had practiced keeping his face still for three years, and it was the one skill his grief had taught him well.

"Then you'll know," Wren said carefully, "that Captain Marsh is dead."

"Will I." Voss crossed to the workbench without touching anything — a discipline, Wren noted, of a man who understood what things cost — and stood looking down at the final plate. The coast there was real: the headland north of Garrow Point, drawn from Marsh's own soundings, every rock and shoal rendered in Wren's best hand. "That's fine work. He'd have been proud of you."

"He was proud of me."

"Were you proud of him?" Voss turned. The pale eyes were not unkind, which somehow made them worse. "Eleven years I served under him. Best navigator on this coast, maybe any coast. A man who could read water the way priests read scripture. And then one night in March three years back, the *Providence* goes out with him aboard and comes back without him, and everyone says drowned, and everyone says tragic, and everyone says what a loss." He picked up a rag from the bench, turned it over, set it down again. "Everyone says. That's a lot of saying, Master Eastwick, for a man who never once gave me an order I couldn't trust."

"Men drown," Wren said. "It's the sea's whole occupation."

"It is." Voss smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Funny thing, though. Drowned men don't write letters."

The rain against the window seemed suddenly loud. Wren became aware of his own heartbeat, of the candle he'd snuffed an hour ago still trailing its thin thread of smoke, of the letter upstairs in the drawer where he'd locked it — locked it, God help him, as though paper could be imprisoned — with its careful, slanted, unmistakable hand. *The margins are the map. Trust the ink, not the man who sent it.*

"I don't know what you mean," Wren said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But your face does, so we'll get along fine." He moved to the shelf where the master's surviving drafts were racked — Wren's stomach tightened; those drafts, those precious, damning drafts — and ran one finger along their spines without pulling any out. "I've had two letters now. Both signed T. Marsh. Both in a hand I'd know blindfolded, having spent eleven years watching him mark up my logbooks. And both letters say the same thing, more or less." He turned back. "They say: ask the boy. Ask Eastwick. He has the atlas, and the atlas has the rest."

"The atlas has coastlines," Wren said. "Depths. Soundings. Lights and landmarks. It's a chart book, Mr. Voss. It's what it says on the title page."

"And the margins?"

"What margins?"

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Outside, somewhere down the street, a door slammed, and both of them glanced toward the window — a small, involuntary synchrony that told Wren more about the man than the scar or the coat had. Voss was afraid too. That was worth knowing. Afraid men were dangerous, but predictable.

"Master Marsh wrote in margins the way other men breathe," Voss said quietly. "Little marks, little signs, numbers that weren't depths and letters that weren't labels. I asked him once, years ago, what they meant. He said, *they mean nothing to you, Callum, and everything to someone else.*" He stepped closer. "So I'm asking the someone else. Decode them. Tell me what he wrote. Because either Tobias Marsh is alive, in which case I have eleven years of loyalty that wants settling, or somebody is writing in a dead man's hand, in which case I want to know who learned it, and why, and who they're writing *to*."

* * *

Wren made tea. It was the only thing he could think to do, and later he would understand that making tea had been a surrender — the gesture of a host, offered to a man who had broken into his safety uninvited. But his hands needed something to hold that wasn't a lie, and the kettle gave him ninety seconds of honest work while his mind raced through the geometry of the trap.

Because it was a trap, however gently laid. If Voss truly had letters in Marsh's hand, then either Marsh lived — and the world Wren had built out of three years of silence, the careful edifice of *the master is dead, the master is dead, the master is dead* — was built on sand. Or someone else had learned that hand, which was worse, because only two people alive had studied it closely enough to forge it, and Wren was one of them.

He carried the tea over. Voss took the cup without thanks and drank standing, looking out the window at the grey street.

"My father worked for him too, before me," Voss said, apropos of nothing. "Coastwise runner. Hanged for it in '39, when the revenue men got lucky." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way men recite wounds they've stopped expecting anyone to dress. "Marsh paid for the burial. Never said a word about it after. That was the kind of man he was. He paid his debts and he burned his papers and he expected the same of everyone around him." The pale eyes came around. "So when a man like that writes to me from beyond his own drowning and says *ask the boy*, I don't come here to chat about the weather."

"I've told you what's in the atlas."

"You've told me what's on the plates." Voss set down the cup. "Now tell me about the night he died. You were here, weren't you? The night the *Providence* went out?"

The question was soft as a knife going in. Wren had rehearsed this answer for three years — rehearsed it in the bath, on the walk to market, in the grey hour before dawn when the rehearsing did its worst damage. *I was here. He went out alone. He didn't tell me where.* Simple. True, even, in its bones.

"I was here," Wren said. "He went out alone. He didn't—"

"He didn't tell you where." Voss nodded slowly. "Aye. That's what the coroner's man wrote down too. Funny, that. The coroner's man asked you the same questions I'm asking, word for word near enough, and you gave him the same answers, word for word near enough." He leaned in, and up close Wren could see the grain of the scar, could smell tar and old sweat under the salt-stiff wool. "Boy, I've watched men lie to magistrates and lie to press-gangs and lie to their wives on the quay with the tide going out. You're the worst liar I've seen in thirty years at sea, and the fact that you're still alive tells me somebody's been protecting you from ever having to get better at it."

The words went through Wren like cold water through cloth. *Somebody's been protecting you.* He thought of the letters arriving plain-wrapped, postage always paid, always on the quarter-day. He thought of Magistrate Halloway's courtesy calls, twice yearly, always brief, always ending with the same mild sentence: *Keep to your work, Master Eastwick. The coast is safer with you on it.*

"Mr. Voss," Wren said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out, "if Captain Marsh had something hidden in this atlas, don't you think the people who drowned him would already have found it?"

Something flickered across Voss's face — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own fear spoken aloud by someone younger.

"They haven't found it," Voss said, "because they don't know it's there. Or because they can't read it. Or—" he paused, and the pause had teeth in it, "—because the person who *could* read it has spent three years pretending he can't." He drained his tea and set the cup down on the bench with a small, precise click. "I'll come back, Master Eastwick. I'm a patient man; the sea teaches patience or it teaches drowning. But I'll tell you what I told the last man who lied to me about Tobias Marsh." He shrugged into the wind as he opened the door, rain gusting in past him, smelling of kelp and cold iron. "He's not lying anymore. Nobody is. Not anymore."

Then he was gone, his boots fading down the wet cobbles, and Wren stood in the doorway watching until the street gave him back his emptiness.

* * *

He did not touch the atlas for an hour. He sat at the bench with his hands flat on the wood, feeling the grain under his palms, listening to the house settle around him the way a ship settles, and tried to think like his master instead of like himself.

*What would Tobias do?*

Tobias Marsh would burn the margins. That was the first answer, and Wren rejected it the way you reject a reflex — because burning them meant burning the only proof of whatever truth the letters pointed toward, and because some stubborn, unforgivable part of him had spent three years wanting to read them and had finally, finally begun.

Because he *had* begun. Last night, alone, with the first letter open on his knee and a magnifier borrowed from the plate-room, he had looked at the margin notes properly for the first time — not as decoration, not as the master's habitual scribble, but as text. And he had seen what he should have seen years ago if he hadn't been so carefully, so obediently, not-looking: that the notes clustered thickest around certain coasts, certain harbors, certain dates. That beside the chart of Halloway's own home waters there was a symbol repeated four times — a small anchor with a bar through it — and beside each instance a sum in shorthand figures. Sums that grew.

Payments. The margins were payments. Or debts. Or a ledger wearing the clothes of navigation.

He had stopped reading there, hands shaking, and put the drafts away, and lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic of his own: three years of silence, weighed against whatever was written in the spaces between the coastlines. Three years of the magistrate's courtesy. Three years of letters from a drowned man.

Now the arithmetic had a new term. *Callum Voss, boatswain, eleven years, father hanged.* A man with a grievance and a claim and pale eyes that missed nothing. A man who would come back, and who might not come alone next time, and who might not be the only one watching.

By noon Wren had made himself three promises. He would finish the plate — the atlas must be finished; whatever else happened, it must exist complete, whole, undeniable as an object. He would learn the cipher faster than anyone could force it from him. And he would tell no one — not Bette, not the neighbors, not the coroner's ghost, no one — because telling people was how constructed safeties came down.

He worked through the afternoon. The light failed early, as it did in October, and he lit the lamps and kept working, and it was only when he rose to trim the wick that he saw it.

Across the street, in the shadowed mouth of the cooper's yard opposite, a man stood smoking a pipe. Ordinary enough — dockside men smoked pipes at all hours. But the pipe had gone out. Wren watched for ten minutes, trimming wicks, moving between the windows with a casualness he did not feel, and the ember never glowed, and the man never shifted his weight, and when the church bell struck nine he simply walked away into the dark without hurrying, the way men walk when they are not going anywhere in particular and have all night to not arrive.

Wren closed the shutters. All of them. Then he climbed the stairs to Bette's door, stood with his hand raised to knock, and — thinking of Voss's voice, *somebody's been protecting you*, thinking of sums growing in margins, thinking of his sister asleep with the household accounts under her pillow the way other girls slept with saints' cards — lowered it again.

In his room, he took the first letter from its drawer and read it once more by candlelight. *Trust the ink, not the man who sent it.*

"But which man sent it?" he whispered to the empty room. "And how many more are asking the same question?"

Outside, the rain began again, soft and steady on the shutters, and somewhere below — or perhaps only in his ears, or perhaps in the walls themselves — the house creaked once, like a ship taking soundings of the dark.
