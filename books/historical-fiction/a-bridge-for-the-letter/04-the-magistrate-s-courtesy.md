## 4. The Magistrate's Courtesy

The rain had turned the lane to pewter by the time Wren opened the door, and Magistrate Halloway stepped across the threshold as if he had been invited — as if the invitation were merely a formality that had gone astray somewhere between his intention and Wren's ignorance of it.

"Master Eastwick." The magistrate removed his hat with the unhurried care of a man who understood that every gesture was a sentence, and that he alone chose the punctuation. Rain beaded on the broad shoulders of his coat and did not fall. "I hope you'll forgive the hour. I find that workshops are best visited before they've been tidied for company. One sees the work as it truly is."

Behind him, the clerk — a narrow man named Pell, whose face seemed assembled from apologies — lifted the leather case an inch in salute.

"Magistrate." Wren stood back from his own door like a man standing back from a tide. "We're honored."

"We are not," Halloway said pleasantly, and came inside.

* * *

He moved through the workshop the way water moves through a hull: silently, finding every low place. His gloved hand hovered over the ink-stained bench without touching it, drifted past the drying rack where the finished plates hung like laundry, paused at the stove — cold these two days, though Wren had lit it that morning out of something like superstition — and finally came to rest at the great table where the atlas lay open under its linen sheet.

"The Eastwick Atlas," Halloway said, with the reverence of a churchwarden approaching relics. "Coastal survey of the southern approaches. Your master's life's work, I'm told. And now yours."

"Tobias Marsh began it," Wren said. "I only finish what he drew."

"Only finish." Halloway smiled at the sheet as though it had said something witty. "Mr. Pell, you've heard that phrase before?"

Pell consulted his memory with visible diligence. "Often, sir. In depositions. 'I only carried the letters.' 'I only kept the books.' A very common construction."

"Common indeed." Halloway let one finger rest on the corner of the linen. "May I?"

It was Wren's table, Wren's workshop, Wren's dead master's work — and there was nothing to do but nod.

The sheet came away like skin.

Halloway bent over the atlas with genuine attention, and this was the worst of it: the attention was real. He read the coastlines the way other men read scripture, tracing the pencil underlays with a fingertip held a hair's breadth above the paper, following the soundings out from Dungeness into the deeper water where Wren's ink still glistened faintly. The lamp above the table threw his shadow across half the room, and the shadow touched everything his body did not.

"Beautiful," he said at last, and meant it. "Do you know, Master Eastwick, what I admire most about charts? They cannot lie. A coastline is a coastline. A sounding is a sounding. The pilot who trusts a false chart drowns believing it, and the sea does not trouble itself with his excuses." He straightened. "Men lie. Coastlines don't. It is the only honest literature in England."

"Some would say the same of ledgers," Wren said.

Something flickered behind the magistrate's eyes — appreciation, perhaps, or appetite. "Some would. And they would be wrong, which is why I keep mine locked." He turned a page with two fingers, delicate as a surgeon. "You knew him well? Marsh?"

"Seven years his apprentice. Three years since—"

"Yes." Halloway said the word gently, cutting Wren's grief off at the root the way a gardener prunes. "Three years since the *Providence* was lost off Fairlight. The ninth of October, 1744. She went down in the evening; the bodies came ashore through the week after. Marsh himself washed up at Winchelsea on the twelfth — identified by the boatman who found him, one Silas Crake, on the evidence of a silver watch chain and a scarred left forearm. Buried the fifteenth in the parish ground at Icklesham, though of course the register entry was amended later when the coroner's inquest returned its verdict. Accidental drowning. Very tidy." He looked up. "Forgive me. I read too many files. Dates are a disease of my profession. But you see how precisely a man can be known by his ending, even a man nobody thought to ask questions about."

The rain filled the silence. Wren heard his own pulse in it.

"You remember all that," he managed, "about a cartographer."

"I remember everything about everyone." Halloway said it without pride, the way a man admits to a limp. "It is not a virtue. It has cost me friendships I never had." He drew off one glove, finger by finger, and stood bare-handed over the atlas, and Wren watched that naked hand the way one watches a snake cross a path. "Your master drowned with his boots off, did you know that? Found without them. Curious detail. A working man loses his boots in the water and someone always remarks on it. No one remarked. Which means either the sea was merciful enough to leave a drowned man decently shod and the finder took them, or—" he smiled, mild as milk, "—or he never wore boots into the water at all, because he never went into the water."

Wren kept his hands flat on the bench behind him so their trembling would have something to press against. "The inquest—"

"Returned its verdict. Yes. Inquests do. That is what they are for — like doors, they close." Halloway replaced the glove. "Tell me about the margins."

The question arrived without warning or approach, the way a wave takes a jetty. Wren felt the whole constructed morning tilt beneath it.

"The margins?"

"Marsh's habit, I'm told. Notes in the borders of his plates. Tides, bearings, corrections?" The magistrate's tone made each item a small test, and Wren understood that Halloway already knew the answers, whatever they were, and was measuring not the truth but the distance between Wren's truth and it.

"Bearings mostly," Wren said. "Soundings disputed between surveys. Where the lead-line disagreed with older charts. He was... particular."

"Particular." Halloway rolled the word around like a coin tested by biting. "A particular man. Who wrote particular notes. In a particular hand." He looked down at the open plate, at the fine border of ruled lines, and Wren followed his gaze and saw — for the first time with another man's eyes — the margin notes sitting in their neat ranks like soldiers awaiting orders, innocent to anyone who could not read them, legible to anyone who could.

"And the hand," Halloway continued softly, "is such a distinctive thing, isn't it? A hand survives its owner. Letters arrive in it. Petitions. Confessions. A hand can be recognized across a churchyard, so to speak, by those who knew it in life." He closed the atlas with a click of buckles and linen. "I knew Tobias Marsh's hand in life. Correspondence, you understand. Professional matters."

The workshop seemed to contract around the lamp. Wren heard himself say, from a great distance, "Professional matters," in a voice that belonged to a tired craftsman with nothing to hide, and hated how well the mask fit, and feared how badly.

"But I weary you." Halloway straightened his hat brim between thumb and forefinger, precise as a man squaring papers. "I came only to admire. The Admiralty does, you know — admires. There is talk of a formal requisition. Charts of this quality serve more purposes than navigation. Courts, for instance. Juries appreciate a map; it spares them imagination, and juries have so little." He was moving toward the door now, unhurried, collecting his clerk with a glance. "When it's finished — and you will finish it, Master Eastwick; I have every confidence, and every intention of checking — I should like first sight of it. Not possession. Sight. Consider it a courtesy extended in advance, so that no one need feel surprised later. I dislike surprises. They make a man abrupt, and I have worked very hard to be a courteous man."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the bench, the rack of plates, the boy in the ink-stained apron holding himself together with both hands flat on the wood.

"Poor Marsh," he said. "Drowned at forty-six, in October, with the best work of his life nearly done. One wonders what a man like that would think, watching someone else finish it. Whether he'd rest easier knowing his coasts were in careful hands. Or whether—" the smile again, brief and warm as a knife drawn from a fire, "—he'd have preferred some things stay unfinished. Good day, Master Eastwick. Mind the damp. It gets into everything, this time of year. Into paper especially. Paper keeps what it's given, you know. Forever."

The door closed on the rain-shining lane, and the two silhouettes merged with the pewter light and were gone, unhurried, patient, certain.

* * *

Wren stood a long while without moving.

Then he crossed to the table and opened the atlas and looked at the margins — at the small, exact, confiding marks his master's hand had left along the edges of every honest coastline — and understood, with the slow cold certainty of floodwater rising over a sill, what he had been keeping all these three years.

Not a book of coasts.

Evidence.

Somewhere out in the wet world, a trial was being assembled, plank by plank, deposition by deposition, and the atlas on his table was its centerpiece — and Magistrate Halloway had just come to look at it the way a hangman looks at rope: professionally, appreciatively, measuring the length.

And the letter upstairs, folded in the false bottom of his master's old drafting box, written in a dead man's hand —

Wren blew out the lamp and stood in the grey light, listening to the rain write its own endless, illegible ledger on the roof.

Ten days, he thought he'd had. Standing there in the dark, he understood the number had changed.
