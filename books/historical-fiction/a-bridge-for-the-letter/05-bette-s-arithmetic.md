## 5. Bette's Arithmetic

Bette Eastwick kept her accounts the way other women kept their tempers: closely, and mostly in private.

The household book lived in the bottom drawer of the dresser in the parlour, beneath a folded cloth that had once been white and was now the colour of weak tea. It was not a handsome book. Its corners were soft with handling, its pages ruled in her own hand because printed ledger paper cost fourpence the quire and fourpence was a loaf and half a candle's worth of oil. On its first page, dated the week after their father's ruin, she had written in a young girl's careful capitals: *What We Have.* She had not needed to write what they had lost. That inventory kept itself.

She opened the drawer now on a Thursday morning while the fog outside pressed its grey face against the glass, and Wren worked in the shop next door at the plate for the atlas — she could hear him through the wall, the small wet sound of the pen, the pause, the pen again. He had worked like that for three years, steady as a clock with one hand. Lately the rhythm had changed. The pauses were longer. Twice in the past week she had heard the pen stop entirely and not begin again for the better part of an hour, and once she had come to the doorway with bread and butter he did not eat and found him sitting with a letter in his lap as though the letter were something dead he didn't know how to bury.

He said it was nothing. He said it the way their father used to say it, which was a thing Bette had never told him.

She took out the book, set it on the dresser, and trimmed the lamp though the morning was bright enough for anything but truth.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this.

Rent on the workshop and the loft above it: eleven shillings the month, owed to a landlord who had raised it twice since the atlas became a thing worth talking about. Coal and candles through a coastal winter: eight shillings if the winter was kind, fourteen if it was not, and winters on this coast had stopped being kind around the time everything else did. Food for two growing appetites grown now and merely hungry: ten. Ink, copper, acid, paper, the oil for Wren's lamp — his materials, which she entered in the book under *The Work* because it felt less like charity that way: twelve, some months fifteen.

Against this: the atlas commission, paid in instalments by the stationer's man who came with soft hands and softer questions. Four pounds the quarter, promised to continue until delivery. And the mending work, the copying, the plain-sewing she took in through the tailor on Fore Street and never mentioned at the supper table, because Wren had enough weight to carry and pride enough to be wounded by hers.

She ran the columns twice, because she always ran them twice, and arrived where she always arrived: they were solvent the way a boat is seaworthy in harbour. Everything depended on no weather coming.

But weather had come. She had seen it in her brother's face before she saw it anywhere else, and then she had seen it in the street itself — in Mr. Quill the stationer's clerk lingering across the way with nothing to linger over; in the way the fish-boy would not meet her eye on Tuesday; in the visit, three weeks past now, of the magistrate himself, all courtesy and polished boots, admiring the atlas the way a man admires a purse he intends to lift. She had not been in the room. She had been in the passage with the tea tray, close enough to hear every word, and Halloway's voice had done a thing she recognised from her childhood: it had been warm the way a snare is warm, oiled and patient, saying *how fine* and meaning *mine.*

And then there were the letters. She had not read them — she would die before reading them — but she knew they came, because she paid the boy who carried them, twopence at the door, and the boy had told her without knowing he was telling her that the last one smelled of tar. Of ships. Of elsewhere.

Her brother was frightened, and her brother lied badly, and so Bette did what she had done since she was fourteen years old and their father's name went into the newspaper beside words like *default* and *disgrace*: she sat down with the book and she made fear into numbers, because numbers could be beaten.

* * *

Flight, then. Say it plainly, the way the book demanded plainness. Flight from what? She did not know exactly, but she knew the shape of it — the magistrate's patience was not patience, it was a rope paying out — and she had learned long ago that by the time you know precisely what is chasing you, you are already too slow.

A passage abroad. Hull, or better Harwich, and from there across to Holland or north to the Baltic ports, where an Englishman with a steady hand for charts might find work and a woman who could keep books certainly would. A cabin berth on a merchant brig: two pounds ten at minimum, more in winter, more again if you wanted to travel as someone with a name rather than someone with a bundle. Coach or carrier to the port, thirty miles of road: half a crown apiece, and horses cost extra after dark. Food for the crossing and the first fortnight ashore before wages began: another pound, honestly reckoned. New shoes for Wren, whose boots had gone thin at the heel — she had noticed in church and looked away so he would not know she had noticed: four shillings.

She wrote the sum at the bottom of the page and looked at it for a long time.

Five pounds, eleven shillings, sixpence. For one person. For one berth, one crossing, one life begun again at the far end of the sea.

For two, double it, and double it wrong, because two travellers ate more than twice what one ate and drew twice the eye besides. Eleven pounds and change, nearer thirteen if the weather turned, and the weather always turned.

She had, in coin and credit together, after today's instalment from the stationer's man: six pounds, two shillings, and a hatbox of things that were not money yet.

Bette closed the book and sat with her hands flat upon its cover, listening to the pen scratch through the wall — scratch, stop, scratch — and let herself think the thought that had been standing at the door of her mind for a week, hat in hand, waiting to be asked in.

There was money for one passage. Not two.

* * *

Their mother's silver lived in the cedar chest at the foot of the bed Bette slept in, the bed their mother had died in nine years ago, in the good years, before the bad ones had a name.

Six spoons, twelve of the smaller kind, two sauce ladles, a sugar tongs shaped like a bird's clawed foot, a cream jug chased with vines so worn you had to tilt it to the light to find them, a pair of candlesticks their mother called *the Paris pair* though no one knew if they had ever been within a hundred miles of France, and a christening cup engraved with a name — *Ann Eastwick, 1701* — belonging to a grandmother none of them had met. Their mother had polished these things on the first Saturday of every month, laying them out on the table on a grey cloth, and Bette, small then, had been allowed to hold the cup and breathe on it and rub.

She laid them out on that same table on a Thursday evening when Wren was below with his plates and would not come up until the candle guttered, and she did not polish them. That seemed to her the whole difference between her mother's world and hers, stated simply: her mother had cleaned silver for the pleasure of the shine. Bette weighed it.

Four ounces the spoon, near enough. Silver fetched by weight from the refiner, and less still from the man behind the Goldsmiths' arch who asked no questions — she had made enquiries already, weeks ago, walking past his door three times before she could make herself go in, and coming out with a price written on a slip of paper she had since hidden inside the cover of the family Bible, which was the only place in the house Wren never looked, God forgive her both the lie and the location.

The christening cup she set apart from the rest. She could not have said why at first, and then she could: because it had a name on it. Because everything else in the box was valuable for being old and heavy, and the cup was valuable for carrying somebody. She put it back in the chest, under the winter flannel, and left it there.

The rest she wrapped, piece by piece, in the grey cloth, and the cloth in a flour sack, and the sack in the market basket under a length of bacon and a head of cabbage, because a woman carrying a basket buys nothing and a woman carrying silver tells the whole street.

She went down through the shop. Wren sat bent over the drawing board with his sleeve rolled back and ink to his wrist, and the lamp made an island of him, and around the island the workshop stood in shadow full of the master's ghost — the empty stool, the coat still on its peg because neither of them could move it, the smell of copper and aquatint and the particular smoke of Tobias Marsh's pipe that had soaked into the beams and would outlive them all.

Wren looked up. His eyes were rimmed red as raw meat, and he smiled the smile he had been using lately, which was a door pulled shut.

"Marketing?" he said.

"There was nothing in the house," she said, which was true, and held up the cabbage as evidence, which was not.

"You're out late for cabbage."

"I'm out at all hours for everything," she said, lightly, and hated how easily it came, the lightness, how many years she'd practised it. "Eat the bread on the shelf before the mice do. I'll not cook at ten o'clock."

He bent back to the plate. She watched him a moment longer than a sister should, memorising him without deciding to — the curve of his shoulders over the work, the bitten nail of his drafting hand, the way his lips moved when he read a difficult line, a habit from childhood he would deny to his grave. Then she went out into the fog with the basket on her arm and the silver riding beneath the bacon like a heart under a rib cage.

* * *

The man behind the Goldsmiths' arch was called Nedd Prynne and had hands like roots and eyes like water in a well — you could drop things into them and they would never come back up. His shop smelled of acid and old fire. He unwrapped each piece without reverence and weighed it without ceremony, and the scale's brass pans trembled and settled, and Bette watched her mother's vines and birds and worn-bright edges sink by degrees into a column of figures on a slate.

"Spot work," Prynne said. "Good weight. Old fashion hurts it some." He named a figure.

"That's not the price we spoke."

"That was before I counted the ladles." He looked at her over the scale, unhurried. "You'll get the same at two other doors and one of them will cheat you worse."

She let him have it. That was the arithmetic too: the difference between his price and the true price was the fee for asking nothing, and tonight she was buying silence along with everything else. He counted coins onto the counter, sovereigns and halves, and she touched them as though testing a stove, and made herself pick them up.

At the door he said, without turning round, "Eastwick. That's a name been in this parish."

"It has," Bette said.

"Names come back," said Nedd Prynne, weighing something invisible in his root-hand. "Like scrap."

* * *

She did not go straight home. The fog had thickened to something almost solid, lamps swimming in it, the harbour invisible and audible somewhere below — the slap of water, the groan of hulls, a bell buoy speaking its one word over and over into the dark. She walked to the head of the stairs that led down to the quays and stood there, because the quay was where the ships were, and she had sent the silver away and gotten coins for it and the coins in her pocket were, by any honest counting, a ticket.

One ticket.

That was the thought she had been circling for a week, and here at the rail above the black water she finally let it land and sit.

The book said one. Five pounds eleven and sixpence for a life; eleven and change for two; and even with the silver melted into her pocket she would not reach the second number before the magistrate's ten days — ten days! she had heard the clerk say it to the stationer's man through the shop's open door, the atlas summoned, requisitioned, *required* — ran out. The numbers were not cruel. They were only numbers. They did not care whom they sent away.

So it came to choosing, and Bette Eastwick had known since she was fourteen that choosing was the part nobody saved you from.

It ought to be Wren. Any stranger passing in the street would say so: the talent was his, the atlas was his, the future worth having belonged to him, and she was the sister who kept the book. But a stranger had not watched him these three years. A stranger had not seen how he had built his grief into a room and locked himself in it, how he could finish a coastline to the tenth of an inch and could not say aloud that he was afraid. Wren would refuse to go. Wren would stand in the doorway of his burning house explaining that the atlas was not finished. And she loved him, and love had made her fluent in him, and fluency told her that if she put the choice in his hands he would choose wrongly, generously, fatally.

Therefore: she would not put it in his hands.

She gripped the rail and the fog wet her face and the bell buoy spoke its word again, out in the channel, marking a danger no one could see. One berth. She would buy one berth, quietly, in a name that was not quite theirs, and she would find a way — there was a way, there was always a way, she had been finding ways since the newspaper and the word *disgrace* — she would find a way to make her brother walk up a gangplank whether he willed it or no. Let him hate her abroad, where hating was safe. Let him curse her in Dutch or Danish across a foreign breakfast table for the rest of his days.

She turned from the water. Behind her the town climbed away in tiers of dim windows, and among them, high on the hill, one window burned in a house she knew by sight and reputation: the magistrate's, where a lamp moved slowly from room to room like something searching the dark for a scent.

Bette counted her coins once more in the fog, by touch alone, and started home, and the coins made no sound at all, and that, she thought, was the beginning of learning to move quietly enough to save somebody.

She was halfway up the hill when she saw the boy.

He was one of the carrier's lads, and he should have been abed, and he was standing under the eaves of the shut chandlery holding a letter out to her as though he had been told to wait all night if it came to that. "From the harbour," he said. "Said to give it to the Eastwick girl, and quick."

The letter was damp with sea air. There was no direction written on it in any ordinary hand — only a short row of small marks, neat as stitching, that meant nothing at all to her.

Except that she had seen marks like them before, that very week, faint in the margins of a proof sheet Wren had left lying by the fire, and covered quickly with his forearm when she came near.

Bette took the letter. Her pocketful of sold silver hung against her hip, heavier than any purse had ever been, and she climbed the rest of the hill with the paper crackling in her fist, toward the lit workshop where her brother sat guarding a language he had lied about not knowing — and she decided, with the calm of a woman balancing a column that finally comes out even, that tomorrow morning, before any ship sailed and before any magistrate called, she was going to teach herself to read it.
