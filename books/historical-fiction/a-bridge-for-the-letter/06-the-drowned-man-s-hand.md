## 6. The Drowned Man's Hand

The second letter came on a Tuesday, with the tide.

Wren knew the tide's schedule the way other men knew their prayers, and so he knew that the boy who delivered it — a gutter-mouthed runner from the customs house steps, paid a halfpenny to climb Cooper's Hill with his collar up against the drizzle — could not have come from any shop or office in the town below. The low water held the packets ashore until noon. This letter arrived at half past eight, which meant it had been written in the town, or very near it, by someone who wanted Wren to believe otherwise. He filed this observation away the way he filed everything now: carefully, in the dark, where the margins lived.

He was working on the Foulness shoals when the knock came. The plate lay half-inked beneath his hand, soundings rendered in lines as fine as hairs, and he did not lift his pen until the third knock, because for three years he had trained himself not to jump at doors. Tobias Marsh had taught him that. *A surveyor's first discipline is stillness,* the old man used to say, standing hip-deep in the shallows off Dengie with his theodolite balanced on his shoulder like a soldier's musket. *The coast does not hurry for you, boy, and neither does anything worth measuring.*

The runner thrust the letter at him without dismounting, rain beading on his impudent grin, and was gone before Wren's fingers closed on it. No caller. No name. Just the paper, heavy and cream-coloured, sealed with plain wax — no signet, no thumbprint, nothing but the smooth press of some anonymous implement — and addressed in a hand that made Wren's stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

*T. Marsh* had written this address. Not *Tobias*. Never Tobias. The master had signed his plates *T. Marsh fecit* and his letters *your servant, T. Marsh*, and he had formed the capital T in a particular way, with a flat crossbar struck left-to-right in one confident motion and a stem that tapered at the base as though drawn with a diminishing breath. Wren had spent four years copying that hand before he was permitted to develop his own. He knew it the way a man knows his mother's face — better, perhaps, because a face changes and a hand, once fixed by practice and pride, does not.

The T on the address was perfect. The crossbar struck left to right. The stem tapered like a breath running out.

Wren stood in the doorway a long time, letting the rain spot his shoulders, while the workshop behind him ticked and settled and Bette moved somewhere upstairs with her quiet household arithmetic, counting coins he didn't know she was counting. He did not go inside. He stood in the wet morning holding a dead man's handwriting and felt the careful architecture of his grief shift on its foundations.

Because here was the thing he had never told anyone, not even the dark: he had wanted this letter. For three years he had half-wanted it, in the shameful cellar of his heart where wanting lives, and every morning when he opened the workshop shutters and found no letter, he had told himself it was relief he felt. Now it had come, and relief was nowhere in him. There was only the T, perfect, and the question underneath it like a sounding-line dropped into black water: *how deep does this go, and what will it strike?*

* * *

He locked the door. He never locked the door; the workshop was a public place, apprentices came and went, neighbours borrowed the good shears. Today he turned the key and then stood with his palm flat against the wood, listening to his own pulse, feeling like a man who has just admitted to something.

The first letter — Voss's letter, the one the stranger had brought with his sea-boots and his terrible patient eyes — Wren had hidden inside the false back of the drafting drawer, beneath the spare points and the gum arabic. He took both letters now to the big table in the window-light, laid them side by side, and made himself look.

*The Kestrel, out of Maldon. She takes her berth at the Quay Hard on the night of the 14th, tide at eleven. One place is kept. Bring nothing that cannot be burned.*

No signature. Only the T, only the hand.

One place. Singular. The words sat on the page like a stone in a shoe — small, grinding, impossible to ignore. Who was the place kept for? Him? Voss? Some third party he had never imagined? And kept by whom — by a drowned man writing letters from the sea's own kitchen, or by someone who had learned to counterfeit the drowned?

He pulled the master's surviving drafts from the plan chest. There were not many; after the drowning, Halloway's men had carried off two full drawers of papers in the name of the Admiralty, and what remained was what Wren himself had saved, folded into his shirt the night they came, standing in the doorway with his heart hammering while men who smelled of tar and government rifled a dead man's life. Three draft sheets of the Blackwater survey. A page of tidal calculations. Half a letter to the Trinity House elder brothers, unsent, its seal unbroken. And the practice sheet — God help him — the practice sheet Wren himself had made at fifteen, copying the master's letter-forms over and over down the page while Marsh stood at his shoulder saying *slower, slower, you are drawing the letter, not being it.*

He spread them all out. He took the strong loupe from its baize bag, the one with the silver mount that Marsh had bought in Amsterdam and pretended was a trifle, and he bent over the papers until the candle-glow of the lamp filled his whole vision, and he compared.

The T was right. He had known it would be. The crossbar, the tapering stem — correct in every particular.

But a hand is not a letter-form. A hand is a rhythm. It is pressure and speed and hesitation, the tiny arrhythmias of a living wrist, the way a man lifts his pen between words (Marsh lifted high, a bird-flick, always) and the way he joins his lower case (he didn't, mostly — Marsh printed his small letters like an engraver, because engraving was his first trade, and only joined when hurried, and when he joined he joined upward with an impatient spur). These were things no copyist could know from samples alone. These were things learned only by sitting years at a man's elbow, watching ink come alive under his fingers.

Wren checked the spurs. Present, in the letter, exactly where they should be. He checked the word-spacing — Marsh crowded his words toward the end of each line like a man saving something for later. Crowded, in the letter. He checked the flick of the final *h* in *burned*, which Marsh habitually left open at the top like a half-closed gate, a laziness Wren had teased him about a hundred times. Open. Open as a gate.

He set down the loupe. His hands were cold, though the stove was lit and the room was warm enough to soften the wax cakes.

Either Tobias Marsh was alive — had been alive these three years while Wren inked his atlas and rehearsed his silences and mourned him with a devotion that had become, without his noticing, the load-bearing wall of his entire character —

— or someone had learned to write him. Someone close. Someone who had watched that wrist for years. An apprentice, say. Another apprentice. A man named Callum Voss, whose hands were scarred with salt and rope, whose eyes had measured Wren across this very table three days ago while he claimed the letters came from a drowned man and demanded the cipher Wren swore he could not read.

Voss had been Marsh's man before he was anything. Voss had sailed on the Blackwater surveys as boat-handler before Wren ever tied a bowline. Voss had sat at that elbow.

Wren walked to the window. The rain had thickened; the town below was smoke and grey slate, gulls hanging motionless over the quay like notes on a stave nobody was reading. Somewhere down there the Kestrel waited, or didn't. Somewhere down there Halloway sat in his warm rooms turning the atlas over in his careful mind, knowing it contained more than coastlines, saying nothing, the way power says nothing. And somewhere out past the bar, past the shoals he knew line by line and depth by depth, there was either a living man in hiding, dying slowly of some wound Wren could not imagine — or there was nothing at all, and the sea had given back only a handwriting, and grief itself had been made into a weapon to move him.

He could not decide which possibility was worse. That was the unbearable part, he understood then. Not the uncertainty — he was a surveyor, bred to uncertainty, to soundings that failed and fog that ate the horizon. What was unbearable was that both answers destroyed something necessary. If Marsh was dead, then the letters were chains being forged link by link around Wren's life by someone who knew exactly how much love to counterfeit. If Marsh lived, then the master had let him grieve — had stood somewhere, perhaps, in those first black months, watching Wren carry the coffin-shaped silence through the streets, and had chosen to remain a ghost. Either way, the dead man's hand was reaching up out of the water, and either way it was closing on Wren's ankle.

And beneath all of it, cold as ballast, lay the thought he could not stop returning to: *bring nothing that cannot be burned.* Burned. As if the letter-writer knew what Wren had read in the margins. As if the letter-writer knew precisely what the atlas carried in its careful coded edges — the payments, the erased names, the confession dressed as navigation — and wanted it near, or wanted it gone.

Bette's step on the stair. He swept the papers together too quickly, too obviously, and when she came in with bread and cold mutton on a board she stopped in the doorway and looked at the scattered drafts, the locked door still keyed behind him, his face doing whatever his face was doing.

"You've had a letter," she said.

"Business."

"From a dead man?"

She said it lightly. She said everything lightly these days, with that new brightness she wore like polished brass, and he saw — in the way her eyes went once around the room, quick and thorough, cataloguing — that she was frightened, and had been frightened since Voss came, and was managing her fear the only way she knew, which was to count it, sort it, file it somewhere he wasn't permitted to look.

"Bette." He almost told her then. The letters, the hand, the berth, the whole black ledger of it rising in his throat like floodwater. But the margins were in the atlas, and the atlas was evidence, and every person who touched that knowledge became a person who could be hurt with it. He had learned that from the master's own marginal hand: *names erased.* "It's nothing. A supplier."

She set the board down. For a moment her brisk mask slipped and he saw his sister underneath, the girl who had held the household together on arithmetic and stubbornness since their father's ruin, and she looked tired beyond any sleep's repairing.

"Wren. Whatever it is — whatever is coming — we have survived worse than paper."

Had they? He thought of the debt, of the father, of the slow dispossession that Halloway had overseen with such courtesy. He thought: *you don't know what the paper says. I barely know, and it is eating me alive.*

"Yes," he said, and lied to her with a smile, and hated himself for how easily it came. "We have."

She left. The stairs creaked her going, lighter than a man's step, lighter than the master's had ever been.

* * *

Night came down on the town, and the rain turned to that fine sea-haar that swallowed the harbour lights one by one, and Wren sat alone with both letters and the drafts arranged in chronological order like exhibits at a trial he was conducting against the dead.

He tried one last test. It was a test Marsh himself had taught him, half-joking, over small beer at the Ship Inn: *if you doubt a chart, boy, don't measure the coastline — measure the errors. Honest error has a shape. Lies are seamless.* Every forger fears the flaw; the true hand commits them freely.

So he hunted flaws. He found none. Worse — he found errors he recognized. In the tidal calculation on the second page, the writer had added a correction for the spring range that was correct to the inch but expressed in the old Dutch units Marsh had never fully abandoned, a habit so buried that Wren himself had forgotten it until this moment. Nobody knew that. Nobody living except—

Except whoever wrote the letters. Except the master. Except possibly Voss, who had handled the survey instruments, who might have seen the working pages, who might—

The circle would not close. It spun and spun like a compass card near iron, and Wren sat in the lamplight until the candle gutters rose and fell with his own breathing, and understood at last what the letter had actually done to him. Not the date. Not the berth. It had taken his grief — the one clean thing, the thing he had tended like a grave for three years, keeping the weeds off it, keeping his silences polished on its stone — and it had shown him that the grave might be empty. That he had been mourning into a hole in the water. That the man he loved and resented and imitated and fled might have simply walked away from the drowning and left Wren holding the atlas, the debt, the danger, and the name.

He thought of Voss's parting warning, delivered soft as a confession: *Whoever holds the margins holds more than paper, boy. Hold it loosely.*

He looked at the letter. *One place is kept.*

And then, because he was his master's apprentice after all, because he had been taught to record what he observed rather than what he feared, he took a fresh sheet and his own pen — his own hand, his own imperfect living hand — and wrote down everything: dates, hands, comparisons, doubts, the Dutch units, the open *h*, the singular berth. Evidence, laid out plain, against a man who might be dead or might be watching. When he finished he read it over and felt, for the first time since the knock that morning, something steadier than dread.

If Tobias Marsh lived, then the master had questions to answer, and Wren would sail to the Kestrel's hard on the 14th and put them to him with the atlas under his arm and the margins decoded in his head.

If Marsh did not live — if this was Voss, or Halloway, or some third hand entirely — then someone wanted Wren at that quay at eleven o'clock on the night of the 14th, and he intended to find out who, before they decided what he was worth.

Either way, the letter demanded an answer. Very well. He would give it one.

He fed the comparison notes to the stove and watched the curl of flame take his own handwriting, and as the paper blackened and lifted he noticed — with the awful clarity that comes at the end of long days — the one thing he had failed to check.

The first letter, Voss's letter. He fetched it from the drawer's false back and set it beside the second, and looked, really looked, at the two hands.

They were not the same.

The words were similar. The message matched, message for message, thread for thread. But the first letter's T was struck right to left, and its stems ended blunt, and its writer — whoever he was — had copied Marsh's forms faithfully the way a man copies a chart of a coast he has seen only from offshore: accurately, and at a distance, and without ever once feeling the ground under his feet.

Two writers. Two letters. One claiming to be a drowned man, one claiming to speak for him — and lying about that, at least, in ink.

Wren snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark of the workshop, among the plates and pens and three years of careful silence, and heard the tide turn far below in the black estuary, going out, going out, dragging the night toward the sea.

Fourteen days. One berth. And now, two ghosts.

He would have to find the living one first.
