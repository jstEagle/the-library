## 7. Voss's Bargain

Callum Voss had learned to wait in the navy and perfected it in the years after, and so he waited now, three days, watching the Eastwick workshop from the upper room of the Salt House where a window gave onto the lane and the rain came off the sea at a slant that kept even the gulls low. Three days of watching a boy come out at dawn to fetch water, go in, come out at dusk to bar the shutters, and between those hours nothing but lamplight moving behind horn-pane like something breathing.

He knew what the boy was doing in there. He had known since the first letter went astray — since the letter he had not sent, that Tobias Marsh had written in his own cursed hand and given to a boatman who talked too much, and that had ended up opened on another man's table before ever it found its address. Voss had spent two years believing Marsh dead. He had mourned him, in the tight-lipped way he mourned everything, standing at a rail with his hat off and the wind doing the weeping for him. Then the letters began, and grief turned in his chest like a fish on a hook, and what it became was neither grief nor relief but arithmetic: *if he lives, then he chose this, and if he chose this, then he chose me for the drowning.*

That thought he carried the way other men carried a bad tooth. It woke him at odd hours. It sat across from him at meals.

The boy came out again just past noon, collar up against the drizzle, heading toward the chandler's with the quick hunched walk of someone who has learned that being seen is safer than being seen looking. Wren Eastwick. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. Ink-stained to the wrist — you could read the apprenticeship on him like a rating's tattoos. Marsh had spoken of him twice, and both times had done that thing with his voice, the softening he probably believed nobody heard, saying *the boy sees coastlines the way other men see faces,* and Voss, who saw coastlines only as places to run cargo and men only as things that did or did not stand when the shooting started, had been jealous of a child of fifteen without ever meeting him.

Well. He had met him now. And the jealousy had curdled into something more useful.

* * *

He did not knock at the workshop door that evening. He knocked at the side entrance where the tanning yard ran behind, because the front door had a bell and bells announced things, and Voss had no wish to be announced. The rain had thickened after dark into proper weather, drumming on the shuttered skylight, and when the boy pulled the side door open with the chain still on, lamplight fell through the gap in a hard yellow blade and cut Voss across the chest.

"Master Eastwick." He let the rain run off his hat brim. "You'll want to take the chain off. This isn't a conversation for shouting."

"We've nothing to discuss, Mr. Voss."

"You've a letter to discuss. The second one." He watched the boy's face do what faces do — the flicker, the clamp-down, the lie assembling itself behind the eyes like planks over a window before the storm. "You got it Tuesday. You've had it four days. I know because I paid the man who carried it to tell me when it landed, and I've been watching your lamp burn past midnight ever since."

The chain did not come off. But it did not get put back up either, and that was answer enough.

"I served under Tobias Marsh eleven years," Voss said. "Survey sloop *Lark*, then the coastal commission, then the work your master never put his name to because the Admiralty pays for charts and the crown takes them back. I laid the sounding lines while he drew the coasts. I held the lead when the current wanted to take my arm off. Eleven years, Master Eastwick, and when the inquiry came, when Halloway needed a man to hang it on so the bigger names could walk away clean, there was one name on every paper that wasn't a gentleman's."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the lane a dog complained about the weather and stopped.

"They said he drowned," the boy said. Carefully. A man walking on ice he'd been told was thin.

"They said. Yes." Voss smiled, and felt his own face make an unfamiliar shape doing it. "And here's what I think, and you'll tell me if I'm wrong, though I don't believe you will. I think Tobias Marsh looked at the choice in front of him — testify and ruin everyone including himself, or refuse and ruin slower — and he chose a third road. I think he drowned a Tobias Marsh that existed mostly on paper. I think a boatman was paid. I think the boatman is dead or gone, because boatmen who help gentlemen disappear always are. And I think—" and here the tooth in his chest ached, honest ache, old ache— "I think the man who took the fall for whatever was in those margins was standing right beside him when the decision was made, and was not consulted."

"That's not—"

"That's not a question. Correct. It's an accounting." Voss put one hand flat against the doorframe, not pushing, just being there, being solid, being the kind of man who had spent thirty years making himself difficult to move. "You've been reading the margins, Master Eastwick. Don't insult us both. Four nights of candlelight past midnight, and a man who translates cipher for a living doesn't burn midnight oil over margins he can't crack. So you know some of what's in there. Payments. Names. Which customs men were bought and which were bought cheap."

The boy's jaw worked. Behind him, deeper in the workshop, the lamplight caught the long table where the atlas lay open under weights, plates fanned like the wings of something pinned.

"If he's alive," Voss said quietly, "he owes me a death."

* * *

They ended up inside, because the rain would have drowned the conversation eventually and Voss had decided this was a conversation worth staying dry for. The boy took the chair by the stove and did not offer the second one, which Voss respected; hospitality was for people with surplus. Voss stood instead, dripping pleasantly on the flagstones, and let the warmth begin the slow unwinding of the cold he'd carried since morning.

The workshop smelled the way all chart-rooms smelled — size, gum arabic, the iron tang of fresh ink — but underneath it something sweeter, peat smoke and old wool, and Voss understood he was smelling the boy's whole life: the house that had gone down with the father's name, the sister holding it together with arithmetic, the apprentice sleeping in the same rooms as the work. Marsh had never mentioned the sister. Marsh had mentioned almost nothing, in the end. That was rather the point.

"How much do you know about the inquiry?" Voss asked.

"What there is to know publicly. Customs irregularities. Two convictions."

"Two convictions of small men." Voss peeled off his wet gloves, finger by finger, and set them on the stove ledge to steam. "Let me educate you in the rest, since your master's letters appear to be short on history. In forty-one and forty-two, half the revenue coast between here and the Solent stopped working for the crown and started working for whoever paid better, and the man who arranged it was careful enough that his signature appears nowhere. Marsh mapped that coast. Every creek, every gutter, every place a lugger could lie up safe from a revenue cutter. Do you understand? The corruption needed the geography, and the geography came from Marsh — willingly sold or thieved, that was never clear to me, but it went through him. When Halloway began pulling threads — and note, Master Eastwick, that Halloway pulled them, Halloway who is himself stitched into the garment somewhere, though proving it is another trade entirely — Marsh's maps were the thread that led everywhere."

"And Marsh ran."

"Marsh drowned," Voss corrected. "Officially. Unofficially, I think he ran. And the inquiry, finding its principal witness dissolved into seawater, required a substitute. Someone who knew the soundings. Someone who'd handled the cargo runs disguised as survey work. Someone expendable." He flexed his hands, feeling in the knuckles the two winters a hulk at Portsmouth had put into them. "I gave them three years for misprision and concealment, most of it spent breaking oakum in a room where they don't light the lamps because the prisoners work cheaper in the dark. Released in March. Time served, good behavior, and a magistrate's clerk who lost my papers twice until someone paid him to find them again."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the only door left." Voss crossed to the long table and looked down at the atlas without touching it — discipline, learned late, but learned. The final plate lay under its weights, the ink still holding a faint sheen where the boy had gone over it that morning. Coastline rendered in a hand so like Marsh's it hurt to look at. That was apprenticeship for you: the master lived on in the pupil's muscles whether anyone consented or not. "There's a brig called the *Kestrel*. Out of a port I won't name, carrying what I won't say, sailing on a tide I'm not yet ready to share. She has a berth aboard her that's been paid for and waiting. Paid for three years, if you can credit such patience, by a man whose name the shipping agent wouldn't give me but described well enough — grey-headed, writes with his left hand hooked like a man guarding his plate, tips like guilt."

The boy had gone very still. Stillness, Voss noted, was in this one's nature; it made him harder to read than most, but also easier to frighten, because frightened stillness and thinking stillness look alike from outside and only the pulse tells them apart. The pulse at the boy's throat was going like a bird in a sack.

"A berth," Wren Eastwick said.

"One berth." Voss let that sit. "Now. Here is the shape of my offer, and I'll make it once, plainly, because I've no talent for the ornamental. You decode the margins. All of them, complete, fair-copied in a hand that isn't yours — I'll want them untraceable. You give them to me, and you take the berth. The *Kestrel* touches ports where a man with your skills eats at the captain's table forever. Charts are the one cargo that's welcome everywhere." He paused, and gave the boy the truth, because lies worked better on top of truth than instead of it. "And I will use those margins how I please. If Tobias Marsh is alive — and your face, Master Eastwick, has been confirming that since I mentioned the berth — then he built his survival on mine, and I mean to present him the bill. What Halloway gets, what the Admiralty gets, what the world gets out of those pages afterward — none of it is your affair anymore. That's the mercy of the offer. You sail, and the paper war goes on without you."

"You'd take them to Halloway." Not quite a question. The boy was watching him with those marsh-colored eyes, weighing.

"I'd consider it. Halloway pays for proof. He also burns people who hold it." Voss shrugged. "It depends on the day, and on the price, and on whether Marsh is reachable by other means. You see the advantage of my position. Everyone wants something from these margins — Halloway wants them, Marsh wants them buried, you want them never to have existed, and I want what they're owed. I am the only party in this negotiation offering you anything except silence and a shallow grave."

* * *

He had expected longer resistance. He had prepared arguments for a boy who loved his master, and different arguments for a boy who hated him, and a third set for a boy too frightened to feel anything at all. What he had not prepared for — what arrived unbidden and unsettled him more than any of it — was watching Wren Eastwick stand up from the stove-side chair and cross to the table and put his own hand, ink-blackened, flat on the closed cover of the atlas, as if laying claim to a country.

"No," the boy said.

Voss waited. Boys often said no first and yes later, and the interval was simply weather to be sailed through.

"My master is dead," Wren said. "Whatever wrote those letters, whoever is alive in some creek with a grudge and a left-handed script — Tobias Marsh drowned three years ago, in every way that matters to me, and I am not going to dig him up so that other men can quarrel over the corpse. The margins aren't mine to sell, Mr. Voss. They're not anyone's to sell. They're a confession, and confessions belong to God and to the burning."

"They belong," Voss said softly, "to whoever suffered for them."

"Yes," the boy said, and did not flinch, and that was the moment Voss understood the apprentice had finished becoming something else. "Yes. And I know exactly how you suffered, because I've read enough of the margins to know the payments, the dates, the names erased — and yours is among them, Mr. Voss. Erased. Page nine of the cipher, third entry. He didn't just leave you to hang. He wrote you out first, and neatly, and in his best hand."

The room went very quiet except for the rain and the tick of the cooling stove.

Voss stood with his gloves steaming gently on the ledge and felt the tooth in his chest turn over. So the boy had read further than he'd guessed. Further, perhaps, than anyone. And Marsh — coward, cartographer, the finest mind Voss had ever served and the poorest — had sat in some hiding-place writing his sins down in cipher, including the sin of Callum Voss, and had apparently lacked the courage even to erase him thoroughly.

"He kept the record," the boy went on, quieter now, and there was something in his voice that was not triumph, nothing so simple, something that sounded almost like apology arriving from the wrong direction. "That's what the margins are. Not navigation. Penitence. He couldn't confess out loud, so he confessed sideways, in a language only I was taught to read. And I will not hand penance to a man who wants it for ammunition. I'm sorry for what was done to you. Truly. But the answer is no."

For a long moment Voss considered, professionally, the various ways the evening could still be bent to his purpose. The workshop had one door worth watching and a back yard with a wall a boy could climb but a man with a lantern could watch. There were things persuasion could do and things force could do and the overlap was smaller than novelists pretended. He could search the place now, tonight, while the boy stood there with his hand on the atlas like a saint on a mosaic. Two years in the hulk had left him strong in the way that rusted iron is strong — it would serve, once.

But force made noise, and noise brought constables, and constables brought Halloway, and Halloway was the one player at this table whom Voss genuinely feared, because Halloway did not want the margins for vengeance or absolution but for leverage, and leverage was the one currency that never devalued.

So instead Callum Voss did something he had done only twice in his life and regretted both times.

He told the truth unprompted.

"When I was twenty-six," he said, "your master pulled me out of the sea off Saint-Malo with a broken arm and a broken everything else, and carried me aboard in the teeth of a gale, and sat up with me two nights because the surgeon was drunk. Eleven years I followed him after that. Not for wages." He collected his gloves from the ledge, worked them on slowly. "So understand what I'm about to say comes from that man, the one he saved, and not the one the hulk made. You're being watched, Master Eastwick. Not only by me. There's a man who takes snuff outside the Salt House and a wherry that's lain off the staithe four days running with no cargo and no reason, and yesterday a stranger asked at the Ship Inn after the Eastwick workshop in a voice that had learned its English somewhere south of Dover. Halloway's courtesy is coming, and it will arrive wearing a warrant. Ten days, two weeks — I can't say. When it does, the atlas goes to the Admiralty, margins and all, unless someone moves first. Decode them, bury them, burn them — I no longer care which, though I'll tell you free of charge that burning is the only option Halloway can't undo. But decide quickly, and decide alone, and trust no one who offers to help you carry the burden. Including me."

He settled his hat. At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the boy standing guard over his dead master's conscience.

"For what it's worth," Voss said, "the berth is still yours if you change your mind. The *Kestrel* sails on next spring's tides, and she waits for no one twice." A lie in the last part — she would wait for the margin pages, whoever carried them — but a kind lie, or near enough.

Then he was out into the rain, and the side door closed behind him, and the chain went on with a sound like a small final verdict.

* * *

He walked the long way back, along the quay where the fishing boats knocked their sides together in the swell, and let the rain wash the workshop's warmth out of his coat. The wherry was where he'd said, riding dark off the staithe with no light showing, and the snuff-taker's window burned its patient yellow above the Salt House. Good. Let the boy be surrounded. Surrounding sharpened decisions wonderfully.

At the harbour steps he stopped, out of habit, to check the tide marks, and found himself reading the water the way Marsh had taught him — slack water, the ebb beginning, the channel buoys already shifting with the season. Eleven years of learning a man's mind from the way he drew a coastline, and what had it bought him? A grave with no body in it, a pardon bought with somebody else's secrets, and a boy with ink to the wrists who pitied him — pitied him, he was nearly certain of it, had stood there with his hand on the atlas pitying Callum Voss in that soft terrible way.

*Page nine, third entry. He didn't just leave you to hang.*

Voss realized he was smiling again, that same unfamiliar shape, and let it stay.

If the margins named him, then the margins proved him. Proof was a kind of property. And property, in Callum Voss's experience, always found its way to its owner eventually — sometimes by sale, sometimes by inheritance, occasionally by fire, but always, always home.

Tomorrow he would call on a certain clerk of a certain magistrate, who owed him a favor from the Portsmouth days and dealt, for a consideration, in other people's correspondence. If the boy would not sell the confession, then perhaps the law would buy it — and the law, unlike smugglers, paid in pardons.

The ebb was running. Out past the mole, black against blacker water, the lights of a brig working down-channel slid by — not the *Kestrel*, too early for her, but a reminder that everything in this world was either arriving or departing and the difference was only a matter of paperwork.

Voss turned up his collar and went to make his arrangements, and behind him in the rain the town slept on, all of it — magistrate, merchant, widow, boy — floating together on a chart drawn by a drowned man who was not drowned, over waters that had never yet been deep enough to drown the guilty.
