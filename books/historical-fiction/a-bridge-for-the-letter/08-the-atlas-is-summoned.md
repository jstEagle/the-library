## 8. The Atlas Is Summoned

Magistrate Elias Halloway owned three coats, and he chose the plainest of them for the visit to the Eastwick workshop. Silk announced intention; good wool merely suggested it. He had learned long ago that a man who arrived dressed for a seizure invited resistance, while a man who arrived dressed for an afternoon of connoisseurship was welcomed through the door by his own victim.

The rain had followed him from the county seat all the way down the coast road, a thin persistent drizzle that turned the lanes to grease and made the marshes beyond the town steam like something freshly cooked. He sat in his carriage with the window down despite the wet, because he liked to smell a place before he entered it. Salt. Tar. The char-smell of a lamp-oil workshop, and under it, faintly, the vinegar tang of ink. A house that lived by its pens. He found it restful, in the way a well-kept garden is restful right up until one notices the wall around it.

His clerk, Mr. Pell, sat opposite with the requisition papers squared on his knee and his hat held upside-down in his lap like a small black boat waiting to be launched. Pell was twenty-six years old and had the gift of appearing to be furniture. Halloway valued this above honesty, above diligence, above nearly every other quality a clerk might offer.

"You'll say nothing unless I look at you," Halloway said.

"I never do, sir."

"No. You never do." He watched the workshops give way to the water-lane where the Eastwick house stood, its lower stones furred green with tide-reach, its upper windows reflecting the grey sea so exactly that they seemed lit from within by drowning. Three years since he had stood at that door. Three years since Tobias Marsh's boat had been found splintered on the Gull Roads and Halloway had attended the inquest himself, taking notes in his own hand because he trusted no other hand to record what needed recording. The verdict had been *misadventure*. The verdict had been expensive. Verdicts usually were.

He stepped down into the rain without waiting for the umbrella and knocked at the door with the head of his stick — two taps, a pause, a third. A magistrate's knock should sound like a question to which the answer is already known.

* * *

Wren Eastwick answered it himself, which pleased Halloway. Apprentices who hid behind servants imagined that walls existed. This one had ink to the second knuckle and the particular pallor of a young man who had been sleeping badly, and behind him, down the passage, the workshop door stood open on a room that smelled of linseed and fear. Halloway catalogued both details without appearing to look at either.

"Mr. Halloway." Eastwick's voice was level. His throat moved twice before the words came, but they came level, and that was the part that mattered.

"Master Eastwick." Halloway let the courtesy hang there a moment, deliberate as a dropped glove. The boy had completed nothing so formal as a mastership, but grief and work between them had promoted him, and Halloway saw no reason not to flatter the promotion. Flattery was a leash. "You'll forgive the hour and the weather. I find myself in need of expert opinion, and there is only one expert on this coast worth the asking."

Something in the boy's shoulders eased, fractionally, and Halloway noted the easing too. He believes he is being asked about tides, Halloway thought. About soundings. About some dispute between fishermen. How lovely, to have so few imaginings.

The workshop was as he remembered it and yet poorer — the master's chair empty at the great table, kept empty these three years like a shrine, the apprentice's stool pulled up to the drafting board where the atlas lay beneath a linen sheet. Plates hung drying along a line strung near the stove: coastlines in blue-black, soundings stippled in fine dots like weather seen from very far away. Halloway walked the length of the room slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and read the plates the way another man might read scripture aloud — silently, moving his lips.

"The North Shoals," he said. "You've corrected the channel. Your master had it a cable's width east."

"New survey. After the storm of '45 the sand shifted."

"After the storm of '45." Halloway smiled at the plates rather than at the boy. "Everything shifts after storms. Sand. Channels. Loyalties." He turned. "May I?"

Eastwick hesitated over the linen sheet for perhaps half a heartbeat — long enough to be interesting, short enough to be deniable — and folded it back.

The atlas lay open at the plate for Whitsand Bay, half-inked, the pencilled soundings marching along the coast in ranks awaiting their color. It was beautiful. Halloway felt the beauty of it the way he felt most things: as a calculation. Fourteen plates complete or near-complete, if the hanging line told true. Two remaining. A title-page promised and not yet cut. An index of lights and beacons in the master's hand, stopped mid-sentence three years ago and never resumed — or stopped there deliberately, which was a different thing entirely.

"It will be a monument," Halloway said sincerely. "To him, and to you. The Admiralty has nothing on this coast to touch it. That is precisely why I've come."

He nodded once toward the door, and Pell came forward with the papers and laid them on the drafting board, squaring the edges with his fingertips before withdrawing to his station by the door. Halloway did not take the single chair offered; he preferred to stand over work when it was being surrendered to him.

"By order of the Vice-Admiralty court," he said, in the tone of a man recommending a wine, "the coastal atlas of the late Tobias Marsh is requisitioned for the use of the Admiralty, such work having been begun upon public waters and therefore belonging, in its completion, to the public service. You will deliver the finished work into my custody within ten days of today's date. You will deliver with it all drafts, working notes, proofs, marginalia, and papers appertaining thereto." He looked up. "*All* papers, Master Eastwick. The phrase is not decorative. Half the disasters of my profession come from men who assumed a word like *all* meant *most*."

The boy's face did an admirable job of remaining still. Admirable, and useless; stillness under pressure has a texture, and Halloway had spent thirty years learning to feel it through his skin like damp through wool.

"Ten days," Eastwick said. "Two plates remain. The index—"

"—will be finished, yes, or finished enough. I have every confidence." Halloway moved along the table and touched, with one gloved finger, the corner of the open plate — nowhere near the margins, but near enough that the boy's eyes went to the margins anyway. There. Like a pheasant flushing at the wrong edge of the wood. "It is a kindness, you understand. There are those at the customs house who would prefer to seize the work now, unfinished, and ask questions of whoever was holding it. I have persuaded them that science proceeds better unseized. Ten days is me defending your inheritance. Do appreciate that."

"I — appreciate it, sir."

"Of course you do." Halloway straightened his cuffs. "One more thing, and then I'll release you to your labors. I think of your poor master often. Drowned, and so close to shore. The Gull Roads in July — flat as a floor that night, by every account. A boat comes apart on a flat sea." He shook his head with genuine, rehearsed sorrow. "The coroner wrote *misadventure*, but I have never cared for the word. It suggests the mischance was nobody's. In my experience somebody can nearly always be produced, given time." He smiled at the drying plates, at the empty chair, at the boy standing very straight beside his inheritance. "You were fond of him."

"He was my master."

"Yes. And you've kept his chair empty three years. Sentiment. Or vigil." Halloway put on his hat. "Ten days, Master Eastwick. Mr. Pell will call on the eleventh. Good day."

* * *

In the carriage, going back, Halloway did not speak for the first mile. Pell knew better than to fill silence. The rain had thickened; the wheels hissed; the marshes slid past steaming, cut with creeks that shone like the seams of opened oysters.

"Note for the file," Halloway said at last. "Requisition delivered, acknowledged, demeanor composed, hesitation observed regarding marginal materials. Nothing further."

Pell wrote it. Then, because he was young and curiosity is the one quality even furniture cannot wholly suppress: "Sir — the boy. If he copies anything before delivery—"

"Then he is a fool, and fools are convenient." Halloway watched the water-lanes go by. "No, Pell. Consider it properly. Whoever holds those margins holds proof against half the customs house. Names, dates, sums — Marsh kept accounts the way other men keep sins, everything written twice, once for the ledger and once for leverage. I have wanted those pages for three years. Not to destroy." He almost smiled. "To own. A page that proves a man took bribes is worth its weight once. The same page, held quietly, is worth its weight every year of a man's life."

"Marsh could have sold them to anyone."

"Marsh wanted them *used* — that was always his weakness, conscience, it makes a man impatient — but he wanted them used against everyone but himself, and so we understood each other. Dead, he wants nothing, which is why someone else must want them for him." Halloway tapped the glass; the carriage slowed. "If the boy delivers clean pages, he knows nothing and lives ignorant, which is a mercy. If he has copied them, then he knows something, and a boy who knows something will eventually try to spend it — and when he does, he will have to spend it with me. Either way the trap closes. That is the whole art of it, Pell. Never chase. Arrange."

"And if he runs instead?"

Halloway looked out at the marsh, at the drowned ground stitched with channels no honest chart ever troubled to draw, and thought of the one chart that drew them all, lying under linen on a drafting table with two plates left to finish.

"Boys don't run," he said. "They're carried. Find me who would carry him, and we'll know the shape of the whole business."

* * *

That evening Wren Eastwick stood a long time in the doorway of the workshop with the requisition papers in his hand, reading them again though he knew them by heart already. Bette watched him from the stairs and did not speak, and he was grateful, because there was nothing yet that could be said aloud.

*All drafts, notes, proofs, marginalia, and papers.*

He looked at the atlas under its linen. He looked at the empty chair. He had believed — he had built three years of quiet on the belief — that finishing the master's work was itself a kind of protection, that beauty and usefulness would stand around him like a wall. Now the paper in his hand said otherwise, in language any lawyer could make unanswerable. Deliver the atlas, and he delivered whatever the margins contained into the keeping of the one man in the county with the power and the appetite to read them. Keep the atlas, and refusal became evidence. Finish it honestly, and honesty armed his enemy.

There was no version of the next ten days in which delivering the atlas protected anyone.

He rolled the requisition tight, as if it could be made smaller, and went to fetch a candle. The margins were not going to translate themselves — and whatever they said, he understood now, with a cold settling certainty, that he would need to know it before Mr. Pell came calling on the eleventh day.

Because Halloway had said *all papers*, and had looked, just once, at the edges of the page.

And a man does not look at edges unless he knows what keeps to them.
