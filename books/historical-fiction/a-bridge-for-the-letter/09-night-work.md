## 9. Night Work

The candle had burned to the width of a thumb by the time Wren admitted what he was doing.

He had told himself it was diligence — that a man surrendering an atlas in ten days ought first to make certain every plate was perfect, every coastline true, every sounding checked against the master's own field notes. He had told himself the copying was incidental, a clerk's habit, the way a man whittles without deciding to. But the third sheet of thin paper laid over Marsh's margins, the lamp-black ground down fine on his palette, the strokes traced in a hand he had practiced until it came without thought — these were not diligence. These were theft. He was stealing the confession out of his master's book, letter by letter, before the magistrate could nail the book shut.

Outside, the rain had settled into its long autumn patience, coming off the marsh in sheets soft as wool, and beneath it he could hear Bette moving through the house. She thought he did not hear her. He heard everything now: the stair that creaked and the one she avoided; the drawer in the parlor that opened too smoothly since she had oiled it; the small bright chime of silver against silver, wrapped in cloth, carried out under her shawl on evenings when she claimed to be calling on Mrs. Tregowan. He had said nothing. There is a kind of love that consists entirely of pretending not to count.

The workshop smelled of ink and wet wool and the tallow-sweetness of the candle, and the rain made a sound like someone reading aloud in another room. Wren dipped his pen. On the copying-sheet, in letters half again smaller than any honest hand would use, he wrote: *Item — to H. at the customs house, forty pounds, Michaelmas term, for the blind eye at Crag* — and stopped, because his hand had begun to shake, and a shaking hand makes a shaking line, and a shaking line in this work was as good as a signature.

He set the pen down and pressed his fingers flat against the desk until the trembling went into the wood instead of the paper.

Three years of silence. Three years of rehearsing, every night like this one, the answers he would give if anyone ever asked. *My master drowned off the Skerries in the October gales. I completed his plates from his drafts because it seemed wrong to let the work die with him. I never studied his margins closely; they were working notes, tide memoranda, nothing.* He had repeated it so often the words had worn smooth as river stones, and he had almost believed them, which was the point of rehearsal. But the margins were not tide memoranda. The margins were a ledger of sins, and he had copied four pages of them now, and every page named a man still living, still collecting, still powerful enough to make a boy's drowning look like weather.

A board sighed in the passage. Not Bette's step — too light, too hesitant — then it was Bette after all, because who else, and the door opened on a wedge of colder dark and her face above the candle she carried.

"You're still at it." Her voice was low, pitched under the rain. "I brought you bread. You didn't eat supper."

"I ate."

"You moved it around the plate. I've watched you do that since we were children; you think rearranging food is eating." She set the plate on the corner of the desk, away from the inks, in the one clean place, and then she saw the thin sheets — the copies — lying fanned where he had not thought to cover them, and the seeing changed her face the way a cloud changes a field.

"Wren. What are those?"

"Notes," he said. "Working notes."

"Don't." The word came out quiet and terrible. "Don't lie to me in this house. We have so little left, but we have that."

The candle flames leaned together, his and hers, two small yellow rooms agreeing on a shape. He looked at the copies, at the master's confessions rendered in his own careful miniature, and understood that the moment had arrived that all his rehearsals had been built to prevent. He could lie badly, as he had lied to Voss, and watch her stop trusting him; or he could tell the truth and put its weight into her hands, and she would carry it whether it crushed her or not, because Bette had never once set down anything she had agreed to hold.

"The atlas isn't only coastlines," he said. "The margins are — payments. Names. Master Marsh was buying silence, or being paid for it, or both, and I don't know which yet, and the magistrate wants the book in ten days, and if I give him the margins whole, they become evidence against—" He stopped himself from saying *us*. It was too late; he heard the word arrive anyway, unspoken, standing between them like a third person.

Bette pulled the stool from the corner and sat down across the desk from him, and took one of the thin sheets, and read it the way she read household accounts: fast, total, without mercy.

"Half the customs house," she said at last. "That's what this is. That's what Father's ruin bought, isn't it — not just debt. Silence."

"How do you know about—"

"I keep the books of this family, Wren. I've kept them since I was fourteen. You think I don't know what our name used to be worth?" She laid the sheet down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something that might go off. "You're copying these. Why? If you give Halloway the atlas, give it to him whole. Burn your copies. This—" she touched the papers "—this is the kind of thing that gets people found in ditches."

"Or it's the only thing that keeps us alive. Proof cuts both ways. Voss wants it. Halloway wants it. Whoever holds it holds something worth more than our lives, which means our lives are worth exactly as much as we can defend it." He heard the hysteria under his own reason and hated it. "I'm not going to burn the only weapon in the house."

"It isn't a weapon. Weapons are held by people who know how to fight. You're a mapmaker." Then she flinched at herself, because she knew — she had always known — that the sentence was unfair, and she reached across and gripped his wrist, ink-stained, cold from the night's work. "That came out wrong."

"No," Wren said. "It came out true. I'm a mapmaker. And I'm sitting in my dead master's chair forging his conscience onto stolen paper while a magistrate sharpens his requisition and a smuggler learns my walking hours. So perhaps you should say what you actually came to say, Bette, because I can hear you counting in the parlor at night, and I'd rather hear it aloud."

* * *

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the lane a dog gave one bark and thought better of it.

Bette withdrew her hand and sat back, and he watched her do what she always did when cornered: arrange the facts into an order she could survive. Her face in candlelight was their mother's face gone harder, the softness spent years ago on rent and flour and the particular humiliation of selling a house's contents room by room.

"There's money for one passage," she said.

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and he listened for the bottom and did not hear it strike.

"The Kestrel sails on the ninth. I've made inquiries — don't ask how, it doesn't matter — there's a berth, one berth, paid in advance, and the captain expects a passenger with a valuable cargo and no questions." Her voice stayed level the way a frozen pond stays level. "The atlas goes in my trunk. A finished coastal atlas of these waters, signed work, that's a credential anywhere abroad — a merchant house will take me on the strength of it, or at least negotiate. And the margins—" she tapped the copies "—stay here, or burn, whatever you decide, but they don't sail with me, because if I'm searched I'm a widow's daughter with her father's maps, and that's sad, and sad is safe."

"Bette."

"And you stay," she said, "because you stay, and here is why." She began to count on her fingers, and God help him, she actually counted on her fingers, the gesture so familiar it broke something loose in his chest. "One: Halloway's grievance, such as it is, is with the atlas and the master's secrets, and you're the obedient apprentice surrendering them on schedule — you're safest exactly where you are, visible, harmless. Two: the workshop, the press, the plates — that's your living and it can't be crated. Three: I can pass as a factor's sister; you cannot pass as anything but what you are, which is the last Eastwick in this county, and everyone knows his face." She folded her fingers closed. "Four. Because I decided it first, and I've been deciding it longer, and you've had three years of being brave and it's my turn."

"That isn't arithmetic," Wren said. His own voice sounded far away. "That's a speech."

"It's both. I'm good at both."

"The berth isn't yours."

Something crossed her face — fast, quickly mastered — and he seized it. "It isn't yours, is what I mean. You arranged silver sales for two berths. Don't deny it; I've heard the chime, I've weighed the shawl. Where's the second berth's money, Bette? Who's it paid to?"

The candles guttered together in a draft off the door she hadn't fully shut, and in the recovered light her jaw was set the way it set over unpaid bills.

"If there were two berths," she said carefully, "and there aren't, because you're staying—"

"If there were two," he insisted, "one of them was bought for me. And if one was bought for me, then you've spent Mother's silver on a plan you never asked me to agree to, which is exactly what Father did with the mortgage, and you know it, and it's why your hands are shaking."

She looked at her hands. They were shaking. She closed them into fists on her knees, and for a moment she was fourteen again, the year the letters from the bank began arriving, learning to make her body obey her while the world came apart on paper.

"Yes," she said. "There were two. Or there were going to be, before I did the sums honestly. Wren, listen to me — truly listen, because I've listened to you rehearse your little answers for three years through the wall and I have never once interrupted, so you owe me this." She leaned forward. "You cannot stay. Halloway summoned the atlas. Whatever he tells the Admiralty, he knows what the margins are, and once he has the original he has no further use for the apprentice who read them. Men like Halloway tidy. You are a loose thread. The berth was yours — the second berth, the real one — because you are the one who decoded the confession, you are the one whose testimony could hang him, you are the one who has to be somewhere he cannot reach. I am nobody. I am a woman who sells silver. Nobody hunts me. Nobody needs to."

"Nobody hunts you," Wren repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, ragged, half fury. "Nobody hunts you. Bette, our father was ruined to buy a dead man's silence. Our name was the payment. Do you think the men who spend families like ours lose the receipt? You sail alone to a foreign port with a stolen atlas and no protector, and you call yourself nobody?"

"I call myself free."

"And I call you my sister!" It came out louder than he meant, loud enough that he stopped and listened to the house, to the rain, to the dog that did not bark again. When he went on it was quieter, and worse. "You want to know why I've stayed three years? Not duty. Not the atlas. Fear, yes, all of that — but underneath, every night, the same sum you do in the parlor. If anything happens, it must happen to the one of us who started it. Master Marsh taught me the coasts; Master Marsh died of his own margins; I inherited his book and his debts and his enemies, and I will not hand them to you gift-wrapped in Mother's silver. You go because I say so. That's my price for everything else."

"And who says so for you?" Bette's eyes were bright and furious. "Who appointed you? You were fifteen. You were his apprentice, not his son, not his heir, not his accomplice — whatever he hid in those margins, he hid it *from* you, and you've spent three years paying for secrets you weren't told! That isn't inheritance, Wren, that's theft. They stole your life same as they stole Father's name, and now you want to finish the job yourself."

"At least it's mine to finish."

"Oh, that—" She stood, and the stool scraped, and the sound cracked the room open. "That is exactly what he would say. Exactly. Sit in the dark, take the blame, call it nobility. You learned devotion from a man who faked his own death, and you learned sacrifice from me, and look how well I've taught it — I sell our mother spoon by spoon and call it love!"

The words hung there. Neither of them moved. Outside the rain went on reading its interminable page, and the two candle flames stood very straight, as if ashamed.

It was, Wren thought absurdly, the first true fight they had had since childhood — since the year of the ruin, when they had learned to be gentle with each other the way survivors of a shipwreck are gentle, each carrying the other's water, neither mentioning the sea. Every quarrel since had been conducted in code: sighs, doors closed softly, kindness deployed like fortification. This — her standing white-knuckled among the inks, him with wet eyes he refused to acknowledge — this was honesty, and honesty between them felt like violence precisely because it was not.

"We're both going," he said finally.

"There's money for one."

"Then we find more. Fen. The smugglers' chapel — Marsh's people move passengers every week; there are channels I've mapped that Halloway's riders can't—"

"You'll walk into a marsh at night on the strength of lines you drew at a desk." But her voice had lost its edge; she was already, he could hear, doing the new sums, because Bette could no more stop calculating than he could stop copying. "And if the channels flood?"

"Then I'll have drawn them wrong," Wren said, "and I'll die knowing precisely where."

It startled a sound out of her — half sob, half laugh — and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and for a moment the old shape of them stood in the candlelight, the two children in the attic making a kingdom out of a broken barometer and their father's discarded charts.

Then she said, quietly, "Nine days, Wren. Whatever we decide, it has to be decided before the ninth."

* * *

She would not leave. She dragged the stool to the doorway and sat in it with her mending — of course, the eternal mending, shirtsleeves and hems, the flag of domesticity she flew over every crisis — and declared herself sentry. Any knock, any footstep on the lane, she would answer it and give him time to pocket the copies and look bored.

"You can't sit up all night," he said.

"I've done worse with less reason. Work."

So he worked. The clock in the parlor struck one, then two, and the candle was replaced twice, and the thin sheets multiplied on the drying rack behind the stove where the heat would not warp them. *Item — to the boatman R., ten pounds, delivered the night of the gale, for silence entire.* He copied that one twice, because his hand had blurred on the first attempt, though he told himself it was the smoke. The boatman. He kept circling that entry the way a tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth. Ten pounds for silence entire — and a boatman lost with his boat in the October gales, the same gales that took Tobias Marsh, and no body for either of them, and a family ashore that had gone suddenly, quietly poor.

He looked up once, past midnight, and saw Bette asleep upright in the stool, needle still pinched between finger and thumb, chin dropped to her breast, the mending pooled in her lap like something surrendered. In sleep her face was young again, and stubborn even in sleep, jaw set against dreams. He got up without noise, took the blanket from the press, and put it over her shoulders, and she did not wake, or pretended beautifully not to.

Back at the desk, he turned to the next margin page, and his eye went first to the last line of it, the way an eye goes to a wound.

The entry was dated the ninth of October, three years past. It named no sum. It said only, in Marsh's quick slanted hand, in letters Wren had traced a hundred times without reading:

*If I am not at the Kestrel, tell the boy the truth, and God forgive the cost of it.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, smaller, almost hidden in the fold of the margin, a single word:

*Ninth.*

The same date as the sailing. The same day, this year, that the Kestrel would swing at her moorings with one berth paid and Halloway's ten days running out — the ninth of October falling again like a debt come due.

Wren sat very still, and the rain read on, and downstairs the clock gathered itself and struck three.
