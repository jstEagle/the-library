## 10. The Smuggler's Chapel

The cipher in the margin of plate fourteen read, at first glance, like a depth sounding: *7 fathoms, black ground, chapel rock*. But the numerals were wrong for a chart — drawn too tall, too deliberate, each one shouldering into the space of its neighbor like men crowding a door. I had copied them three times onto scrap paper before I understood that they were not numbers at all, not entirely. They were letters wearing numbers' clothes, and when I finally stripped the disguise away, the words that came out were simple enough to stop my breath:

*Where the old bell hung. When the tide forgets the causeway.*

The old bell of Saint Maren's. Everyone in Ashmoor knew the story — the chapel swallowed by the dunes two centuries past, its tower left standing like a broken tooth until the storm of 1691 took that too. Only the foundations remained, out on the shingle beyond the saltmarsh, half a mile from anything, reachable twice a day when the tide pulled back its wet grey hem. A place where no honest person had reason to go.

Which was, I supposed, precisely the point.

I told Bette I was walking to check a bearing against the shoreline. She looked at me across the workshop table — at the ink stains to my elbows, the candle guttering into its fourth hour, the scrap papers I swept under my palm too slowly — and she did not believe me. Three years of shared grief had taught us both to read each other's silences fluently, and lately mine had been shouting. But she only said, "Take your oilskins. It'll spit rain before noon," and went back to her ledger, her pen moving through columns of figures with the grim tenderness of a woman counting what was left.

I took the oilskins. I did not tell her where the tide would take me.

* * *

The marsh path out to Saint Maren's was one I had drawn but never walked. That is a strange confession for a chartmaker to make, but it is the truth of my trade: I have stood on headlands and copied coastlines I will never touch, sounded depths from the safety of a boat's gunwale, traced rivers to their sources on other men's testimony. An atlas is mostly secondhand sight. My master used to say that a map was a promise made about a place by someone who might never keep it.

Walking the path now, I understood how much of the promise I had made in ignorance. The way wound through samphire and mud-glint, following a ridge of shingle I had rendered as a single confident line and which was in truth a treacherous spine of loose stone, slick with weed, dropping away on either side into channels the color of tarnished pewter. Twice I went in over the ankle. Twice I stood cursing quietly, cold water filling my boot, feeling the whole geography rearrange itself around me — the map in my head correcting itself with every step, patient and merciless.

The sky had done what Bette predicted. Rain came in fine and sideways off the sea, beading on the oilskin, blurring the horizon until water and air were the same grey substance and I walked through a world with no edges. Gulls argued somewhere out of sight. The tide was going out — the cipher had been precise about that too, *when the tide forgets the causeway*, and I had checked the almanac four times — and with every hour the sea drew back and laid bare more of the flats: ribbed sand, stranded weed, the white curved bones of boats long broken.

I smelled the chapel before I saw it. Old mortar and burnt wood, a smell like a hearth gone cold centuries ago but never quite forgotten by the stones. Then the fog shifted and there it was: the foundation walls of Saint Maren's rising perhaps four feet above the shingle, roofless, windowless, the flint black with wet. Grass grew from the joints of the masonry in small defiant tufts. Where the altar had been, someone had built a low platform of driftwood planks, and on it sat a barrel, a coil of rope, crates stenciled with names in languages I did not know. Smugglers' goods, cached between tides, stored in the one place the customs men would never think to look because the place was, officially, nothing at all.

A ruin. A nothing. A blank on every official chart.

Except, apparently, on mine.

"You're late," said a voice behind me, "and you walk like a surveyor."

I turned so fast I nearly went down on the wet stone. He was sitting in the lee of the north wall, where I would have sworn no one sat — a lean man of fifty-odd in a fisherman's jersey the color of the weather, mending a net with fingers that moved without his attention, the way a musician's do. His face was brown and creased as old sailcloth, and his eyes went over me the way mine went over a coastline: taking bearings, noting hazards.

"I walk," I said, when I had recovered my voice, "like someone who has never been here before."

"Aye." He tied off a knot. "That's what I said. Surveyor." He jerked his chin at my boots. "You picked the ridge line instead of the channel path. Only a man who'd read it off a chart does that. The channel path dries an hour earlier." He unrolled the net another arm's length. "Good chart, though. Whoever drew it knew the water. Knew it secondhand, mind. But knew it."

"Who are you?"

"Fen. Idris Fen." He said it the way other men give directions — no weight to it, no ceremony. "Sit down before you fall down, boy. Tide won't be back for three hours and we've less to say to each other than that, but more than you think."

I did not sit. My heart was going hard against my ribs, and my eyes kept going to the wall behind him, to the fog beyond the ruin, cataloguing exits the way he had catalogued me. "You know why I've come."

"I'd be surprised if you didn't." He set the net down at last and studied me properly, and something in his face altered — a softening, or a sorrowing; with weathered men the two are hard to tell apart. "You have his brow. Did you know that? Same stubborn shelf of bone above the eyes. Seen him stand just so, a hundred times, working out a problem nobody else could see."

My mouth had gone dry. The rain ticked on the oilskin hood. "Then he's alive."

"He's alive." Fen repeated it slowly, as if testing whether the word would bear weight. "Alive the way a gutted herring is fresh. Alive, and hiding in a place I'll not name, and dying — slow, boy, slow as a wick burning down — of a wound he took three years ago come Martinmas. The blade went in below the ribs and did its work badly, which is the only reason he's had three years to be sorry in." He picked up the net again, needing his hands busy. "He was meant to drown clean. Instead he got to rot."

* * *

We sat then, both of us, on a driftwood plank beneath the ghost of Saint Maren's tower, while the fog breathed around us and the flats gleamed and emptied toward the sea. And Idris Fen talked, in a flat unhurried voice, of the night Tobias Marsh died.

Or did not die.

"There were six of us that night, ferrying him out to where the Kestrel would pick him up. Halloway's inquiry had teeth in it by then, and Marsh knew — he'd seen the ledger pages, knew what was wrote against his name if he stayed. So we staged it. Boat swamped off the Point, body lost to the sea, all very sad, very proper. The boatman was to get thirty guineas and a new boat, and Marsh was to vanish, and everyone's troubles were to float away on the tide." Fen's hands had stopped on the netting. "Only the boatman talked. Not to us — to his wife, or his priest, or the bottle, I don't know which. But talk travels, and some of it traveled to a man who was paid to make sure it didn't. There was a knife, and there was a scuffle in the dark, and Marsh took the steel meant for the boatman. Took it and still went into the water, because stopping was death of a surer kind."

"And the boatman?"

Fen looked out at the flats. "What do you think? They can't have witnesses. His family got a drowning story too." He let that sit a moment. "You'll want to ask why your master didn't come back after. Why he let three years go by. I've asked him the same, and he gives an answer every time, and it's always the same answer dressed different clothes: that coming back would kill the boy. That the atlas had to be finished and delivered innocent, with no drowned man's shadow on it, or Halloway would take it apart board by board looking for what was hidden in it. That silence was protection." His jaw worked. "It's partly true. It's also what a proud man tells himself so he doesn't have to call his cowardice by its name. But don't mistake me—" and here he turned and fixed me with those chart-reading eyes— "he loves you, boy. Loves you like a lighthouse loves a ship it can't reach. He has watched you from a distance these three years. Paid Fen here to make sure the rent got found when times went thin. Never once let himself be seen. It near broke him, and he near deserved it."

The world had gone slightly unreal, the way it does in high fever. I heard myself ask about the letters. About the hand.

"He writes them himself. Right hand's failed him since the wound, so he's learned to write with his left — that's why it looks like a stranger imitating him, if you've been holding his drafts up to the light. Which you have, or I'm a Dutchman." Fen almost smiled. "He knows you'd check. He counted on you checking. Said you were the only one who would."

* * *

Then he reached inside his jersey and brought out the third letter.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, heavier than paper had any right to be — and when I cut the cord I found why. Inside the outer wrapping was a second packet: a sheet of good rag paper covered in that unmistakable hand, the backward-leaning letters of a man writing against his own habit, and beneath the letter, folded in quarters, a single removed leaf from a book — a ledger page, dense with figures and abbreviations, the ink faded to the color of dried blood.

"Read the letter first," Fen said. "Then the page. Then decide."

I read.

*Wren —*

*I write with my wrong hand, which is fitting, for I have lived three years by my wrongs. You have decoded the margins, or you would not be reading this at Saint Maren's, and so you know already that the atlas is not an atlas. Forgive an old man his disguises; I hid the confession in the only safe I ever trusted, which is the diligence of a careful apprentice.*

*The enclosed page is one of eleven. It names payments. Yours is named on it, though you will not have known it as payment — it wore the clothes of misfortune, as most of Halloway's work does. Read it. Be angry. You are entitled.*

*But here is what I ask, and I ask it knowing I have no right: bring the atlas itself. All of it. The margins in my hand are the key to everything, and keys must be kept together or they open nothing. Halloway has summoned it — Fen will tell you I know this, for little moves on this coast that I do not know — and once it sits in the Admiralty's hands, the truth in it is dead forever, filed and forgotten, and the men it condemns go free.*

*I am dying, Wren. Slowly, but surely, and I find I mind less than I expected, provided the thing outlives me in the right hands.*

*Bring it to me. Trust Fen on the road. He has earned far worse than trust and repaid it better than most Christians manage.*

*Your master, who owes you more than a letter can carry,*
*T. Marsh*

I read it twice. Then I unfolded the ledger page with hands that did not feel like mine.

The entries ran down the page in a clerk's neat column: dates, sums, initials. Most of the names meant nothing to me — men in the customs house, presumably, bought and entered like cargo. Near the bottom, dated a month after my father's ruin, I found the Eastwick entry. Eleven pounds paid to one T. Calloway, solicitor, *for manufacture of debt against E., per instruction.* Manufacture. The word sat on the page in plain ink, ordinary as any other, and I read it four times and felt nothing, and then felt everything at once, so that the fog seemed to roar.

Below it, in my master's left-handed script, someone — Marsh himself, later, annotating — had added: *They broke the father to buy the son's silence. E. does not know he has been on the payroll since he was nineteen.*

"Wren." Fen's voice came from very far away and very close. "Boy. Breathe."

I breathed. The flats swam back into focus, grey and glistening, indifferent. Somewhere out toward the channel a bell buoy clanged twice, mournful and mechanical, keeping its own faithful count of the water.

"The atlas," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "He wants the atlas brought here."

"He does."

"Halloway has summoned it. Ten days. If it doesn't arrive, he comes for the workshop, for me, for Bette." I was thinking aloud, thinking with my voice, the way I used to in the workshop while Marsh bent over his drafting table pretending not to listen. "And if it does arrive, it goes into the Admiralty's vault, and everything hidden in it dies there quietly. Either way the margins are lost unless—"

"Unless they're delivered to the one man who knows what they're worth and means to spend them right." Fen gathered his net into his lap, coiling it with those blind sure hands. "That's the bargain he's offering. I know how it looks from where you stand — he asks, and you risk, same as always, and he stays safe in his hole. I won't pretend otherwise. He's not the saint you grieved for. He's something rarer and harder to bury: a man who knows exactly what he is."

The rain had thickened. Out on the flats, the light was changing — the tide beginning its long stealthy return, the channels filling from their far ends first, the way they do, quietly cutting off the paths one by one. I thought of the chart in my head, the confident single line of the ridge, and how much of the promise it had made that I had never kept. I thought of Bette at her ledger, counting silver into secret sums. I thought of a man learning to write with his ruined side, alone, for three years, so that his apology would arrive in a hand I would recognize.

"He's really dying," I said. It was not a question, but Fen answered it anyway.

"Dying," he said. "And asking."

I stood. Down the shingle, the first of the returning water slid across the path I'd walked in on, thin and silver and final, and I understood that whatever I decided, I would have to decide it soon — that the sea itself was closing the options behind me, tide by tide, the way Halloway closed his polite traps, the way my whole life had narrowed without my noticing into a corridor between two doors.

"Tell him—" I started, and stopped, because everything I could say was too large for the words I had. I tried again. "Tell him I'll bring it."

Fen nodded once, unsurprised, and looked back down at his net. "Three nights hence. Tide serves at moonrise. Follow the channel path this time, not the ridge — and boy?" He glanced up, and for a moment the flatness fell away from his face entirely. "Whatever you're carrying besides the atlas — whatever's in your heart against him — bring that too. He'll want to see the weight of it. It's the only inheritance he's got left to leave you."

I was halfway along the shingle before the full meaning of what I had agreed to caught up with me — the atlas, the evidence, the thing Halloway wanted and the Admiralty had summoned, carried out across the marsh in a chartmaker's satchel to a drowned man in a hole in the earth. Ten days. One chance. And somewhere behind me, in the fog-bound town, a magistrate who noticed everything would notice a workshop emptied of its masterpiece.

The bell buoy clanged again, closer now, as the water rose.

Three nights, I thought, and walked faster, and did not look back at the chapel where my dead master waited to become alive.
