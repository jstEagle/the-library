## 11. What Master Wanted

The night Tobias Marsh died, the sea was behaving exactly as his charts said it would.

He had always taken a small, unseemly pride in that — in knowing the water off the Kentish coast the way other men knew their wives' tempers, its sulks and sudden generosities, the sandbanks that shifted a cable's length in a hard winter and then pretended they had never moved at all. On the night of the ninth of October, 1744, the tide stood at half-ebb and the wind came round to the south-west with rain folded inside it like a letter inside a coat. The *Gannet*, a lugger he had borrowed from men who would want it back, sat low in the roads off Dungeness with her lamp swinging at the stern, and forty yards out the water over the Shingle Reach was doing precisely what Plate Fourteen of his atlas promised it would do: breaking white in two places, slack and treacherous, over a bank that appeared on no Admiralty chart because he had never sold them the truth.

He stood on the shingle in the dark with his hat in his hands and let the rain have his hair, and he thought, absurdly, of ink.

Of how a man's whole reputation could be reduced to the pressure of a nib against paper. His hand was known from Plymouth to the Texel — customs men, shipwrights, half the hydrographers of Europe could pick a Marsh sounding out of a drawer by its confidence alone, the way the numbers marched across the vellum like soldiers who trusted their officer. He had spent thirty-one years building that hand. Tonight he intended to spend it.

"Mr. Marsh." The voice came out of the dark to his left, low and unhappy. Idris Fen, wrapped in oilskin that shone like a seal's back, rain running off the brim of his hat in a beaded curtain. "Tide won't wait for your thinking."

"The tide," Marsh said, "will do what I tell it. That's rather the point of me."

Fen did not laugh. Fen was not a man who laughed on nights when boats were about to burn. He had done this work too long — the moving of goods past men who believed themselves entitled to them, the quiet arithmetic of coves and moonless calendars — and he knew better than most which nights the sea kept receipts for. "Boatman's aboard already," he said. "The one you sent for. He's asking after his money again."

"He'll have it."

"He's asking what it's *for*."

Marsh put his hat on. The rain drummed once, twice, and went quiet inside the felt. "He's being paid not to know," he said. "That is the entire shape of the arrangement, Idris. Go and light your candle."

* * *

The boatman's name was Tam Hobb, and he had a laugh like a bucket of loose gravel and a wife in Hythe who believed he was at Rye buying rope. Marsh had chosen him for three reasons, and he recited them to himself now as the lugger stood away from the shingle with her lamp burning bright and unnecessary: Hobb was greedy enough not to ask questions that might reduce his fee; Hobb was stupid enough to believe a story told to him with money in it; and Hobb owed Fen for a cargo gone astray the previous spring, which meant he would sail where Fen said sail and say nothing afterward out of the particular shame of a man in debt.

Three reasons. Marsh, who dealt all day in soundings and bearings, in measurements that could be checked against the world, had built a plan out of three reasons that were not measurements at all but hopes wearing arithmetic's clothes. He would think of that often in the years to come, in the bad hours, in the wet dark before dawn: that he had been a careful man his whole life and had spent his carelessness all in one night, on a boatman with gravel in his throat.

The plan itself was simple, as the best lies are. The *Gannet* would run in toward the Reach under full lamp, the way an honest vessel runs when her master wants to be seen wanting shelter. At the mark, she would strike the bank — gently, gently; Fen knew how to kiss a hull against sand without waking the timber — and in the confusion of the grounding, with the lamp swinging and the sea coming white over the shoal, a man would go into the water. A man would drown. The coastguard would find the wreck at dawn, and the lugger's owners would get their hull back, and the Customs would enter into their ledger the name of Tobias Marsh, hydrographer, suspected smuggler, lost with his vessel off the Shingle Reach, no body recovered but then the sea seldom provides.

No body. That was the hinge of everything. Halloway wanted him dead or discredited, and either answer served so long as the atlas stayed out of the magistrate's hands. But a drowned man could not be questioned, could not be tried, could not be made to give up under a slow fire the names of every man from Dover to the Orwell who had ever moved a cask past the revenue. A drowned man's silence was total. And silence, Marsh had decided, was the only inheritance he could leave his apprentices that Halloway could not confiscate.

There were two apprentices. He thought of them now, standing in the stern sheets with the spray coming over cold as coins: the girl first, because the girl frightened him more — Bette Eastwick, who kept accounts in a hand cleaner than his own and asked questions with the patience of a woman who knew the answers would come eventually. And then the boy. Wren. Seventeen that autumn, with ink permanently under his nails and a way of looking at a coastline as though it were speaking to him personally and he must not interrupt until it finished. Marsh had taught him for four years and had never once, in all that time, managed to teach him doubt. The boy believed that if a thing were drawn truly — if the lines were honest, the soundings faithful, the margins exact — then the drawing would protect itself.

Tonight Marsh was going to test that belief with the boy's whole life.

"Mark!" Fen's voice forward, sharp. "Mark and stand by!"

The *Gannet* ran at the bank with her lamp streaming in the wind, and Marsh watched the white water rise up ahead of them like a held breath, and he did the last honest work of his public life: he read the sea, and the sea read true, and Plate Fourteen was correct in every particular.

They struck. Gently. The hull sighed along the sand and stopped, and the lugger lay over at her ease like a woman pretending to faint, and Fen's men were already moving — the boat over the side, the lamp swung wild, a cry raised in the dark that any watcher ashore would take for a drowning man's. Marsh stripped his coat. Underneath, tied flat against his ribs in oilcloth, he carried the things he would need on the far side of his death: three hundred guineas in mixed coin, letters of introduction written in hands that were not his, and one page only — torn from the atlas, the margin cipher intact — because even a dead man needed one key.

"Your money's in the boat," Fen said, not looking at him. "Hobb's holding it."

"I know."

"He'll talk, Mr. Marsh. Sooner or later, a man like that talks."

Marsh looked at him then. Fen's face in the lamplight was old leather and rain, and there was something in it Marsh had not expected and had no time to examine — something that was almost pleading, as though Fen had seen the shape of the future lying along the boatman's shoulders and wished to warn it away.

"Then I'll owe him another fee," Marsh said, and went over the side into the black water, and died.

* * *

Dying, it turned out, took three years and counting, and was the hardest work he had ever done.

The first year he spent north, in a rented room above a chandler's in Leith, growing a grey in his beard that age alone had not yet earned, learning to sign himself *Nathaniel Cobb* with a hand that trembled slightly, as a stranger's would. He wrote no letters. That had been the covenant with himself: the dead do not write. Every instinct of thirty years rebelled — the habit of correspondence was in him like marrow, the daily web of notes and instructions and gentle corrections by which he had run his workshop, his apprentices, his whole small empire of paper. Twice he drafted a line to Wren and burned both drafts in the chandler's stove. *Your Plate Nine runs shallow at the western mark.* Even now he could see the error; he dreamed of it sometimes, that little arrogance of a young hand trusting its eye over its lead line.

But a letter was a wake. Letters left trails — dates, postmarks, servants who remembered faces. Halloway's people opened anything that smelled of Marsh, and Halloway's people were patient, and somewhere in the town of Rye a boy and his sister were living under the shadow of the atlas, and the atlas was the one thing that must never be traced back to its maker while the magistrate still drew breath and salary. So long as Marsh was dead, the atlas was an orphan — valuable, suspect, but orphaned. Let it be finished by the apprentice. Let it carry no hand but Wren's. Then it was a young man's honest work, and Halloway could turn its pages forever and find nothing in them but England's coast, beautifully rendered, and no proof of anything at all.

That was the story Tobias Marsh told himself, in Leith and later in Flushing and later still in a room over a chapel ruin within an hour's walk of the shop he could not enter. It was a good story. It had the great advantage of being true, in the way that charts are true — accurate in every sounding, and silent about everything else.

Because there were days — and the days multiplied as the years went, like cells in warm milk — when he understood perfectly well that the atlas did not need him dead. Only his confession did.

* * *

He went down to Rye the first time in the spring of 1745, telling himself it was operational necessity: he must know whether Halloway's interest in the workshop had cooled, whether the summons had come, whether the boy needed warning. He stood in the Saturday market among baskets of sprats and wore a fishmonger's old jersey and heard his own name spoken twice, once in grief and once in gossip, and neither speaker looked at the stooped man in the jersey buying oysters he had no intention of eating.

From the corner of the churchyard he could see the upper window of the workshop, the one above the drying press. A candle burned there past midnight. He knew without seeing who tended it.

In the second year he learned the household's rhythm the way he had once learned tides. Monday, market; Tuesday, the girl walked to Winchelsea with letters and came back with news and no expression at all, which meant the news was bad, because Bette Eastwick carried good fortune openly, like a lantern. Wednesday, the boy went down to the quay with the finished plates wrapped in canvas, courting subscribers, and Marsh once got near enough — near enough to smell the tar and the wet stone, near enough to hear Wren's voice lifted in that particular earnestness he used on strangers, as though convincing them of the coast's importance were a matter of personal honor — near enough to touch him.

He did not touch him. He stood behind the fish crates with his heart going like a hammer in a dropped chest, and he watched his apprentice sell the atlas plate by plate to men who would never know what the book had cost, and he thought: *this is protection.* And then he thought, in the next heartbeat, in the small cold voice that had begun keeping its own ledger: *this is cowardice wearing protection's coat, and you know the difference, Tobias, you have always known the difference.*

A man protects what he loves by standing between it and harm. What Marsh did was stand at a distance with a spyglass, taking bearings on his own conscience, and calling the readings safety.

And yet — the third reason, the one he admitted only on the worst nights, when the wind came off the flats carrying the smell of the marshes and he could not sleep for the sound of water — there was the matter of Tam Hobb.

* * *

It had gone wrong within a fortnight, the way he had always known in some chamber of himself that it would.

Hobb had taken his fee — twenty guineas to row the lamp-boat and swear to the drowning — and Hobb had talked. Not much. Not immediately. But a boatman flush with strange money buys rounds, and rounds invite questions, and by November there were men in the Rye taverns who had heard that Tam Hobb had been paid gold for a night's work the night the *Gannet* went down, and some of those men drank with customs riders. Halloway heard it by Christmas. What Halloway did with it, Marsh learned through Fen's network slowly, in installments, each worse than the last: Hobb arrested in February; Hobb released — why released? — because Halloway did not want a trial, he wanted leverage; Hobb turned, quietly, into the magistrate's creature, his debts bought, his freedom conditional, his wife's kin suddenly subject to inspections of their premises whenever the revenue grew bored. A man unmade by inches, at leisure, officially untouched.

And Marsh had paid him for it. Had counted the guineas out into that gravel laugh of a hand and called it wages. He had known — he *had known*, he could not plead the darkness of the night, he had stood on the shingle with the rain in his collar and chosen the cheapest instrument available — that Hobb was greedy and stupid and owned, and men who are owned are spent. He had written the boatman into the plan the way one writes a depth onto a chart: a number, a necessity, nothing personal. And the number had drowned instead of him.

That was the confession. Not the payments — Halloway had half the customs house in his ledger already and needed Marsh's pages only to hang the other half. No. What the margins held, what Marsh had written into the atlas in cipher because he could not bear to write it plain and could not bear to destroy it, was the account of Hobb. The fee. The lie. The slow ruin. Thirty years of soundings, and the truest chart he ever drew was of the depth of his own cowardice, and he had hidden it in the margins of a masterpiece where only his best student would ever think to look.

Why hide it rather than burn it? He asked himself that too, in Flushing, in Leith, in the chapel dark. Because it was evidence? Because it might someday be needed against Halloway?

No. Because a man who has spent his life making things permanent cannot bear to make his own guilt temporary. Because if the page burned, then the drowning had been only strategy, and the boatman only expense, and Tobias Marsh only a survivor — and he knew better. The page existed so that someone, sometime, would know the truth of him. He had simply lacked the courage to deliver it while alive.

So he had left it to Wren. To the boy who believed honest lines protected themselves. Marsh had watched him for three years and told himself: *let him find it, let him read it, let him judge me — that is what an heir is for.*

It was a beautiful arrangement. It protected everyone. It required only that a seventeen-year-old boy pay the freight of a grown man's sin, carry it through the same waters, and never be asked whether he consented to the voyage.

Marsh saw the toll of it from his distances. Saw the boy's face go grey over the winter of 1746, saw the silences grow up between Wren and his sister like a second wall around the house, saw the apprentice begin to rehearse his answers before questions were asked — he recognized the habit; it had been his own, once, before Halloway taught it to him. He watched the boy learn distrust the way one watches a child learn to limp: with grief, and without intervening, because intervention would cost more than the injury.

Protection. He still used the word. He found, in the third autumn, that he had to say it aloud to himself some mornings, quickly, before the meaning dried up.

* * *

On the night the decision finally broke in him — though he would pretend for weeks afterward that it had been made gradually, sensibly, in response to intelligence about Halloway's requisitions — Marsh sat in the ruined chapel above the marsh paths with Fen's candle guttering between them, and heard the news that changed the arithmetic.

"Halloway's summoned the atlas," Fen said. "Requisition papers, Admiralty seal, ten days' grace. My cousin's boy clerks in the customhouse yard. He saw the schedule go up on the board."

Marsh closed his eyes. Behind them, with terrible clarity, he saw the workshop: the boy bending over the final plate, the girl's ledgers open on the other table, the two of them holding the whole edifice of their lives together with tallow light and stubbornness — and the magistrate's polite hand descending, and the atlas passing out of their keeping into the machine that would strip its margins, break its cipher — Halloway had cryptographers; Halloway had time — and hang half the coast with its testimony, beginning, always beginning, with whoever was nearest the ink.

"They'll question the apprentices," Marsh said. "Formally, this time. Not courtesy calls. Under the requisition they're witnesses of record."

"Aye." Fen fed the candle a length of wick. "And there's this besides. Voss has been asking after them. Callum Voss, that served under you before you pensioned him off. He's telling anyone who'll buy it that you're alive and he can prove it, and he's offering the margins to Halloway's people for a pardon."

The name went into Marsh like a sounding lead. Voss. Voss whom he had removed from the network not for incompetence but for cruelty, quietly, with a purse and a reference, the way one rehomes a dog that has bitten a child — and Voss had nursed the insult three years into a religion. Of course he had. Marsh had spent the man's grievance as cheaply as he'd spent the boatman's honesty. Everyone he had ever tried to protect the atlas from, it seemed, he had first manufactured.

"There's a brig," Fen said. "The *Kestrel*, out of Flushing, master's a Dutchman who owes me. She lifts on the spring tide, nine days out. There's berth money enough for one passage, maybe two if we stretch. For the boy and the girl, I mean." He paused, and old leather face showed the pleading again, the warning he had tried to give on the shingle three years ago. "One berth clean, Mr. Marsh. Two if we're lucky and quick."

Marsh looked at the candle flame for a long time. Nine days. Ten days until the requisition fell due — the two clocks set against each other like gears, and somewhere inside the meshing of them, a boy and his sister, each of whom had spent three years silently deciding the other deserved salvation more.

He thought of Wren at seventeen, inking a coastline he had never doubted. He thought of the letters he had not written, the drafts burned in the Leith stove, the three years of spyglass love. He thought of Tam Hobb, walking free through Hythe with a magistrate's hand in his collar, paying every day for a lie Tobias Marsh had purchased at the price of a coat.

"No," Marsh said.

Fen waited.

"Not the boy. Not the girl. Not yet, and not by my ordering — they've been ordered about by dead men long enough." He straightened, and felt his damaged side protest, the wound from the old night that had never wholly closed, ticking like a slow clock of its own. "Write to the *Kestrel*'s master. Pay the berth. One berth, anonymous, held under a name that isn't theirs and isn't mine." He was aware of Fen watching him write the fiction in the air, and understood that Fen already knew whose hand would guide whichever Eastwick finally took the passage, and pitied him for it.

"And the letters?" Fen said carefully. "The ones you've been drafting and burning these three years? You'll send one now?"

Marsh looked at the candle. Outside, the marsh wind moved through the broken chapel windows, and somewhere below, in the dark, the channels he had charted and never walked ran silver toward a sea that kept all receipts.

"One letter," he said. "Signed with a drowned man's name. It's the only honest signature I've got left."
