## 12. The Berth

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded into a loaf of bread the way debts and secrets always came into the Eastwick house — hidden inside something meant to nourish.

Bette found it because she found everything. Three years of keeping the household solvent had taught her that nothing in a house went unnoticed by the person who counted its candles. She had learned the weight of every coin that crossed the threshold, the exact number of spoons in the drawer, which floorboards complained under Wren's restless pacing at two in the morning. She knew he had not slept properly since the letters began arriving, and she knew he thought she didn't know.

She unwrapped the paper from the bread in the chandler's back room where she bought flour on credit she was slowly making good. It was a single sheet, no salutation, written in a cramped seaman's hand that smelled faintly of tar.

*The brig Kestrel clears the outer roads on the ninth day of June, tide at dawn. One berth paid. Ask no names at the quay; ask for the man with the blue lantern.*

There was no signature. There did not need to be one. Bette stood very still among the sacks of meal and heard her own heart counting, the way it had counted through her father's ruin, through her mother's dying, through every winter when the coal ran short before the cold did.

One berth.

She read it again, and this time she smiled — a small, private thing, fierce as a struck flint — and folded the paper small enough to disappear into her glove.

Because she had already done the arithmetic. She had been doing it for months, in fact, in the ledger she kept locked inside her own skull where no magistrate and no brother could read it. The household accounts were a fiction maintained for Wren's benefit: *flour, coal, candle-tallow, mending.* The true ledger ran beneath it like water under ice. And the true ledger said, with brutal clarity, what flight cost. Passage out of England on a ship that asked no questions: eleven pounds, perhaps twelve if the captain was greedy. A trunk that would pass inspection. Clothes without mending visible at the seams. Food for the crossing. Sixteen pounds all told, seventeen to be safe.

Seventeen pounds was more than the Eastwicks had seen at once since their father died. But it was not impossible. Not for a woman who had spent three years learning exactly what a family's possessions weighed when sold by weight and not by grief.

She had started with the least-loved things, the way you start a fast with bread and save the meat. A sugar dish with a dent no guest had ever remarked upon. Two tablespoons monogrammed with initials that belonged to no one living. Her mother's second-best cream jug — never the first-best; some things were load-bearing walls in a house made of memory. Then, as spring ripened toward summer, she had gone further down the list than she had ever believed her courage would carry her. The fish forks. The salt cellars shaped like shells. Last week, in a silversmith's shop off Fish Street where the scales hung like gallows in the window, she had laid her mother's grape shears on the counter and felt the loss go through her like a needle finding bone.

Forteen pounds, nine shillings, and threepence. Wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her grey wool skirt, riding against her hip everywhere she went like a second heartbeat.

It was almost enough. By the ninth of June it would be entirely enough. And now here was the Kestrel, arriving like an answer to a question she had only dared to whisper into her pillow — one berth, paid, waiting.

*By whom*, though? That was the splinter in her thumb of a thought. Who paid for a berth and sent word through a loaf of bread to a house that was watched — she had seen the man across the lane twice now, the one who read the same page of his newspaper for an hour — and who asked for no repayment?

Wren, she thought first. But Wren had no money she couldn't account for, and whatever else her brother was, he was incapable of concealing an expenditure. He confessed to buying ribbons. He had once apologized for a week over a shilling lost through a hole in his coat.

The master, then. Marsh, risen from his grave in the shipping lists, paying passage for the family of the apprentice who finished his atlas. That made a certain grim sense. Debts among the dead were still debts.

Or it was something worse, and the berth was bait, and the blue lantern would turn out to burn for Halloway's men. Bette turned this possibility over the way she turned coins to check for clipping, and set it down beside the other, and refused to let either one rule her. Caution was a currency too, and she had learned to spend it precisely, not hoard it. Whoever had paid, whoever waited — she would go to the quay early, watch the man with the blue lantern from a distance, and judge him the way she judged every tradesman who came to her door: by his hands, his boots, and how he looked at her when he thought she wasn't looking.

But she would go ready. And she would say goodbye to her life in the only language that life understood: quietly, usefully, without alarming anyone.

* * *

She began that same evening with the garden.

It was a poor scrap of ground behind the workshop, more brick shadow than soil, but her mother had coaxed roses from it once — three bushes, two long dead, one stubborn survivor that bloomed white every July like an act of defiance. Bette knelt beside it in the last light and pruned it harder than she had ever dared, cutting back the leggy growth, baring the old wood, doing the thing you did in autumn to make a rose survive its winter.

"You'll kill it," Wren said from the doorway. He had ink to the second knuckle and shadows to the second eyelid, and he was watching her with that expression he'd worn lately, the one that seemed to be looking at her from across a wide river.

"No," Bette said. "I'm saving it. There's a difference." She snapped a stem and held it up. "Hard pruning looks like cruelty. It isn't."

He gave her the ghost of a smile and went back inside, and she stayed on her knees a while longer, pressing her palms flat against the cooling earth, memorizing its grain. Nine days. She would not see the white bloom. Someone else would, some other tenant or none, and the rose would go on being stubborn without her, which was, she supposed, the entire point of pruning.

After supper — she cooked it herself, refusing on this night to let any hand but hers touch anything — she took up the mending basket and worked her way through it item by item. Wren's shirts, darned at the elbows where he leaned on drafting tables. His good coat, brushed and hung where the moths couldn't reason it out. The patchwork quilt from the bed they had shared as children, every square of which held somebody's dead dress or dead ambition, stitched flat and made warm again. She mended the tear along its border that had been there since her mother died, using thread she matched by candlelight until her eyes ached, and when she finished she spread it over the chair and looked at it a long time.

"Bette." Wren's voice, careful. "You've been quiet."

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

"About whether you've been eating at that desk instead of coming to table." She said it lightly, and watched him take the deflection the way he took all her deflections now — gratefully, guiltily, glad to be let past the question without having to answer a deeper one. They had become fluent in each other's evasions, she and Wren, two people speaking the same mother tongue of silence. It occurred to her, folding the quilt's corner just so, that this fluency was the loneliest thing about the house. When he came home with secrets in his pockets she stopped asking after the first lie, because asking twice would break something between them that she needed whole for nine more days.

Later, when he had gone up to bed — or up to pretend to bed while he worked at whatever it was that kept his candle burning past midnight — Bette sat in the kitchen with her mother's prayer book, the one book in the house that was hers alone, and read the collects for travelers. *Preserve those that travel; bring them to the haven where they would be.* She read them aloud in a whisper, learning them by heart, because there would be no book where she was going, or if there was a book it would be an atlas, and atlases were maps of havens, weren't they, and God was entitled to His metaphors.

She wrote nothing down. That was the discipline of it. Goodbyes made solid became evidence.

* * *

On the fourth day she visited Mrs. Tarrant.

Mrs. Tarrant had been her mother's friend, which meant she was now less a person than an archive of Bette's childhood, and she received visitors in a parlor smelling of lavender and slow decay, where the afternoon light fell in bars like something escaped from a cage. They drank tea. They discussed the price of worsted. And then Bette said, apropos of nothing, "Do you remember teaching me to fold a letter properly? I was seven. I got the crease wrong and you said a badly folded letter tells the recipient what sort of person sent it before they read a word."

"I remember you crying over it."

"I remember you saying it. I think of it every time I write to anyone." Bette turned her cup on its saucer, aligning the handle with the painted flower, an exercise in steadiness. "I want you to have the rose cuttings. From Mother's bush. I've layered two runners and they'll be ready to lift within the month."

Mrs. Tarrant's face performed the small confusions of the aging — surprise, then suspicion, then the soft settling of a woman who had buried enough friends to know what certain sentences meant. "You're giving me her roses."

"If you'll have them."

"They'll do better in my ground anyway. Yours is all brick and grievance." But the old woman's eyes stayed on her, sharp under the softness. "Bette Eastwick. Is something wrong?"

"Everything is wrong. Everything has been wrong for three years. The roses are simply roses." Bette smiled and meant it, which surprised her. "Take them. Think of me when they bloom."

"And when will they bloom, in your estimation?"

"July," Bette said. "They always manage July."

She left via the churchyard, as she often did, and stood a while at her parents' stones, where the lichen had colonized the carved letters so that her father's name was becoming weather itself. She had rehearsed speeches here at midnight over the years — explanations, accusations, apologies — and delivered none of them aloud, on the theory that the dead heard enough and owed her nothing. Today she only said, quietly, "I'm taking the silver. You'd tell me to. I'm leaving him safe, or I'm leaving him free; I haven't decided which, and I don't believe I get to choose."

The yew tree dropped its shade across the stones like a hand closing a book.

On the fifth day she gave away the sewing box. On the sixth, her mother's recipe book went to the neighbor girl who wanted to learn preserves, and Bette walked her through the plum cheese recipe personally, hands guiding hands, so the knowledge would survive the parting of the paper. Each evening she inventoried the oilcloth packet against her hip. Fourteen pounds and change, and the last few items — the cream jug, God forgive her, and the little pepper box shaped like an acorn — would close the gap by Sunday.

And each evening Wren grew stranger, and quieter, and watched her with that river-crossing look, and once — she was nearly sure of it — she caught him starting to say something to her and swallowing it whole. His jaw worked around it like a man chewing glass. She pretended not to see. There is a kind of love that consists entirely of pretending not to see.

* * *

On the seventh day, the knot pulled tight.

She had gone to Fish Street to sell the acorn pepper box — the last of the silver excepting only the cream jug, which she had decided, in a fit of sentiment she couldn't afford, to leave behind on the mantel for Wren, a small monument to the idea that someone had loved this house once — and she was crossing back by the harbor road when she saw the notice pasted to the customs house wall, fresh paste still gleaming at the corners.

*SAILING INTELLIGENCE.* A column of vessels, ports, tides. Her eye went down it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

*Kestrel, brig, 84 tons, Fenwick master. For Lisbon and parts south. Clears the roads 9th June.*

Lisbon. South. Away. The words rang in her like a struck bell, and for one whole breath she felt pure joy, uncomplicated, a child's joy — and then she saw the man standing at the corner of the customs house, reading the same notices, and recognized the posture if not the face: shoulders squared to the wall, eyes angled to catch reflections in the window glass opposite. The watcher from the lane. Here. At the sailing lists. On the same morning she had come.

Coincidence was a coin she had stopped accepting at face value years ago.

She did not hurry. Hurrying was a signature on a confession. She walked home at market pace, bought onions she didn't need, greeted the fishwife by name, and all the while her mind was laying courses of brick: *he knows the Kestrel's date; they know; Halloway knows; or Voss knows — Voss, whose name Wren says in his sleep sometimes, she has heard it through the floor, low and bitter like a stone turning over.*

At home she found Wren asleep at his desk, cheek on a half-finished chart, pen still in his fingers, ink drying on his skin like a bruise. She stood in the doorway and looked at him a long moment — twenty-three years old and gray with work, guardian of a dead man's atlas, keeper of whatever it was the margins said, drowning in the same house as her and calling it dry land.

She could wake him. She could shake him and say *there is a berth on the Kestrel and I have fourteen pounds sewn in my skirt and we are going to settle tonight who goes and how.* She opened her mouth to do it.

Then she saw the letter lying open on the desk beside his elbow — a new one, sealed with wax she didn't recognize, the handwriting on it elegant and wrong, and even upside down she caught the phrase *the chapel* — and she understood that whatever current was pulling her brother under had deepened overnight, and that if she showed him her secret now he would do what Wren always did: he would try to solve it, and he would fail to sleep, and he would walk straight into whatever was waiting at that quay with his chin up and his honesty showing, and honest men at quays ended in press gangs and prison holds.

No. Her plan required only one thing of her: that she board first and explain after. Wren forgave everything afterward. That was his nature. It was also, she thought fondly and furiously, his flaw.

She covered him with the patchwork quilt, the one she had mended stitch by stitch for exactly this kind of cold night in June, and he murmured something — a name, maybe two — and did not wake.

That night she sat by the banked fire and took out the oilcloth packet and counted the money one final time, coin by coin, letting each one speak its history as it passed through her fingers. The dented sugar dish. The nameless spoons. The grape shears that had cut nothing but celebration for forty years. Fourteen pounds, sixteen shillings, threepence, and tomorrow the cream jug would stay on the mantel and the pepper box's six shillings would make it fifteen, two, and three, and the man with the blue lantern would take it or he would not, and on the ninth day of June, at dawn, she would stand on the shingle with her mother's prayer book learned by heart and her brother's whole future unspent behind her.

"One berth," she whispered to the embers, and the embers breathed and settled, and somewhere above her Wren slept inside her mended quilt with his secrets in his fists.

Nine days. She began composing the letter she would send him from the first safe port — beginning, in her mind, with the crease folded perfectly, telling him before he read a word what sort of person had sent it.

She only wished she knew whose hand had paid for the berth. Because whoever it was — the drowned man, the stranger, the magistrate himself — had set a place at a table she hadn't known existed, and in Bette's experience, which was considerable, no one bought a seat for an Eastwick without expecting change.
