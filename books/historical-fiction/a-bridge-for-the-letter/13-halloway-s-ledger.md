## 13. Halloway's Ledger

The ledger told him before the boy did.

Magistrate Ambrose Halloway kept three ledgers in his office at the Customs House, and only one of them was honest about being a lie. The first was the official volume, bound in calf, its entries written in his clerk's tidy hand — tonnage, duties, seizures, the respectable arithmetic of the port. The second was the private one, locked in the drawer beneath his right hand, where the true accounts lived: who paid him, how much, and for what silence. The third ledger had no binding at all. It was kept in his memory, in the place where other men kept their consciences, and it recorded only debts that could never be entered anywhere — debts of ruin, of reputation, of men who had drowned on schedule and yet somehow continued to breathe.

It was the third ledger that woke him before dawn that October morning, nagging at him like a tooth gone bad.

He rose, dressed without waking his wife, and walked down through the sleeping town with his collar turned up against the river fog. The lamplighters were still making their rounds; one of them touched his hat, and Halloway nodded back with the particular courtesy he extended to all men whose names he might one day need. The Customs House rose out of the mist ahead of him, grey stone going silver at the eaves, and he let himself in with his own key because he had never trusted a door he could not open alone.

He lit the lamp, sat, and unlocked the drawer.

Not the private ledger. He knew that book by heart, entry by entry, the way a priest knows his catechism. No — what he wanted this morning was the thin bundle tied in ribbon that lay beneath it: the copies. For eleven years he had employed copyists, discreet men, to transcribe documents that passed through hands worth watching. Bills of lading. Letters of instruction. And once, three years ago now, a set of working papers recovered from a drowned man's boat — papers that had turned out to be far more interesting than coastlines.

Tobias Marsh had been careful. Halloway granted him that even in death, or whatever stood in death's place. The master cartographer had written his secrets not in any code the Admiralty kept books on, but in the margins of his own work — small annotations, corrections, sailing directions that looked like sailing directions to anyone who did not know Marsh's habit of reversing his abbreviations. Halloway had spent four months and forty pounds learning to read them. What they contained had been worth every shilling: dates, sums, names. Payments routed through the customs house. His payments. Arranged by Marsh as the price of the atlas's quiet completion, and then withdrawn, withheld, when Marsh's conscience — that great lumbering organ the man had carried about like an extra cargo — had finally objected.

Marsh had refused to pay. Marsh had begun, it was whispered through channels Halloway could not openly use, to keep records *in order to refuse*. And so Marsh had drowned, and the boatman who ferried him to his own resurrection had drowned also, properly, no sleight of hand there, and the atlas had passed to the apprentice along with the workshop and the silence, and everything had been settled. Everything had been arithmetic.

Until the margins began to move.

Halloway untied the ribbon and spread the copyist's transcript across the desk, weighting the corners with the brass instruments he kept for exactly this kind of ceremony. He had engaged a woman — Mrs. Quill, absurd name, magnificent discretion — to make a fair copy of the atlas's marginalia from the photographs... no. Not photographs. From the impressions taken during the requisition survey, when his men had handled the plates under pretence of assessing them for the Admiralty. He had those impressions still. And last week he had ordered a fresh comparison made, impression against transcript, because a magistrate who does not verify is merely a man with opinions.

The comparison had come back yesterday evening. He had not slept.

Now, in the grey light, he went through it again, line by line, with the patience he had learned watching his father lose money slowly at cards.

There. Entry the thirty-first in the sequence. In the impression taken in spring, the marginal note beside the Selsey shoals ran nine words. In the transcript made from that same impression — a transcript he had personally verified at the time — it ran eleven. Two words added. And they were not Marsh's two words. They were rendered in a hand that imitated Marsh's reversals but pressed too hard, a young man's hand, an ink-stained hand, a hand that belonged to Wren Eastwick and no one else alive.

*Copied,* said the difference between nine words and eleven. *And understood.*

Halloway sat very still and listened to the building settle around him, beams ticking as the fog lifted, the river beginning its morning argument with the quay. Somewhere below, his clerk Pell would be arriving soon, whistling something mournful. There was time yet.

So. The boy had read them.

He should have expected it. He had, in fact, half expected it, which is why the requisition notice had given ten days rather than three — a courtesy, he had told himself, to allow completion of the plates, though courtesy was a word he used the way sailors used weather, as something to be waited out or worked around. Ten days was long enough for a diligent apprentice to finish an atlas. It was also long enough for a frightened one to do something stupid, and Halloway had counted on the fear to produce either diligence or stupidity, both of which he knew how to harvest.

But the boy had done neither. He had done the third thing, the thing Halloway himself would have done at that age, the thing that had made him briefly proud of Wren Eastwick across the whole width of his intention to destroy him: the boy had copied the margins first. Quietly. Neatly. Adding nothing, altering nothing in the original — Halloway checked again, twice — no, the originals were untouched, which was worse than tampering, because tampering could be prosecuted while understanding could only be feared.

Wren Eastwick now possessed, in some hiding place Halloway would enjoy finding eventually, a complete transcript of eleven years of corruption. Every payment. Every diverted seizure. Every name — including, Halloway noted with the detached admiration of one craftsman for another, the entry concerning the Eastwicks' own father.

Ah, thought Halloway. He's found that one by now. Certainly he has. A boy reads a ledger and looks first for his own name, or failing that, for the name that broke him.

He remembered old Eastwick. Everyone had. A merchant of the old school, honest to the point of illness, the sort of man who paid duty on smuggled goods out of principle. Halloway had ruined him with a signature and a manufactured debt — eight hundred pounds, invented in a morning over this same desk — and had done it for the purest of professional reasons: to purchase Tobias Marsh's silence. Ruin the father, the reasoning ran, and the master will understand what refusal costs. Marsh had understood. Marsh had refused anyway, which had cost the boatman his life and taught Halloway a lasting lesson about the elasticity of other men's consciences. But the Eastwick ruin had remained in place, compounding quietly these three years like interest on a loan nobody had formally called in — the widow dead of it, the daughter keeping the household upright by means Halloway could guess at and did not care to confirm, the son inking coastlines in a rain-stained workshop under the eye of a magistrate who owned the ground the workshop stood on.

Halloway poured himself a measure of the brandy he kept in the drawer with the private ledger, for the private ledger's business, and considered.

The obvious move was seizure. Send Pell with four men before the workshop opened, take the boy, take the transcript, take the atlas, and let the assizes sort the wreckage. He turned this over the way he turned all options, looking for the flaw in its face.

The flaw was the same flaw that had spoiled every blunt instrument he'd ever been handed: seized things raised questions. An apprentice arrested with copied papers becomes a story, and stories travel to London faster than warrants do. Who gave him cause? Why were the margins worth copying? What magistrate's attention had fallen on a humble atlas of the coast? A dead apprentice invites questions — he had told himself that once already this year, and it remained true. Worse: a silenced boy confirms the margins' value. Destroy the transcript and the boy together, and you have announced to every enemy Halloway had accumulated in eleven years that the atlas contained something worth destroying boys over. Half the customs house would be at his throat within the month, not from virtue — none of them had any — but from the oldest instinct of the guilty, which is to dig up the evidence before it digs you.

No. The boy holding a copy was dangerous. The boy holding a copy who *believed himself forgiven*, who believed his family's debt dissolved by an act of magistrate's mercy — that boy was an asset. That boy would deliver the originals into Halloway's hands with his own arms, and thank him for the privilege, and never once wonder why mercy had chosen that particular season to call.

Corruption, Halloway reflected, was often accused of being greedy, but greed was the vulgar word for it. What corruption actually offered, at its best, was absolution. Absolution at interest. You forgave a man his past in exchange for his future, and the future, unlike the past, kept paying.

He finished the brandy, re-tied the ribbon, and locked away all three ledgers — the calf-bound lie, the private truth, and the unwritten one behind his eyes. Then he sent Pell up to fetch his hat and gloves, and his good cane, the one with the silver ferrule that clicked on cobbles in a rhythm people had learned to listen for.

"The workshop," he said. "Before the boy has his breakfast. Fear is sharpest on an empty stomach."

* * *

The fog had burned off into a high pale sky by the time the click of the silver ferrule reached the yard outside the Eastwick workshop, and Halloway paused at the door a moment longer than courtesy required, letting the sound announce him. He heard the scramble within — a chair pushed back, the muffled clink of pens in a jar, Bette Eastwick's low voice saying something urgent and unfinished — and permitted himself a small satisfaction. Good. Let them be mid-secret. Secrets interrupted confess faster than secrets cornered.

The door opened on Wren Eastwick, and Halloway studied him with the attention he otherwise spent only on ledgers.

Three years ago the boy had been all wrists and wonder, an apprentice who looked at charts the way other boys looked at the sea. Now the wonder had been sanded off and something harder sat where it had been — but the tell was still there, the same tell it had always been: the eyes going first to Halloway's hands, checking whether they held papers. Checking whether today was the day the requisition arrived in person.

"Magistrate." The boy stepped back to admit him. "This is an honour."

*Nobody,* thought Halloway, stepping over the threshold into the smell of ink and damp plaster and cold tea, *has ever meant that sentence less.* "Master Eastwick. And Mistress Eastwick." He bowed slightly to Bette, who had placed herself, quite naturally, between her brother and the window — placing herself, though she likely didn't know it, between her brother and the sightline of the street. A girl who thought in terms of sightlines. He adjusted his estimate of her upward and filed it away. "I am sorry to call so early. I find the mornings belong to me before they belong to the town."

"We were working," said Bette. "The atlas keeps its own hours."

"I don't doubt it. May I?" He moved toward the great table without waiting for permission, because waiting taught men they had a choice, and drew off his gloves finger by finger. The final plate lay there, half-dry, the coastline rendered in that luminous precision that had made Marsh's name and would, in a juster world, have made the boy's. Halloway bent over it as a pilgrim bends over a relic.

"Extraordinary," he said, and meant it, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "You know, when I was a boy I believed maps showed the world as it is. It took me until my thirtieth year to learn that a map shows the world as someone wishes it navigated. Every chart is an argument, Master Eastwick. Every fairway a recommendation. Every shoal a warning that somebody, somewhere, paid for." He straightened. "Your master understood that better than any man I ever met."

Something crossed the boy's face — quickly, badly masked, the way grief masks badly when it has been rehearsed instead of felt. *He knows I know,* Halloway translated. *He wonders how much I know he knows.* The pleasant labyrinth of guilty knowledge. "You flatter Mr. Marsh," Wren said carefully.

"I eulogize him. There is a difference, and the difference is money." Halloway let that sit in the room like fog, then turned it gently aside. "But I have not come to talk of the dead. I've come to talk of the living. Of your father, Master Eastwick."

The silence that followed had texture. He watched the boy go white in the specific way of a man who has just learned that the weapon pointed at him has been aimed all along. Beside the window, Bette's hands closed together at her waist — not clasped in a lady's fashion but gripped, one over the other, the grip of a woman holding a ledger shut.

"My father has been dead six years," Wren said.

"His debts are not." Halloway drew from his coat the folded paper he had brought for exactly this moment and laid it on the table beside the wet plate, close enough that the corner took a smudge of ink, and he watched the boy register the desecration and choose not to object. Eight hundred pounds, itemized. Dated. Signed. All of it false, every figure, manufactured at the desk where he'd eaten breakfast this morning, and every word of it enforceable, which was the only truth a debt required. "Eight hundred pounds, contracted to the Crown, inherited by his estate, and the estate consists, as I understand it, of this workshop, this atlas, and two Eastwicks. The Commissioners have been patient. Patience, however, has a fiscal year."

"You've come to seize the house." Flat. The boy was steadier than he'd expected. Good. Conversations with the broken were tedious.

"I've come," said Halloway, "to forgive it."

He let the word do its work. Watched it land — watched suspicion arrive first, because the boy was no fool, then hope fighting suspicion for the territory, hope losing ground, retaking it. Beside the window Bette had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic at speed.

"Forgive," Wren repeated.

"Entirely. The debt annulled. The estate clear. Your name — your father's name — restored to the rolls unstained." Halloway picked up the paper again, turned it once in his hands, and fed it, with theatrical unhurriedness, toward the stove. Not in. Toward. "In exchange for a service. A small one. Administrative, really."

"The margins," said Bette.

Halloway turned to her with genuine pleasure. "Mistress Eastwick. Yes. The margins."

The boy moved then — one step, involuntary, placing himself between his sister and the magistrate, and Halloway noted the movement, catalogued it, admired it. Family was a currency too; most men simply spent it badly.

"I don't know what you mean," Wren said.

"No," Halloway agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Which is why you copied them — because one does not copy what one does not know. The logic of ignorance is very demanding, Master Eastwick; it requires that we ask nothing, look nothing, trace nothing. And yet someone traced my transcript's errors against my impressions, or had them traced, and found eleven words where nine had been." He watched the blow land. Did not smile — smiling was for men who could afford the luxury. "I am not angry. I want you to believe that. Anger is for surprises, and you have not surprised me. You have done precisely what I would have done, and I have never punished a man for competence. I am offering you the only trade worth making in this town: your father's ghost, bought back clean, for a bundle of papers that were never yours and can only hang you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the debt stands, the atlas goes to the Admiralty in"— he consulted no watch, because he did not need to —"nine days, and you go on as you are: innocent, impoverished, and watched. Refusal costs you nothing today. That is what makes it so expensive." He retrieved the paper from the table, folded it, and returned it to his coat — absolution withdrawn, held, pending. "You have until the atlas is surrendered. Think of it as a grace period. I am told grace is meant to be difficult."

At the door he paused, gloved hand on the latch, and delivered the rest of it gently, the way one sets down something breakable.

"There is one more thing you should know, since you have read so widely. The debt I hold — the true one, beneath the paper one — was incurred the night your master drowned. Ask yourself, Master Eastwick, why a boatman had to die for a man who was never in the water." The silver ferrule clicked once on the threshold stone. "Forgiveness is cheaper than the answer. Most men find."

He walked out into the bright cold morning, and behind him he left the workshop in that particular silence he had produced many times before in many rooms — the silence of a household recalculating everything it believed about its own history. Nine days. The Kestrel would sail when it sailed; the boy would run when runners ran; but debts, unlike men, always kept their appointments.

In his coat pocket, next to the folded paper, his fingers found the second document he had carried and not used — the warrant, drawn and signed, blank in every material particular except his own name. He had known, walking up through the fog, that he would leave it unused.

Mercy, after all, was simply the most patient form of appetite.
