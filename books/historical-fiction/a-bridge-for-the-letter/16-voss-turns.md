## 16. Voss Turns

The rain in Saltmarch came sideways, the way it did when the wind ran up the estuary with its collar turned against the town, and Callum Voss had learned to read weather like a ledger because everything else in his life had become arithmetic. He stood beneath the dripping eave of the Rope & Anchor with a pot of small beer going cold in his hand and watched Wren Eastwick cross the market square with his collar up and his shoulders carrying something heavier than shoulders were built for.

Three days Voss had followed him now. Three days of learning the boy's rhythms — the workshop at first light, the chandler's on the corner where he bought candles by the dozen more than any honest draftsman needed, the long walk east along the shingle toward the marshes where no man went without a reason.

No man went toward the marshes without a reason, and the reasons that existed in this county could be counted on one hand, and every one of them was supposed to be dead.

Voss drank the cold beer because it gave his standing there a shape, and he thought about the shape of his own life, which had once been a fine shape — a brig under his feet, a master's voice in his ear saying *Voss, mind your leads*, a name that men said without spitting after it. He thought about the night the *Kestrel* had gone out short-handed into weather she had no business facing, and how Tobias Marsh had not been aboard, and how Voss had stood at her rail watching the lightning walk white fingers across the water where the master's boat should have been, and understood, in the slow cold way understanding comes to sailors, that he was going to hang for a death that had never happened.

He had not hanged. He had escaped — a bribe here, a dark hold there, four years of being nobody in ports where nobody asked — and being nobody had a taste, and the taste was iron.

And now here was Eastwick's boy walking east through the rain toward the marshes with candles enough to light a crypt.

* * *

Voss let him get half a mile ahead before he moved. He knew these paths better than the boy ever would; he'd run cargo through the channels as a younger man, before Marsh took him up, before everything got complicated by the terrible luxury of trusting someone. The marsh in rain was a country of gray veils. The samphire stood black along the gutters. Somewhere out in the wet a curlew cried its two falling notes, over and over, like a question nobody would answer.

Wren went to ground at the ruined chapel of St. Aldhelm's — Voss saw him drop below the ridge of the dune where the old walls humped out of the sand like a whale's ribs, and Voss smiled, and the smiling felt strange on his face, an expression he hadn't worn honestly in years.

St. Aldhelm's. Of course. Fen's chapel. Idris Fen, who kept a door for smugglers the way other men kept a Bible, who owed Marsh debts older than Voss's service, who could be trusted to hide anything except the fact that he was hiding it.

So the master lived.

Voss stood on the dune in the rain and let the knowledge move through him the way tide moves through a channel, filling every hollow. He had suspected — the letters had told him enough to suspect, the letters signed by a drowned man that had set him on this whole hunt — but suspicion was a coin you carried and never spent. Now he had seen it spent. The boy brought candles to a ruin in the marshes, and the only flame worth lighting candles for was Tobias Marsh.

*Marsh lives.*

He said it inside himself and the words tasted like salt and like blood, both, because with that sentence everything he had suffered rearranged itself into meaning. Four years of running. Four years of watching his name spoken in assize records beside the word *mutineer*, beside the word *thief*, beside a price on his head that Halloway kept fresh the way a butcher keeps meat fresh — not because Halloway wanted him caught but because a live threat was useful leverage on everyone who'd ever sailed with them. Voss had served under a good man and been paid in infamy, and the good man had walked out of the sea and simply — begun again. Left him. Left all of them. Let the boatman drown for real in his place, let Voss carry the legal corpse's weight, and watched from some warm room while his people rotted.

The anger came up in him slow and enormous, like water rising in a hold, quiet until suddenly it was everywhere.

He crouched in the dune grass and watched the chapel through the afternoon. Smoke from a low fire, thin and careful. Once, briefly, a figure crossed the gap of a broken window — stooped, one arm held close to the body, a figure that moved like a man favoring a wound three years unhealed. Too far even for Voss's eyes to make a face of it. He didn't need a face. He knew the way Marsh stood the way he knew the pitch of the *Kestrel*'s bell.

He waited until full dark, until the boy came back out and took the path west toward town, and then Voss sat down in the wet sand with his back against the dune and made his arithmetic, because everything was arithmetic now, and this sum at last came out clean.

The berth aboard the *Kestrel*. One berth, paid by a drowned man's gold, waiting somewhere at a date he didn't yet know. And the confession — because he was certain now it was a confession, the margins the boy was translating, pages naming payments and names erased, proof against half the customs house, proof against Halloway himself. Proof that could buy a pardon. Proof that could buy a passage. Proof that belonged, by every right that suffering conferred, to the man who had paid most for keeping it.

Not the boy. The boy had lost a master, which was grief, and grief healed. Voss had lost a life, which does not.

*I have suffered longest.* He spoke it aloud to the rain, testing it, and found it held his weight. It was not generosity that decided him — he had no generosity left, it had been sold off port by port like everything else — but justice, or what passes for justice among men the sea has used up: that the longest debt collects first.

He rose, shook the sand from his coat, and turned not toward the town but south, along the cliff road, toward the manor house where Magistrate Halloway kept his lamps burning late behind glass, like lures.

* * *

Halloway received him in the long room with the charts on the wall — charts of the very coast Marsh had drawn better, hung like trophies taken from a body — and Voss stood on the worn square of carpet before the desk and felt the old animal fear rise in him, because this room had featured in his nightmares for four years, and nightmares do not prepare you for how ordinary the monster smells: pipe smoke, ink, a damp dog somewhere in the house.

"You have nerve," Halloway said, not looking up from his papers, "coming to this house. There is a bounty on you, Mr. Voss. Two hundred pounds." He turned a page. "I could summon the constable from his supper and collect it before the wine decants."

"You could," Voss said. "Except you've had four years to hunt me and never sent anyone who mattered. A man doesn't guard a thing he wants kept safe." He watched the magistrate's pen stop moving. "You've kept me alive, sir, the same as you keep a knife alive. For cutting."

Halloway laid down the pen. He was a neat man, silvered, with the patient face of a clergyman and the eyes of something that had stopped attending sermons decades ago, and he looked at Voss now with the unhurried attention of a man pricing goods.

"And what," he said, "does the knife want?"

"Absolution," Voss said. "And I've come to sell it to you cheap."

He told it plainly, because plainness was the only currency Halloway trusted. The letters. The boy Eastwick. The marginal cipher in the atlas — and here Halloway's stillness acquired a different quality, the stillness of a man listening to his own account being read aloud. The chapel of St. Aldhelm's. The smoke. The figure at the broken window, stooped, wounded, unmistakable.

"Tobias Marsh is alive," Voss said, "and dying anyway, slowly, out in Fen's keep, and your atlas — the one you've requisitioned so politely from the boy — is sitting in a workshop ten days from your custody with its margins copied into a private notebook, and every page of that cipher names a payment, and some of those payments, Magistrate, were made to you."

The clock on the mantel counted five seconds into the silence. Rain worked at the windows.

"You are either the bravest man in this county," Halloway said at last, "or the most desperate, and in my experience they are frequently the same man." He folded his hands. "Say what you want."

"My pardon. Full and formal. My name struck from every list you keep it on. Passage money to anywhere I choose to be a new man. In exchange—" and here Voss heard his own voice go quiet, the quiet of the truly resolved, "—you take the chapel tomorrow night, or the night after, whichever serves your purpose, and you take Marsh, and you take the confession entire, and you burn it in front of witnesses so it can never come back on you. And the boy—"

"The boy," Halloway repeated, softly.

"Is mine to deal with. He's done nothing but grieve and translate. Let him. What I want isn't his ruin." Voss was surprised to find that this was true, or mostly true, or true enough to say. "What I want is what he's carrying. The berth, if there's a berth. The pages, before you burn them — I'll read them once, to know my own name was written in a living hand, and then you can have them to the fire."

Something moved behind Halloway's face — not warmth, nothing so human, but the recognition one craftsman gives another across a shared trade. Corruption, Voss thought, recognizing corruption, the way two smiths will nod over an anvil.

"Tomorrow night," Halloway said. "I have six men who can be on the marsh road within the hour of my word. If your drowned man is where you say he is—" he rose, and extended his hand across the desk, pale and dry and perfectly steady, "—then by Friday, Mr. Voss, you will be a free man with a clean name, and I will sleep better than I have in three years."

Voss took the hand. It closed on his like a document being sealed.

"One condition of mine," Halloway added, as Voss turned to go. "The confession burns. All of it. No reading first. You've sold me the proof of your own crimes, sir — you don't get to browse the merchandise." A thin smile, the first. "Consider it interest."

* * *

He walked back through the town in the dark with the pardon already warming in his chest like a coal sewn into his coat, and the strangest thing happened on the way, which was that he passed the workshop — the Eastwick workshop, lamplit still, the shadow of the boy bent over his table thrown large onto the curtain — and he stopped, and stood in the street, and could not make his legs carry him past.

Through the small panes he could see Bette Eastwick at the far bench, grinding ink or sharpening pens, her head bent, her hands moving with the economy of a woman who had done a man's work for years without ever being thanked in a man's words. He had watched this family for days now. He knew their hours like he'd known the tides.

They didn't know yet. That was the thing about betrayal that no one tells you: it has to be carried ahead of itself, alone, for hours or days, a message from a future the betrayed haven't reached. Tomorrow night the riders would come. Tomorrow night Fen would die at his door — Voss knew Fen, knew Fen would die at that door rather than yield it, knew it the way you know weather — and Marsh would be taken or burned, and the boy would learn that the stranger who'd sat at his table and drunk his tea had sold the last living piece of his master to the magistrate for a clean name and a ticket out.

*You have suffered longest,* he told himself again, on the dark street, in the rain that had never once let up all week. *The berth is yours. The confession is yours. Four years in hell buys the first seat on the boat out.*

And it was true, and the truth of it was exactly the weight of the lie beside it, and he could no longer tell which was which, and he suspected — walking on now, fast, past the workshop light, past the harbor bell moaning its fog-note out in the Roads — that he had stopped being able to tell somewhere around the fourth year of being nobody, and that everything since had merely been the sound of a man justifying the shape of his own survival.

At the corner by the chandler's he paused once more. Behind him, the town slept its complicit sleep. Ahead, the marsh lay black under the last of the rain, and somewhere out in that blackness a wounded man was writing letters in his own dead hand, and a boy was copying margins by candlelight, and a smuggler kept a door that had one night left to stand.

Voss counted his hours like coins. Thirty, perhaps thirty-six, before Halloway's men rode.

Enough time to warn no one. Enough time to save himself.

He pulled his collar up and went to find out exactly where the *Kestrel* lay.
