## 17. The Raid

### Halloway

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Magistrate Halloway stood in the lee of the ruined chapel's boundary wall and listened to his men move through the wet dark like a thought moving through a guilty mind. They were good men. Not honest — honesty was a currency he had never found worth its exchange rate — but good at this: the soft closing of circles.

He had dressed for the occasion in his second-best coat. The best was for rooms with candles and witnesses. This was weather's business, and weather did not care for broadcloth.

Voss had come to him at midnight smelling of marsh mud and desperation, and had given up the chapel with the eagerness of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long his shoulders had grown around it. *Marsh lives*, Voss had said, and Halloway had felt something in his chest that he declined to name — not surprise; he had never believed in that drowning, not truly, because Halloway had arranged enough disappearances in his years at the customs house to know how rarely they stayed disappeared — but a kind of grim satisfaction, the collector's pleasure at a set finally completing itself.

*The chapel*, Voss had said. *He is dying there anyway. He wants to burn the archive.*

Then let him want, Halloway had thought. Wanting was free. Everything else had a price, and tonight he intended to collect three years of arrears: the confession pages, the atlas, the boy who could read the margins, and Tobias Marsh himself, alive, which was worth more to Halloway than any dead man had ever been. A dead traitor was a rumor. A living one, delivered to the Admiralty in irons, was a career.

"Positions," he said quietly, and Rook, his clerk-turned-captain, passed the word down the line of eight men with hand signs they had practiced in a warehouse all winter.

The chapel crouched ahead of them on its low knoll, roofless these sixty years, its tower stump black against the lightening east. One window still held candlelight, thin as a coin seen edge-on. Someone inside was awake. Someone inside was always awake, Halloway had learned; the guilty slept the way cats did, in shallow laps.

He checked his watch by feel. The tide would turn within the hour, and with it the marsh channels would begin to fill or empty according to their own arithmetic. He knew the roads. He did not know the water. It was the one map in the county he had never bothered to buy.

A mistake, he would think later. The only one he made that night.

"On my word," Halloway said. "Alive, the old man. Alive, the boy if he's there. The papers matter more than either."

He raised his hand. The circle closed.

### Fen

Idris Fen heard them before he saw them, because twenty years of smuggling had taught his ears to distrust silence. Silence on the marsh at this hour was manufactured. Real quiet had birds in it.

He put down the mug of nettle tea Marsh had drunk none of, crossed the chapel's fallen nave in four strides, and looked out through the broken east window into a darkness that had too many straight lines in it. Men. Eight, nine. Moving wrong — not smugglers' wrong, which was fast and low, but soldiers' wrong, patient, spreading along the wall like ink along a blotter.

"Fen," came Marsh's voice from the vestry, thin as paper. "What is it?"

"Halloway." Fen said the name the way you'd spit a bone. He turned back into the candlelight and took inventory in the old habit of his trade: the archive chest against the north wall, three years of ledgers and letters and the confession pages Marsh had guarded like relics; the oil he had laid in against exactly this night without ever saying so aloud, because saying it aloud would have made it a plan and plans could be jinxed; the boy Wren Eastwick, asleep two hours against the vestry doorframe, waking now with the particular stillness of a young man who has just understood everything at once.

And Bette — God help them all, Bette, who was supposed to be waiting at the eel-fisherman's hut a mile south and instead was standing in the vestry doorway with her brother's coat around her shoulders because she had followed him across the marsh rather than sit and wait, because of course she had. Fen had known her three days and already understood she was the dangerous one.

"The channels," Wren said. He was on his feet, voice low, eyes on the window. "The map — the western gutter runs at half flood till the turn, then it drains to the sea in—" he counted silently, lips moving— "under an hour. If we go now, wading, we reach the shingle before their horses find a ford."

"You've never walked it," Fen said.

"I've drawn it four hundred times."

Fen grinned at that, quick and crooked, and clapped the boy's shoulder once, hard, the way he'd have wanted someone to clap his own if anyone ever had. "Then tonight the ink learns to swim." He turned to the chest. "Get your sister south. I'll wake the old man and give you time."

"There are nine of them," Wren said.

"Aye," said Fen, and began unstopping the oil jars. "They'll want to come through the door, and doors are where I've always done my best work."

* * *

### Wren

There was no time to be afraid, which Wren would remember afterward as a mercy. There was only the chest, and the bundle Marsh pressed into his hands with fingers that shook and did not stop shaking, and the smell of lamp-oil thickening the cold air until every breath tasted of what was coming.

"Take it," Marsh whispered. His voice had been failing for weeks; now it was almost gone, a sound like a quill running dry. "All of it. The confession. The berth papers. The Kestrel sails on tomorrow's tide and there is one passage paid and it doesn't matter anymore who paid it, do you hear me? None of that matters. What matters is that these pages leave this county in hands Halloway cannot buy."

"Come with us," Wren said, and heard how young he sounded, how much the apprentice still, reaching for the master's sleeve across three years of silence and a lie shaped like a drowning.

Marsh smiled. In the candlelight his face was all angles and yellow skin, and yet for a moment Wren saw him whole again — the man who had taught him to hold a graver like a pen, who had smelled of rosin and salt, whose hand over young Wren's had steadied the first line of the first chart he ever drew. Brilliant. Cowardly. Loving. All of it true at once; Wren had finally learned that people were charts with more than one sounding on them.

"No," Marsh said. "I have been hiding for three years, Wren. Hiding is the only work I was ever good at, and I mean to finish it properly." He nodded toward the chest, toward the archive that was half confession and half evidence and entirely the reason five people were about to die or not die in a ruined chapel before sunrise. "I'll burn what stays. What leaves, lives. That's the whole arithmetic of it. Go."

From the nave came the crash of the outer door going in — not forced, Fen's voice rang out, *not yet, come on then, come on then* — and the first crackle of flame finding oiled stone.

Bette's hand closed on Wren's wrist. Her grip was stronger than her hands had any right to be, hands that had kept a household alive on arithmetic and stubbornness since their father's ruin. "Wren." Just his name. It was enough.

He ran. Behind him, Marsh turned to face the fire he had spent three years outrunning, and began, methodically, with the calm of a man inking a title-cartouche, to feed it the rest of his life.

* * *

### Fen

They came through the door in a rush and he met them with a boathook, of all things, because the chapel had offered him nothing better and because there was a poetry in it he suspected he'd have appreciated later if later had been available. The first man went down clutching a split shoulder. The second hesitated, and hesitation at a door was death wearing a helpful expression; Fen put the hook's iron butt into his knee and listened to him scream.

Three more came together after that, and the mathematics stopped being charming.

He fought in the burning doorway because it was the only ground worth holding: behind him the vestry, the marsh window, the escape route; before him the fire and Halloway's men, who had begun to understand that this barefoot smuggler in a ruined cassock meant to cost them exactly as much blood as he could purchase with his own.

Rook shouted for them to take him alive. Fen laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising him, rusty as an unused hinge — and swung the hook in a flat arc that bought another six seconds.

Six seconds. He had sold cargoes on less.

Somewhere beyond the fire, out on the dark water, he could hear the slap and suck of the tide beginning its turn, the marsh draining seaward, carrying — he chose to believe, because a man should get to choose his last belief — carrying the boy and the girl and the dead man's pages out along channels no horse could follow.

The blade, when it came, came from the side, where he hadn't hands enough left to guard. Fen went down into the doorway he had held, and held it even lying in it, and his last coherent thought was not of heaven nor of Marsh nor even of the money owed him, but of the tide tables, and whether the boy knew — *really* knew, boot-deep knew — that the eastern gutter lied about its depth on a falling tide.

Then the fire leaned over him like a kind host, and he stopped keeping accounts.

* * *

### Bette

The water was colder than her imagination had managed, and deeper, and it moved.

That was the part no chart could teach, Bette realized, gasping, her arm locked through Wren's, the oilskin bundle strapped beneath her coat against her heart like a second pulse. Standing water was geometry. This was appetite. The channel pulled at their legs with long patient fingers, wanting to keep them, and above them the sky was going grey at the edges in that treacherous way that meant dawn was coming to make them visible to anyone on any rise.

"Feet wide," Wren said through his teeth. "Feel for the firm sand along the northern bank — the margin notes say the southern side scours out." He was reciting his master's cipher, she realized. Three years of those tiny coded annotations she had assumed were navigational trivia, and he had memorized them the way other men memorized psalms, and now the psalms were holding them upright in a freezing river in the dark.

"The margins say," she echoed, half a sob, half a laugh. "Wren Eastwick, if we live through this, I am going to make you read me every one of those notes aloud, twice."

"Once will do," he said, "if you're polite about it."

She loved him ferociously in that moment, her brother, this boy who had gone silent and grey for three years and was suddenly, dangerously, here — present in the water and the cold and the talking — and she loved him and was furious at him and did not waste breath on either. Somewhere behind them the chapel burned; she had seen the glow reflected on the cloud base like a wound in the sky, and she had not let herself think about who was still inside it, because thinking was a luxury and walking was not.

Twice they went down. Twice his hand found her collar and hauled her into the shallows and they knelt in the sucking mud breathing like landed fish until their legs agreed to be legs again. Once a shout came from the seawall behind them — riders, small and distant, wheeling along the bank like flies along a cut — and the channel bent just then, as the map had sworn it would, and put forty yards of reed between them and the sightline, and the riders' attention slid off them like rain off oilcloth.

The map, she thought. The map was saving them. Every bend Wren had drawn from other men's reports and his master's drowned knowledge was proving itself under her boots, and she understood suddenly why her brother loved the work, why he had given his youth to a dead man's coastlines: because a chart was a promise that the world could be known, and tonight, for once, the world was honoring the contract.

"There." Wren pointed with his chin. Ahead, past the last curtain of reeds, the marsh fell away into pale sand and shingle, and beyond the shingle the sea lay flat and pewter-grey, and riding at anchor in the roadstead, small and exact as a drawn thing, was the Kestrel.

A boat was pulling away from her side. Toward them. Toward the one berth, whoever had paid for it.

Bette's heart did something complicated and painful, because she knew who had paid, and she knew what the money had cost, and she knew — though she had told no one, not even the merchant of silver, not even herself in plain words — that whatever happened on that shingle in the next ten minutes, her brother was getting on that boat if she had to put him aboard by force and row it out herself.

* * *

### Halloway

By full light the chapel was a smoking ribcage and the ledger of accounts was ruinous.

Two of his men hurt, neither mortally. The smuggler Fen dead in the doorway he had apparently considered rentable property. Tobias Marsh dead among the ashes of the archive he had chosen over his own life — Rook found him seated against the vestry wall, hands folded, half-charred pages scattered around him like black petals, most of them burned past reading, and Halloway stood over the body for a long moment feeling the specific displeasure of a creditor whose debtor has died solvent.

No confession. No proof. Half the customs house would sleep tonight behind doors that no longer needed locking, and Halloway's great reconciliation, three years in the compounding, had gone up in smoke in the hands of a dying man who had at least had the decency to look apologetic about it.

But.

The boy had run. The boy and the girl, tracked to the marsh edge, lost where the channels braided — lost precisely, Rook reported, along a line no rider could follow and no local guide could be trusted in daylight with the magistrate watching. And the boy had carried something out. Voss, sulking at the perimeter with the face of a man who had sold everything and been paid in ash, swore the archive was destroyed, all of it. But Halloway had watched Wren Eastwick leave his workshop for three weeks now, had catalogued the boy's habits the way he catalogued everyone's, and a boy who walked into a fire-risk at midnight did not do it empty-handed.

More: the Kestrel rode at anchor, sails loosed, ready to warp out on the turn. One berth paid, the harbormaster's book said, in silver sold piece by piece through three dealers in a fortnight — Halloway had read the entries before dawn, because he read everything — and a boat already pulling for the shingle where, if his riders' glasses were honest, two figures had just emerged from the reeds like things the marsh had decided, on reflection, to give back.

One berth. Two Eastwicks. Papers of some value in an oilskin wrap.

Halloway climbed into the saddle himself, which he had not done in six years, and turned his horse toward the sea.

It was not yet finished, then. Very well. He had come for a confession and found a fire; but fires left embers, and embers could be negotiated with, and the younger Eastwick, he suspected, had inherited just enough of his master's cleverness and just too little of his master's ruthlessness to use either when it mattered.

"One berth," Halloway said to Rook, kicking the horse into a canter along the seawall road, the wind carrying his voice sideways. "We will see which of them the morning prefers."

Ahead, the tide had turned. The Kestrel's anchor came up dripping into the grey light, and on the shingle, small as figures on a chart, brother and sister stood facing the water and each other, and between them, held out in the sister's steady hand, glinted a folded paper the color of dawn.

The berth papers. And only minutes left to decide whom they named.
