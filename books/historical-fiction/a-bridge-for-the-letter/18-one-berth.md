## 18. One Berth

The shingle at low tide was the color of old pewter, and it spoke under their feet — a thousand small stones shifting and clicking, announcing them to the whole gray world. Wren had mapped this coast down to its last contour, had drawn these banks and channels in ink finer than a hair, and he had never once imagined standing on them at dawn with his sister's hand cold in his and the sea smelling of rot and iron.

The Kestrel rode at anchor beyond the bar, a dark sliver against a sky just beginning to bruise pink. Her boat was already coming through the channel, oars muffled with rags, and Idris Fen was not in it, because Idris Fen would never be in anything again. The thought arrived without shape, just weight, the way grief does when there is no time to give it its proper ceremony.

"They're coming," Bette said.

Wren turned. Behind them, up the lane between the black hedges, lanterns swayed. Three riders, maybe four, moving at that unhurried canter Halloway's men used — the pace of men who knew the quarry was cornered by geography if not yet by rope. The marsh behind the chapel had slowed them; Wren's own charts, the ones Halloway now believed safely requisitioned, were what would slow them further, because Fen had torn the true channel-markings from the archive before he died and the copies burned with everything else.

"Then we run for the waterline," Wren said. "There's a gully —"

"Wren." Bette did not move. Her hand tightened on his. "Stop. Stop drawing us an escape for one moment and listen to me."

He looked at her. In the half-light her face seemed made of the same stuff as the shingle — pale, hard, glinting. She had not slept either. Neither of them had slept since the chapel, since Marsh had pushed the oilskin packet into his hands and turned back into the smoke, since Fen had gone down holding the door with a boat-hook and a courage Wren would spend the rest of his life trying to draw worthily.

"The berth papers," Bette said. "Give them to me."

"You don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking. I've been asking it for weeks. You just haven't been listening." She held out her free hand, palm up, steady as a shopkeeper's scale. "The papers, Wren. And the atlas."

* * *

It had begun, as everything in their family began, with arithmetic.

Bette Eastwick had learned sums the hard way, at fourteen, the winter their father's debts came home like wolves. She had learned that a household is a ledger wearing a roof, that love without accounting is just sentiment burning fuel, and she had done the sums every year since without complaint and without mercy. So when the silver began leaving the house — piece by piece, wrapped in flannel, carried to Mr. Quill's back room in town where a man who never asked names paid fair weight — she had done those sums too.

Two passages aboard the Kestrel. Not one.

She had known about the single berth since the night she found the first letter's cipher half-translated under Wren's candle stub, and she had said nothing, because saying something would have made it real, and because she needed time. Time to sell the teapot their mother had poured from on Sunday mornings. Time to sell the sugar tongs, the cream jug, the little salt cellar shaped like a shell that Bette had loved best of all and sold first, because the thing you love most should be the first thing you spend for the people you love most. Time to find the Kestrel's purser in a tavern where honest women didn't go, and lay coin after coin on the table until the man's eyes stopped being suspicious and started being respectful.

One berth had been bought with a dead man's money or a dying man's guilt. The second had been bought with their mother's silver, and it had been bought for Wren from the very first evening, though she had let him believe otherwise. She had watched him these past days working himself hollow over the copied margins, watched him rehearsing the speech he thought he'd hidden from her — *you go, Bette, you're the one who can use it, you go* — and she had said nothing, because a choice forced early is a choice refused, and she needed him to arrive at the waterline believing the sacrifice was his to make.

So that he could make it freely. That was the whole trick of it. You couldn't give a man freedom; you could only wait where he'd walk into it.

Now, on the shingle, with the lanterns swaying nearer and the Kestrel's boat grinding toward the stones, she finally told him.

"I sold it," she said. "All of it. Every piece. The shell salt cellar first." Her voice did not waver, which cost her more than the silver ever had. "I paid for two berths, Wren. Weeks ago. The second one was always yours. It was always going to be yours. There was never any version of the arithmetic where I sailed and you stayed."

* * *

For a moment the world went very simple and very quiet. Wren heard the oars, muffled. Heard the shingle ticking beneath his boots as his weight shifted. Heard his own heart doing something clumsy.

"Two berths," he said.

"Two."

"And you let me argue with you all week—"

"You argued beautifully," Bette said. "You argued like your master taught you to draft a coastline. Very precise. Very wrong in places." A breath went out of her, and with it some of the armor. "I couldn't tell you sooner. If you'd known there was a place for you, you'd have found ten reasons why it should be mine anyway, and we'd have stood here arguing while Halloway's men arrived to settle the question for us. So I waited until waiting could do no more harm."

The boat's bow touched stone with a scrape like a drawn breath. A sailor leapt out, knee-deep in the shallows, and beckoned once — no words, just the jerk of a head and the flat urgency of a man who measured safety in minutes.

And Wren understood, in the space of that scrape and that beckon, what she had done. She had spent three years keeping the household solvent and the last month keeping him alive without his consent, and now she stood offering him the escape he had spent the whole night deciding to refuse, and the refusal died in his throat — not because it was wrong, but because she had built the moment so that refusing meant spending both their lives for the pleasure of his pride.

Except.

Except that the atlas was in his satchel. Except that the confession pages were against his chest, sewn into his coat lining where Marsh had told him to put them, and except that Halloway's riders were close enough now that he could hear the wet crunch of hooves on the lane's mud. One berth had become two, but two berths did not change the oldest sum of all: someone had to stay behind to hold the door, and Fen had held it last, and doors did not hold themselves.

"No," Wren said quietly.

Bette's face went white. "Wren—"

"Listen. Listen to me, because I've only got the time it takes that boat to load." He took her shoulders. Through the wool of her coat he could feel her shaking, and he marveled, as he always marveled, that she shook only after the fighting was decided, never during. "If we both sail, Halloway burns the workshop tonight and calls us fugitives by morning, and the atlas becomes salvage — stolen goods recovered, evidence against nobody, worth nothing but paper. But if the atlas leaves in *your* trunk, in *your* name, with the berth papers signed and witnessed — then it's cargo, Bette. Documented cargo, carried under charter. Halloway can't call it stolen without admitting what it contains. He can't seize it abroad without making noise he can't afford. The margins name half the customs house. As long as the atlas exists somewhere he can't reach, you're not a fugitive. You're a merchant with collateral."

"That's not—" She stopped. Because she was Bette, and she could see the ledger balancing even as she hated it.

"It's the only way the atlas protects anyone," Wren said. "Marsh knew it. Why do you think he wanted it finished? Not for the coasts. For exactly this." His hands tightened. "You go. You take the atlas. You get work with the merchant houses off my master's plates — they'll hire you in a week once they see the drafting, and every plate you show them is a plate Halloway has to fear. And I stay, because someone has to stand on this shore and be found, so that the story holds together. A brother who grieves his lost sister. That I can play. I've had three years' practice playing safe."

"You'll hang."

"Not if the pages burn right." He touched his coat, over the sewn lining. "Halloway gets nothing but ash and a boy who knows nothing. Dead apprentices invite questions — Voss told me that much truth. And Halloway needs me quiet more than he needs me dead, because I'm the only witness who can swear the atlas was complete when it left my hands. My silence is worth more to him than my corpse. I finally understand the currency of this coast."

Behind them, up the lane, the first horse came through the hedge gap. A lantern swung, throwing long shadows across the marsh grass.

"There's no time," the sailor called, low and harsh. "Now or the tide takes the chance with it."

Bette was crying, silently, furiously, the way she did everything — efficiently, without waste. She gripped his lapels. "You gave me the arithmetic my whole life," she whispered. "Don't you dare leave me a sum I can't check."

"Check it," Wren said, and smiled, and felt his face crack with it. "You raised me on ledgers. You'll find mine balances. One life spent buying two free — that's the best rate anyone on this coast has gotten in years."

She struck him then, open-handed across the cheek, not hard, more a seal than a blow, and then she kissed the spot she'd struck, which was worse, which undid him entirely. Then she stepped back and became, before his eyes, the other thing she had always been underneath the sister — the survivor, the accountant, the woman who kept households standing when better-built ones fell. She took the satchel. She took the atlas.

"Write to me," Wren said. "In cipher. Margins only. No one else will ever read them."

"In margins only," she agreed. Her voice broke on the second word, and she let it, once, and then closed it away forever. "Goodbye, Wren."

"No," he said. "Not goodbye. Say it the way cartographers say it. *See you on the next survey.*"

She laughed — one broken note — and then she was in the shallows, and the sailor was hauling her up over the gunwale, and the boat was turning, and Wren stood alone on the shingle with the tide coming in around his boots and the dawn coming up behind the riders like something being forged.

The last he saw of her, she had turned in the stern, one hand pressed flat against the air between them, holding nothing, holding everything.

Then the mist took the boat, and the Kestrel's anchor chain rattled out its length like something being counted, and the riders came down onto the stones.

* * *

Magistrate Halloway did not ride down himself. He sent his men, four of them, with a warrant folded in a leather case, and they surrounded Wren with the weary competence of men doing a job at an hour they resented. Their leader dismounted and read the warrant aloud — flight of persons unknown, recovery of certain documents — while the eastern sky turned the color of a healing wound.

Wren stood in the rising tide and let them search him. They found no satchel. They found no atlas. They found a young man soaked to the knees, alone on a shingle bank at dawn, and when their leader asked what he was doing there, Wren Eastwick looked at him with red-rimmed, honest-seeming eyes and gave him the performance of his life.

"My sister," he said, and let his voice fail, and did not have to feign the crack in it. "My sister has gone. Taken by smugglers. I followed the lanterns down from the lane and I was too late. There was a boat. It went east into the mist. Please — please, you have to find her, she has nothing, she doesn't even know the waters—"

It was good work. Even he could hear how good it was. Grief, it turned out, required no rehearsal; it had been rehearsing itself in him for three years, waiting only for an audience.

They questioned him until the sun cleared the horizon, and then, finding nothing on him but salt water and despair, they took him back along the lane at a walk, past the blackened ruin where the chapel had been, past the place where Fen's body had already been carried off under sacking, and Wren rode with his hands bound loosely before him and his cheek still stinging where his sister's palm had sealed the bargain, and inside his coat, against his heart, the confession pages rode with him — the one cargo no one had thought to search for, because no one had thought to look at the man left standing on the shore.

Which was, of course, the whole design.

At the top of the lane, Wren turned once. The sea was empty. Somewhere beyond the bar, a brig was making for deep water with his sister and his master's life's work in her hold, and the tide was coming in over the shingle, erasing, stone by stone, the marks of everyone who had stood there.

He faced forward. The riders closed around him. And in his pocket, folded small, he kept the one page he had cut from the atlas before surrendering it to Bette's trunk — the page naming Halloway's own payments, the page he would need before this day was done.

Because the magistrate would come for him soon, politely, with tea and threats. And Wren Eastwick had one thing left to spend, and he meant to spend it slowly — reading it aloud, piece by piece, while the man who had ruined his father watched his own name burn.
