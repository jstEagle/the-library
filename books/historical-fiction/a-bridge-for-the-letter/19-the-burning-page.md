## 19. The Burning Page

The tide was going out when they took me, and I remember thinking — absurdly, in the moment the rider's hand closed on my collar — that the marsh channels would be draining now, that every path I had memorized from the margins of the atlas was becoming a mud-flat wide enough to see a man across. Fen's channels. Fen's door. The chapel burning somewhere behind me in the grey morning, and Bette gone ahead with the atlas sewn into the lining of her trunk, and none of it worth anything if I could not get myself off this shingle alive.

They did not bind my hands. That was Halloway's courtesy, and I understood it for what it was before we had ridden a mile: he wanted me whole, and he wanted me to know he wanted me whole, because a man who is wanted is already half owned.

We rode inland to the customs house at Graystone Quay, where the fire in the magistrate's private room had been laid and lit against the damp, though it was nearly June. The room smelled of sealing wax and old smoke and the particular sourness of ledgers kept too long in a locked drawer. A decanter stood on the table with two glasses beside it. Two glasses. He had been certain.

"Sit, Mr. Eastwick."

I sat. My hands would not stop their trembling, so I folded them in my lap like a boy at prayers, and watched the magistrate across the small bright wall of the fire.

He had taken the trouble to dress properly for the occasion — not his riding clothes but the dark good coat with the silver chain of office, as though this were a hearing rather than a murder postponed. His face was smooth and pink and pleasant, the face of a man who signed things. Only his eyes moved, quick and dry, taking inventory of me: the ink-stained fingers, the burn on my wrist from the chapel, the marsh-mud dried grey along my boots.

"Mr. Voss tells me you have something of mine."

"Voss." The name came out flat. "He sold the chapel."

"He made a reasonable arrangement with the law," Halloway said mildly. "As men do. As your master declined to do, three years ago, at considerable expense to a boatman whose name I doubt you ever learned." He poured the wine himself, unhurried. "You have the pages, Mr. Eastwick. Fen died holding a door; Marsh is presumably ash by now, or will be by noon. There is no one left who wants those pages except me — and you, perhaps, if you have any sentiment left over after today. Give them to me, and we need never speak of this again."

"And my sister?"

"Sails free. I keep my arrangements." He said it without pride, the way a man says the sun rises. "You have my word as a magistrate."

*As a magistrate.* I looked at the fire. I thought about my father, who had once believed in a magistrate's word, and what that belief had cost us — the house, the name, the eleven years of my mother's silence before she died. I thought about the margin note I had decoded by candlelight three nights past, the one written in Marsh's smallest hand, the hand he saved for sums: *H. paid the boatman's widow from the fund. Considers the account closed. It is not closed.*

"I don't have the pages," I said.

Halloway sighed, and set down the decanter, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. Outside, gulls were working the quay, and somewhere below a clerk was stamping papers with a sound like a slow heartbeat.

"Mr. Eastwick," he said at last, kindly. "I have hanged better liars than you before breakfast."

"Then hang me," I said, "and hang the pages too, wherever you think they are. Because you'll never find them, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering where they washed up. Every night. Over your supper. In your own handwriting, Master Magistrate, in sums, in dates, in your own fair round signature — because Tobias Marsh kept accounts the way God keeps rain, everything falling where it falls and all of it written down."

The trembling had stopped. I noticed it only then, the way you notice rain has ended. Something had come into me in that room, some cold clean thing, and I understood that it was not courage. It was arithmetic. Bette's arithmetic. I had one thing left, and its value lay entirely in how I spent it.

Halloway studied me. And then — this is the part I would not have believed if I had not seen it — he smiled, sat back, and crossed his legs, and waited. He was a patient man. He had waited three years for a drowned man to stay drowned.

* * *

"You should understand something," he said eventually, into the quiet. "Whatever you think you hold, it cannot hurt me more than it hurts you. If those pages surface, yes, there is an inquiry, a scandal, perhaps worse. But inquiries require witnesses, and witnesses require living apprentices. You are not a threat, Mr. Eastwick. You are an heirloom. I am deciding whether to keep you or break you, and I confess I am finding the decision dull."

"Then let me make it interesting."

I reached into my coat — slowly, showing him my empty palm first, the way you'd show a horse — and drew out the folded page.

Not all the pages. Marsh had given me the confession entire, wrapped in oilcloth still warm from the chapel fire, and somewhere between the chapel and the shingle, crouched in the reeds with Bette's hand gripping my sleeve, I had done the thing I had known since chapter and verse of my apprenticeship that I would someday do: I had divided them. Most were gone — folded into the false back of the chart-case Bette carried, sewn beneath the atlas she would use as her passport in some foreign port. What I held was a single leaf. One page. Enough.

Halloway's eyes fixed on it the way a thirsty man's fix on a well, and for the first time the pleasant pink face went still, and I saw underneath it the other man — the one who had manufactured my father's debt, bought silence by the yard, and let a boatman drown in a story about smugglers. Power looks very ordinary when you meet it at close range. It looks like a tired official wanting his paperwork back.

"What is that?" he asked, and his voice had changed by one degree. That one degree was worth everything.

"The page that names you."

"There is no such page."

"There is," I said. "And I'm going to read it to you."

* * *

I unfolded it by the fire. The paper was good paper — Marsh never used less — and his hand ran across it in the neat descending columns of a ledger, each entry dated, each sum exact, each name abbreviated the way he abbreviated everything, trusting to memory and cipher to fill in what discretion omitted. But I had lived inside that cipher for weeks. I knew his shorthand the way I knew the smell of the workshop: *Hlwy. 4th Sept., forty guineas, *for the quieting of the Ramillies matter*. *Hlwy., Christmas, sixty, *the boatman's people removed to their satisfaction*. And then the entry that had stopped my breath when I first translated it, the one I read aloud now, slowly, while the fire cracked and spat:

"*Item: to Hlwy., the debt of E——, manufactured per agreement, security against M.'s silence. M. declines. Cost: one boatman, one name, two children unruined only by oversight.*"

Two children. Bette and I. *Unruined only by oversight.* He had written us into the ledger like a line item he had failed to pay.

When I looked up, Halloway was watching the page, not me, and his lips had thinned to a grey line.

"Forgery," he said.

"Yes," I agreed. "Almost certainly. A dead man's hand is so easy to counterfeit. Of course, forgery or no, imagine it read aloud in a courtroom. Imagine the questions. Imagine the clerks going back through eleven years of customs ledgers looking for the entries that match." I turned the page toward him, let the firelight fall across the columns. "You can hang me. But paper is patient, Master Magistrate. Paper waits. Someone finds this in twenty years, in a trunk, in an estate sale — and everything you built comes down anyway, when you're too old to run and too proud to hang yourself first."

The clock on his mantel ticked. Below us the clerk stamped his papers. A log shifted in the grate and sent up a fist of sparks.

"What do you want?" Halloway said.

"The same thing you want," I told him. "For this page to stop existing."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about power in the space of one morning, cornered on a shingle and carried inland like contraband: power cannot abide an open question. Halloway could survive scandal, probably; he had survived everything else. What he could not survive was *not knowing* — the page out there in the world, unsigned by time, waiting. Men like him do not fear the knife. They fear the drawer they cannot inventory.

So I offered him the only bargain he could accept without seeming to lose: the page, destroyed before his eyes, by my hand, so that its destruction was witnessed and complete and the question closed forever.

"In exchange," I said, "my father's debt dies with it. Struck from your books. The Eastwick name cleared — quietly, a clerical correction, nothing dramatic. And Bette sails untouched, forever, whatever Voss whispers in your ear."

"And you?"

"I stay. I keep my workshop. I finish charts of coastlines and nothing else." I held the page over the fire but did not yet let go. "Do we agree?"

He considered me for a long moment, and I want to set down honestly what passed across his face then, because I did not understand it until years later. It was recognition. He saw in me the thing he himself was made of: a person willing to burn evidence to buy a life. He had spent thirty years doing precisely that, page by page, witness by witness. He was looking, in that firelit room, at his own reflection wearing a young man's ink-stained hands — and he could no more kill me than a man can strike his mirror.

"We agree," he said.

I did not feed the page to the fire all at once. I do not know why, unless it was that Marsh deserved a proper reading, having written so carefully; or unless it was cruelty, which I would like to deny; or unless it was that Halloway needed to watch every column go, needed the theatre of it, the ritual, the proof performed. I tore it in quarters. The first quarter I read aloud once more — the date, the sum, the *quieting of the Ramillies matter* — and then laid it on the coals and watched the flame take the words in order, letter by letter, the way dusk takes a coastline.

The second quarter burned faster. The third caught sideways and curled like a living thing. With the fourth — the last, the one naming my father's manufactured ruin, the boatman, *two children unruined only by oversight* — I hesitated.

"That entry," Halloway said quietly. "Burn it thoroughly."

"Oh, I will," I said. "But hear me first, and hear me once. My father was honest, and you broke him for leverage. A boatman died for a story you and Marsh wrote together. My master died — is dying, this morning — loving a map more than any of us, which is its own kind of crime, and I'll grieve it my own way. You let me live today because it's convenient. Fine. But convenience runs out, Master Magistrate. Everything burns eventually. Ask the chapel."

I fed the last quarter to the fire and stirred the ashes with the poker until there was nothing but flakes and grey, indistinguishable from the rest of the grate's history. Then I sat back, and found that my hands had begun trembling again, now that it was over, the way a rope trembles after the load comes off.

Halloway rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the quay with his hands clasped behind him — a magistrate surveying his jurisdiction, orderly and unafraid.

"You will receive notice of the debt's discharge within the week," he said. "Your sister's passage will not be troubled. And Mr. Eastwick—" he did not turn around— "you read aloud very well. A pity you wasted it on charts."

I let myself out. No one stopped me. The riders on the quay looked through me as though I were weather.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the water, because my legs wanted distance between me and Graystone Quay and would not be hurried. The tide was coming back in, filling the channels I had memorized and never walked, and the light on the water was the pale gold of a page held near a flame.

At the workshop door I stopped. Through the window I could see the mess the searchers had left — drawers pulled out, pens scattered, the great table stripped of everything but its scars. They had taken the atlas, or thought they had; it was a hundred miles at sea by now, riding in the lining of Bette's trunk. They had taken the drafts, the plates, three years of my master's careful coastlines. Let them have the coasts. Coasts never change, and never tell the truth.

Inside, everything smelled of smoke — the chapel's smoke had followed me in my coat, or the fire's smoke had soaked into my memory of the room, I couldn't say. I gathered my master's pens from the floor, one by one, and wiped each nib, and set them in their stand the way he had taught me: points down, always, so the ink settles true.

Then I sat at the stripped table, and drew a fresh sheet toward me, and began to rule lines.

Not coasts. Not soundings, not lights, not the pretty conventions of the Admiralty. I ruled the frame for a different kind of chart entirely — one with the marsh channels drawn from the margins, the chapel marked where Fen had died holding the door, the shingle where the choice was made, and here at Graystone Quay a small square building with a fire burning in it that no atlas would ever record. An atlas of what actually happened. Private, uncoded, meant for no one living — and therefore the only honest map I would ever draw.

Outside, the evening came on, and the gulls settled, and somewhere down the channel the Kestrel's boat would be pulling away with my sister and my life's work aboard, and I bent over the empty page and began, in a hand that was my own and no one else's, to write the truth.

It was nearly midnight when I heard the letter come through the door.
