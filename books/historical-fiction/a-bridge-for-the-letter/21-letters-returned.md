## 21. Letters Returned

* * *

**The first letter — Bette to Wren, from Vlissingen, in the spring of 1750**

Dear Wren,

Three weeks at sea and I did not once look over the rail without thinking of you below it, on the shingle, getting smaller as the boat pulled away. I have tried a hundred times to write this letter and a hundred times begun with the wrong thing — with grief, or gratitude, or argument, all of which are only love wearing different coats. So let me begin instead with work, which is the language we Eastwicks trust most.

I am employed.

You will not believe how it happened, so I will tell you plainly. The trunk went into my lodgings like any passenger's luggage, and the atlas stayed wrapped in oilcloth beneath my winter things until I could bear to open it. When I did — alone, on a grey morning with gulls quarrelling outside the shutters — I understood for the first time what you had put into my hands. It is not a book of coasts, Wren. It is a book of decisions. Every soundings column, every note on tide-slack and shelter, reads like advice from someone who has stood on that shore in bad weather and lived.

There is a merchant house here — Vandermeer & Zoon — that fits out two brigs a season to the Baltic and has lately been losing them to sandbanks their own charts insist do not exist. Their junior partner, a Mr. Cornelis Vandermeer, is a blunt man who initially mistook me for someone's wife come about an account. I let him. Then I asked to see his charts, and I showed him, plate by plate, where his pilots were being lied to by their own maps — where the channels had walked eastward a half-mile since the survey everyone trusts was drawn. He sat very still. Then he asked who had made the atlas, and I said, *my brother*, and it was the proudest sentence I have ever spoken aloud.

He has engaged me to keep the house's coastal papers in order and to annotate against your plates as new soundings come in. It is honest pay, and lodging above the counting-house, and a window that looks out on water, which I need the way other people need firelight.

The town is strange to me in ways I expected and in one way I did not: nobody knows our name. There is no debt attached to *Eastwick* here, no lowered voice behind me in a shop. I had forgotten a name could be simply a thing you are called.

Tell me the workshop stands. Tell you have kept the fire lit. Do not tell me what you gave up, because I already know, and if you write it down I will have to answer it, and I would rather this first letter be kind.

Your sister,
Bette

*P.S.* — In the margin, in pencil, a line of small figures you will know better than I do. I copied them off the flyleaf before I left, from memory, wanting to carry something of yours that customs men could not read. If I have got them wrong, correct me. Teach me, if you will. It seems right that we should have a language the magistrate cannot follow us into.

---

*The margin figures, transcribed as received:* **IV · VII · II · IX** — *and beneath them, smaller:* **the channel holds**

*Wren's reading, set down the same night the letter came: the numbers fall in the old order Master Marsh used for his private memoranda — chapter, leaf, line, word. The fourth leaf of the seventh quire of the atlas, second line, ninth word. In the finished atlas that line falls on the plate of the Frisian coast. Second line, ninth word: **sound**.*

*Bette was telling him she had found her sounding. Her footing. She was telling him she was safe.*

*He sat with the letter until the candle guttered, laughing once, badly, and wiping his eyes with the heel of his ink-stained hand like an apprentice again.*

* * *

**Wren to Bette, late spring 1750**

Bette,

I have your letter, and your figures, and I have read them both more times than a grown man ought to admit. You got them perfectly right, which frightens me a little — it means you carried the cipher away in memory alone, which means it lives in you now the way penmanship lives in the hand. Good. Keep it there. Never write it where a stranger might see, and never teach it to anyone who has not earned it.

Here is what the margin said back to you, though you likely worked it out: the plate of your own coast answers. Second leaf, fifth line, seventh word. Look when you have a quiet moment and a steady heart.

I am glad of Vandermeer. I am gladmost that no one knows our name. I turned that sentence over for an hour. I had not thought of a name as a coat one might take off.

The workshop stands. The fire is lit. The roof leaks in March but only over the press, as if even the rain respects the tools. Halloway has not troubled me — a man cannot prosecute what he cannot prove, and what he could have proved went into your trunk or into the fire. He passes me in the street now with the smallest of nods, like a chess player acknowledging a draw he does not enjoy. I nod back. We are both fluent in that dialect.

As for what I gave up — you told me not to write it, so I will write around it, the way we were taught to shade a coastline: show the shape by leaving the interior blank. What I gave up was a berth. What I kept was the person who deserved it. There is no arithmetic in that worth doing, because the sum comes out the same in every column, and the column is called sister.

Work well. Annotate boldly. When your own soundings contradict my plates, believe the sea, not me — the sea has never once flattered me to spare my feelings.

Your brother,
Wren

*P.S.* — The pencils figures you sent had a small error after all, though not where you'd fear. The stroke on the second numeral should be doubled — Master Marsh doubled every second mark, a habit from his ledger days. That you remembered them singly means you learned them from watching me, not from watching him. I find I like that lineage better.

**The third letter — Bette to Wren, autumn 1750**

Dear Wren,

Cornelis has done something extraordinary and I must lay it out in order or I will not believe it myself.

The *Vrouwe Anna* came home last month twelve days overdue, and half the harbour had given her up. Her master had followed the house charts, struck ground where no ground was marked, and saved her by a trick of local knowledge he was ashamed to admit in writing. Cornelis convened the whole navigation committee of the house — four men and a parrot, I am nearly not joking about the parrot — and put your atlas on the table beside theirs. He asked me to defend it. Four men, Wren, older than Father ever got to be, and I stood at the head of that table with your plates under lamp-light and argued tide-coefficients with men who have sailed thirty years.

I won. Not because I spoke best — I did not; my Dutch goes to pieces under pressure and I resorted to pointing — but because the atlas was *right*. Where their charts guessed, yours measured. Where theirs hoped, yours had stood on the shingle in the wet and counted the seconds between wave-trains. One of the old masters, a weathered Dane named Skovgaard, went through the Frisian plates twice and then said something I only partly understood and everybody laughed, and Cornelis translated: *This was drawn by a man who loved the coast more than he feared it.*

I cried later, privately, in the counting-house stairwell, and I am not ashamed to tell you because you of all people know that is not grief. It is the thing grief turns into when you feed it and keep it warm.

The house has commissioned a companion volume — the Baltic approaches, surveyed fresh. They asked who might undertake it. I said I knew a hand, and quoted a price that made Cornelis blink, and he paid it without another word because by then he had learned what the atlas was worth.

So: work is coming to you, brother, if you will take it from my hands. I know you will argue that the credit is yours and the commission should come to you direct, and you may write all of that, but consider — it comes to you *through* me either way, and there is a justice in that current which I intend to enjoy.

The autumn here is cold and smells of tar and apples. I have taken to walking the dyke on Sundays with the wind coming straight off the water, and I talk to you while I walk, silently, the way one talks at gravesides — except you are not dead, which makes it either madness or prayer, and I have decided not to inquire which.

Say you will take the Baltic work. Say it in the margin, where the customs men and the magistrates and the whole plain-reading world will see only a string of harmless numerals, and I will stand on the dyke and translate it into yes.

Your sister,
Bette

*Margin figures:* **II · III · VI · XII**, and beneath: **the light still burns**

*Wren's reading: the third leaf, sixth line, twelfth word of the atlas — the plate of the southern marshes, the one they had escaped through by night with Halloway's riders behind them. Sixth line, twelfth word: **home**.*

*And the second numeral, doubled as he had taught her — she had corrected herself, had learned the doubled stroke, had taken the inheritance one generation further from its thief.*

*He wrote the answer that same evening, and then sat a long while with the pen dry in his fist, unable to say the plain word in the plain alphabet, because some words are too heavy to send through the post unarmoured.*

**Wren to Bette, winter 1750–51**

Bette,

Take the commission to Cornelis and tell him the hand accepts. Tell him the hand also requests patience, because a coast surveyed honestly takes as long as it takes, and the sea does not honour deadlines. Tell him the price includes materials and one condition: that every plate carries, discreetly, in the lower corner, a mark of attribution — *Surveyed in the Eastwick method, after T. Marsh.* He died unnamed and unforgiven in the world's ledgers. He will not be unnamed in mine.

Winter here is the long grey kind. I have taken a boy apprentice — yes, really — a dockside orphan named Tam who thieved a chart from a stall and could not explain why, which is the correct reason to steal a chart. He sweeps, grinds pigments, and asks questions in the relentless way you used to, and when I catch him holding a pen wrong I hear Master Marsh's voice come out of my mouth and have to sit down afterward. Grief is patient. It waits years and then disguises itself as instruction.

Halloway has been promoted. Of course he has. Corruption rises like warm air. But he leaves me entirely alone now, and I have stopped flinching at knockers, and some mornings I work until the light goes without once checking the window. Those are the good days. On the others I read your letters.

I read them all, Bette. In rotation, like lessons. Your first taught me my name was lighter abroad. Your second taught me the sea keeps honest accounts. This last one taught me the word I could not send in plain letters, so here it is in ours:

**IX · I · IV · VII** — the ninth quire, first leaf, fourth line, seventh word. The plate of the chapel ruins. Fourth line, seventh word: ***always***.

That is my answer to the light. It burns. It burns because you carry it.

Come spring I begin the Baltic soundings. Come spring, write and tell me the colour of the water off the dyke, because I am drawing coasts I have never seen and I would rather borrow your eyes than imagine them wrong.

Your brother,
Wren

* * *

**The last letter — undated, unsigned, in a hand three years drowned**

It came in February, among a bundle of ship-papers forwarded through Vandermeer's agent, and Wren knew it before he broke the seal — knew it from across the room, from the slant of the address alone, the tall ascenders, the impatient crossbar on the *t*, a hand he had mixed ink for six years and buried once already.

Inside, one page. No salutation. No date. Only the old cipher running down the margin in that unmistakable hand, longer than any message yet, and beneath the figures, in plain letters at last, five words:

*the berth was yours all along, and you gave it well*

He read it standing. He read it sitting. He took the atlas's working draft from the press — the copy, the one that had never gone to sea — and turned with shaking fingers to the ninth quire, first leaf, fourth line, and translated the margin figures one by one, and what they spelled was not a word but a place, and a month, and a request:

*April. The bridge. Come and see what your sister built.*

Wren set the letter down beside the lamp and looked at the hand for a long time — the hand of a dead man, or of a living one, or of someone who had learned, somewhere across the water, to write as the dead.

Then he reached for his coat.
