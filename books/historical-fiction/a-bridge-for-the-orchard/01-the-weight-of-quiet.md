## 1. The Weight of Quiet

The first rule of silence was this: never let them see you counting.

Juniper Ashgrove had learned it at eleven years old, standing in the doorway of her father's print shop while a magistrate's clerk read aloud the new ordinances concerning seditious materials. She had counted the clerk's rings—seven, one for each district of Vellamark—and her father had caught her eye and pressed one finger to his lips, and she had understood, in the wordless way children understand such things, that some arithmetic was dangerous. That noticing itself could be a crime if you were seen to notice.

Twelve years later, she still counted. She simply did it where no one could see.

She counted now, sorting type in the composing stick with her thumbs: forty-one minutes since the shop bell had last rung, which meant forty-one minutes of uninterrupted work before the morning's first customer. She counted the cases before her—eighty-six drawers of leaden letters, each one set by her father's hand or her own—and she counted, without meaning to, the sounds of the city drifting up Tallow Lane: the fishwives crying the morning's catch from the river quarter, the groan of the crane at Marrowdock, the creak and splash of the ferry chains.

And underneath all of it, faint as a watermark, the sound of boots.

* * *

Ashgrove & Daughters—the sign said, though there had never been daughters, only Juniper, and her father had refused to change it after her mother vanished because, as he put it, *the shop will be what it says it is, even if we are not*—occupied the ground floor of a narrow stone house that leaned slightly toward the river, as if listening for it. The front room held the press itself, an old iron beast her grandfather had bought secondhand from a failed almanac printer in Corvane's home district, back when such purchases required no explanation. The middle room held the type cases, ranked floor to ceiling like the shelves of an apothecary, each drawer labeled in her father's small, exact hand. The back room held the stove, the kettle, the two chairs, and the stairs to the rooms above where they lived.

And everywhere, on every surface, the smell of ink and oil and lamp-black, so deep in the grain of the wood that Juniper had long ago stopped smelling it and only noticed its absence, when she traveled to other parts of the city and breathed air that smelled of nothing but river and horse and other people's lives.

Her father worked at the far bench, as he had worked every morning for twelve years, bent over his loupe, cutting punches.

Aldous Ashgrove was not a large man, but he gave the impression of size the way a mountain does—from patience. His hands were broad and scarred, nicked a hundred times by gravers and burnishers, and they moved over the little steel punches with a delicacy that seemed almost devotional. He was cutting a new letter this morning. Juniper could tell by the angle of his shoulders, the particular hush of his breathing. A new letter meant a new face for an old character—a refinement, a small perfection pursued for its own sake, the kind of work no client paid for and no magistrate had ever thought to forbid.

"Your *a* is crooked," he said, without looking up. "Third line."

Juniper looked down at the galley she'd been proofing. He was right. It always unsettled her a little, how he saw things at a distance that she couldn't see close.

"It's the casting," she said. "That batch from Halloran's foundry runs narrow on the left stem."

"Then you should compensate." He straightened, pressing his thumb into the small of his back, and came to stand beside her. Up close she could see the wear of the loupe around his right eye, the pale band of skin where the sun never reached. Fifty-nine years old, and he had spent forty of them making letters. "Compensate, or reject the piece. Never let a bad letter stand because it's easier than replacing it. A printed lie is bad enough. A printed carelessness—" he tapped the crooked *a*— "that's a lie you've agreed to."

"You say that about punctuation."

"I say it about everything." But he smiled when he said it, the small dry smile that lived mostly in the corners of his eyes, and went back to his punch.

This was the whole of their morning conversation, most mornings. This was the liturgy of the shop: the kettle filled and set on the stove, the shutters opened, the type sorted, the proofs pulled, her father's corrections given and received—all conducted in a language so spare that a stranger would have thought them cold, or simple, or both. But Juniper knew the truth, which was that every word between them had been weighed, and the weighing was itself a form of love. They spoke quietly because quiet was safe. They spoke little because little could be denied.

After her mother vanished, silence had been the only country that would have them.

* * *

She had been eleven. That was the year of the Dock Riots, though no one called them riots anymore—they had been edited out of the almanacs and gazetteers, and if you mentioned them in public you invited a visit from men with ledgers who wrote your name down very slowly, watching your face the whole time. Her mother had been a compositor too, trained in the orchard district, and she had gone out one evening in October to deliver wedding invitations to a family in Hallowgate, and she had not come back.

Not *taken*. No one said taken. The word that appeared, eventually, in a printed notice pinned to the Exchange board, was *unaccounted for*, and beneath it, in smaller type, the phrase that had become the government's signature: *citizens are reminded that movements during periods of civil unrest may result in administrative relocation.*

Administrative relocation. As if her mother were a ledger entry that had been moved to another column. As if the column itself were somewhere far away and mildly embarrassing, and no further questions would be taken.

Her father had gone to every office that would receive him, and several that would not. He had stood in queues in the rain. He had written letters—careful, obsequious, grammatically immaculate letters—and received back careful, obsequious, grammatically immaculate replies assuring him that the matter was under review, that records were being consulted, that the citizen Aldous Ashgrove's patience was appreciated. After eight months the replies stopped coming, and her father came home from the Exchange for the last time, hung his coat on its peg, walked into the shop, and began sorting type. He did not speak for three days. When he finally did, it was to tell Juniper the rules.

There were five of them, and he made her repeat them until she could recite them in her sleep.

One: answer every question with less than you know. Two: never be the first to speak in any room. Three: keep your hands busy; busy hands ask for nothing. Four: if soldiers come to the door, be boring. Five—

Five: whatever you notice, do not let them see you noticing.

She had lived by those rules for twelve years. She had gotten so good at them that she sometimes wondered whether the rules had eaten the girl who made them—whether underneath the silence there was still a Juniper, or only the shape where a Juniper used to be, the way a forme holds the shape of letters after they're removed.

She was twenty-three. She had not raised her voice above conversational pitch in six years. There were people in Vellamark—not friends, exactly, but regulars, the fishmonger's daughter, the widow who ordered funeral cards—who believed she was mute. She had never corrected them. Being mute was safe. Being mute was invisible. And invisibility, her father's rules taught, was the closest thing to safety the city offered anyone.

* * *

The bell over the door rang at half past nine—she had been off by four minutes—and Mrs. Quennel came in for the week's broadsheet notices, the ones the Exchange required every business to post: tariffs, curfew amendments, the standing reward for information concerning unauthorized assemblies. Juniper set them while the old woman chattered about her lodger's cough and the price of tallow, and Juniper nodded in the right places and said "yes" and "indeed" and "that will be four coppers," and Mrs. Quennel left satisfied that the Ashgrove girl was a simple creature but honest, and Juniper closed the door behind her and stood with her hand on the latch and allowed herself, for exactly three breaths, to be tired.

It was while her hand was still on the latch that she saw the soldiers.

They were across the lane, at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, and at first she took them for a patrol, because patrols passed through Tallow Lane twice a day and everyone had learned to go on with their work while they passed, the way you continue talking through thunder. But these were not walking. These were measuring.

There were three of them and a man in civilian dress with a clipboard and a length of knotted cord. One soldier held a lantern pole upright against the corner of the cooper's shop; the civilian sighted along the cord, muttering numbers to an assistant with a slate. They stretched the cord across the alley mouth at shoulder height. They measured the width of the lane. They measured, Juniper realized with a slow cold turning in her stomach, the *doorways*—the soldier stepping back, the civilian marking something on his slate, then moving to the next door along, and the next.

Checkpoint frames. She had heard of them being installed in the Weavers' Quarter the previous spring, though she had never seen one. Iron frames bolted into the masonry of a street's narrowest point, wide enough for one person, tall enough that no one could slip under. A gate that turned a street into a throat. In the Weavers' Quarter they had been installed after the strike calls began, and now no one entered or left that quarter without papers, and the queue at morning check stretched past the fountain, and old women stood in the rain for an hour to buy bread on the other side of their own street.

And now they were measuring Tallow Lane. Her lane. Forty paces from her father's door.

She watched them work with her hand still resting on the latch, and she made herself count instead of feel: the civilian marked the cooper's door, then the dyer's, then paused opposite the print shop. He looked up at the lintel. He looked at the shop window, at the sign—*ASHGROVE & DAUGHTERS, PRINTERS*—and Juniper saw his eyes move across it, unhurried, reading. Then he wrote something on his slate and moved on.

He hadn't lingered. He hadn't done anything at all, really. A man taking measurements for the city's safety, writing the names of businesses into a ledger of businesses. Nothing had happened.

But rule five sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: *whatever you notice, do not let them see you noticing.* She stepped back from the window, quietly, and drew the curtain on the door glass, and stood in the dim of the shop with her heart going hard against her ribs.

"Aldous," she said. Her voice came out at its usual pitch, level, unremarkable. Twelve years of practice. "Soldiers in the lane. Measuring for gates."

Her father set down his graver before he answered. That was his way—he finished nothing carelessly, not even a moment. He came to the window and looked out through the gap in the curtain, and Juniper watched his face, which showed nothing at all, which showed less than nothing, which showed the practiced blankness of a man who had also learned the rules and lived by them longer.

"The Weavers' Quarter got theirs in April," he said. "Before the strike votes."

"This isn't the Weavers' Quarter."

"No." He let the curtain fall. For a moment he stood with his hand flat against the type case, the way another man might rest a hand on the shoulder of an old friend, and Juniper saw his fingers move slightly, reading the labels the way Maren Odile read her banned books—by touch, by memory. "The dockworkers voted to strike again on Tuesday. All three bridges. And Corvane has been elevated to the Assize."

Everyone knew Magistrate Corvane's name. It appeared on every enforcement notice in the city, set in a stern condensed face that Juniper suspected, professionally, of being cut by someone who was paid too little and watched too closely. *By order of Magistrate E. Corvane.* His notices had gotten longer that autumn. His rewards had gotten higher.

"He'll want the presses accounted for," her father said, in the tone he used for estimating a job. Flat. Practical. As though he were pricing someone else's funeral. "Every licensed house. What type we hold, what we print, who works here. A gate at the lane means inspection at the gate. Papers going out get read coming and going."

"Then we print nothing that matters," Juniper said, "until it passes."

Her father looked at her then, and something crossed his face too quickly for her to name it—there and gone like a fish under ice.

"Yes," he said. "Nothing that matters."

He went back to his bench. He picked up his graver. And Juniper stood a moment longer by the curtained door, listening to the faint scrape of steel on steel, and understood—with a certainty she had no evidence for, an animal certainty, the kind that lives below language—that her father was afraid, and that he was not telling her everything, and that the fraying thing she had felt all morning at the edges of their quiet was not weather, was not imagination, but the sound of routine tearing somewhere just out of sight, the way fabric tears first along seams you cannot see.

* * *

The afternoon brought the ordinary work that kept them respectable: two hundred visiting cards for a wool factor, hymn sheets for a chapel in Hallowgate, and the job her father had accepted that morning from the bride's mother in Copper Row—one hundred and fifty wedding invitations, cream stock, two colors, the bride's initials entwined in a border of vine leaves that Aldous had designed himself and was visibly pleased by. He set the invitation forme himself, humming—an old habit, tuneless, more breath than music—and Juniper cast a glance at the forme as she passed and felt, briefly, absurdly, comforted. There was no work in the world more harmless than a wedding invitation. Lead and ink and cream paper, announcing that two young people intended, despite everything, to be happy.

The light was failing when the knock came—not at the shop door, which faced the street, but at the yard door, the low one in the back wall that opened onto the tannery path, which no one used anymore since the tanneries upstream had flooded and the path had gone to mud and reeds.

Three knocks. Paused. Then two more.

Juniper looked up from her case. Her father had gone still over his bench—not frozen, exactly, but attentive, the way he went still when a proof revealed a flaw.

"We're not expecting deliveries," Juniper said.

"No."

"Through the yard?"

"Nobody comes through the yard. The yard floods."

The knocks came again. Three, pause, two. And then—faintly, unmistakably—a child's voice, small and worn thin by wind and distance, calling words that Juniper could not make out except that one of them, repeated twice, was *please*.

Her father rose from the bench. He crossed the shop and put his hand on the yard door's bar, and he hesitated there, and Juniper saw him look back once—at her, at the cases, at the iron press standing in the dusk like a sleeping animal—and in that look was all twelve years of the rules, all the arithmetic of safety, the whole careful architecture of their silenced life weighing itself against a child's voice in a flooded yard.

Then he lifted the bar.

Cold air poured in, smelling of river rot and reeds, and standing in the mud with her arms wrapped around a bundle wrapped in oilcloth was a girl of perhaps nine or ten, soaked to the knees, white-faced, her lips blue with cold—and behind her, already retreating into the dark of the tannery path, the sound of running feet.
