## 2. A Child at the Door

The rain came off the river in the last hour before dusk, and Juniper Ashgrove counted its rhythm against the window glass the way she counted everything: silently, precisely, without being asked.

She had been sorting quoins again. It was work that required no thought, which was why she saved it for evenings when her mind would not settle—the small brass wedges tumbling through her fingers like river stones, each one wiped and returned to its drawer in the case her father had built the year she was born. Below her, in the shop proper, Aldous Ashgrove stood at his composing stone with a candle guttering beside him, his broad back curved over a galley of type as though he were praying over it. Perhaps he was. Her father had never been a devout man, but he handled letters the way other men handled relics, and Juniper had long ago decided that setting type was simply another kind of prayer—one addressed not to any god but to whoever might read the words afterward, in whatever world they woke into.

"Junie." His voice came up the stairs, low and roughened by lead dust. "The Kessler wedding order. Bring down the ornament stock, if you've finished fussing up there."

"I've finished," she said, which was true, and came down with the box of fleurons in both hands, careful on the narrow stair. This was the choreography of their evenings, worn smooth as a threshold stone by twelve years of repetition. He worked the press; she fetched and sorted. He spoke; she answered—briefly, evenly, never more than the words required. Neighbors called her the quiet Ashgrove girl, and it was meant kindly enough, but the name fit her like a garment cut for someone else's body. She had not been born quiet. She had been made quiet, stitch by stitch, in the autumn after her mother vanished, when she learned that questions were doors and doors opened onto streets from which people did not come back.

Her mother had asked questions. That was all anyone ever said about it, and even that only in whispers, and only after checking the shutters first.

The shop smelled of ink and tallow and the cold iron breath of the press, that old Albion hand-press her grandfather had bought secondhand from a bankrupted Bible house two cities over. Aldous ran his thumb along the line of type he'd been setting, and Juniper saw him frown—not at his work, which was flawless as always, but at something else. He had been frowning for three days now, ever since the soldiers came through Fetterman's Row measuring doorways.

Measuring doorways. She kept returning to the strangeness of it. Two men in the gray coats of the city garrison with a knotted cord and a slate, walking the row like undertakers taking orders, marking numbers against lintels and writing nothing else. One of them had looked at her—looked directly at her, standing in the doorway of the shop with flour still on her hands from the morning errand—and she had done what she always did. She had become furniture. She had let her eyes go soft and empty and thought about quoins, about the weight of brass in her palms, until his gaze slid off her like rain off oiled canvas. It was a skill she had refined until it hardly cost her anything anymore. That frightened her more than the soldiers did, some nights—that disappearing had become easy.

"Papa," she said, setting the ornament box by his elbow. "Why would the garrison want our doorways measured?"

Aldous didn't look up. His fingers kept moving among the sorts, selecting letters with the blind fluency of forty years. "Bridges need guarding," he said. "Gates need hinges. Everything in this city is becoming a checkpoint, and checkpoints need dimensions same as anything." A pause, the small click of an em quad set in place. "It doesn't mean anything, Junie."

He was lying, or at least he was smoothing the truth the way he smoothed a forme before locking it up, pressing down every irregularity so the surface would print clean. She knew his lies the way she knew his silences, and this one had the particular hollowness of a lie told to protect her. She didn't press. Pressing was asking, and asking was a door, and she had spent twelve years learning to keep doors shut.

Instead she lit the lamp above the stone sink and began cleaning the distribution board, and the evening arranged itself around them the way it always did: the hiss of the kettle, the scratch of his pen as he drafted tomorrow's jobbing work—a handbill for Tuller's seed warehouse, an announcement of a lost dog, the ordinary sediment of a city pretending to be calm—and outside, the rain thickening from whisper to roar as the river swelled in the dark.

* * *

She heard the knock at half past the ninth hour, and knew immediately that it was wrong.

Not because knocks were unusual. Customers knocked at all hours, printers being a trade that lived on other people's deadlines. But customers knocked briskly, twice, with the confidence of people who expected to be admitted. This knock was three strokes, very soft, spaced strangely—as if the person knocking had rehearsed the sound and then forgotten how hands worked. And beneath it, faintly, she heard something else: breathing. Quick and shallow and small.

Aldous raised his head from the composing stone, and across the length of the shop he and Juniper looked at each other in the lamplight, and she watched her father arrive at the same arithmetic she had: too late for customers, too wet a night for neighbors, too soft a knock for anything honest.

He crossed the shop and put his eye to the peephole drilled through the door's iron plate—an old precaution, older than Juniper, installed in her grandfather's day when printers had needed precautions of a different flavor. Whatever he saw made his shoulders change. Not relax exactly. Lower, the way a man lowers himself before lifting something heavy.

He drew the bolts.

The child who stood on their doorstep could not have been more than nine, perhaps ten, and the rain had gotten to her before the light could. She was soaked through in the boneless way of things left out in weather, her dark hair plastered flat to her skull, her shoes—good shoes, once, with small silver buckles—blackened with river mud. She held a bundle wrapped in oilcloth clutched to her chest with both arms, and she gripped it the way drowning things grip driftwood: not as property but as identity, the last solid fact of herself. Behind her, Fetterman's Row stretched empty and silvered with rain, the lamps haloed in mist, the cobbles running black water into the gutter. Whoever had brought her here was already gone—or had never been seen at all.

"Wren," the child said, before anyone had spoken. As if answering a question. Then, when neither of them moved: "My name. It's Wren."

"Come inside, Wren," Aldous said quietly, and stepped back, and the child crossed the threshold of the Ashgrove printing shop like someone crossing a border into a country whose laws she did not know.

Juniper shut the door and shot the bolts, and the ordinary sounds of the night were sealed away, and there remained only the small drowned girl dripping on their floorboards, and the bundle in her arms, and the silence that all three of them seemed to agree upon without negotiation.

Aldous built up the fire in the back room stove while Juniper found a blanket—the wool one, her mother's, kept folded on the high shelf for reasons neither of them had ever discussed—and wrapped it around the child's shoulders. Wren accepted it without thanks, without apparent awareness, her eyes moving around the shop with a strange fixed attention. Not the wide wondering gaze of a child seeing a printer's shop for the first time. Something more deliberate. Searching. As if she had been given a description of this place and was confirming it against the evidence: *the press by the north wall, yes; the staircase, yes; the girl with the quiet face, yes.*

"There's bread," Juniper offered. "And honey."

Wren's stomach answered before she could, a small treacherous sound, and something in the child's composure cracked just enough to show the animal underneath—she took the bread Juniper brought and ate it in quick desperate bites, standing, watching the door between mouthfuls. Watching the door. Juniper knew that watchfulness. It had lived behind her own eyes for years. You learned to eat facing the danger, to sleep near exits, to measure every kindness against the possibility that it might be bait. Whoever this child was, she had not been safe for a while, and she had not been merely hungry either. There was a hollowness in her that food would take days to reach.

Only when the bread was gone and the fire had begun to dry the steam off her shoulders did Wren finally set the bundle down. She did it carefully, kneeling to place it on the floor by the stove, unwinding the oilcloth with the concentration of someone performing surgery. Beneath the wrapping was a second layer, sacking, tied with cord. Beneath that, cloth. Layer inside layer inside layer, and Juniper understood, watching, that the wrapping was not protection so much as postponement—that the child had been unwrapping this thing in her mind for miles, and each fold was a way of not arriving.

At the center lay a rectangle of lead, roughly the size of a psalter, wrapped in a final square of linen.

And a note, written in a fast slanting hand on paper gone soft with damp:

***Forget the face.***

Nothing else. No name, no plea, no instruction. Just those three words, and then, smaller, cramped at the bottom edge of the page as if written after a long hesitation, after the writer had already turned away: ***Burn what can be burned. Read what cannot.***

"What is it?" Juniper asked, though she already knew by its weight, by the particular density of it, by the way her father went still as stone when he lifted the linen away. She had grown up with the substance under her fingernails. She knew lead the way farmers know soil.

"A plate," Aldous said. His voice had changed—flattened out, gone careful. "Locked type. Set and cast whole. Someone made this on purpose, so it couldn't be distributed back into sorts. So it couldn't be... undone."

He tilted it toward the lamp, and Juniper leaned in, and the room seemed to tilt around her.

The plate bore a full page of text, cast mirror-wise in relief, and she had read mirrored type fluently since she was six years old—it was the first alphabet her father had ever taught her, backwards letters on his knee like a game—and so she read it now without turning it, and the words went into her the way a nail goes into wood: all at once, and permanently.

It was a list.

Twenty-two names, set in a dense justified column, each followed by a date and a place. And Juniper knew the names. Not all of them—but enough. Tam Hessel, whose wife sold eels on the Fetterman bridge and had stopped selling them in the spring, and everyone had said *relocated*, that bloodless administrative word, as though the man had been moved like a shipment of hides. The Ostrow brothers, who had worked the ropewalk and been loud men, laughing men, and then were nothing, and their lodging-house landlord had been seen carrying their belongings to the pawnshop in two neat crates. Old Sarei, who had taught three generations of Vellamark children their letters at the charity school by the tanners' gate. Names she had heard spoken in the shop, in the market, in the low furious conversations her father had with other tradesmen when they thought she wasn't listening—names attached, always, to the same official phrase, stamped on the same official notices posted at the gates: *relocated for the duration of the public emergency.* Twenty-two names, and beneath them, in slightly larger type, a single line of attribution, formal as a court record:

*By order of Magistrate E. Corvane, Office of Public Order and Relocation.*

"This is a printing plate," Juniper said, and heard her own voice come out strange, very steady, very far away. "Someone set this page. Someone composed these names. Someone intended them to be printed."

"No," Aldous said, and the word fell between them like a struck bell. He had already begun wrapping the plate again, his hands moving fast, folding the linen over the names as though covering a face. "No, Junie, look at the casting—this isn't a working forme. It was made from one. Cast in a private foundry, a mold taken off original type. Someone made a copy that couldn't be traced to a shop. Couldn't be traced to a press. Do you understand what that means? It means whoever set those names knew what would happen to the shop that printed them."

The stove ticked. Rain drove against the shutters. Wren sat wrapped in the dead woman's blanket, watching them both with those searching eyes, and Juniper felt the floor of the familiar evening tilt and give way beneath everything.

"They'll have killed the compositor," Aldous said softly, almost to himself. "If they catch who set this. They'll have—" He stopped. Looked at the child. Started again. "This is sedition, Juniper. Not the gray kind, not the kind you pay a fine for. This is the kind they hang for. Corvane's name on a list of the disappeared, ready to print—it's proof, and proof is the most dangerous thing in this city, more dangerous than knives. Whoever carried this risked everything, and now it's in my shop, under my roof, with my daughter standing next to it."

Juniper looked at the wrapped bundle by the stove. Through the linen she could feel its shape with her eyes alone, the way she could feel the shape of the press, the shape of the stairs, the shape of every fixed and known thing in her life—which was to say: she could no longer stop feeling it.

Tam Hessel's wife had cried at the fish stall only once, that Juniper had seen, and then never again, and everyone had agreed this was sensible. The Ostrow brothers had been loud men. Loud men got relocated. Old Sarei had taught children their letters, and there had been no notice of trial, no record, no grave—just the phrase, *relocated*, doing its work, sealing twenty-two lives shut like books closed forever.

*Read what cannot be burned.*

"Then we should destroy it," Juniper said, and was surprised at how easily the words came, how practiced her obedience still was after all these years. "Melt it down. Tonight. Before anyone knows it was ever here."

"Yes," her father said, with visible relief, and began planning aloud—the crucible, the tongs, the lead poured into a ingot mold and buried, the child questioned gently in the morning and sent wherever children were sent—and Juniper nodded at all the right places and said yes, Papa, of course, and helped him rewrap the plate and carry it to the back room where the crucible sat on its iron stand.

And all the while, some voice she did not recognize was speaking underneath her own agreement, quiet and clear as a line of freshly set type: *You know how to melt lead. You also know how to print it. Your father taught you both, and he never told you that one of them was a sin.*

She banked the fire. She did not light the crucible. Tomorrow, she told herself. We will do it tomorrow, when we've slept, when the rain stops, when—

When, said the voice underneath, will you next have the chance to read the names?

Aldous went up to bed eventually, his tread heavy on the stairs, pausing at the landing to say, "Lock up well, Junie. And don't—" whatever he had been going to say, he swallowed it. "Good night."

The shop settled into its nighttime sounds. In the corner by the dying stove, Wren had fallen asleep sitting upright, the blanket to her chin, her head tipped against the wall, her small mud-blackened buckled shoes still on, still ready to run. Even asleep she faced the door.

Juniper knelt beside the wrapped plate a long time. The lamp burned low. Outside, the river pushed against the city walls in the dark, patient as everything in Vellamark that waited.

Very carefully, she unfolded the linen one last time, and by the last inch of candlelight she read the twenty-two names through again, slowly, mouthing each one without sound—reading them the way her father set type, with reverence, with precision, with the sense that somewhere, someday, they would be read back.

Then she heard a small voice behind her.

"You're going to burn it," Wren said. She wasn't asleep after all. Her eyes were open, fixed on Juniper across the dark room, and there was nothing childlike in them at all—only a weariness vast and ancient and terribly awake. "Everyone wants to burn it. But my mother said—" Her voice caught, steadied. "My mother said there would be a shop on a street with a green door. She said the people there would know what to do."

Juniper looked at the child, and at the plate, and at the green-painted door with its bolts shot home, and felt the last of her invisibility begin, very quietly, to come apart.
