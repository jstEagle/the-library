## 3. What the Plate Says

The workshop below the shop smelled of lamp oil and cold lead, the way it had smelled every night of Juniper's remembered life. She came down the stairs with the plate wrapped against her chest like something newborn and damned, and her father looked up from his composing stone, and in the lamplight his face did the thing it always did when he was frightened: nothing at all.

"Set it down," Aldous said. "Slowly. On the felt."

She laid the bundle on the green baize of his proofing table and unwound the cloth with hands that wanted to shake and were not permitted to. The plate emerged by degrees—a slab of lead alloy, dense as sin, its face crowded with columns of reversed type that caught the lamplight in little silver teeth. Beside the names, someone had cast a border of laurels, and above them a seal: a bridge over water, beneath a crown.

Juniper had seen that seal before. Everyone in Vellamark had. It was stamped on the tax notices, on the curfew proclamations, on the broadsheets that announced, each season, which gates would be closed and which families would be assessed for the privilege of keeping their shops.

"That's the magistrate's mark," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat, which was how she kept it from breaking.

Her father didn't answer at once. He had taken up his loupe—the little brass lens he used for inspecting type—and was bent so close to the plate that his breath fogged the lead. Twelve years of silence had taught Juniper to read her father the way she read everything else: by posture, by the length of a pause, by what his hands chose to do when his mouth would not cooperate. Now his hands were very still on either side of the plate, hovering, not quite touching it, as if it were hot or holy.

"Aldous," she said. "Whose type is it?"

"Give me the lamp."

She brought it. He angled the light low, raking it across the plate's face so that every letter threw a hair-fine shadow, and this was how he read type—not by the ink but by the shadows, by the tiny imperfections no two casts ever shared. He had taught her that much before the silence closed over him: that type was like handwriting, that every font carried the fingerprints of its maker, the nicks in the matrix, the worn curves, the habits of the hand that cut the punches.

He moved the lens along the first column of names. Then he stopped moving altogether.

"Dad." Her voice again, flatter still. "You know this face."

"I know this face," he said quietly.

And then he sat down. Not gracefully—he simply lowered himself onto the stool as though his legs had resigned without notice, and he pushed the loupe up onto his forehead, and he looked at the plate the way a man looks at a debt collector on his own doorstep.

"It's Halric's work," he said. "Halric Voss."

The name meant nothing to Juniper. She waited. Waiting was the thing she was best at.

"Halric was the finest punchcutter in the eastern districts," her father said, to the plate rather than to her. "Cut a text face fifteen years ago that every shop in the city envied—mine included. Crisp stems. A beautiful, generous *e*. He called it Orchard Roman, because he cut it in the season the orchard workers struck, and he said—" Aldous's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't— "he said a face should be named for whatever it hopes to set. He hoped to set the truth about the strike. He never got the chance."

Juniper looked down at the plate. At the crisp stems. At the generous *e*.

"He was one of the relocated," she said. Not a question. The word *relocated* had lived in Vellamark for four years now, smooth as river stone, worn of all its edges. Workers who vanished. Families who asked and were answered with stamps bearing a bridge and a crown.

"He was the first," Aldous said. "The night before he was taken, he brought me his matrices for safekeeping. I've kept them in the floor safe since. I thought—" He stopped. His thumb moved along the edge of the plate, feeling the bevel, and Juniper watched him arrive at some conclusion the way one watches ice shift on the river: slowly, then all at once. "This isn't a casting from matrices. Look at the bevels. Look at the shoulders of these letters. This plate was cast directly from a composed forme—set in metal, locked up, and pressed into a mold while the ink was barely dry on the proof."

"Which means?"

"Which means whoever made this plate didn't just have Halric's type." Her father's voice had gone very quiet, the quiet he reserved for prices and bad news. "They had Halric himself, setting type. Under orders. In a press room somewhere in this city, there is a private press running Orchard Roman, and the only man alive who could teach it to set properly was taken four years ago to a place the magistrate says doesn't exist."

He turned the plate toward her. The lamplight slid across the names.

"And this press," he said, "this plate proves it exists. Cast from Corvane's own formes. Set in Corvane's own stolen type. A record of every worker his men 'relocated'—in the magistrate's hand, as it were. It is a confession, Juniper. It is a confession cast in lead."

* * *

Aldous Ashgrove had survived twelve years in Vellamark by understanding one thing better than most men understood anything: that the city did not run on cruelty, whatever the pamphlets said. Cruelty was merely the tool. The city ran on the management of evidence. What was written down could be seized; what was seized could be destroyed; and what was destroyed had, in the eyes of the law and of God and of the gray-faced clerks who administered both, never existed at all.

Magistrate Corvane understood this. That was his genius, if you could call it that. He did not kill loudly. He relocated. He filed. He burned paper in proper season and kept the ledgers balanced, and the bridge on his seal was not an ornament—it was a promise. Everything in Vellamark crossed his bridge eventually. Everything flowed toward his records and was lost there.

And now here was the counter-ledger, sitting on his daughter's proofing table, smelling of lamp oil.

Aldous turned it over in his mind while Juniper pretended to study the typeface, and he catalogued, with the cold thoroughness of a man composing a difficult page, everything the plate would cost them.

If soldiers found it here, they would not ask questions. Questions were for people whose guilt was uncertain. They would take the shop, because the shop was seizable. They would take Juniper, because witnesses were seizable. And they would take him to the Graykeep, where men went and from which paperwork occasionally emerged—properly filed, properly stamped, noting the date of death of a seditionist who had been, per the record, processed.

If they destroyed it—if he took it out to the yard tonight and melted it in the smelting pot he used for recasting spoiled type—then none of that would happen. The plate would join the long ledger of things that had never existed. Juniper would grow up. The shop would go on printing wedding invitations and grain notices until one of them died of old age, which was, in Vellamark, an achievement available to almost no one, but which he had privately begun to hope for.

It was the only arithmetic that worked. He ran it three times. It came out the same all three times.

"We melt it tonight," he said.

Juniper's head came up. For a moment—a single unguarded moment—he saw on her face something he had not seen since her mother disappeared: not silence, but the whole roaring thing the silence was holding back.

"No," she said.

"Juniper—"

"You said yourself it's a confession. You said it in his own type, in his own press's marks. There are forty names on this plate, Dad. Forty." She was standing very straight, the way her mother used to stand, and Aldous hated her a little for it, and loved her helplessly, and hated himself for both. "Forty people the magistrate says were moved to the eastern farms. Forty letters that never got answers. If we melt it, they're gone. Truly gone. Nobody will ever even know they were real enough to need destroying."

"They will be real to us."

"To *us*?" Her voice cracked on it, and she mastered herself immediately, jaw tightening, the old discipline snapping back into place—and that, more than anything, frightened him, because he recognized it. It was his own trick. He had taught it to her without meaning to, the way he had taught her to read shadows instead of ink. "We're two people in a cellar, Dad. We'll be real to ourselves and dead anyway. Is that your plan? Keep the memory warm while they process us?"

"The alternative is keeping it warm while they process *you*." He stood, and his knees protested, and he ignored them. "Listen to me. I have spent twelve years making sure that nothing in this shop was worth dying for. Nothing on the walls, nothing in the cases, nothing in the safe under the floor. Do you know what that costs, to arrange a whole life so that it can be taken from you without loss? I did it for your mother, and when they took her anyway, I did it for you. I am not going to undo twelve years of care for a slab of lead, however many names are cut into it."

"However many—" She stared at him. "You knew him. You *liked* him. He trusted you with his matrices."

"And I keep them safe," Aldous said, "by keeping my head down. Which is what we will do. Which is what we have always done." He reached across the table for the plate.

Juniper got there first.

She picked it up—both hands, hugging it to her chest the way she'd carried it downstairs—and stepped back, and the lamplight put her whole face in shadow except her eyes, and her eyes were blazing.

"One night," she said. "Let me hold it one night. Just hold it. You can melt it in the morning if you still want to."

"That's not how it works, and you know it isn't."

"Then how does it work?" The words came out of her fast and low, twelve years of them, all the sentences she had swallowed in this shop while the world outside sealed another gate. "Tell me the rule, Dad. Tell me exactly how quiet I have to be, and how small, and for how long, and what it buys. Because it didn't buy Mother anything, and it hasn't bought Halric Voss anything, and tomorrow morning some child is going to knock on that door upstairs with the next terrible thing in her arms, and I will stand behind you and say nothing, because that is the rule. So tell me what it buys."

The cellar was silent. Somewhere above them, the shop ticked and settled, and beyond that, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of boots on the quay—more boots than usual, moving in step.

Aldous held out his hand. Steady. Reasonable. A father's hand.

And Juniper Ashgrove, who had mastered silence the way other children mastered letters, stood holding a confession in lead and did not give it to him.

* * *

Later—he would never afterward remember whether it was minutes or an hour—Aldous climbed the stairs alone to lock the street door, leaving the plate on the proofing table between them like an unfinished argument. He turned the bolt. He checked the shutter hooks, all six of them, in the order he had checked them for twelve years.

Through the gap in the shutters he could see the quay, and the quay was wrong.

Soldiers. Not passing—*measuring*. Two of them with knotted cords and a slate, walking the line of shopfronts, pausing at each doorway. Measuring doorframes for something. He had seen it done once before, eleven years ago, before the barricades went up in the tanners' row, and he had known even then what the cords were for: timber braces, gate hinges, the skeleton of a checkpoint. They were building the checkpoints in advance. Which meant the order to build them already existed. Which meant the arrests, when they came, would come with paperwork already stamped.

He stood in the dark of his own shop and did the arithmetic a fourth time.

The plate on the table below. His daughter's burning eyes. The measured doorways marching up the quay toward his own threshold, one knot of cord at a time.

Destroy it, said twelve years of careful life. Melt it tonight and sleep.

But the names. Forty names, set in the type of a murdered friend, cast in the private hand of the man who had ordered them erased. Evidence such as this came through a person's hands once in a generation, and usually it arrived already ash.

Aldous Ashgrove pressed his forehead against the cool wood of the shutter and found, to his surprise, that he did not know anymore which way he would answer in the morning. He only knew that the fraying rope of their quiet life had finally begun to part strand by strand—and that somewhere out in the dark city, boots were measuring the width of his door.
