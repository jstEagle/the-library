## 4. The Knock at Midnight

The knock came at midnight exactly, and Juniper had known it was coming for three days.

Not the hour—no one could have told her that—but the fact of it. She had learned to read Vellamark the way her father read type: by touch, by weight, by the small imperfections most people overlooked. For three days the city had been setting its letters wrong. Soldiers had stopped haggling with the fishwives and started counting them instead. The lamplighter on Cordwainer's Row had walked his route twice in one evening, which he only did when someone paid him to keep the streets bright. And on Thursday, a boy of perhaps ten had stood across the canal from the print shop for an hour, doing nothing but standing, until Juniper went to the window and looked back at him and he flushed and fled like a bird startled off a wire.

She said nothing to her father. That was the old habit, the deep one—the silence she had worn for twelve years like a second skin, soft on the inside, impenetrable on the outside. But that night, as they banked the press and swept the floor of lead dust and paper trimmings, she found herself saying it anyway.

"They're going to come."

Aldous Ashgrove did not stop folding his apron. He was a long man made longer by age, all elbows and stoop, with ink worked so far into the creases of his knuckles that no lye would ever lift it out. He hung the apron on its peg behind the door, straightened it twice though it needed no straightening, and only then turned to face her.

"Yes," he said.

"You knew."

"I suspected." He crossed to the workbench where the forme sat—the forme of wedding invitations, forty locked chases of ornamental type waiting for tomorrow's run for the Halloran girl, who was marrying a cooper from the tannery district and wanted silver ink on the borders if Aldous could manage it. "There is a difference between knowing and suspecting, Juniper. Knowing is what gets people hanged. Suspecting is what keeps them alive long enough to be careful." His eyes went to the forme, rested there a moment too long, then moved away. "Have you eaten?"

"Father."

"There's bread left. I put honey on it. You always say you don't want any and then you eat half the loaf."

"Father." Her voice came out strange to her, thin and high, the voice of a much younger child. "What happens when they come?"

He considered the question honestly, which was somehow worse than if he'd lied. She could see him weighing it, the way he weighed a page of type before locking it into the chase—not whether it would hold, but how much pressure it could bear before it buckled.

"They will ask me questions," he said. "I will answer some of them. They will not like my answers. And then they will take me somewhere, probably the Graykeep, because Corvane likes his prisoners close enough to hear the river." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen, that smile—gentle and rueful and entirely without fear. "You will lock the door after them and you will not open it for anyone until morning. Not for soldiers, not for neighbors, not for me. Especially not for me."

"I'm not letting them take you."

"You are." He came to her then, and took her hands, and his were cold and smelled of solvent and thirty years of other people's words. "Listen to me. This is the last lesson I have to teach you, and I need you to learn it faster than you've ever learned anything. A printer who is arrested is an inconvenience. A printer's daughter who fights soldiers in her own doorway is a confession. Do you understand? Everything we've kept safe stays safe only if you are boring. Be dull, Juniper. Be forgettable. Be the grayest little mouse in Vellamark, and live."

"And what do you be?"

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of him—and pressed her hands once, hard, and let go. "I'll be inconvenient," he said. "Someone has to."

* * *

They did not sleep, or rather she did not, and she suspected he didn't either, though when she checked on him past one o'clock he was lying in his narrow bed above the shop with his breathing arranged so carefully that she knew it was performance. She sat in the dark of the composing room with her back against the stone of the hearth and listened to the city.

Vellamark at night had a language. The canals spoke first—water slapping stone, the groan of mooring ropes, the occasional splash of something living under the black surface. Then the bridges: cart wheels on the Old Span, boots on the Ferry Stair, the bell at the customs house that rang the quarter hours whether anyone wanted it or not. Juniper had spent twelve years learning this language because it was the only conversation available to a girl who did not speak. Her mother had vanished when she was seven—taken, whispered, gone, no body and no explanation and no justice—and in the weeks after, Juniper had discovered that silence was not emptiness. Silence was full. Silence held everything: every footstep catalogued, every voice identified, every danger mapped and measured. The loud people of the world heard nothing. She heard everything.

So she heard the wagon at twenty minutes past midnight, and she heard it stop.

Wheels on cobbles, then nothing. No voices yet. She counted—her father had taught her to count, *a compositor sets four ems to the second, count your seconds like ems*—and at eleven seconds the voices began, low, several men, the particular clipped cadence of men receiving orders they already understood. At forty seconds, boots on the alley stones. At sixty-one, the knock.

Three blows, spaced evenly, hard enough to rattle the shutters. Not a neighbor's knock. Not a customer's. The knock of men who expected doors to open because they had never met one that didn't.

Juniper was moving before the echo died. Up the stairs, two at a time, through her father's door. He was already sitting up. In the dark his eyes found hers and there was no surprise in them, only a terrible calm readiness, as if he had been dressed for this moment for years and had simply been waiting for it to arrive.

"The forme," she breathed. "It's still—"

"It's locked and it's wedding invitations and it's going to stay that way." He swung his legs off the bed. "Where did you hide it?"

"Under the invitations. It *is* the invitations." Three days ago, after he had ordered her to destroy the plate and she had promised and lied, she had done the only thing her hands knew how to do: she had composed around it. The stolen plate—that flat slab of cast lead bearing names that should never have been cast anywhere, names of workers officially *relocated* who had in truth been disappeared by order of Magistrate Corvane himself—she had set it into a chase with border ornaments and flourishes and the Halloran wedding text, locked it tight with quoins and furniture until it was indistinguishable from any jobbing forme in any print shop in the city, and racked it with the others. Hiding in plain sight. The first thing her father had ever taught her: *type is honest, Juniper; it says what it says; but the compositor decides what gets said.* She had simply decided differently than he had.

The knock came again. Harder.

Aldous pulled on his coat. He took his time with the buttons, and she realized he was giving her time too, time to arrange her face into something small and frightened and harmless, which required no arrangement at all because it was already true. Only the harmlessness would be the lie.

"Juniper." He gripped her shoulders. His eyes in the dark were wet but steady. "Whatever they ask you, you know nothing. You're a girl who sweeps floors and sets wedding type. Your mother vanished and your father works too much and you have never, not once in your life, been interested in anything bigger than this street. Can you be that?"

"That's what I've been my whole life."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You've been silent your whole life. It isn't the same thing. Tonight you get to choose it, and choosing makes all the difference there is." He kissed the top of her head, once, the way he had when she was small enough to stand under his elbow at the case. "Lock the door. Count to a hundred. Then light a candle and look bored."

The third knock splintered something in the doorframe.

* * *

They came in like water finding cracks—four of them in the shop below, boots tracking canal mud across her father's clean floor, lanterns swinging shadows up the walls until the whole room seemed to writhe. Their sergeant was a heavy man with a waxed mustache and the patient manner of someone who had done this many times and expected to do it many more. Two more remained outside. She counted everything. It steadied her.

"Aldous Ashgrove?" the sergeant asked, though the warrant in his hand plainly said so.

"I am he."

"You're charged with seditious printing. Possession of proscribed materials. Conspiracy against the peace of the city of Vellamark." The sergeant read it in a flat recitation, the way a man reads a bill of lading. "You'll come with us to the Graykeep for examination."

"Examination," Aldous repeated mildly. "By whom?"

"Magistrate Corvane's office."

"Ah." Her father folded his hands. "And may I ask what materials? My stock is all licensed. Every font registered with the Guild since before the girl was born. You're welcome to search."

"We intend to." The sergeant's eyes moved over the shop with professional thoroughness—the cases, the press, the racks of dried sheets, the formes stacked in their rack like sleeping animals. Juniper followed his gaze and felt her heart hammering against her ribs so hard she was certain everyone could hear it. The rack against the north wall. Third shelf. The Halloran forme, locked and innocent, forty wedding invitations wrapped around a dead man's worth of evidence.

A soldier opened the type cases one by one, running fingers along the rows of sorted letters. Another pulled sheets at random and held them to the lantern—ballads, almanac pages, a funeral elegy for the cooper's mother, all of it licensed, all of it gray. The sergeant himself drifted toward the rack, and Juniper watched his hand rise toward the third shelf and thought with perfect clarity: *this is the moment. This is where it ends, and it ends because I was proud, because I couldn't destroy it, because I thought I knew better than—*

"Papa?"

The word was out of her mouth before she knew she'd chosen it. She hadn't called him Papa since she was six years old. She was crying, too, great ugly tears she hadn't decided on, and she crossed the room and buried her face in her father's coat and clung to him with both fists, and she meant none of it and every part of it was true.

The sergeant's hand paused over the rack.

"She's simple," Aldous said quietly, over her head, his voice carrying a lifetime's apology. "Her mother's going broke her. She doesn't understand what's happening. Sergeant, if you have daughters—"

"I have two," the sergeant said, and something in his voice shifted by a degree, a hair's width of weather. "All right. Enough. Pull him out." He turned away from the rack, and Juniper felt the moment pass over her like a shadow crossing a window—there and gone, unrepeatable, purchased with tears she had not planned and might never be able to spend again.

They marched her father to the door. At the threshold he stopped, and looked back—at the shop, at the press, at her, standing small and wrecked-looking in the lamplight with her arms wrapped around herself—and he said, in the tone of a man leaving ordinary instructions for an ordinary absence:

"Mind the press. Don't attempt the silver ink on the Halloran job—it needs a lighter touch than yours yet. And lock up properly, Juniper. There's been thieves on this street."

"I will, Papa."

The door closed behind them. Boots faded down the alley. The wagon wheels ground away over the bridge, toward the Graykeep, toward Corvane, toward whatever examination waited in rooms where the river ran close enough to drown the sound of questions.

Juniper stood in the empty shop for a hundred seconds, exactly as instructed.

Then she lit a candle, walked to the rack, and lifted out the forme of wedding invitations. It was heavier than it looked. It had always been heavier than it looked. She carried it to the press and set it on the stone bed and unlocked the quoins one by one, and when the furniture came free and the top layer of ornamental border lifted away, the plate lay beneath in the lantern light, its cast lines sharp and unforgiving, and she read the names again, all nine of them, the way you'd read a wound.

*I'm sorry,* she told her father silently, wherever he was in the dark of the Graykeep tonight. *I'm sorry I lied. I'm sorry I'm not the mouse you need.*

But even as she thought it, she understood—with the sudden vertiginous clarity of a person stepping off a ledge they'd been standing on for twelve years—that the silence had ended somewhere between the second knock and the sergeant's turning hand. Whatever happened next, she would never again be invisible by nature. From tonight forward, if she was invisible, it would be because she chose to be, the way a blade chooses its sheath.

She locked the plate back into its wedding clothes, racked it, and sat down at the composing stone with a candle and a blank sheet of paper.

If they were going to examine her father in the Graykeep, then somebody in this city owed him an answer. And if the law wouldn't provide one, she knew a trade that had toppled kings before now, back in her father's stories of the old troubles—knew a press, knew a route, knew the orchard district where the underground printers kept their lamps burning behind shuttered windows.

She dipped her pen. At the top of the sheet, in a hand as steady as she could force it, she wrote:

*Bridges sealed. Gates watched. One week, maybe less, before they burn whatever proof remains.*

Then, beneath it, the beginning of a list: every checkpoint she could name, every soldier's post she had memorized in twelve silent years of listening, every route across the drowned and divided city—and beside each one, a question mark, because a plan built on memory alone would fail.

She needed help. She needed people she could not yet name, on routes she had not yet walked, and somewhere in Vellamark tonight they were waiting without knowing it—an archivist who remembered with her fingers, a guard who could not sleep, a child who had not yet told her everything.

Juniper blew out the candle, and in the dark began to plan.
