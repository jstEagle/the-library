## 5. Three Checkpoints

The map of Vellamark hung behind the shop's counter for as long as Juniper could remember — a woodcut Aldous had printed himself in his younger years, back when the city still let a printer make pictures of its own streets. It showed the river Lisse coiling through the city like a dropped ribbon, the eleven bridges stitched across it in fine black thread, the orchard district blooming at the northern edge in a cluster of little printed trees that had faded, over the years, to the color of weak tea.

She took it down on the third morning after her father's arrest, when the ink under her fingernails had finally begun to wear away and there was nothing left of him to touch in the shop except his spectacles, folded on the composing stone where he'd left them, and the forme of wedding invitations with its secret heart.

Wren sat on the floor beneath the type cases, playing a game she'd invented: sorting loose quads and spaces into piles by size, solemn as a magistrate at inventory. She had not asked where Aldous had gone. Children who carried printing plates through dark streets knew better than to ask questions whose answers might need to be forgotten. But she watched everything, and this morning she watched Juniper lift the map from its nails and spread it flat across the imposing stone, weighting the corners with quoins.

"What are you doing?" Wren asked. Her voice was still new to the shop, unpracticed at belonging anywhere.

"Counting," Juniper said.

"Counting what?"

*Soldiers,* she did not say. *Bridges. Ways out.* "Distances," she said instead, and began to work.

* * *

Vellamark was a city built on water and therefore a city built on gates, and whoever held the gates held the city's throat. Juniper had understood this abstractly her whole life, the way one understands weather — but understanding weather is different from standing in it. Now the checkpoints were real things, measured doorways, lanterns burning past midnight, and she needed to know each one the way her father knew his type cases: by weight, by placement, by what it could do in a practiced hand.

She started with the bridges.

There were eleven once; the map said so. But three of them were lies now. The Weavers' Bridge had been closed since autumn, its iron lattice chained at both ends, and the Ferry Steps were guarded by nothing but mud and rumor — no one crossed there since the tolls went up. That left eight working crossings, and of those, four were useless to her: the two nearest the Graykeep would swallow anyone carrying contraband before they ever reached the far bank, and the eastern pair served only the tanners' quarter, which had been locked down entirely since the strikes began. Tanneries made good places for disappearances. Everyone knew it and no one said it.

That left three. Three checkpoints between the print shop in Cooper's Row and the orchard district in the north, where — if Tam the errand boy could be believed, and Juniper had spent a sleepless night deciding he could — there was still a press running in secret, still setting type that the magistrate had not yet found.

Three gates. She drew them in pencil, faint as breath.

The first was Saltgate Bridge, the southernmost crossing, five minutes' walk from her own door. She had passed through it yesterday carrying a basket of laundry, hood up, eyes down, and had counted what there was to count: two soldiers at the near end, one at the far, a brazier, a ledger where names were written down. *Names written down.* That was the part that mattered. A soldier with a ledger wanted your name and your business, and both were easy enough to invent — she had given the name Mistress Parlow and the business of a sick sister, and the soldier had copied it into his book without looking up. But a search was another matter. Her basket had been prodded once, lazily, with a bayonet. If the plate had been under the linens instead of hidden in the wedding invitations at home, it would have been found. She was sure of it. The plate was heavier than it looked. Everything true was heavier than it looked.

The second gate was the Millrace Bridge, mid-city, where the river narrowed between the old grain mills. Harder. There she'd watched from the window of the chandlery for most of an hour yesterday afternoon, pretending to compare candle prices while soldiers turned back a carter and unloaded his cart crate by crate onto the cobbles. They were thorough at Millrace. Thorough meant slow, and slow meant visible, and visible meant every minute she stood in that checkpoint was a minute someone might notice a girl from Cooper's Row who had no reason to be going north.

And the third was the Orchard Gate itself — not a bridge but the old toll arch where Tanner Street met the orchard road, the last mouth before the district closed around you like a hand. She had never been stopped there in her life; it had been a formality, a soldier waving through carts of apples. Now, according to the fishwife who gossiped at the morning queue, it had a garrison. Twelve men, or twenty, depending on how frightened the telling was. A captain who checked papers twice.

Juniper looked at the three marks on the map and felt something cold settle in her chest, and realized, with a strange detachment, that the cold thing was useful. Grief was a fog; it got into everything and made the world soft-edged and impossible to walk through. But planning — counting, measuring, weighing routes against one another like pieces of type in her palm — that was a kind of clarity, and clarity kept her upright. Her father had taught her the trade the same way. *Don't feel the letters, Juniper. Feel their order.* Order was survivable. She would plan until the grief came back, and then she would plan some more.

* * *

On the fourth day she walked the route herself.

Not all of it, and not carrying anything — she was too careful for that, and besides, the plate stayed where it was, buried in the forme of invitations under the false floor of the paper cabinet, with Wren instructed to sit beside the cabinet like a little guardian and scream if anyone touched it. But maps lie by omission. Maps don't show you sightlines, or where a sentry stands in shadow, or which alley behind the chandlery has a second exit. So Juniper put on her plainest shawl and her mother's old gray cloak and became, by deliberate craft, invisible again — the girl nobody looked at, the silence she had spent twelve years perfecting — and she walked.

Saltgate first. She crossed it twice, once northbound, once south, buying chestnuts from the brazier-woman at the far end so the crossing had a shape to it, an errand, a reason. Second crossing, she noted, they searched more often than the first. The soldier with the ledger was relieved at midday by a taller one who wrote nothing down at all — that mattered too. A man who doesn't keep records can't be contradicted by them. She memorized the shift change: midday, and again at dusk, when the braziers were lit and every soldier on the bridge was watching the light change instead of the faces passing through it.

*Dusk,* she wrote that evening, in pencil, in the margin of the map, in a shorthand of her own devising. *Saltgate wants dusk.*

Millrace she approached only from a distance, walking the mill lane with a covered basket as if delivering bread, counting steps from the corner to the bridgehead. Forty paces of open ground with no cover at all. Two sentries at the entrance, and beyond them, on the span itself, a table set up — an actual table, like a market stall — where bundles were opened and shaken out. She watched a woman's bundle of sewing spilled across the planks and kicked, casually, half into the water, and watched the woman gather it with her face perfectly blank, and Juniper understood that Millrace was not a checkpoint but a lesson: this is what we can do, and you will smile while we do it.

But Millrace had one flaw, and Juniper found it because she had been raised to find flaws — in justified pages, in lockups, in the tiny gaps where the truth leaks through. Below the bridge, the millrace turned the wheels of the granary, and beside the sluice-gates there was a maintenance walk, a plank path barely wider than a shoulder, that the millers used when the gates jammed. It ran under the bridge itself, under the table and the searching hands and the ledger, and came up on the north bank through the granary yard. It was flooded ankle-deep and loud as a waterfall and utterly forbidden, and it was the only way across the middle of the city that no soldier was watching, because no soldier believed anyone would be fool enough to use it.

She stood at the mouth of that plank path for a long time, listening to the water thunder through the sluice, feeling the spray settle cold on her face, and thought: *my father would forbid this.* And then she thought: *they have my father in the Graykeep,* and stepped back from the edge, and marked the path on her map with a small careful x.

* * *

The third gate she saved for last, because the third gate frightened her most, though she couldn't have said why until she got there. Saltgate was routine and Millrace was cruelty, but the Orchard Gate was the end of the line — the place where failure would be final, where the plate would pass out of her hands forever, into the orchard district where the underground press waited like a heartbeat under a bandage. Everything funneled there. Every name on that plate had walked through that arch on their way to work. Wren's mother, maybe. Maren's brother — though Juniper didn't know about Maren yet, wouldn't know for days; she only knew the names on the plate, thirty-one of them, which she had copied out by lamplight onto a slip of paper she now carried sewn into her hem, because names were the whole point and the plate alone could burn.

She came at the Orchard Gate from the east, along the cemetery wall, so she could see it without passing through. The old toll arch was still there, honey-colored stone, carved with a motto so worn it read only as suggestion — something about fruit and honest labor. But the arch now bristled. A barricade of carts and barrels blocked the roadway on either side. Lanterns hung in a row like a stage set. Soldiers moved between them in pairs, and at a trestle table in the center, a sergeant in a good coat examined papers with the unhurried attention of a man who enjoyed his work.

And there was no crowd. That was the wrongness she'd been sensing. At Saltgate there was traffic — laundry, chestnuts, gossip, life. Here there was almost none. A cart of turnips, waved through after long inspection. Two women with a coffin.

Juniper went very still against the cemetery wall.

The coffin was cheap pine, shouldered by the two women, and the sergeant looked at it, and looked at a paper one of the women held out, and stepped aside. No search. No table. The coffin passed under the worn arch and the women followed it, and the soldiers returned to their lanterns and their boredom as if nothing had crossed at all.

*Death travels freely,* Juniper thought, and the thought arrived cold and complete, like a piece of type dropping into its exact place in the stick. The dead were the one cargo this city still trusted. Grief was the one passport no sergeant dared question — not yet, not even Corvane's men, because the moment they started turning away coffins, the moment they searched a mourner's burden in front of a crowd, they would have a riot on their hands, and even magistrates afraid of printers were more afraid of crowds.

She filed it away. She did not know yet, standing there with the wind off the orchard road pushing at her back, exactly how she would use it. She only knew that the knowledge had a weight and a shape, and that somewhere ahead of her it would fit into a lock.

* * *

She got home after dark, taking the long way, doubling back twice, the way she'd seen Davor — no. Not Davor. Not yet. The way she'd seen the checkpoint soldiers walk when they didn't want to be followed. Wren had fallen asleep beside the paper cabinet, one small hand still resting on its side, and Juniper eased the door shut behind her and lit only the lamp at the composing stone, low, shuttered, the flame no bigger than a thumbnail.

Then she unrolled the map and looked at what she had made of it.

Three marks in red chalk — she allowed herself red chalk, tonight, because pencil felt too temporary for decisions this large. Saltgate at the south, passable at dusk, searchable, ordinary. Millrace in the middle, impassable above, possible below if she was willing to drown or be caught in the sluice. Orchard Gate at the north, hard as a closed fist, except — her eyes went back to it, again and again — except for the dead.

It wasn't a route yet. It was three problems separated by miles of hostile streets. Any one of them could kill the plan, and the plan was the only living thing left in this shop that mattered: the plate in its hiding place, the names in her hem, her father in the Graykeep with an accusation of sedition gathering around him like frost. Get the evidence north. Get it printed. Let the city read what had been done in its own name. That was the whole of it, and the whole of it required her to become three different liars in a single day, at three different gates, with thirty-one names riding in lead against her body.

She rubbed her eyes. In the corner, Wren murmured in her sleep — a word, half a word, maybe *Mama*, maybe nothing.

And that was when Juniper heard the sound from the street. Not boots. Not the knock she dreaded. Something smaller: a tap at the shutter, light and precise, three times, like someone tapping a composing stick against a stone. Then silence. Then, unmistakably, a voice — low, a woman's voice, speaking not to the shutter but seemingly to herself, in the unhurried cadence of recitation:

*"…Hollis, Anselm. Hollis, Maret. Ivell, Tomas…"*

Names. The names from the plate. Being spoken, softly and in perfect alphabetical order, to her closed shutters in the middle of the night.

Juniper's hand went to the lamp. The voice paused — patient, unafraid — and said clearly enough to be heard through the wood:

"You have her typeface, girl. I would know it anywhere. Open the door."
