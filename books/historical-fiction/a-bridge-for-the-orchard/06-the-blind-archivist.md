## 6. The Blind Archivist

Maren Voss counted her days in shelves.

There were four hundred and eleven of them in the Undercroft Archive, and she knew each one the way other people knew the faces of their neighbors—by breadth and temperament, by the small irregularities that made them singular. Shelf nineteen leaned westward where a mason's apprentice had jostled it thirty years ago. Shelf two hundred and six smelled faintly of beeswax because she had once spilled a tin of polish across its boards and had never quite managed to clean the grooves. And shelf three hundred and forty—the one behind the locked gate at the corridor's end—held everything the Magistracy wanted forgotten, which was to say, it held almost everything worth remembering.

She ran her fingers along the spines now, reading them the way sighted folk read headlines: quickly, hungrily, with the constant fear of missing something. *Ordinances of the River Courts, annotated.* A hymnal with psalms excised and replaced by loyalty oaths—she could feel the paste where pages had been razored out, the paper thinner there, like a mouth missing teeth. A treatise on bridge engineering, banned not for sedition but because the Magistracy feared what citizens might build without permission. She had memorized that one entire, all six hundred pages, over the course of a winter, lying awake reciting load-bearing calculations to the dark.

That was the work, after all. That was the covenant she had made with herself twelve years ago, when the first carts rolled toward the tanneries and the first names vanished from the census rolls: whatever they burned, she would keep. Whatever they unwrote, she would write again inside her skull, letter by letter, in a script no soldier could confiscate.

The other archivists—the sighted ones who worked the day floors above—called her the Blind Index, half in affection, half in the uneasy reverence people reserve for things they don't understand. Maren didn't mind. An index was a useful thing. An index did not weep at night because it could still recite, word for word, the last proclamation her brother Tam ever set in type.

* * *

Tam had been a compositor before he was a statistic.

Everyone in the trade had known his hands. He could set a column of eight-point Baskerville faster than any man in Vellamark and make no more errors than God makes in weather. When Maren was a girl she would sit beneath his frame at the Signet Press and listen to the music of him working—the tick and slide of type being gathered, the soft metallic rain of it—and she had learned her letters that way, by ear and fingertip, before she learned them by any other means. *This is a lowercase e,* he'd tell her, guiding her thumb across a loose sort. *Feel how the eye of it opens toward the light? Every letter wants to face something. That's the whole secret of the craft.*

He had been gone nine years now. Not dead—that word at least would have left a grave, a document, a shape to grieve. Gone the way the Magistracy made people go: *relocated*, the notices said, in a typeface so deliberately bland it might have been designed to insult the reader. Relocated for labor redistribution, pursuant to the Public Order Act, effective immediately. Nine years, and Maren had never once believed it, because Tam had sent her a letter the week before from the striking workers' hall, full of jokes about the smell of the picket-line cookfires and ending, as all his letters ended, with a line pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the groove: *Whatever happens, little star, remember it all.*

So she remembered. It was the only rebellion available to a blind woman with no press and no vote and no weapon but a memory trained across four hundred and eleven shelves.

On the morning Juniper Ashgrove came down the Undercroft stairs, Maren was re-inking her memorized copy of Tam's last public statement—a handbill calling the tannery workers to assemble—by tracing it in her mind while her hands sorted returns into their crates. She heard the visitor three flights up: a hesitation at the top step, the particular pause of someone deciding whether to descend, then footsteps that were quiet in the way of a person long practiced at being unheard. Young. Female. Light on the outer edge of the foot, weight carried forward, the walk of someone used to hugging walls.

And under the sound of her, another sound that stopped Maren's breath: the flat, dense knock of lead against canvas.

A plate. Wrapped in cloth, carried against the body, held the way you hold something alive and dangerous. Maren had not heard that specific weight in nine years, but her ears had been keeping the ledger of it since childhood—the mass of a broadside forme, roughly fourteen inches by ten if it was a standard placard plate, and this one was smaller, denser, set tight. Set close. The way type is set when there are many words and the compositor does not want to waste a single point of space.

Many words. Names, perhaps. Her heart began a slow, treacherous hammering.

"You're below the archive," the girl said from the foot of the stairs, and her voice was low and roughened, the voice of someone who had recently done too much crying or too much swallowing of tears. "They said—I asked at the day desk, and an old man pointed me down. He said you were—" a hesitation, and Maren braced for the usual flinching euphemism— "that you'd know what I was carrying."

"And do you?" Maren set down the crate of returns with deliberate calm. "Know what you carry?"

"I think it's proof." The girl's steps crossed the stone floor, stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that Maren caught ink on her, deep in the pores of her, the kind of ink that lives in a print shop the way smoke lives in a chimney. And under the ink, flour and tallow and fear-sweat. "I think it's proof that people who were supposed to be relocated were killed. But my father says destroy it, and I can't—I need someone to read it who isn't afraid to say what it says."

"Your father," Maren said quietly. "Would he be Aldous Ashgrove."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, like felt. "You know him."

"Every compositor in this city knows Aldous Ashgrove's hands. He cut a new text face seven years ago—*Ashgrove Roman*—and I have read three hundred books through my fingertips since, and I know every letterform that has ever passed through a Vellamark foundry." She extended her hand, palm up, into the space between them. "But that isn't what you brought me, is it. Give it here. Both hands. Like handing over a child."

There was a rustle of canvas, a moment of reluctance she could hear in the girl's breathing—and then the weight settled into her arms, and Maren Voss stopped being an index and became, once again, a sister.

* * *

The plate was cold. Lead always was, at first, until your hands gave it some of your warmth—a transaction Maren had always found fitting, since type took warmth from everyone who touched it and gave back words.

She worked it the way Tam had taught her to work unfamiliar settings: borders first, to find the measure. Fourteen inches by nine and a half—not a standard placard size. Private. Then the margins, to find the compositor's habits. This setter had left generous margins, confident margins, the margins of a man working on his own time with his own materials and no overseer looking over his shoulder. Then the typeface itself, and here Maren went slowly, her fingertips moving across the raised letters like a woman walking through a house she had lived in as a child, touching doorframes, testing whether the familiar things stood where memory had put them.

The serifs came first. Fine, hairline serifs with a slight forward cant, like a person leaning into wind. The lowercase e with an unusually open eye. The capital R with a straight leg kicked back at a defiant angle—Tam had always said a letter's leg told you its character, and this R looked like a man refusing to stand at attention.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. There had never been another face like it, because its maker had refused to copy anyone, and its maker had been dead to the world for nine years.

"This is Tam's type," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth. "Tammas Voss. My brother cast it himself, piece by piece, matrices he cut with a graver by lamplight because he couldn't afford a punchcutter's outfit. He called it *Witness*. He said—" and here the memory arrived whole, unbidden, in his voice, in his laugh— "he said a typeface should be named for what it does, not how it looks. Anyone can make letters that look fine. Witness is the hard part."

Juniper Ashgrove made a small sound, quickly mastered. "Then you can read it."

"I can read anything set by hand. Type has accents, the way speech does. Every compositor misaligns according to their own nature." Maren knelt, laid the plate flat on the reading table, and bent over it, and for the next several minutes the Undercroft Archive held only the whisper of her fingertips traveling line by line, and the sound of her own pulse, loud as a press.

It was a bill of particulars. That was the first thing. Not a pamphlet, not an argument—a ledger dressed as a proclamation, set in the grand manner the Magistracy reserved for its own documents, which meant someone had wanted it to look official long enough to be believed. It listed dates. It listed quantities. *Forty-one persons conveyed from the Tanners' Row holding, night of the ninth of Frostmonth, under order of—*

Maren's fingers stopped. They stopped the way a bird stops mid-flight, all at once, and she knelt there with her hand hovering above the line because she already knew what the next words were; her fingertips had touched the tall ascender and the doubled vertical strokes and some part of her brain that never stopped reading had assembled it in the dark.

Under order of Magistrate Edmun Corvane. By means of the Graykeep ferry. For disposition at the flooded quarries.

Disposition. Such a tidy word. The same word that appeared on the inventory slips she filed upstairs, applied to damaged books: *disposed of by pulping.* Forty-one persons, pulped at the quarries, and the order signed in a private typeface by a man who had thought himself so far beyond accountability that he'd set the evidence in his own hand like a signature on a love letter.

"There's more," Juniper said softly. "Read the end. Read the end, please."

Maren moved her fingers down. The final block was different—smaller setting, hurried, the margins tightening the way a man's handwriting tightens when he knows he is writing against time. And the letterforms changed subtly, the alignment growing human, imperfect, urgent. This portion had not been set by Corvane's house compositor. Someone else had taken the plate and added to it.

Someone who had stolen Corvane's own type to accuse him.

*Set by the hand of T. Voss, of the Signet Press,* the closing line read, *who sets these names in Witness that they may outlive the men who silenced them. If you read this, you are the witness now. Pass it on.*

Nine years. Nine years she had recited his last handbill to the dark, and all the while he had been ahead of her, casting a trap for a magistrate out of his own beautiful alphabet, hiding it somewhere in the city like a coal banked under ash, waiting for a pair of hands to carry it the last mile.

"He was at the tannery strike," Maren said. Her voice cracked down the middle and she let it. "He was the first name struck from the rolls. Before your Wren's mother, before any of the others—they took him first because he could set type, because he could make the strike *legible*. They thought taking him would blind the rest." She laughed, or sobbed, or both; from the outside they sounded alike. "Instead he left them a mirror with their crime printed in it."

* * *

When she could speak steadily again, Maren rose, walked the length of shelf three hundred and forty by touch, and unlocked the gate at its end with the key she wore on a cord around her neck.

"What's back there?" Juniper's footsteps followed, hesitant at the threshold.

"The burned books," Maren said. "Or rather, the burned books' ghosts. Everything the Magistracy has confiscated and destroyed in thirty years, I have memorized. Seditious almanacs. The strikers' bulletins from Tanners' Row, all eleven issues. Tam's handbills. A history of the orchard district written by a woman who died in the last quarantine, naming every family that worked those rows." She drew out the thin folder she kept there—not many physical pages survived down here; paper was the most dangerous material in Vellamark—and laid her palm flat upon it. "I am the only remaining edition of perhaps two thousand volumes. When I die, they burn again."

Juniper was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the careful flatness she'd carried down the stairs. "My mother used to say something like that. About my father's type. She said a letterform is just a memory that learned to reproduce itself."

"She sounds as though she'd have liked Tam."

"I never knew her. She vanished when I was five." A beat of silence, and then, with the terrible matter-of-factness of the young: "Relocated. That's what the notice said."

*Relocated.* The word landed in the archive like a coin dropped down a well, and Maren heard it fall all the way, past every shelf, into the place where she kept her own grief. Twelve years old and orphaned by a filing term. And still she had come down those stairs with a stolen plate against her chest, asking strangers to read what she could not bear to leave unread.

"Listen to me, Juniper Ashgrove." Maren reached out and found the girl's shoulder—found her flinch, and did not withdraw. "You came here tonight believing you were asking a favor. You were not. You were delivering a debt nine years overdue." She pressed the plate back into the younger woman's hands and closed the girl's fingers over it with both of her own. "Corvane's men will burn this archive the moment it becomes inconvenient—my sighted colleagues will help them carry the lanterns, because that is what fear does to institutions. But this—" the lead, warm now between their four hands— "this cannot be memorized away. It must be *printed*. In quantity. On every corner where his notices were posted, so that the same eyes that read *relocated* read *murdered*, in the same alphabet, and understand."

"That's why I came to you and not to the resistance cells," Juniper said. "Wren's note said—someone wrote, before they wrapped it—*take it to the ones who keep words.*"

"The ones who keep words," Maren repeated, and something old and patient in her chest turned over like soil. "Yes. That's us. That has always been us." She tilted her head, listening to the girl's breathing, quick and shallow, running on a fuel that could not last. "Now tell me the rest. All of it. Your father in the Graykeep. The checkpoints. The child. You've been carrying this alone through a sealed city, and you'll carry it no longer—not while there's an archivist breathing in Vellamark. I know every tunnel document, every smuggler's route ever described in confiscated literature, every bridge in this town by the sound the water makes under it. And I know something else you'll need."

"What?"

Maren smiled, and knew it must look fierce, because Juniper drew a small breath. "Tam's *Witness* face exists in only two places in the world. One is in your arms. The other is in the Graykeep—because Corvane kept a specimen book of every typeface his victims ever worked in. He collects them." Her grip tightened. "Which means when we print the truth in Tam's letters, magistrate, we'll be printing it with the very matrices you catalogued from the men you disappeared. You built your own indictment, sir. We've only to pull the proofs."

Somewhere above them, boots crossed the archive's day floor—heavy, unhurried, two pairs at least, the tread of men who had decided the building was theirs. Juniper went rigid; the plate clinked softly against its wrapping.

"They've started coming already," Maren murmured. "Down the back stair, quickly. And on the way, you'll tell me where the underground press keeps itself—because if we mean to reach the orchard district before Corvane burns the evidence and everyone who holds it, we have until dawn to plan the road."
