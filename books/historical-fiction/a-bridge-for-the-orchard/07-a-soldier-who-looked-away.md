## 7. A Soldier Who Looked Away

The lanterns along the Quay of Sighs were lit at dusk, as they always were, and Davor Stane counted them the way other men said their prayers. Eleven. One had gone dark three nights ago and no one had come to relight it, which meant either that the lamplighter was dead or that he had decided, in his own quiet way, to stop pretending the city still needed its rituals. Davor thought about it more than he should have. A man who lights lamps for a living, and one night simply doesn't. He understood the temptation.

He stood at the mouth of Tallow Street in his gray coat with the brass buttons polished dull on purpose, because shine drew eyes and eyes drew questions, and he watched the third door on the left the way he had been watching it for two evenings now. *Ashgrove & Daughter, Printers.* The sign hung by one chain, the lettering gold once but gone the color of old honey, and beneath it a window full of printed specimens—wedding invitations, mostly, sheets of them pinned in neat rows like moths to a board. Somewhere behind that glass a girl was alive, and somewhere behind her a plate of type sat hidden that could hang every man who had stood on the weir bridge eleven months ago.

Including him.

Davor shifted his weight and felt the ache in his knee where he'd been struck by a rifle butt years before, in some border squabble no one remembered, fought over some river crossing no one could find on a map. The knee always spoke first when the weather turned, and the weather tonight had turned toward rain—a fine cold mist beading on his collar, running down the crease of his neck. He didn't move out of it. He had decided long ago that discomfort was a form of payment, though he could never have said to whom the debt was owed, or when he intended for the account to close.

Eleven months. He measured everything against it now. Before, he had been a soldier in the way most men are soldiers in Vellamark—not a zealot, not a believer, just a second son of a tanner's widow who needed wages and a coat and something to do with his hands besides cut leather. He had taken the oath with the flat indifference of a man signing a lease. He had stood gates and checked papers and confiscated contraband cheese and once, memorably, an entire barrel of smuggled canaries that sang through their confiscation hearing and made even the magistrate laugh. It had been, for seven years, an honest enough living done in small dishonesties. Every profession has them. Bakers short-weigh. Priests doze. Soldiers look away.

That was all the eleventh month of last year had asked of him. To look away.

They told the gate watch nothing, that was the thing he returned to in the dark hours—the *nothing*. No orders read aloud, no lists posted, no explanation offered beyond a sealed envelope with Corvane's seal and a single line: *Night movement authorized. Weir Bridge. All traffic to pass unexamined between the second and fifth bells.* He had assumed smuggling, at first. Grain, perhaps, or arms, or some magistrate's mistress being moved discreetly upriver. Men in gray coats learn early that curiosity is a luxury item, priced beyond their wages. So when the carts came—flat-bedded, canvas-covered, rolling heavy and riding low—he had lifted the lamp, seen the seal, and stepped aside. And when the second cart came, and the third, and he heard from under the canvas a sound he would spend the rest of his life failing to unhear—a sound like many people trying very hard to be quiet, because they had learned that quiet was what kept them alive—he had lifted the lamp, seen the seal, and stepped aside.

Twelve times he stepped aside. He had counted the carts because counting was the only rebellion available to a man holding a lantern, and twelve was a number that had lodged in him like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

By morning the story was already written: the striking workers of the orchard district, the ones who had walked off the ropewalks and the tanneries and the print-houses, had been *relocated*. Relocated where, no one said. Notices appeared on the bridge posts in fine engraved type, thanking the departed for their service to the city's harmony. And Davor Stane, who could read well enough, understood with a clarity that never once left him, even in dreams—even especially in dreams—that he had held the light while they were carried past into the dark, and that the light itself had been his contribution.

A man can live with a great deal if he tells the story right. *I didn't know. I followed orders. What could one lantern do?* Davor had tried all three tellings and found each one rotted through, like boards over standing water. The truth was simpler and heavier: he had known, in the way the body knows before the mind consents to. He had heard people under the canvas. People trying to be quiet.

So now he stood in the rain outside a printer's shop, guarding no one, ordered nowhere, off shift and out of uniform in everything but the coat, and rehearsed—for the fourth time, the fifth—what in God's name he was going to say.

* * *

He had learned about the girl from the apprentice.

That was the shame of it, that his first thread into the Ashgrove shop had been betrayal dressed as gossip. Three weeks ago, drinking off-shift at the Anchorstone with Pell Harrow, the Ashgrove boy—sharp-faced, quick-fingered, always a little hungry-looking around the eyes—Pell had leaned across the table and said, too casually, *Your magistrate's got an interest in Aldous Ashgrove these days. There's a piece of type missing from a certain private press, and word is it landed somewhere it shouldn't have.* And Davor had felt his whole body go still, the way water goes still over something enormous moving underneath, and had said, *Word from who?* and Pell had smiled the smile of a man trading currency and said, *Word costs nothing to repeat.*

Davor had bought him three more drinks and understood three things by closing time. First: the missing plate was real, and Corvane wanted it back with the particular patience of a man who always, eventually, got what he wanted. Second: the plate had passed through the hands of a child, because Pell let slip the phrase *the little bird* and then went pale at his own tongue. Third—and this was the thing that had brought him here, standing in the wet dark like a penitent outside a church whose religion he didn't hold—if Corvane knew the plate existed, then the Ashgroves were already dead. They simply hadn't been informed yet. The Graykeep worked slowly, but it worked surely, and it never worked halfway.

He could have said nothing. That was, God knew, his oldest skill. He could have finished his drink, saluted his fellows, walked home to his room above the chandler's, and let the machinery grind forward without him, exactly as it had ground forward on the weir bridge while he counted carts. No one would ever know he had known. That was the terrible arithmetic of looking away—it cost nothing, ever, except everything you were going to become.

Instead he had spent two evenings watching the shop, confirming what he needed to confirm: that the girl still came and went, that the soldiers had taken the father to the Graykeep four nights ago on charges of sedition that were really charges of *eventually*, that the shop's lights burned late and the window of wedding invitations stayed pinned in its neat, defiant rows. And that no one had followed him here, because no one was watching him. Davor Stane had spent seven years making himself the kind of man nobody watched. For the first time, the talent felt like an inheritance rather than an evasion.

On the third evening, the rain thickening, the lamplighter still absent from his dark eleventh lamp, Davor crossed the street and knocked.

* * *

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap—young, gray-eyed, level in a way that startled him, because he had expected fear and found instead assessment. She looked at his coat. She looked at his face. She took longer over the second than the first.

"Soldiers don't knock," she said.

"Soldiers kick," Davor agreed. "I'm off duty."

"Off duty isn't a thing. You're wearing the coat."

"It's the only coat I own." Which was true, and also a kind of confession, and he watched her understand both layers of it. "My name is Davor Stane. I stand the third gate—the tanners' gate, the one before the flooded yards. I need to speak with you, or with your father, and every minute I stand on this street doing it, we're both in worse danger. So either chain me out or let me in, but choose quickly."

The eye studied him a moment longer. Then the door closed, the chain rattled, and it opened wide enough for a woman—not quite a girl—to step back and admit him into air that smelled of ink and oil and lead, a smell so specific and so old to him that his chest hurt, because his mother's brother had been a compositor in Gorse Lane before the trade died, and the smell of a print shop was the smell of a childhood kitchen he'd forgotten he'd had.

She did not offer him a chair. She stood by the workbench with a composing stick in her hand, holding it the way another woman might hold a knife, and said, "Say what you came to say."

Up close he could see the family in her face—the same long jaw as the prisoner Aldous, whom Davor had processed into the Graykeep and therefore would never forget—the same economy of expression, everything pared down to necessity. But there was something else, a quality he'd noticed in certain people who had survived being overlooked for a long time: she watched *him* watching, catalogued his hands, his boots, the set of his shoulders, and missed nothing. A person like that, he thought, had been made by hard instruction. He wondered what had taught her silence and decided it was none of his business tonight.

"Eleven months ago," Davor said, "twelve carts crossed the weir bridge between the second bell and the fifth. I held the lamp. I saw the seal and I stepped aside, and I heard—" His voice did something he hadn't authorized; it caught, roughened, started again. "I heard what was under the canvas. I've stood that gate seven years. I know what grain sounds like. I know what arms sound like. This wasn't either."

The composing stick did not lower. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because four nights ago I watched soldiers take your father on charges that mean nothing except that Corvane wants something you have. Because the man who drinks with my regiment buys his beer with information, and sooner or later the information he sells will finish what the arrest started. Because the orchard burns next—I've heard the requisitions for lamp oil, triple measure, marked *agricultural*, and nobody requisitions agricultural oil in winter." He stopped himself. He was saying too much, faster than he'd planned, the words coming up like water finding cracks. Eleven months of held silence, breaking against a stranger's bench.

"You were there," she said. Not a question.

"I was the light," Davor said. "There's a difference, and it's smaller than I'd like, and I've had eleven months to think about how small." He reached inside his coat—slowly, showing his hand the whole way, because he knew exactly what a hand moving inside a coat meant to a woman alone in a locked shop—and drew out a folded paper. He laid it on the bench between them. "The third gate. My gate. I stand it four nights from now, the midnight watch, and the rotation puts me on the causeway side with only Haldrin beside me, and Haldrin sleeps standing up and sees nothing he doesn't have to. If you have something that needs to cross the river, it crosses that night, or it doesn't cross at all—because after that the rotation changes, and the new men aren't sleepy, and the oil requisitions arrive."

She looked at the paper without touching it. He had drawn the gate from memory—the sentry positions, the timing of the patrol rounds, the stretch of flooded tannery yard behind the wall where the drainage grate gave onto dry footing at low water. It was, he knew, a map of his own treason, written in his own hand. Somewhere in the Graykeep there was a cell with his name reserved on it, and he had effectively signed the register tonight.

"What do you want for it?" Juniper Ashgrove asked.

And there it was. The question he had walked three evenings in the rain to reach, turning it over like a coin he couldn't read. What did he want? Absolution? He'd earned none. Forgiveness? Not hers to give—the dead under the canvas had never met him, and strangers cannot pardon you for what you did to other strangers. Safety? Passage out of the city when it broke? He had considered all of it on the walk over, testing each price and finding it false currency.

"There's a price," Davor said. "But not tonight."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. If I named it now, it would be a small thing, because small things are what a man allows himself when he hasn't yet decided how much of himself he's spending." He looked at the rows of wedding invitations pinned in the window, absurd and lovely against the night, and then back at her. "When I know what the price is, you'll hear it plain, and you can refuse it, and I'll still open the gate. That's the offer. I know it's a poor bargain shape. I'm not a bargainer. I'm a man who counted twelve carts and has been counting them ever since."

For a long moment she said nothing, and he stood in the smell of ink and lead and let her decide, because letting other people decide was the discipline he had set himself—the opposite, the exact opposite, of stepping aside with a lamp.

Then, from the shadows at the back of the shop, where he had honestly believed there was only stored paper, a small voice said: "Juniper. He cried. Just now. When he talked about the carts. I saw his face."

Davor closed his eyes. Of course. The child. The little bird, sleeping—or pretending to sleep—in the place children slept when home was no longer safe, listening to everything, the way children in dangerous cities learned to do.

"The price," the small voice said, from the dark, in the tone of a magistrate far older than her years, "is that you don't get to feel better. That's what it costs. You help us, and you still did what you did, forever."

Davor opened his eyes and looked at the shadow where the voice lived, and felt something in his chest turn over—not relief, exactly, and not dread, but the strange settling weight of a thing finally put down after being carried too long.

"Yes," he said quietly, to the child, to the woman, to the twelve carts. "That sounds right."

Juniper Ashgrove folded his map and put it inside her shirt, against her skin, where important things go. "Midnight watch, four nights," she said. "Come back tomorrow and tell me what you haven't told me yet—about the oil, about the requisitions, all of it. And Davor Stane—" she paused at the door, hand on the chain, and for the first time her voice carried something that was almost, almost warmth, "—if they ask how you found us, lie beautifully."

He was nearly to the corner, rain streaming, the dark eleventh lamp above him still unlit, when the thought arrived with total clarity, the way the worst thoughts did: Pell Harrow had sold the Ashgroves' secret for beer and pocket money. And Pell drank at the Anchorstone, and Pell talked, and Pell knew that Davor Stane had gone quiet and strange three weeks ago and had been seen, more than once lately, walking toward Tallow Street.

Behind him, the print shop light went out.

Ahead, in the window of the Anchorstone, Pell Harrow raised a glass to someone in a gray coat that wasn't Davor's.
