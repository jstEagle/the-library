## 8. Wren Speaks

Wren counted the cracks in the ceiling of the loft above the print shop the way she used to count the stitches on her mother's apron — not because it helped anything, but because her fingers needed a task and her eyes were the only fingers she had left to work with.

There were eleven long cracks and one that branched like a winter tree. She had named them. The branching one was Mother, because it split into two directions at once, going everywhere and nowhere, the way her mother's voice did when she sang while shelling peas.

Below her, through the floorboards, the shop breathed its night noises: the tick of the iron press cooling, the sigh of paper stacks settling, Juniper's boots crossing and recrossing the same six feet of floor. Juniper did not sleep much either. Wren knew this without being told, the way she knew rain coming in her knees. Two sleepless creatures in one house made a kind of company, even with a floor between them.

The bundle sat where it always sat now, behind the loose brick near the cold hearth. Wren could feel it from here, or imagined she could — a dull heaviness under the floorboards, like a stone swallowed by the house itself. Lead had a presence. Her mother had taught her that much about letters before she taught her anything else. *Every letter is a small weight,* she used to say, pressing a piece of type into Wren's palm. *You can hold a whole sentence if your hand is strong enough.*

Her hand had not been strong enough. That was the whole of it, wasn't it? That was why she lay awake counting cracks instead of sleeping like a child ought to sleep.

She had not told them. Six days in this shop and she had told them nothing but her name, and even that felt borrowed. Wren. A bird's name, a nothing name, a name you'd give a child you wanted to be able to fly away. They hadn't asked many questions. That was the strange mercy of the Ashgroves — Juniper moved through the world like someone who understood that some silences were load-bearing walls, and pulling at them would bring the whole roof down on both your heads. And the father, Aldous, was gone now, taken in the night by soldiers with gray gloves, and the house had been holding its breath ever since, and still no one had asked Wren to explain herself.

But tonight the questions were in the room before Juniper climbed the ladder.

Wren heard her come up — the careful weight of her, the way she stepped on the third rung and the seventh because the fifth groaned — and pretended to be asleep. This was an old trick. It worked on nobody.

"You're awake," Juniper said. "I heard you turn over twice."

"I was turning for a reason."

"Insomnia has reasons?"

"The crack above my head moved."

"It didn't." Juniper crossed the loft and sat at the foot of the pallet Wren had been given, folding her legs under her the way she seemed to do everything — precisely, quietly, as though taking up less space were a craft she'd spent years perfecting. In the light of the single lamp turned low, her face looked younger than it did downstairs. Softer around the mouth. "I couldn't sleep either. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That the company might help."

Wren pulled the blanket up to her chin. Down in the street, somewhere far off, a boot-heel struck cobbles in the rhythm of a patrol, three-and-turn, three-and-turn, and they both went silent until it passed.

"They're going to kill him," Wren said. It came out smaller than she meant. "Your papa. The magistrate. Everyone knows what the Graykeep does."

"No one knows what the Graykeep does. That's rather the point of the Graykeep." But Juniper's hands had gone still in her lap, and Wren saw the flinch she'd tried to fold away. "He isn't going to die. He's too useful alive. Corvane wants him to confess to something, or make plates, or simply to stop existing as a free man. My father is very good at being quiet. He learned it the same place I did."

"Where'd you learn it?"

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. The lamp guttered, righted itself.

"After my mother went away," she said. "When you live with a hole in the house, you learn to walk around it without looking down."

Wren's chest did something painful and complicated. *A hole in the house.* Yes. That was exactly the shape of it. She had been living inside a hole for six days, and before that inside a different hole, and the holes were all the same hole really, just wearing different rooms.

"She didn't go away," Wren said. "Did she. Like my mother didn't go away."

"No," Juniper said softly. "She didn't go away."

The words sat between them like the plate itself — heavy, leaden, impossible to hold and impossible to put down.

* * *

Here is what Wren had been carrying besides the plate, and it weighed more:

Her mother's hands had been shaking when she pressed it into them. Not trembling — shaking, the full-arm shake of a person who has already decided something terrible and is only catching her body up to the decision. They had been in the kitchen of the dye-house dormitory where the relocated workers' families were kept, which everyone called the Holding House and no one called home. It was past curfew. The lamps were out except the stub of one on the sill, and Mother had been out — out-out, over the wall, into the meeting at the tannery yard where the strike committee still dared to gather — and she had come back wrong. Pale as unprinted paper. Wet-eyed and dry-mouthed at once, somehow.

She had knelt down on the kitchen stones so their faces were level. Mother never knelt. Kneeling was for scrubbing and praying and she claimed to do neither.

"Wrenny," she'd said. "Listen to me the way you listen to nothing else in this world."

And then she had pushed the wrapped bundle into Wren's arms and it was heavier than it looked, heavy like a sleeping animal, and through the oilcloth Wren had felt the ridged edges of it and known — not understood, but known, the way you know a stove is hot — that it was type. Cast type. A whole plate of it, which was wrong in every direction, because plates like that didn't belong in kitchen bundles, they belonged in presses, and presses belonged to the magistracy, and her mother was a dyer's assistant with ink-stained cuticles and no business at all touching anything cast from a magistrate's private works.

"There are names on it," Mother said, and her voice cracked down the middle like the crack in the ceiling Wren would later name after her. "Names of people the papers say were relocated. Do you know what I'm telling you?"

"No," Wren had lied, because she was ten and lying was easier.

"Yes, you do," Mother said. "You're cleverer than me and always have been. There are names on this plate that no press in this city will print, because the man who owns the press is the man who made those people stop existing. Corvane's own foundry. His own letters. It fell into our hands tonight and there are men who died getting it out of his cellar and I will not let it sit in mine waiting to be burned with everything else." Her hands closed over Wren's, over the bundle, squeezing until it hurt. "You take the canal path. You know it — the towpath, all the way to the old city, to the print shop with the ash-tree sign. Ashgrove. The printer there is honest, or as close as this city grows them. You give it to him or to his daughter and then you forget my face. You forget this kitchen. You forget—"

"I won't."

"You will," Mother said, and kissed her forehead, and her lips were cold, colder than the room, cold like she'd already begun leaving. "Forget me, Wrenny, because remembering is dangerous now, and I would rather have you alive and forgetting than—" She stopped. She stood up. She became, in the space of one breath, not Wren's mother at all but somebody brisk and hard and efficient, somebody Wren had never met. "Canal path. Don't take the road. If anyone stops you, you're delivering washing for the widow at Tanners' Row and you cry if you have to. Cry well. You've always cried well."

"I don't want to deliver washing. I want to stay with—"

"Doors," her mother said, and it was the last word she ever spoke to her daughter, standing there in the lamplight with ink on her cuticles and a future she'd already traded away folded up inside her apron pocket where the pea-shells used to go. "Doors open and doors close, and you're going through one now, and I love you more than every door in the world."

Then the back gate, then the wall, then the dark, then the towpath with the water sliding black beside her and the bundle against her chest like a second heart, heavy and stubborn and beating its own strange beat.

She had not looked back. That was the part that broke her open at night. Her mother had told her to run and she had run, obedient to the last, and she had not looked back, and so the last picture she had of her mother was a doorway with light in it and a woman standing straight-backed in front of it, getting smaller, getting smaller, gone.

They said afterward — the neighbors who weren't taken, whispering through fences — that the Holding House had been emptied two days later. Relocated again. Further south. Nobody said the other word, the truer word, but Wren was ten and cleverer than everyone believed, and she had read the names on the plate by candlelight in a stranger's shop, and one of them was her mother's.

* * *

"And that's how I got it," Wren finished, and discovered her face was wet and her voice had held almost to the end. "She said forget her face. On the note too, the note wrapped round it — *forget the face.* I thought it meant the soldiers'. But it doesn't. It means hers. She wanted me to survive her by forgetting her, and I can't, and I won't, and I'm sorry, but I won't."

Juniper had not moved through any of it. She sat absolutely still, the way people sit at gravesides, giving the grief its full measure of ground. But her eyes were bright and wet in the lamplight, and when Wren finally ran out of words, Juniper reached across the blanket and took both of Wren's hands in both of hers. Her grip was cool and firm and did not let go.

"You listened," Wren said, surprised. "All the way through. Most grown-ups start fixing things halfway."

"I'm not most grown-ups." Juniper swallowed. "My mother went into a crowd twelve years ago and never came out of it. No note. No bundle. No door with light in it. Just absence. I used to think a goodbye — even a horrible one — would have been a kind of gift." Her thumb moved over Wren's knuckles, back and forth, an unconscious rhythm like a press at work. "Now I think your mother gave you the hardest gift there is. She trusted you with something worth more than her own safety. Worth more than yours, even. She decided the names mattered more than everything, and she put that decision into a ten-year-old's arms because she couldn't carry it herself anymore."

"I dropped it once. On the towpath. When I slipped."

"Did anything break?"

"A corner. A little bent. The E at the end of 'Esterhaze.'"

"Esterhaze," Juniper repeated, and something in her voice changed — sharpened, settled, became purpose. "That's one of the names. One of the relocated."

"My mother's name is on it too. Halven. Sera Halven." Saying it aloud in the dark loft, to another person, for the first time since the doorway — Wren felt the name leave her body and become real in the air between them, solid as type, permanent as ink. Her breath hitched. "Nobody says the names anymore. At the Holding House they'd correct you. *Relocated workers*, they'd say, like it was a town you could visit."

"Well," Juniper said, and now she leaned forward, and the lamp caught her face and Wren saw that the quiet girl downstairs — the one who moved like silence itself — had a fire banked somewhere behind her ribs that had been waiting all these years for exactly this armful of fuel. "Then we're going to say them."

"Say them where? Everyone whispers here. Everyone hides."

"Not forever. Listen to me, Wren." She squeezed Wren's hands, hard enough to mean it. "This shop is a print shop. That plate is proof — real proof, cast from Corvane's own foundry, and my father confirmed it before they took him. Names, dates, orders. Everything he's spent years erasing, sitting in lead behind a loose brick ten feet below us. Right now it's hidden and silent, and hidden and silent is exactly what Corvane wants, because hidden things can be forgotten and forgotten things never happened at all." Her voice dropped lower, fiercer. "But that plate was made to speak. Type exists for one reason. It exists to be read. And I am going to get it to a press — a real one, a free one, in the orchard district — and every name on it is going onto paper, hundreds of sheets, thousands, and they'll be scattered across this whole rotten beautiful city by morning. And when they read it — when people stand in the squares and read it aloud, and they will, people always read such things aloud — your mother's name will be in their mouths. Sera Halven. Said by strangers. Over and over. Louder than Corvane and all his gray-gloved men combined."

Wren stared at her. Something enormous was happening inside her chest, some structural shift, like ice breaking on the canal in early spring — painful and cracking and impossibly, recklessly hopeful.

"You promise?" she whispered. It was a child's word and she hated it even as she said it, but there was no bigger word for the thing she needed.

"I promise," Juniper said, and did not look away, and did not soften it, and Wren understood that this was the first true thing anyone had promised her since a doorway with light in it.

They sat like that until the lamp burned low. Then Juniper rose to bank the light, and Wren burrowed deep into the blanket, and for the first time in six days sleep came toward her like something friendly.

It was almost upon her when the sound started below — not the shop's familiar breathing at all, but something sharp and wrong: the scrape of the loose brick being pried aside, followed by Juniper's voice, low and urgent, drifting up through the floorboards along with a stranger's measured tread.

"Wren," Juniper called up, quiet as a closing door. "Wake up properly. There's someone downstairs who says he guarded the gate the night they took the tannery workers — and he says he's seen our faces before."
