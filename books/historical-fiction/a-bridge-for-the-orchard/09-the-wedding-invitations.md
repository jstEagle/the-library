## 9. The Wedding Invitations

The plate had lived three days inside the forme, and Juniper had begun to dream in its language.

She slept badly now—that was nothing new—but when sleep came, it came as *type*. She dreamed in reversed letters, in the strange mirror-world of the compositor's stick where every word must be built backward so that it might read forward. In her dreams the names on the plate rearranged themselves into wedding blessings, into the looping formalities of an invitation she had never been asked to design for anyone who was actually going to be married. *Mr. and Mrs. Ellery Vance request the honour of your presence.* The words sat in her mind like stones dropped into still water, and beneath them, always, she could feel the other words pressing up—the true ones, the heavy ones—*Halla Voss, present. Tomas Bright, present. Ren Ivo, present.* Present. As if the plate itself were a kind of invitation, summoning the disappeared back into the world one pulled sheet at a time.

She rose before light, as she always did, and went down into the shop with a candle stub cupped in one hand.

The Ashgrove print shop had changed since her father's arrest. Not visibly—Juniper saw to that. The type cases still lined the north wall in their oaken tiers, each drawer labeled in Aldous's small, precise hand. The press still squatted in the center of the floor like a patient beast, its iron screw polished by forty years of palms. But something had gone out of the room, the way warmth leaves a house when a clock stops. Her father had wound himself into every gear of this place. Without him it ticked only because Juniper remembered how he used to wind it.

She knelt at the stone table where the forme lay locked in its chase—the iron frame that held the type fast—and ran her thumb along the quoins, checking the pressure as he had taught her when she was nine years old. *Firm, not fierce, little wren. Wood splits under fierceness.* Underneath the layer of wedding-invitation type, wrapped in a sheet of soft waste paper and hidden among the furniture of spacing and leads, lay the plate: lead-heavy, cold even in summer, cast from a press that had no business existing outside the magistrate's private rooms.

Corvane's foundry mark was on its reverse—a small stamped coronet above the letter C—which was precisely what made it proof and precisely what made it death to be caught holding. Aldous had wanted it melted down within an hour of decoding it. *A plate is not justice, Juniper. A plate is just lead until someone is left alive to read it.* She had nodded and said yes, Father, and had done nothing of the kind, and every morning since she woke with the same question sitting on her chest like a cat: *how long can you keep being two people?*

The answer, as it turned out, was eleven days. That was when the knock came—not the soft double-rap of a customer but the flat, official percussion of a fist that expects doors to open for it.

* * *

It was Tam Wilder who brought the warning, breathless, sliding through the alley door with his cap in his fist and his eyes too wide. Tam was the shop's apprentice, sixteen and all elbows, and he had been Juniper's shadow for four years, learning the trade the way she had learned it: silently, hungrily, by watching hands.

"Miss Ashgrove." He swallowed. "There's a requisition wagon two streets over. Graykeep livery. They're stopping at every printer on the register. Halvorsen's already been turned out into the street—he's standing in his own doorway watching them carry his galleys off."

Juniper's hands did not shake. She noticed that they did not shake, and was distantly surprised, the way one is surprised by weather. "How long?"

"Minutes. Less."

"Tam." She caught his sleeve. "Listen to me. You've swept the floor and banked the fire and you know nothing about any plate or any forme beyond what's on the stone. Can you do that?"

"I'm not—" His jaw worked. "I'm not a very good liar, miss."

"Then don't lie. Be confused. Confusion is honest. Go."

He went, and Juniper stood alone in the shop with perhaps two hundred heartbeats between herself and ruin, and she made the choice that would define the rest of her life in the space of one of them.

She could hide the plate again—under the floorboards, down the privy, in the flour bin. She had considered each hiding place a dozen times and rejected each, because soldiers who search printers' shops know printers' shops, and a loose plate is a thing that catches candlelight. No. If they came, let them find exactly what a lawful print shop ought to contain: a job in progress, dull and domestic and utterly unremarkable. Let the plate sit where no searcher looks twice—inside the work itself, locked under twenty pounds of legitimate type, invisible because it was buried in plain sight beneath the most boring commission in Vellamark.

The wedding invitations. Someone's actual wedding—she'd taken the order a month ago from the Vance family, prosperous wool merchants, and had set the first forme before everything happened and had simply never pulled the proofs. Well. Now the proofs would get pulled.

Her fingers flew. She had set half the invitation already; now she finished it, assembling the reversed characters in the composing stick with the speed her father had drilled into her until speed and accuracy stopped being separate things. *Mr. and Mrs. Ellery Vance request the honour of your presence at the marriage of their daughter Celestine to Master Odo Ferris, on Saturday the nineteenth day of September.* She justified the lines, slid the type into the forme around the hidden plate, locked the quoins, inked the platen, and began to pull sheets—crisp white rag paper, one after another after another, laying them across the stone to dry in a fan of pale, elegant, entirely harmless evidence.

By the time the door opened she was dusted to the elbows in ink, hair escaping its knot, looking precisely like what she was: a tired woman finishing a tedious job.

There were four of them. Three soldiers in the gray-and-black of the Graykeep, muskets sloped, boots loud on her father's clean floor. And behind them, unhurried, came the inspector.

He was softer than the soldiers—middle-aged, comfortable, with the pallor of a man who worked indoors and the careful eyes of a man who counted things for a living. He wore the sash of the Magistrate's Office of Print and Registry rather than a weapon, which told Juniper he was more dangerous than any of them. A soldier could break your bones. An inspector could break your license, your ledger, your father's case file, your whole legal existence.

"Mistress Ashgrove." He consulted a leather folio. "Daughter of Aldous Ashgrove, master printer, currently detained at his Majesty's pleasure. This establishment remains registered in his name, which makes you, I suppose, the acting custodian." He smiled without warmth. "I am Inspector Quillon. We are conducting a survey of printing materials. Sedition has a way of hiding in shops like this one, between the psalters and the price lists."

"My father would want us to cooperate fully," Juniper said, and heard, with a small cold pride, how steadily the sentence came out. Twelve years of silence had taught her voice to be level under anything.

"And yet he sits in the Graykeep on a charge of sedition." Quillon let that hang in the air, watching her face the way a man watches water for fish. "Curious trade, printing. Everything a printer does is visible, isn't it—ink on paper, plain to see—and yet everything that matters happens invisibly, beforehand, in the setting of the type. A man can print a psalm and a proclamation of treason in the same hour, and the paper cannot tell the difference." He drifted toward the press. "You may carry on working, mistress. Don't mind us."

That was the worst thing he could have said. Juniper inclined her head, returned to the stone table, and kept pulling sheets while three soldiers began to turn her home inside out with methodical courtesy.

They went through the type cases drawer by drawer, running gloved fingers along the ranks of lead letters, lifting out sorts and letting them clatter back. They pulled books from shelves and riffled them by their spines, feeling for hidden pages. One of them pried at the floorboards near the hearth and found only coal dust. Another took the stair to the living quarters overhead, and Juniper listened to his boots cross the ceiling above her head, directly over the room where Wren sat—where Juniper had put Wren ten minutes ago, under the bed with her thumb in her mouth and instructions to be a stone, be a stone, be a stone.

*Don't look at the forme,* she told herself, and so looked at it, casually, the way a worker looks at work. It lay there under the drying sheets: a locked rectangle of type and wood, and inside it, wrapped in waste paper and cushioned in leads, the weight that could kill everyone in this building. She had considered prying it loose and dropping it in the river cistern in the last minutes. She had decided against it in the same breath, and the reason was simple and terrible: the plate was the only reason her father might live. Melt it, sink it, lose it, and Corvane needed Aldous for nothing. Keep it, deliver it, and it became a lever long enough to move a magistrate.

So it stayed, and she printed wedding invitations over it, and her hands were steady.

Quillon had come to stand beside the stone table. He picked up one of the drying sheets between finger and thumb, tilting it to the window light, and Juniper felt the whole world narrow to the width of that single sheet of paper.

"The Vance-Ferris wedding," he said, reading. "Nineteenth of September. Handsome work." He turned the sheet slightly. "Though I notice your justification is uneven here in the third line. See—the word-spacing opens up toward the margin. Careless setting, or hurried?"

And there it was. The moment her father had spent twelve years preparing her for without either of them knowing it.

"Hurried," Juniper admitted, and let her shoulders drop a fraction, let exasperation color her voice like any journeywoman embarrassed before an expert. "My father usually corrects my justification himself. He's particular about word-space—says you should be able to draw a pencil line down a page of even setting and touch every gap. With him gone, the apprentice and I are doing our best, but—" she gestured at the forme, inviting him to inspect it, *please, inspect it*— "the third line fought me and I let it win. I'll reset it before delivery."

Quillon looked at her. Then he leaned over the forme, and Juniper's heartbeat filled her ears like surf.

But he did what any man of the registry would do: he read the visible type. *Request the honour of your presence.* He checked the spelling against the copy taped to the frame. He pressed his thumbnail against a quoin, testing the lock, and the plate beneath shifted less than a hair's breadth because Juniper had packed it in leads like a mummy in linen. He saw exactly what she had built him to see: a slightly imperfect invitation, locked and ready, boring as bread.

"You know type," he said, straightening, and there was something almost wistful in it. "Your father's faces are famous. The old Ashgrove text especially—there are men in the Graykeep who could identify it blindfolded, by feel alone." He closed his folio. "We're finished here. Mind you fix that third line, mistress. Weddings forgive many things, but not bad spacing."

"Thank you, Inspector. I'll tell my father you were thorough."

"Do." At the door he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the street light. "When you write to him—and detainees are permitted letters, if you didn't know—perhaps mention the wedding. The date, the families' names. Little domestic news. Detainees find such letters comforting." A beat. "Or don't. It's of no consequence to me either way."

The door shut behind them. The boots receded. Somewhere above, the soldier's footsteps crossed the ceiling a last time and went down the outer stair, and then Vellamark's ordinary noises came seeping back—cart wheels, a gull, the river doing what the river always did.

Juniper stood without moving until Tam crept in through the alley door, white-faced, and Wren's small feet appeared on the ladder from upstairs, and then, and only then, her knees informed her that they had opinions about the last quarter hour. She sat down hard on the stone bench amid her fan of drying invitations and laughed once—a strange, cracked sound she barely recognized as coming from herself.

"He saw the third line," she said to no one. "Of everything in this shop, he saw the third line."

* * *

Wren climbed into her lap without asking, the way she had begun to do in the past few days, a small bony weight smelling of soap and fear slowly leaving a body. Tam hovered. Outside, the afternoon went on being an ordinary afternoon, which felt like impertinence.

"What did the letter mean?" Tam asked quietly. "What he said about writing to your father."

Juniper had turned it over four times already while she sat waiting for her legs to return. *Mention the wedding. Or don't. Of no consequence either way.*

"It means he knows Aldous reads nothing that comes into the Graykeep unless it's copied first," she said slowly. "It means whatever I write will be read by Corvane's clerks. It means—" and here the shape of it settled onto her, cold and clear, "it means Corvane wants me to know he's listening. That was the message. The whole raid was the message. *We can walk into your shop whenever we like, and leave again, and there is nothing you can do.*"

"But they didn't find it," Wren whispered into Juniper's collar. "Did they?"

"No, little bird. They didn't find it."

"Because you hid it in the wedding papers."

"Because I hid it in the wedding papers."

Wren considered this with the grave attention she gave everything. "That's clever," she pronounced at last, with a child's terrible sincerity, "like a beetle hiding under a leaf that looks exactly like the beetle."

Juniper pressed her face into the girl's hair so neither of them could see the other's expression, and thought: *my father taught me that a compositor's first duty is to make the reader see only the words and never the type. Today I made a magistrate's inspector see only a wedding and never a weapon.* It was the first thing in eleven days that felt like winning, and it tasted like iron.

Tam was re-inking the brayer, restless, and he paused with the roller halfway up his wrist. "Miss Ashgrove—if they're surveying every printer on the register, they'll reach the ones south of the canal tomorrow. And the orchard district presses aren't on any register, but the men who run them still buy ink and paper from suppliers who talk." He hesitated, choosing words like sorts from a case. "If Corvane is tightening the net around the trade, he's not hunting invitations. He's hunting the underground press. And if the plate is ever going to reach it—"

"I know, Tam."

"We can't wait for safe anymore. There isn't one coming."

She knew that too. She looked down at the drying sheets, twenty identical futures for a wedding she'd never attend, and beyond them at the locked forme with its buried dead, and understood with sudden perfect clarity that the next days would demand more than silence and steady hands. They would demand allies. A route. Someone on the inside of the checkpoints, someone who knew the bridges.

As if the city itself had heard her thinking, a knock came at the alley door—tentative, twice-repeated, nothing like the Graykeep's fist. Through the fogged glass she could make out a broad silhouette in a soldier's greatcoat, standing in the dusk with its hands empty and its head bowed, like a man who had been walking past this door for a long time and had finally stopped.
