## 10. Corvane's Offer

The Graykeep smelled of wet stone and old decisions.

Magistrate Elandor Corvane had occupied the same corner office for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned that the building itself did half his work. Prisoners arrived already broken by the stair—three hundred steps of narrowing corridor, each landing a little darker than the last, so that by the time they reached his door they believed the darkness had been waiting for them all their lives. He had never raised his voice in this room. He had never needed to. The room raised it for him.

He sat now behind a desk of blackened oak that had belonged to three magistrates before him and would belong to three after, and he read the morning reports the way he read everything: twice, slowly, with a pencil held loosely between two fingers as though it were an afterthought rather than an instrument. The pencil was the true scepter of Vellamark. Every name it touched was either entered into a ledger or struck from one, and Corvane had long ago stopped distinguishing between the two acts. A man struck from a ledger was simply entered into another. There were ledgers beneath ledgers. That was the whole architecture of order.

"Twelve years," he said to the room, though no one else occupied it. "Twelve years the Ashgrove shop has sat on Tanner's Row printing wedding invitations and market bills, and not once—" he tapped the report with the pencil, gently, the way one taps a sleeping animal "—not once has Aldous Ashgrove given me a reason."

It was, he reflected, almost admirable. Almost a rebuke. In nineteen years he had catalogued every press in the city: the great state works by the river with their steam-driven cylinders; the chapel presses that turned out hymnals with suspicious margins; the little hand-presses hidden above chandlers' shops like mice in wainscoting. Each one had eventually produced something worth a visit from the Graykeep's men. Each one, in time, had taught him its owner's weaknesses—a brother owed money, a daughter with a cough, a temper, a debt, a taste for drink. People were legible. That was the first principle of governance, and Corvane held to it the way other men held to scripture: *people are legible, if you read them long enough.*

Aldous Ashgrove had been the exception. Twelve years of reading and the man remained, infuriatingly, a printer. Nothing but a printer. Ink under his nails, a widower's stoop, a reputation for beautiful typefaces and no politics whatsoever. Corvane had even, in the fourth year, arranged a small test—a commission for pamphlets whose text contained, buried in the third paragraph, a sentence that could be read two ways if one wished to read it that way. The returned proofs had been flawless. Not a single letter altered. Either Ashgrove had not seen the second meaning, or he had seen it and printed it faithfully precisely because he understood that fidelity was the deeper defiance. Corvane had never determined which, and the not-knowing had sat in him for eight years like a stone in a boot.

And now this.

He drew the second report toward himself and looked again at the sketch his inspector had made: the child, Wren, small for her years, dark-haired, seen at the window of the print shop on the morning after the relocations. Seen by a neighbor who had been paid to watch Tanner's Row since before the child was born. And below the sketch, the line that mattered—the apprentice, Pellam Ostrow, twenty-two years old, ambitious, frightened in exactly the correct proportion, sitting across from Inspector Hask in a tavern back room, saying: *there's a bundle came into the shop the night the girl arrived. The master burned tallow late over it. I saw the shape of it through the door glass. Plate-sized.*

Plate-sized.

Corvane set down the pencil and laced his fingers and looked out the window at the river, which ran brown and patient beneath the morning fog, carrying its barges of grain and coal as though the city were still an ordinary place where ordinary things happened.

He knew about the plate. Of course he knew about the plate. One did not run a private press—one did not run *any* press—for eleven years without understanding what a plate was and how it traveled and what it cost to make one go missing. The night of the relocations, when the wagons had gone out to the orchard district and come back empty, when six names had been transferred from the census rolls to a file kept in his own strongbox, Corvane had conducted an inventory of his private works with the calm of a man checking his spoons after a dinner party. And the mold for the commemorative plate—the one cast to honor the mill settlement, the one that had never been commemorated because settlements, in the end, were not commemorated—they had found the mold. They had found the mold and they had not found the plate that had been pulled from it.

Someone in his own house had taken it. Someone with access, with hands, with a reason. He had narrowed the possibilities to four servants and one clerk, and then he had done nothing at all, because doing something would have confirmed that there was something to do. Better to let the plate travel. Stolen things wanted to move. Stolen things moved toward the only people foolish enough to shelter them.

And here it was, or nearly here: a plate-shaped bundle on Tanner's Row, and a printer who had spent twelve years being no one, and a daughter whom the files described in a single sentence Juniper Ashgrove had written herself, years ago, on a school examination: *I am quiet, and I notice things.* Corvane had laughed aloud when Hask brought him that paper. *I am quiet, and I notice things.* The girl had announced her danger to the entire bureaucracy of Vellamark and no one had bothered to read it. He had filed it himself, in the thin folder marked ASHGROVE, J., which now contained three pages, and which he suspected would soon require a thicker one.

There was a knock. Two knocks, spaced evenly—the interval of a man who respected doors.

"Enter."

Inspector Hask came in the way he always came in, sideways, as though apologizing to the frame for using it. He was a long, gray man with a voice like a drawer closing. Corvane had chosen him fifteen years ago from a field of candidates precisely because he was unmemorable; a memorable inspector collected enemies, and an unmemorable one collected only information.

"The prisoner has been settled," Hask said. "Third level, west cell. He asked for ink and paper. To write to his daughter, he says."

"To tell her what?"

"He didn't say, sir."

"He wants to tell her to destroy it." Corvane said it mildly, the way one identifies a bird by its song. "That is what fathers say in cells. *Destroy it, forget it, keep yourself safe.* It's practically liturgy." He rose and went to the window. Below, in the exercise yard, two soldiers walked the perimeter in the gray light, their shadows long and obedient. "Which means we must ensure his message never arrives in any form she can act upon. She will receive letters, of course. Letters are important. A daughter must believe the channels are open, or she does something creative."

"You mean to offer terms, sir. For the plate."

"I mean," Corvane said, turning, "to be reasonable. Reasonableness is the most terrifying instrument we have, Hask, because it cannot be argued with. A threat invites courage. An offer invites arithmetic. And printers"—he allowed himself the ghost of a smile, the first of the day—"are arithmetical creatures. They count everything. Impressions per hour, sheets per ream, letters per line. Give them numbers and they will sit down and calculate until dawn. Give them a cause and they will march. So we will give Aldous Ashgrove numbers."

He returned to the desk, drew a sheet of the Graykeep's heavy cream stock toward him—he used the good paper deliberately; cheap paper was a threat, fine paper was a fact—and began to compose in his small, upright, merciless hand.

*To Aldous Ashgrove, printer, Tanner's Row — currently guesting at His Majesty's pleasure.*

He paused over the phrase, enjoyed it, kept it.

*You will forgive the informality of these quarters. The city has grown anxious—it does that, in autumn—and anxiety requires certain temporary arrangements that I trust you understand better than most, having lived so long by the principle of minding your business.*

*Here is my business, briefly. An object belonging to my office has gone astray. Objects of this kind have a way of finding their way to sentimental people, and sentimental people, in my experience, suffer more than guilty ones, because guilt at least knows what it has done. You have a daughter. I have read her schoolwork. She writes a fine clear hand and describes herself as quiet. Quiet girls do not do well in the Graykeep. The Graykeep is loud.*

*Return the object—unmelted, unaltered, complete—and you will walk home by Friday. Your accounts will be reviewed and found clean; they always were, but the finding matters. Your shop will be left to its invitations. This is not generosity. It is bookkeeping. I dislike open entries on my ledgers, and you, Master Ashgrove, are presently an open entry.*

*If the object is not returned within six days, the entry closes. I will not insult you by describing how. You are a careful reader. Read carefully.*

*—E.C.*

Corvane read it through twice, adjusted one word (*sentimental* became *sentimental*, unchanged; he considered and rejected *foolish* as too crude), and sanded it. Then he took a second sheet, smaller, cheaper—deliberately, pointedly cheaper, the kind of paper a stationer sold by the hundred—and wrote a note of an entirely different character.

*Juniper Ashgrove.*

No title. No courtesy. Just the name, and beneath it:

*I know what came through your door. I know what your father would ask you to do with it, and I know what you are deciding to do instead, because you wrote once that you notice things, and noticing is a disease with only one cure. Six days. Bring me the plate and you may keep your silence—I find silence useful in a woman and will even employ it, should you ever want work. Keep the plate, and I will teach you the difference between quiet and silenced. Ask the orchard district which word they prefer.*

*—E.C.*

He sanded both, sealed the first with the Graykeep's arms and the second with plain wax, no seal at all—an unsigned thing could always be disavowed, and disavowability was the soul of correspondence—and handed them to Hask.

"The first goes to the prisoner. Let him read it with his supper. The second—" he weighed the small envelope on two fingers, "—the second is to be delivered to the shop by someone who looks like a courier and isn't. Have him watch the door for a quarter hour before and after. Note everyone who comes and goes. If the soldier Davor calls there again, I want the hour, the direction he came from, and whether he was walking like a guard or like a man."

Hask wrote it down. He always wrote everything down; it was why Corvane tolerated his breathing.

"And the apprentice? Ostrow?"

"Pay him a little," Corvane said. "Not enough to live on—enough to need. A man paid enough stops informing; a man paid too much starts inventing. Keep him hungry and grateful and ashamed, in equal parts. Shame is the preservative." He looked out at the fog thickening over the river. "And Hask—if the plate surfaces anywhere, anywhere at all, in any press in this city, I want to know within the hour, not within the morning. Within the hour."

"It will surface nowhere, sir. No press in Vellamark would dare."

Corvane turned from the window then, and for a moment the patience in his face thinned, and Hask saw—or thought he saw, and would spend the rest of his career declining to confirm it—something almost like hunger pass across the magistrate's features, quick and cold as a fish under ice.

"No press in Vellamark would dare," Corvane agreed softly. "That is what I told myself eleven years ago. And yet a plate left my house anyway, and a wagon came back from the orchard empty, and somewhere in this city there is a machine that turns lead into memory, and machines of that kind, Hask, do not stay idle forever. Dare is not the question. Dare is never the question. The question is always who, and when, and how many copies."

He dismissed Hask with a nod and stood alone in the office while the fog pressed against the glass like something reading over his shoulder.

On the desk lay the thin folder marked ASHGROVE, J. He opened it, read the schoolgirl sentence once more—I am quiet, and I notice things—and smiled without warmth, filing the page away with the care of a man putting a knife back in its drawer.

Six days. Paper traveled slowly; panic traveled fast; and Corvane had built nineteen years on knowing which arrived first.

* * *

The reply came sooner than he expected, and it was not a reply at all.

It was Hask who brought it up the three hundred steps, breathing hard, holding the small cheap envelope as though it might go off—which, Corvane reflected, taking it, was not entirely wrong. The courier had delivered the magistrate's note to the print shop at midday and waited, watching, as instructed. The door had opened. The girl had opened it—small, dark-eyed, ink-stained at the cuff exactly as the file promised. She had taken the envelope, read it standing in her own doorway with the street wind pulling at her hair, and then, according to the courier's sworn account, she had done something no one in nineteen years of such deliveries had done.

She had torn a strip from the bottom of a printed bill lying on the counter behind her—wedding invitations, the courier said, stacks of them, gold-rimmed—and she had written four words on the blank margin in a hand so steady the courier assumed she must have practiced it. Then she had handed the scrap back, closed the door, and turned the shop's sign to *Closed* with a small decisive click audible from the street.

Hask laid the scrap on the black oak desk.

Four words, in a fine clear hand, in printer's ink still faintly wet:

*Come yourself. Or don't.*

Corvane read it three times. Outside, the fog was lifting off the river, and somewhere far below in the Graykeep, on the third level west, a printer named Aldous Ashgrove was presumably writing his own letter home, full of the old liturgy—*destroy it, forget it, keep yourself safe*—never knowing that his daughter had already answered a different question entirely.

Corvane set the scrap down, aligned its edges square with the blotter, and reached for his pencil.

"So," he said quietly, to the room, to the river, to the girl in the shuttered shop. "Not quiet after all."

He began to draft new orders, and this time the pencil moved quickly.
