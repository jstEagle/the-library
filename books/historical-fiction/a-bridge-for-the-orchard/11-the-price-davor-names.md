## 11. The Price Davor Names

Davor had not slept in the Graykeep barracks for six nights. He told his captain he was ill—there was fever enough in the city to make the lie plausible—and instead he rented a cot above a chandler's shop on Rope Lane, where the smell of tallow was so thick it coated the back of the throat and made a man forget, briefly, what he had done.

He did not forget. That was the trouble. Sleep, when it came at all, came with the sound of cart wheels on wet cobblestone, and the particular silence of people who had been told to walk toward the river and not look back.

So on the morning after Corvane's note reached the Ashgrove print shop, Davor rose before the chandler's boys lit their vats, put on his uniform because there was nothing else to wear and no honest way to be seen out of it, and walked to the shop in the gray hour when the fog off the Vella still owned the streets.

He did not knock at first. He stood across the lane in the doorway of a shuttered cooperage, watching the windows of Ashgrave & Sons—or Ashgrove & Daughter, as it truly was now, though no sign said so. Smoke rose from the chimney. Somewhere inside, a press thumped like a slow heart. It occurred to him, standing there with his hands empty at his sides, that he had spent four years learning how to enter a house without being invited, and he had no idea anymore how to knock like a man.

He knocked anyway.

The door opened the width of a chain. Through the gap: one eye, dark and level, belonging to the girl who was not really a girl anymore—the printer's daughter, Juniper, whom he remembered from three years ago as a silent figure in an ink-stained apron who never once looked up when soldiers came to inspect the ledgers. She looked up now. She looked like someone who had learned recently and quickly what it cost to look at things.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"We have nothing for you." The chain did not slacken. "We are a small shop. Wedding invitations. Almanacs. Nothing worth a checkpoint officer's morning."

"I'm not here for anything." He kept his voice low and his hands visible, palms out, the way you'd approach a dog that had been kicked. "I'm here to give something."

"People like you don't give."

"People like me take. That's rather the whole of it." He heard the bitterness come out of his own mouth and let it stand. "May I come in, or would you rather conduct treason negotiations on your doorstep where Corporal Hesse can see us from the corner? He's there every day now. You know that, I assume. He's supposed to be watching for you. I'm supposed to be watching for you."

Silence. The eye studied him with a patience he found more unnerving than anger. Behind her, deeper in the shop, another presence shifted—a woman's voice murmuring, and a smaller sound, a child's breathing or movement, quickly hushed.

Then the chain scraped back.

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled of lead and oil and lamp-black, and beneath all of it, faintly, of apples—stored fruit, perhaps, gone soft in some cellar. Davor stood just inside the door because he had not been asked further and did not believe he deserved to be. Juniper barred the door behind him and then simply looked at him, arms folded, waiting the way her father must have waited on customers: letting silence do the work of speech.

He understood now why Aldous Ashgrove had survived twelve years under this regime. Silence ran in the family like good bone structure.

"The blind woman," Davor said, nodding toward the back room, where Maren sat with her fingers resting on an open type case like a musician's hands on keys she could not see but knew by heart. "And the girl. Wren."

"You know a great deal for a man who says he isn't watching us."

"I know everything my post lets me know. That's the curse of it." He took off his cap—a small gesture, but he meant it as one—and held it against his chest like a man at a funeral. Which, he supposed, he was. His own. "I came to tell you what I want. For the crossing. You've been mapping the gates—I'd have to be blind not to see it, and Maren there is the only blind one in this company. Three checkpoints between this door and the orchard district. You need a gate that opens."

Juniper's jaw tightened. "You said last time there was a price. You wouldn't name it."

"I'm naming it now."

"Then name it."

He had rehearsed this on the walk from Rope Lane, and again at the cooperage door, and the words had been clean in rehearsal, simple, almost noble. Standing in the warm ink-smell of the shop, they turned heavy as cast type.

"My name," he said. "In whatever you print."

Juniper blinked. Whatever she had expected—money, passage out of the city, silence bought and sealed—it was clear this was not it. "Your name."

"Davor Milic. Late of the Third Garrison, currently of the Bridge Gate. Sergeant, though I'll likely lose the rank for this, if I live." His mouth twisted. "I want it set in type. In the same broadsheet where you print Corvane's crimes. I want it printed next to the names of the workers who were taken, and I want it printed plainly: *this man stood at the gate on the night of the relocations and did not stop it.*"

The shop went very quiet. Even the press seemed to hold its breath.

Maren spoke from the back room, her voice low and precise, a voice accustomed to being listened to. "Say the names of the workers. All of them, if you remember them."

Davor looked at her. Her eyes were fixed slightly left of him, on nothing, on everything, and her fingers had not moved from the type case.

"Olek Ferrin," he said. "Tomasz Ferrin. Her brother—" he gestured vaguely, uselessly, and hated himself for the vagueness. "Bram Odway. Sela Odway. The Kessler brothers, Hans and Pieter. A woman named Anouk whose surname I never learned, only that she worked the fruit presses in the orchard district and sang while she worked, and everyone on the dock knew her voice." His throat closed on the last one. "And others. Eleven in all that night. Forty-one across the three nights, though I only saw the one."

Maren's fingers curled slowly around the edge of the type case. When she spoke again her voice had not changed at all, and that was more terrible than weeping. "Forty-one. I have thirty-eight. You will give me the other three."

"Anouk of the orchard presses. Yorik Salem, a tanner. And a boy called Pip who was fourteen and lied about his age to get on the strike committee."

"Pip," Maren repeated, softly, committing it somewhere no soldier could reach. Then she turned her face toward Davor fully, and even unseeing, the weight of her attention was like a hand on his chest. "Now say again what you want printed."

"That I stood at the gate and let it happen."

"And do you understand," she said, "what you're asking? Not the risk of it—that much is plain. Do you understand what it means, to have your name in the same column as Olek Ferrin's?"

"No," Davor admitted. "That's part of why I'm asking. I think I need to find out."

* * *

Juniper had not spoken through any of this. Now she pulled a stool from beside the composing stone, set it down in front of him with deliberate care, and pointed at it.

"Sit," she said. "If we're going to do business over treason, you'll sit like a person and drink tea like a person, and I'll stop thinking of you as a wall with a spear."

He sat. Tea appeared—weak, oversteeped, the tea of a household stretching its stores—but hot, and handed to him in a chipped cup without ceremony. Wren watched him from the stair that led to the loft, half-hidden in shadow, and he pretended not to see her because he could see that pretending was kinder.

"You understand," Juniper said, settling across from him, "that printing your name endangers everything. If Corvane's people get a broadsheet before it scatters—if the raid comes before dawn—they won't just read that a plate exists. They'll read who helped. Your name becomes a rope they can pull until something breaks."

"Yes."

"And once it's printed it can't be unprinted. Type doesn't forgive. My father taught me that before he taught me letters. You set a word wrong and strike it a thousand times, and now the world contains a thousand wrong words that were yours."

"Yes," Davor said again. "I've thought about that part every night since the relocations. Only backward. I keep thinking about how the world already contains forty-one absences that were mine—mine and eleven other men's—and nobody struck them anywhere. Nobody set them in any kind of record. Corvane wrote them down as *relocated*, which is a word for furniture." He set down the cup because his hand had begun to shake and he did not want to hear it rattle against the saucer. "I can't give them back. There is no crossing, no price, no act of courage in a tannery ditch that gives any of them back a single hour. The only thing left that I can do about any of it is refuse to erase myself from the account. If there's going to be a reckoning, I want my line item in it."

Juniper regarded him for a long moment. Then, quietly: "My mother used to say that a confession is a bridge built in the wrong direction—you cross it hoping to arrive where you started."

"She sounds like she's missed."

"She vanished when I was nine." Juniper said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report weather, and Davor understood he had been given exactly one plank of that bridge and no more. "So. Your name in the broadsheet. Stated plainly, next to the victims' names, owning the night at the gate." She leaned forward. "Do you also want it to say what you're doing now?"

He hadn't expected the question, and it showed.

"Because those are different sentences," she went on. "*This man did nothing* is one thing. But if I print *this man opened the gate*, then Corvane reads it too, and Corvane knows precisely which guard to hang. Whereas if the sheet only carries your guilt, you stay deniable. You stay useful. You could survive this." Her eyes were steady and very dark. "I'm telling you the merciful option exists, Sergeant, because my father taught me that a printer owes her customer the truth about what she's selling. And then I'm going to ask which sentence you want."

Davor sat with it. Outside, muffled by fog and shutters, boots passed in the street—he counted them by habit, four men, unsynchronized, a patrol and not a squad—and the press behind him ticked as the metal cooled.

"The second one," he said at last. "*This man opened the gate.*"

"Why?" It was Wren's voice, thin from the stairs, and both of them turned. She came down two steps, no further, fists on the rail, her chin thrust out in the way of children bracing to be lied to. "Why would you want them to know it was you?"

Davor considered how to answer a child, and then decided that the whole of his errand had been about refusing, finally, to manage the truth into smaller pieces.

"Because of your mother," he said. "And because of a woman named Anouk who sang on the docks. Because when they were taken, they were taken past a gate, and gates are manned by men, and men choose—even when the choice is only whether to look at the thing or away from it. I looked away. Then I looked back, and it was too late for looking to matter, and I have been living in that *too late* ever since. If my name goes in the record, then the record says somebody chose. Not the magistrate's machine. Not fate. A man, with a name, at a gate." He spread his hands. "People read that, and maybe the next man at the next gate remembers his name is also printable. That's all I want. That's the entire price. Make my complicity permanent so that my refusal has to be permanent too."

Wren stared at him for a long moment with her mother's eyes, or what he imagined were her mother's eyes, and then she went back up the stairs without a word. But she went slower than she'd come, and he thought—not proudly, nothing so clean as pride, but with a strange loosening in his chest—that she had climbed back up carrying something she hadn't had before.

* * *

They settled it at the composing stone. Juniper fetched a stick of type and, working by touch and habit, set the sentence before his eyes so he could read exactly what would be struck into paper:

*Davor Milic, sergeant of the Bridge Gate, opened the way for this truth. He stood at the gate on the night of the disappearances and did not stop it. Both things are true. He offers both.*

"Both things are true," Maren echoed, and her hand found the line of type and moved along it, letter by letter, reading with her fingertips the way she read everything. "That's the part most men cut. Keep it. The cutting is how lies start—one sentence at a time, trimmed for comfort."

"It's dangerous phrasing," Juniper said. "'He offers both.' It invites the reader to judge you."

"It invites the reader to judge *himself*," Maren corrected gently. "Which is what makes it worth the space it costs."

Davor looked at the little ranks of reversed letters, mirror-wise and strange, holding his name in their metal teeth. Twelve years he had served the state; four years at the gates; and never once had he seen his own name set down anywhere except a pay ledger and a promotion list. It looked different in type. Smaller. Accountable. A word among words, subject to correction, unable to hide.

"When do we move?" he asked.

"Two nights hence," Juniper said. "There's a moonless stretch coming, and Maren needs the time to teach me the tannery route by sound. You'll open the Bridge Gate at the third bell after midnight. We come through with a coffin."

"A coffin."

"There's a burial in the orchard cemetery. Genuine. The mourners are genuine too—they know what they're carrying, and they've chosen to carry it, which is more than I can say for most of this city." She began distributing the type back into its case, each character home in its cell, and did not look up as she added: "Sergeant. One more condition. Mine, this time."

"Name it."

"If it goes wrong at your gate—if you're caught, questioned, broken—my father's name dies with whatever you confess. So the price runs both directions. Your name goes in the sheet, and your silence goes in the ground. Both things. Forever."

Davor thought of the Graykeep's cells, of which he knew more than he wished to, and found that the fear arrived and was set aside like a coat he no longer needed, because he had finally found a place to hang it.

"Both things," he said. "Forever."

He rose, put his cap back on, and became again a wall with a spear for anyone watching from the street. At the door he paused, hand on the latch.

"The apprentice," he said quietly. "The boy who sweeps your steps. He's been seen speaking with a quartermaster from the Graykeep. Twice this week. I thought you should know before I tell you the rest."

Juniper's face went still. "What rest?"

Davor pulled his collar up against the fog waiting outside. "The rest is that Corvane has stopped searching for the plate," he said. "Yesterday he ordered the orchard district surveyed. Every building, every row of trees, measured and mapped." He met her eyes. "He isn't looking for where you're hiding it anymore, Miss Ashgrove. He's decided where you'll have to run—and he means to make sure there's nothing left of it but smoke."
