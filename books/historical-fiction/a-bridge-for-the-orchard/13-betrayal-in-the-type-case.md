## 13. Betrayal in the Type Case

The trouble began, as most ruin does, with something small and kind.

Juniper had given Tam the key to the type case three years ago, on a morning much like this one, when the light came through the shop's south window in long dusty bars and her father was still whole and laughing at his own jokes. *You've steady hands,* she'd told him, pressing the small brass key into his palm the way her father had once pressed one into hers. *Don't lose it. Don't lose anything it opens.* Tam had been fifteen then, all elbows and ink stains, an orphan from the tanner's row who swept the floor for bread and stayed for the smell of molten lead. He had learned to set type faster than any apprentice Aldous had trained in thirty years. He had learned to read Juniper's silences better than anyone alive.

That was why it took her so long to see it.

It started with the small things, the way rot starts inside a beam. Tam began arriving late—not dramatically late, only ten minutes, twenty, the kind of lateness that could be explained by a slow stove or a sick neighbor. He stopped humming. Juniper noticed that first, actually, before she noticed anything else: the shop had always had Tam's humming under everything, tuneless and constant as water, and then one day it was gone and the silence he left behind was the wrong kind of silence, the listening kind. He stopped asking questions. For three years he had asked about everything—the proportions of her father's new face, why garamond's sermons curled like that, what she thought the bridge toll would rise to after midwinter—and now he asked nothing at all, and worked with his head down, and when the soldiers passed on the street outside he did not look up, because he already knew they were there.

Juniper knew what looking-away looked like. She had invented half its postures herself.

On the fourth morning of the new quiet, she found the proof, and it was so ordinary it almost broke her before she understood it. She had come down before dawn to pull proofs of the wedding invitations—the forme that held more than invitations now, though only she and Maren knew it—and she found the type case's sliding lid not quite closed. A finger's width. Tam was scrupulous about the case; he closed it the way monks close doors, ritually, both hands. She stood in the gray light looking at that gap and felt the whole shape of the last four days rearrange itself around it.

She slid the lid open. The compartments were all in order except one. The small drawer where loose sorts went to be sorted—she kept her private marks there, her own cast-off ligatures, the little proofing symbols her father had cut for her when she was nine and still signing her name *Juny*. The drawer was clean. Swept. Someone had gone through it with a brush, gently, thoroughly, the way you search a room you do not want to seem searched.

She closed the lid and stood very still while the kettle ticked on the stove behind her and the first cart rattled over the cobbles on Foundry Street, and she let herself understand, slowly and completely, that Tam had been reading her drawers.

Not stealing. Reading. Looking for what she might be hiding. And finding nothing, because the plate slept inside the wedding forme under plain paper jackets, and even Tam, even Tam, would not open a locked job her father had sealed.

But he had been looking. Which meant someone had asked him to look. Which meant someone knew there was something to find.

* *

She said nothing that whole day. Silence was the oldest tool in her kit, and she used it now like a blade laid flat across the workbench between them. Tam set type for a run of funeral notices and did not hum, and twice she caught him watching her hands when she unlocked the case, and both times he looked away too quickly, and both times something in her chest tore a little more along a seam she hadn't known was there.

At noon Wren came out from the back room where she'd been sorting damaged sorts into jars, a task Juniper had invented for her partly to keep her hands busy and mostly to give her somewhere safe to be. The girl had filled eleven jars with obsessive precision, arranged by letter, and she carried the twelfth now like a treasure.

"Juny," she said—she had never once managed *Juniper*, and Juniper had stopped correcting her the second week, because the old childhood name in Wren's mouth sounded like something returned to her from a locked room. "The man across the street. He's been there since I opened the jar of E's."

Juniper did not look up. That was the first rule: never confirm a watcher by feeding him your eyes. "What man?"

"Gray coat. Standing in the doorway of the chandler's. He's eaten two apples and thrown both cores in the gutter, which is wasteful, and he keeps folding and unfolding a paper." Wren set the jar down with a small decisive click. "Mama said when someone stands still too long in a city, they're not waiting for a person. They're waiting for you to move."

Her mother had said that. Her mother, who had pressed a stolen printing plate into her daughter's hands and sent her running through checkpoint streets. Juniper looked at the child—at the sharp, serious, unchildlike face above the jar of letter E's—and felt the same reluctant responsibility she'd felt the night at the door harden into something more like resolve.

"Tam," she said, without turning, "take the delivery slip to Kessler's for the linen stock."

Tam's composing stick paused mid-line. It paused only a heartbeat—but Juniper had spent twelve years learning to hear heartbeats in other people's pauses, and this one was loaded. "Now? I'm at the R's in the notices."

"Now," she said pleasantly. "Kessler closes early."

He cleaned his hands longer than he needed to. He took his coat from the peg by the door, and he went out into the street, and through the shop window Juniper watched him turn—not toward Kessler's, but toward the chandler's doorway, toward the gray coat, without hesitation, without surprise, the way a man turns toward a door he has stood at before.

The gray-coated man handed him nothing. They didn't even speak. Tam simply stopped beside him, adjusted his collar, and walked on, and the man folded his paper one final time and left it on the chandler's step.

Wren fetched it. She crossed the street with a jar-return excuse she improvised on the spot, and brought back a single sheet of cheap paper, and Juniper read it at the back counter with the light angled away from the window:

*Confirmed: goods remain on premises. Report any removal attempt immediately. Watch the girl. Payment as agreed.*

No signature. No need. The seal on the fold was a small stamped coronet, and Juniper knew that coronet the way she knew her own father's maker's mark, because she had seen it embossed on every summons, every decree, every polite threatening notice that had come out of the magistrate's office for twelve years.

Corvane.

She read *Watch the girl* twice before she understood it meant Wren, and when she understood, something happened in her that was not fear at all. It was colder than fear and steadier. Somewhere across the city, in an office that smelled of sealing wax, a magistrate who made workers disappear had taken time from his important paperwork to worry about a nine-year-old girl with a jar of letter E's. He was afraid of them. He was afraid of a child and a typesetter and a blind archivist, and the knowledge of that fear settled into Juniper like ballast.

"Tam sold us," Wren said. Not a question. She had watched the street exchange through the window with those too-old eyes, and she had done the arithmetic children do faster and crueler than adults.

"Yes," Juniper said. Because lying to Wren would have been a second betrayal, and she was not going to spend the truth like it was cheap.

"He seemed nice."

"They mostly do, at first." Juniper folded the note into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little square. "My mother seemed nice, my father says. Before she vanished. The city is full of people who seem nice right up until the moment it costs them something."

"What does it cost him?" Wren asked. "Tam?"

And Juniper, who had asked herself the same question all day, answered honestly: "Less than it would cost him to refuse. That's all betrayal ever costs. It's priced to sell."

* *

Maren arrived at dusk, tapping her white cane through the door with the particular triple-knock rhythm she and Juniper had agreed on, and Juniper told her everything in the back room with the lamp turned low and Wren asleep on the pallet against the wall, one hand curled around the jar of E's she had refused to surrender at bedtime.

Maren listened the way she listened to banned texts—with total attention, her face tilted, her fingers moving slightly as though reading the words off some surface only she could see. When Juniper finished, the archivist was quiet for a long moment.

"The boy sorted for me twice," Maren said finally. "Last month. He has good hands. Gentle with the plates." She sighed, a sound like wind finding a crack. "Gentle hands are the ones that can be gripped easiest. Corvane will have something on him. Debts, or a sister in the Graykeep's registers, or simply fear—it hardly matters which. Fear is the cheapest currency in Vellamark and always has been."

"It doesn't matter why," Juniper said. "It matters that the shop is watched and he'll tell them if we move anything. The note said *report any removal attempt*. They're not coming for the plate yet. They're waiting for us to run with it, so they can take it in the open where no one asks questions."

"Then you won't run with it."

"No." Juniper looked at the forme on the workbench, the wedding invitations sleeping on top of their lethal cargo. Her father's seal still sat unbroken on the paper jacket—he had locked and sealed it himself, back when he still believed the plate should go into the river. "We move tonight. All of it. If we wait, Tam reports tomorrow evening that we're still here, and Corvane stops being patient. Patience is the one luxury he can't afford anymore either—he moved up the arrests on the orchard families last week. He's sweeping. We're in the path of the broom."

"Tonight," Maren repeated. Her blind eyes found Juniper with unnerving accuracy. "Where? You cannot take it to the orchard press yet—the route isn't rehearsed, and Davor's gate changes watch at dawn. Where does a plate of evidence sleep safely in a city full of ears?"

"I don't know yet," Juniper admitted. "But I know someone who remembers where everything sleeps."

Maren smiled slowly. "You mean my cellar."

"I mean your cellar. And the archive, and every hiding place the old archivists cut into the walls when the first bans came down sixty years ago. You told me the shelves have backs that swing. You told me you memorized the building the way Papa memorizes a typeface. So tonight the plate goes where the banned books sleep, and tomorrow we rehearse the tannery route, and the day after—" Juniper heard her own voice steady in a way that surprised her, "—the day after we carry it through Davor's gate, and it never sleeps in this shop again."

They planned by lamplight for an hour, Maren reciting the archive's geography in her precise archival voice—the third shelf, the false back, the cavity wide enough for folios and therefore for a forme—and it was nearly midnight when Juniper heard the sound that stopped her breath.

A key in the front door.

Only three keys existed. Hers, her father's, and Tam's.

* *

She put the lamp out with two fingers and stood in the dark of the back room, one hand on Wren's shoulder to keep the child still, and listened to Tam come into his own shop like a thief—which, Juniper thought with a strange detached clarity, was exactly what he was now, though perhaps he had been one for weeks without knowing it, and perhaps he had come tonight to stop being one.

His footsteps crossed the front room. Paused. Crossed to the type case—the slide of the wooden lid unmistakable even in darkness—and stopped there for a long moment. Then she heard him sit down on the workbench stool, and she heard something else, something she had not permitted herself to hope for: breathing that hitched. Once. Twice.

He was crying. Quietly, badly, the way fifteen-year-olds cry, the way boys cry who have been taught there is no safe place to do it.

Juniper weighed it in the dark: go out or stay hidden. Trust him again or trust the evidence of the drawers and the gray coat and the note in her pocket. And underneath the weighing, cold and practical, ran the thought that whatever Tam wanted tonight—forgiveness, warning, absolution—she could not afford any of it. The plate had to move within hours. A weeping apprentice at midnight was a complication, and complications were luxuries.

She slipped past the sleeping pallet, eased open the back door on hinges she had oiled herself two nights ago, and stepped into the yard where the forme waited wrapped in sacking atop the handcart they'd prepared. Wren, awake after all—of course awake, the child slept like a cat in a strange house—rose silently and took the far handle without being asked, and together they rolled the cart out through the alley gate, away from the shop, away from the boy crying over a type case he had already sold.

Halfway down the alley, Juniper heard the front door of the shop open behind them and Tam's voice come low and broken across the dark yard:

"Juniper. *Juniper.* There are six of them. They're coming at first light—Corvane himself wrote the order, I saw it, I saw the coronet—I'm sorry, God, I'm sorry, they have my brother, they've had him since summer—"

She stopped the cart. Wren's hands tightened on the handle.

Everything in her wanted to turn around. Everything in her—the girl who had taught Tam his first em-quad, who had defended him to her father when he broke the chase and scattered three pages of a sermon across the floor—wanted to walk back through that gate and say *I know, I know, tell me about your brother.* But the plate was on the cart, and six soldiers were coming at first light, and grief—her mother had taught her this, or her mother's absence had—grief was a room you could enter later, when the doors were barred and the work was done.

"Then help me one last time, Tam," she called back, not turning, her voice carrying flat and clear down the alley. "Go home. Sleep. Answer their questions tomorrow exactly the way you answered them today, and when this is over—if it ends the way I intend it to end—I'll read your brother's name myself, in print, on every corner of this city. That's the only forgiveness I have to offer tonight. Take it or don't."

Silence. Then, thick and wrecked: "Third shelf. False back. Maren's cellar. I know—I've always known—that building has two ways out and the soldiers don't. Take the Tanner's Cut behind the archive, not the lane. The lane has a sightline to the keep."

Then the door closed, and he was gone, and Juniper stood in the dark alley with her heart hammering and the plate heavy between her hands and Wren staring up at her.

"You forgave him," Wren said.

"I gave him a job," Juniper said. "Forgiveness can wait for the printing."

She took up the cart handles. Behind them the shop sat dark and watched and lost to them now, probably forever—twelve years of her life sealed inside it like type in a chase—and ahead of them the city lay under a thin cold moon with six soldiers sleeping before dawn and one blind woman waiting up with a lamp she couldn't see.

"Come on," Juniper said. "We have until first light, and the truth doesn't carry itself."
