## 14. Through the Tanneries

The tanneries smelled the way guilt smelled, Davor thought—of lime and rot and old blood soaked so deep into the boards that no amount of water would ever lift it out. He had walked this stretch of the Marrow Canal twice in his soldier's years, once on patrol and once carrying a drunk sergeant by the armpits, and both times he had breathed through his mouth and thought about nothing at all. That had been the trick of the uniform. You could pass through the worst places in Vellamark if you made your head an empty room.

Tonight his head was full to bursting, and the smell seemed to know it.

He crouched at the edge of the drying yard behind Harrow & Sons, where hides hung in long gray curtains that moved without wind, stirred by the heat rising off the lime pits. Beyond them, maybe two hundred paces, stood the Saltgate checkpoint—a lantern on a pole, a brazier, four men, and a barrier arm across the towpath where it squeezed between the tannery wall and the canal. The only crossing on the south side still letting foot traffic through after dark, because the tanners' guild had bought that privilege with coin and flattery, and Corvane's men honored it the way they honored everything: as long as it cost them nothing.

Behind him, in the black between the hide racks, the others waited. He could not see them but he could feel them the way you feel weather coming—the quick light breathing of the girl, Wren; the slower, deliberate breath of Maren, who saw with her ears; and Juniper Ashgrove, who breathed hardly at all, as if she had practiced her whole life at being no more present than the dark itself. And pressed against Juniper's chest under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false bottom of a wedding-invitation box: the plate.

*Don't think about the plate,* he told himself. *Think about the gate.*

"Say it again," he murmured, barely louder than the drip of the pits.

Juniper's voice came from the darkness, low and even. "You go first. Alone. You show the watch commander your discharge papers and tell him you're escorting your sister's widow and her family to the ferry at Two Saints, because the widow is simple-minded and afraid of soldiers, and you didn't want her frightened at the barrier."

"And if he asks why a discharged man is walking his sister's widow through a city under curfew corridors—"

"He won't. Sergeant Ovett runs Saltgate. He drinks. He owes the tanners' factor money, which means he owes the factor's patience nothing, which means he takes bribes and hates paperwork. A family with papers is no paperwork." A pause. "That's what Maren said, anyway. She asked the factor's clerk last week, buying parchment."

Davor almost smiled. The blind archivist had spent three days assembling the checkpoint like a woman reading a recipe: who stood where, who wanted what, who could be bored past and who had to be paid. He had served eleven years in the Vellamark garrison and she knew more about its gates than he did.

"And if it goes wrong," Juniper said, and now something changed in her voice, the flatness cracking just enough to show the thing underneath—"you let it go wrong for you. Not for us. You swore it, Davor. You cross, you draw their eyes, and whatever happens, we keep walking."

"I remember what I swore."

"Say it anyway."

He stared into the dark where her voice lived. In the brazier-light beyond the hides he could see the barrier arm, the lantern, the shape of a soldier leaning against the wall picking at something on his sleeve. Ordinary men. He had been one of them. That was the whole sickness of it—that there was nothing extraordinary required to stand at a gate and turn people away, or worse.

"If it goes wrong," he recited quietly, "I stay wrong. I take the questions, the blows, whatever comes. I never saw a widow, never heard of one. I'm a drunk discharged coward looking for a fence to sell my boots." He exhaled. "Happy?"

"No," Juniper said. "But go."

He rose from the crouch, and his knees complained—he was thirty-four years old and the army had taken the cartilage as taxes—and for a moment he stood among the hanging hides feeling them brush his shoulders like cold hands. Then he stepped out of their shadow and walked toward the light.

* * *

Sergeant Ovett was exactly what Maren's intelligence promised: thick-necked, red-eyed, a man whose anger at the world had calcified into a kind of administrative boredom. He sat on a stool beside the brazier with a mug of something steaming, and when Davor approached the barrier he looked up with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own death warrant.

"Gate's shut," Ovett said. "Curfew corridor. You can read the notice."

"I can." Davor held up his papers, keeping his hand steady. Eleven years of standing at gates had taught him exactly how a man stands when he has nothing to hide: slightly too relaxed, faintly annoyed. "Discharge papers. I'm not asking for myself, Sergeant. My brother-in-law's widow and her family need to reach the Two Saints ferry before morning tide. She's—" he let embarrassment creep in, a small shrug, "—she's not right in her head since her husband died. If I bring her to a full post with halberds and all, she'll scream the district awake. Thought I'd walk her across quiet, save everyone the noise."

Ovett squinted at the papers. Behind him, one of the younger soldiers drifted closer, curious. Davor kept his eyes on the sergeant, giving the young one nothing to be curious about.

"Tanners' factor says family traffic comes through," Ovett said, half to himself. "Guild arrangement."

"That's what I heard."

"You one of ours? You've got a garrison look about you."

"Was. Third company. Mustered out in the spring." He let a beat pass, then added, with the precise weight of a man confessing something small: "Left leg's shot. They didn't want me limping around reminding anyone of anything."

That did it. Sympathy between broken men was cheaper than coin and spent easier. Ovett grunted, waved at the barrier, and the young soldier lifted the arm halfway—

And then the night went wrong.

It began as a sound: Wren. A small cry, quickly stifled, from the direction of the hide yard—but direction meant nothing at night over open ground, and it carried, and every soldier at Saltgate turned toward it the way a dog turns. Davor's heart dropped through the floor of him. The girl was supposed to be thirty paces back, muffled, patient. But somewhere between the drying yard and the gate she had come forward—perhaps frightened by the waiting, perhaps trying to stay close to Juniper, perhaps simply being six years old in the dark—and now she had stumbled against a hide rack, and the racks were chained together at the base, and the chain had sung like a plucked wire.

"What was that?" The young soldier let the barrier drop. It clattered down.

"Dog," Davor said instantly. "Tanneries are crawling with them. They fight over the trimmings—" 

"Nobody said dogs." Ovett was off his stool, hand on his baton, suddenly entirely awake, and Davor understood with sick clarity that boredom had been the plan's entire foundation and it had just been demolished. "Lights. Get the yard lights."

Two soldiers took lanterns off the pole and started toward the drying yard. From deep between the hide rows came a sound Davor prayed to every god he no longer believed in that they couldn't hear: a woman's voice, very low, very calm, murmuring. Juniper, steadying the child. Maren's hand surely on both their shoulders, reading the disaster through her palms.

Davor did the arithmetic in one breathless instant. Thirty paces of open yard. Two lanterns sweeping. Three people who could not outrun soldiers—one blind, one child, one carrying a leaden secret worth her father's life. Four men at the gate, plus Ovett. If the searchers reached the hide rows, it was finished—not just tonight, but Wren, Maren, Juniper, Aldous Ashgrove rotting in the Graykeep, every name on that plate staying buried under Corvane's tidy signature forever.

There was only one movable object left in the equation.

He stepped directly into the path of the lanterns.

"Sergeant!" His voice cracked out loud, ugly, drunk-sounding. "Sergeant, you're making a mistake, and I'll tell you for free what the mistake is—"

"Hold there." Ovett turned. Good. Half-turn. Keep turning.

"It's not dogs you should worry about." Davor staggered, deliberately, theatrically, and let his voice climb into the whine of a man who has had too much and resents the world for it. "You know what's coming through this gate tomorrow? New schedules. New *inspectors*. Corvane's own man, coming to check the checkpoints, and do you know what they're checking first? Whose ledgers are light. Whose gates ran loose. Yours, Sergeant. Guild arrangement or no guild arrangement, when his man finds out how many souls crossed Saltgate for a smile and a copper—"

"Shut your mouth." Ovett had come fully around now, face purpling. Both remaining soldiers were watching the exchange. The lantern-bearers paused at the edge of the yard, uncertain, their lights swinging back toward the argument like moths.

"Or what?" Davor laughed, and hated the sound, and made it worse. "You'll beat the truth out of me? Go ahead. It's the truth whether you beat me or not. Ask the factor's clerk what he writes down and who reads it. Ask him who else reads—"

Ovett hit him.

It was not the first time Davor had been struck by a fellow soldier, but it was the first time he had engineered it, priced it, and accepted payment in advance. The baton caught him above the ear and the world went white and sideways and he went down onto the towpath stones with his papers scattering like startled birds. Somewhere above him Ovett was shouting—"insolent drunk guttershite"—and Davor curled around himself, not to protect against the blows, though they came, boots and baton finding ribs and kidney and the meat of his shoulder, but to make sure his body stayed between the lanterns and the hide yard. As long as they were looking at him, they weren't looking there. As long as he screamed, any smaller sound stayed swallowed.

He heard, beneath the roaring in his skull, the thing he was buying: footsteps, soft ones, retreating along the far side of the drying yard. The scrape of a chain lifted and set down silently. Juniper's murmur moving away, taking Wren and Maren with it, toward the tanner's cut where the boat waited. Going. *Going.*

A boot found his mouth and he tasted iron and felt a tooth loosen, and he thought, with a strange floating clarity, *this is what it costs, this is the actual coin, and it's cheap, it's horribly cheap compared to what they took from those workers on the relocation night, and you were there, Davor, you stood at the water and you looked away, so pay, pay, pay—*

"Enough." A new voice. The young soldier, sounding ill. "Sergeant. Enough. He's done."

Ovett stood over him, breathing hard, baton dripping red in the lantern light. The rage drained out of his face and left behind the older thing, the fear, and Davor understood suddenly that he had guessed right about the inspectors, or close enough—that he hadn't invented a threat at all, only named the one already living in the sergeant's chest. Men beat hardest at the truths they're most afraid someone will say aloud.

"Search the yard," Ovett rasped. "Quick. Dogs or no dogs."

They searched. Davor lay on the stones and listened to the lanterns move through the hide rows, closer to the place where his friends had been, and he could do nothing, absolutely nothing, except bleed onto the towpath and keep his breathing loud and wet so that every ear at the gate stayed angled toward him. He heard a rack shift. He heard a soldier say, "Rats been at these ties." He heard the dark give up nothing, because the dark had already given everything it had across the canal and away.

Afterward they hauled him up and propped him against the brazier, and Ovett crouched to eye level, and for a long moment the two of them regarded each other with the strange intimacy of men on opposite sides of a beating.

"Your papers say Davor Rehn," Ovett said. "Third company." His voice had gone flat, careful. "Third company did escort duty in the autumn relocations."

The words landed harder than the baton had. Davor held the sergeant's gaze and felt the whole of that night rise between them—the barges, the fog, the names read off a list, the water, the looking away.

"I don't remember any relocations," Davor said, blood threading from the corner of his mouth. "Do you?"

Something passed across Ovett's face. It might have been relief. It might have been its opposite.

"Get him to the guardhouse till he sobers," the sergeant said, standing, turning away. "Lockup, not a cell. And log it as public disturbance. No names in the book." He paused at the edge of the lantern light, his back to Davor. "No paperwork."

* * *

The lockup at Saltgate was a converted salt cellar with a grate for a door, and they gave him no blanket, which suited him; his ribs needed the space. He lay on the stone floor listening to the building settle and the river work at its pilings, and he took inventory of himself the way he'd once inventoried ammunition: two ribs cracked, probably, on the left. A tooth loose. One eye swelling toward closing. Hands functional. Legs functional. Everything paid for, then, and the purchase delivered—somewhere on the far bank of the Marrow, in a boat poled by a tanner who owed Maren a favor, a widow and her simple-minded family were sliding toward Two Saints with a box of wedding invitations, and none of it had ever touched the ledger at Saltgate.

No glory in it. He turned that over in his mind, testing it. He had grown up on soldier's stories, all of them lit like the brazier—charges, flags, the moment of visible courage. Nobody wrote stories about a man lying in his own blood in a lockup so that a blind woman and a child could walk away uncounted. There was no name for what he'd done tonight, and that was proper. Names were for the plate. His name belonged there too—he had insisted on that, demanded it, and Juniper had agreed with a gravity that had frightened him more than any checkpoint—and when the broadsheets flew over Vellamark, Davor Rehn would stand printed among the guilty as well as the useful. Complicity and sacrifice, inked side by side. Let the city decide what to do with a man like that. He no longer expected to understand himself.

Sometime in the smallest hours, boots came down the stair, unhurried. Keys. The grate swung.

He expected a guard with water. Instead he got Juniper Ashgrove, holding a shuttered lamp, with Maren's white eyes catching the glow behind her and Wren peeking around Juniper's coat with her mother's stubbornness written all over her small face.

"How," Davor started, and had to stop and start again. "How in the god's name—"

"The tanner's nephew works the Saltgate lockup," Juniper said. "Maren says half the city works for the other half if you ask politely enough." She crouched beside him, and her face in the lamplight was terrible to see—not pity, worse than pity: witness. She looked at his eye, his split lip, his shallow careful breathing, and she took all of it in without flinching. "You shouldn't have done that."

"You ordered me to."

"I ordered you to draw their eyes. You drew their fists." Her voice was very quiet. "There's a difference, and you crossed it on purpose, and I don't know yet what to do with that."

"Learn to live with it," Maren said from the doorway, her cane whispering on the stone. "We haven't time for gratitude properly performed. Davor." She turned her face to him, and it always unsettled him, the way her blindness aimed straighter than sight. "The apprentice sent word to Corvane today. The one who betrayed you, Juniper. He sold the route—all of it, the orchard press included. Corvane's men move on the orchard at dawn, and Aldous's execution has been moved up to answer it. We have until first light, no more. Can you stand?"

Davor looked at the three of them framed in the grate—the archivist calm as scripture, the child gripping Juniper's hand, and Juniper herself, invisible no longer, lit from within by something that had stopped being fear weeks ago and become a kind of fuel.

He laughed, and it hurt, and he did it anyway.

"Ask me tomorrow," he said, and reached up for her shoulder, and pulled himself onto his feet.
