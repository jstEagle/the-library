## 16. The Second Gate

The coffin was pine, unfinished, the kind of box that announced itself as poor. Juniper had stood at the print shop window a thousand mornings watching carts pass on the Weavers' Row and had learned to read the city by its silhouettes, and so she knew this particular shape of grief the way she knew the weight of a galley of type: narrow, plain, carried on the shoulders of men who could not afford to weep loudly because loudness cost money in Vellamark.

She had never expected to be grateful for poverty. She was grateful now.

"They're from the Tanners' Walk," Maren murmured beside her, her head tilted toward the sound of the mourners gathering at the mouth of Bridge Street. Her fingers rested lightly on Juniper's arm, reading the tremor there the way she read embossed pages. "Four of them. Boots—one man is a tanner still, the lime has eaten his soles. A woman with a cane. And someone small."

Wren. Juniper looked down at the girl, who had insisted on coming this far and who now stood very straight, her chin lifted in an imitation of mourning that was not entirely imitation. Wren had buried people before. She knew how mourners stood.

"The plate," Maren said quietly.

Juniper's hand went to the satchel at her hip without her permission, and she forced it away again. That was the first rule her father had taught her about carrying anything dangerous through a checkpoint: *never touch the place where it sleeps*. Hands remember what they guard. Eyes follow hands.

Three days ago the plate had slept inside a forme of wedding invitations, and the forme had slept beneath the floor of the type room, and Juniper had believed—if belief could be called that—that they would have more time. Then Tam had sold them. She still could not say his name without something in her chest pulling tight, a stitch in cloth cut too short. Tam, who had set type beside her for two years, who had whistled off-key when Aldous wasn't listening, who had taken coin from Corvane's man and told him where the evidence lived. The shop was watched now. Every hour they stayed was an hour borrowed against a debt that would come due with soldiers' boots.

So tonight the plate would travel. Tonight it would cross the second gate of three, hidden in the one place Corvane's inspectors would not look: a coffin bound for the orchard cemetery, where Wren's mother had once worked the pressing sheds and where, if the mourners got their wish, an old tanner named Hollis would be laid down in consecrated ground before the magistrate could burn the whole district to ash.

"You don't have to do this," Juniper said to the tall man standing apart from them in the shadow of the cooper's awning.

Davor shook his head slowly. In the lamplight leaking from a shuttered window, his face looked carved out of some wood that had weathered too many winters. He wore no uniform tonight—his soldier's coat hung loose over civilian gray—but he stood like a gatepost anyway, planted, immovable. "My gate is the second gate," he said. "I've waved through coffins for eleven years. Grain shipments. Wine barrels. Once, God forgive me, a wagon I chose not to count too carefully." His jaw worked. "I'll walk you across."

"If they recognize you—"

"They won't. They'll see what I tell them to see." He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, that smile. "It's the one skill the army gave me that was worth having."

* * *

They moved in ones and twos, as Maren had instructed, the blind archivist conducting their little procession by ear the way she conducted everything—attentive, patient, unhurried. Juniper walked with the mourners, because that had been decided in Maren's cellar two nights ago while rain drummed overhead and Wren slept against a wall of archived ledgers.

*"A stranger crossing alone is a question,"* Maren had said, her sightless eyes turned toward the sound of Juniper's breathing. *"A mourner among mourners is furniture. No one interrogates furniture."*

But furniture did not carry a leaden printing plate worth seven lives—or seven hundred—and so the problem had remained, until old Hollis's widow, a woman named Sereth with a cane and a voice like gravel in honey, had heard them out and said only this:

"My husband worked forty years for wages that bought him a cough. Your names and mine come from the same press, girl." She had tapped her cane twice on the cellar floor, a judge's gavel in miniature. "He'd have wanted to be useful one last time. He always complained nobody let him be useful."

And so Juniper now walked three steps behind a coffin that held both a dead tanner and the truth about thirty-one disappeared workers, and the strangeness of it kept rising in her throat like laughter at a funeral, inappropriate and impossible to swallow.

Bridge Street narrowed as it approached the water. Gaslight did not reach this far; the city had stopped spending money on the approaches to the orchard district years ago, as if light were a form of investment and the orchard no longer promised returns. The river made its low constant sound beneath the pilings, that patient grinding whisper Juniper had fallen asleep to for the first ten years of her life, before her mother vanished and silence became a discipline instead of a lullaby.

She thought of her mother now—not the vanishing, but before. Her mother had crossed this bridge every market day. Her mother had hummed while she worked the hand-press, back when Aldous still let anyone else touch it. These were thoughts Juniper usually kept locked in the far room of her mind, the room with the shutters closed, but fear opened strange doors, and tonight all the rooms in her stood open, lamps lit, everything she loved laid out where any passing soldier might see it.

Wren slipped her cold hand into Juniper's. Juniper did not startle. Twelve years of practice had taught her to register every touch without flinching, though this one she returned.

"Sereth says the cemetery keeper owes her," Wren whispered. "She says he'll dig the grave slow. Slow means we have till morning before anyone—" She stopped herself, having been coached not to say certain words aloud near strangers. *Before anyone lifts the lid.* "Before anything happens."

"Then we have till morning," Juniper said, and was surprised by how steady it came out. Cold planning, she had learned, was armor. Grief rattled around behind it like coins in a dropped purse, but the armor held.

Ahead, the second gate resolved out of the dark: a timber barricade across the bridge's throat, lanterns hanging from a crossbeam, four soldiers in the blue-gray of the civic watch. Beyond them, the bridge planks ran pale as a spine into the darkness of the far bank, and beyond that, invisible, lay the flooded tanneries and the old orchard and the underground press, and everything.

* * *

Davor went forward alone.

Juniper watched from the edge of the lamplight as he approached the barricade with the rolling, unhurried walk of a man going somewhere dull. One of the sentries straightened. Even at forty paces Juniper could see the recognition happen—the slight stiffening, the sideways glance to a comrade, the calculation of rank. Davor had been a sergeant. Sergeants were remembered the way scars are remembered: precisely, unwillingly.

She could not hear what he said. The river took his words and shredded them into the general hush. But she watched the exchange unfold like a play staged too far off: Davor producing papers—forged transit documents Maren's network had prepared, bearing a dead clerk's seal—gesturing back toward the mourners with the weary authority of a man inconvenienced by other people's deaths. One sentry peeled away and walked toward the coffin. Toward Sereth. Toward the pine box and the plate sleeping inside it beneath a shroud of coarse linen.

Juniper's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of sabotage against her ribs.

*Never touch the place where it sleeps.* She folded her hands in front of her like a woman at church. Beside her, Sereth leaned on her cane and began, in a cracked half-voice, to sing—a work song, low and droning, the kind sung over dye vats and pressing sheds, words worn round with use. The woman with the cane's daughter took it up. Then the tanner with the ruined boots. It was not a beautiful song. It was a working song, meant to keep many hands moving in time, and it did exactly that: it moved the moment forward, gave the sentry nowhere to stand except inside it.

He reached the coffin. He put his hand flat upon the lid.

The singing held. Juniper held. Somewhere behind her, Wren's breath came quick and shallow, and Juniper pressed the child's hand once—*steady, steady*—the way her father used to press her shoulder when the compositor's stick overflowed and letters clattered everywhere and panic seemed reasonable.

The sentry looked at Davor. Davor looked at the sky, bored.

The sentry removed his hand, made a small gesture—*move along, then, misery is not contraband*—and returned to the barricade.

Mourners were furniture. Grief was not worth inspecting. Juniper filed the fact away next to everything else her father had taught her about how power sees the world: it looks at type and sees ink; it looks at people and sees categories; it looks at a coffin and sees an ending, never a beginning.

"Bridge is clear," Davor called back, his voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the mourners and no farther. "Widow, my condolences. Watch the third plank, it's warped."

* * *

They crossed in the order Sereth directed: bearers first, then the widow, then the rest trailing like the tail of a slow comet. As Juniper's feet found the bridge planks she felt the whole structure shift and murmur under them, alive with current, and she thought—absurdly, urgently—of the book's title she had once joked about with her father, *a bridge for the orchard*, which he had written on a scrap of paper and pinned above the hand-press like a promise neither of them understood yet. Bridges were not neutral things. Every bridge in Vellamark had been raised to move somebody's goods and somebody's soldiers, and tonight, for once, one was moving the opposite of soldiers. It was moving witness.

Halfway across, the sentry with the suspicious eyes called out again.

"Hold."

The procession stopped. The river spoke underneath, patient as arithmetic.

Davor turned, unhurried. "Something else?"

"Your papers list six mourners." The sentry's lantern rose, its light swinging across the group, catching Sereth's cane, the tanner's boots, the coffin's raw pine. "I count eight."

For a moment the whole world balanced on the head of a pin, on the difference between six and eight, and Juniper felt the counting begin behind the sentry's eyes—*widow, daughter, tanner, bearer, bearer, bearer*—working its way toward the two extras at the rear, the woman with the satchel and the child.

It was Maren who saved them, though Maren stood twenty paces back at the street's mouth, blind, unable to see the lantern or the sentry's face. She had heard the pause lengthen past comfort, and she did what archivists do with silences: she filled them with text.

"*Hollis Brant,*" her voice rang out, thin and clear over the water, cracking deliberately like an old woman overcome, "*forty years at the Walk, and they send a boy to count us like sacks! Count him, boy—count the cough he brought home in his lungs, count the wages they docked him for dying too slow—go on, count!*"

The sentry blinked. The tanner spat ceremonially over the rail. Sereth raised her cane as if to strike the young man where he stood, and something in the ancient grammar of funerals—the universal law that you do not impede the grieving, that to do so invites not just anger but *omen*, bad luck sticking to your uniform like tar—made him step back.

"Six," he corrected himself stiffly. "Six mourners. Move along."

Later, in the dark of the far bank, Davor would explain it in his flat way: *No sentry wants to be the one who delayed a widow's funeral over arithmetic. If it goes wrong, that's the name in the report. Better to miscount and forget.* But walking off the bridge onto the soft ground of the orchard road, Juniper understood something larger and quieter: the sentry had not merely been lazy. He had heard Maren's voice and chosen, in a space smaller than a heartbeat, not to hear it as a threat. Somewhere under his blue-gray coat was a person who had also buried someone badly, cheaply, in this city that disappeared its workers and docked the wages of the dying.

Corvane's whole edifice, Juniper thought, was built of such men—men who would step aside if only someone gave them a sentence to hide inside.

Her job, and Maren's, and Wren's, and the plate's, was to give every one of them that sentence. Thirty-one names, printed ten thousand times, each one a door left open for a sentry's conscience to slip through.

* * *

The cemetery keeper was waiting at the iron gate with a lantern shuttered to a slit and a spade already leaning against the wall, and he dug exactly as slowly as Sereth had promised, pausing often to complain about the hardness of the ground, the dampness of the season, the ingratitude of magistrates. Juniper knelt at the graveside with Wren and together, in the smell of turned earth and pine resin, they eased the wrapped plate out through a panel Sereth's sons had loosened that afternoon, and laid it flat in the bottom of the coffin beneath the shroud's final fold, directly beneath where Hollis Brant's folded hands would rest.

"Sleep well," Wren whispered to the plate, or perhaps to the tanner, or perhaps to her mother, who was buried here, three rows east, beneath a stone with a name on it that Juniper had already memorized for the broadsheet. "Someone's going to read you soon."

They left the mourners at the gate—strangers an hour ago, kin now, refusing with waves and gruff blessings any thanks Juniper tried to offer. Sereth caught her sleeve at the last moment.

"The keeper digs slow," she said, "but come dawn, soldiers will dig fast, if Corvane learns where his plate spent the night. Don't waste the hours, girl. And when you print it—" her grip tightened, gravel-and-honey voice dropping to almost nothing, "—print Hollis's trade too. Not just the name. He was a tanner. Forty years. Let it say so."

"It will say so," Juniper promised. "Every one of them. Name and trade and street."

* * *

They regrouped in the shadow of the orchard wall, where the air changed—the river-smell giving way to cider and rot and wet leaves, the smell of the district the city had decided to burn. Maren touched the bark of the nearest apple tree the way she touched the faces of friends.

"One gate left," she said softly. "The hardest one."

Davor was cleaning dirt from under his nails with a knife, methodical, unbothered, a man rationing his fear like everyone else rations candles. "The third gate isn't mine," he said. "It's the Graykeep's inner cordon, and after tomorrow it won't matter how good your papers are. There's word moving through the watch—I heard it at the changing of the shift." He paused, and Juniper watched something cross his face that she had never seen there before. Not fear exactly. Its older, colder cousin.

"What word?" Juniper asked.

He looked up at her, and past her, at the dark bulk of the orchard where three hundred trees stood holding their breath.

"They've requisitioned lamp oil," he said. "Barrels of it. Army wagons, moving tonight." He closed the knife. "Corvane isn't waiting for spring to clear this district. Whatever you mean to do with that plate—you do it before dawn."
