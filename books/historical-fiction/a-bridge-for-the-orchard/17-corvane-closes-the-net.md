## 17. Corvane Closes the Net

The lamp in Magistrate Corvane's study had burned down to its last finger of oil, and he did not trim it. He liked to watch things diminish. It was a discipline, of a kind—the same discipline that kept his ledgers balanced and his city quiet and his hands steady at this hour of the night, when lesser men would have poured themselves a drink and let the trembling begin.

He sat before the long table where his maps lay pinned, and he looked at Vellamark the way a physician looks at a body he already knows is diseased. The river split it neatly. Six bridges. Four gates. The Graykeep on the north bank like a fist closed over the throat of the water. And there, in the southeast bend where the tanneries stank along the flooded lowlands, the orchard district: a ragged constellation of lanes and leaning houses and old apple trees gone half-wild, the whole of it stitched together by footpaths and grievance.

That was where it lived, whatever it was. He was certain of it now. A press, or the makings of one, or merely the will to make one—something had passed through his checkpoints in the dark, something small enough to be carried in a coat, and it had gone to ground among the trees.

Corvane did not think of himself as a cruel man. Cruelty was imprecise, and imprecision offended him the way a crooked line of type offended a compositor. What he practiced was administration. The workers who had been relocated—nine of them, though the number mattered less than the principle—had been an administrative correction, no different from trimming a plate's margins so it would sit flush in its chase. They had struck. They had made the city's commerce stutter for eleven days. And when they would not return to their benches, they had been removed, quietly, in the backs of grain wagons, and the record of them had been adjusted accordingly. *Relocated.* It was even true, in a fashion. Everyone was relocated eventually. He had simply expedited the paperwork.

The trouble—and he acknowledged it as trouble, coldly, without flinching—was the plate.

Somewhere in this city existed a leaden rectangle, cast from his own private press, bearing the names he had struck from the rolls. He had commissioned that casting himself, years ago, in a fit of thoroughness he now regarded as his single uncharacteristic indulgence: a proof-copy of the truth, locked away, because a man of order kept duplicates of everything. He had melted most of the run. One plate had been miscounted. One plate, and a printer's daughter clever enough to know what she held, and a child quick enough to carry it through four checkpoints, and an archivist, and—yes—he knew about Davor too. He had known about Davor since the man had let a coffin through the Second Gate without looking inside it. Grief made guards careless; guilt made them generous. Corvane had built his career on the certainty that everyone had a crack in them somewhere, and that cracks could be found by lamplight if one was patient.

He drew the orchard district toward himself across the table and studied it. Old rows of apple trees, planted when the district had been a working orchard before the tanneries came. Narrow lanes the wagons could not enter. A hundred hiding places and a thousand sympathizers.

Fire, then. Fire was precise, in its way. It burned what needed burning and it taught the rest of the city the cost of sheltering sedition, and by morning there would be nothing left in those lanes for a broadsheet to hide behind.

He dipped his pen. His hand did not shake. It never shook.

*By first light,* he wrote, *the orchard rows are to be cleared and burned. Displacement of residents is authorized. Resistance is to be treated as evidence of complicity.*

He read it over twice, corrected *cleared* to *evacuated*, and signed.

* * *

The Graykeep's cells smelled of wet stone and the tallow they were allowed instead of candles, and Aldous Ashgrove sat on the edge of the pallet with his back straight, as if he were still at his workbench and the darkness merely another kind of lamplight he had declined to use.

Corvane came down himself. This was unusual—he had not descended into the cells in six years—but he wanted to look at the printer's hands. He had been thinking about the hands all evening.

"You're being moved up," Corvane said, without greeting. "Your execution. Dawn. Not the day after."

Aldous turned his head. In the gloom his face was mostly shadow and grey beard, but his eyes found the magistrate with the ease of a man who had spent forty years reading small letters by bad light. "Moving me up," he said. "You make it sound like an appointment."

"It is an appointment. Everything is." Corvane stood where the torch from the stair threw him into silhouette; he preferred to be a shape rather than a face when he conducted business of this sort. "I'll tell you plainly, Ashgrove, because you and I are the only two men left in this city who understand plainness. Bring me the plate tonight and you die of old age in your bed, in your own house, with your daughter beside you. I will even have the charges struck from the record. There will be no record. That is my gift to you: erasure, done kindly."

"And if I don't?"

"Then at dawn the orchard burns, and your daughter is pulled out of it or dies in it, and you will have chosen a rectangle of lead over both of you." Corvane let the silence do some of the work. "I've seen your typeface, you know. The one you cut for wedding invitations. Very fine hairlines. A man who makes letterforms that delicate understands precision. Understands that a single flaw ruins the whole forme. You are the flaw, Ashgrove. You always were. Your wife was another."

Something moved in the cell then—not the prisoner's face, which stayed still, but the air itself, the quality of his attention, going hard as cooled metal.

"My wife," Aldous said quietly, "has been gone twelve years, and you still can't resist using her as a lever. That's not precision, Corvane. That's a man reaching for whatever's nearest because his hands are empty."

"Are they? Yours seem quite full lately. A defected soldier. A blind woman who memorizes forbidden books. A child." Corvane took a slow step closer. "Do you know what all of them have in common, printer? Every one of them is someone whose name appears nowhere in any ledger I keep. Invisible people. And invisible people imagine themselves safe, because they have confused being unseen with being beyond reach. But I reach everyone. Eventually. It is the one thing I have never failed at."

Aldous rose from the pallet. He was older than the magistrate and thinner, and the chains at his ankles dragged, and still he managed to stand like a compositor at his stone: settled, certain, occupying exactly the space his craft required.

"When my daughter was small," he said, "she asked me why letters had serifs. Little feet, she called them. I told her the feet were there so the words could stand up and be walked across—so a reader's eye wouldn't fall off the end of a line in the dark." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Corvane had seen in a year of sad things. "You should have put feet on your lies, Magistrate. Then people might have stood on them longer."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned to the turnkey at the stair.

"He refuses," he said. "Dawn stands. And send word to Captain Ilse: the orchard operation moves to nightfall, not morning. I want the press found before the fire, not under it." He paused at the bottom step, one hand on the wet stone. "And bring me the apprentice. The boy we took off Bridge Street. If he hasn't talked by the time I finish my accounts, wake me anyway."

* * *

Pip Halloway was seventeen, and he had been Juniper Ashgrove's apprentice for three years, and until two nights ago the largest dishonesty of his life had been watering down the shop's ink to stretch it further, which Aldous had known about and allowed because the boy's family was poor.

Now he sat in a room above the Graykeep's offices with a table, a chair, a lamp, and a man named Sergeant Brell who was very patient and very tired-looking, as though interrogation were shift-work he had grown bored of, and perhaps it was.

They had not beaten him. That was the worst part. Pip had braced for beating the way he'd braced for his first kiss, with a mixture of terror and a strange desire to get it over with, and instead they had given him bread and cheese and a stool for his aching feet, and Brell had simply talked. About printing, mostly. Brell's father had been a bookbinder in Callowmarket; Brell knew what a quoin was, and a frisket, and the difference between a bodkin and a burnisher, and every time he used one of the words correctly Pip felt a little more of himself come loose, like type worked free by a careless thumb.

"We know the girl carried something out of your master's shop," Brell said, for perhaps the ninth time. "We know it went to ground in the orchard. We don't know where. And here is the thing, lad, here is the thing I want you to hold on to." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, weary and reasonable. "It doesn't matter whether you tell me. Truly. By tomorrow noon the orchard district is ash, and whatever hole the plate is sleeping in burns with it. The only variable is you. Whether you're standing in that ash when it falls, or sitting in a room with bread and a future. That's the entire question. I couldn't care less about the rest."

"That's not true," Pip said. His voice cracked on it. "You care about the plate. That's the whole reason I'm here."

Brell smiled, and for a moment the weariness fell away and something else looked out of his face, something with teeth in it. "Good," he said softly. "There he is. The boy who knows things. Now. The plate doesn't matter, because the plate is dead either way. But the *press*, Pip. Presses are alive. Presses have people. Ink, paper, a frisket, a stone, four hands minimum to run a job that size. People, lad. Warm bodies who will be standing in those lanes when the torches come through. Tell me where the press is and I can move them out first. I'm not asking you to save lead. I'm asking you to save throats."

And that was the knife of it, and Pip knew it was a knife and felt it go in anyway, because he thought of Wren with her solemn bird-face and her bundle clutched to her chest, thought of Maren's quick fingers reading type like scripture, thought of Juniper—Juniper who had spoken to him exactly four times in three years and each time as though his presence in the shop were a small miracle she was politely declining to comment on—and he imagined torches moving down the orchard lanes and the trees going up like tapers and everyone he had failed to protect standing in the smoke.

He told himself he would lie. He told himself, right up until the moment his mouth opened, that he would give them the tannery road and nothing accurate, a wrong turning, a false door.

What came out instead, in a voice he did not recognize as his own, was: "The old cider house. Under the orchard. There's a cellar with a double floor—the press is under the floor. I helped carry the stone for it last spring. I thought—I told myself it was for a cider press. A real press. Apples." He was crying now, ugly and silent, the way he had learned to cry in the shop where Aldous slept in the back room. "I carried the stone myself."

Brell wrote it down without hurry. When he finished, he looked at the boy for a while, and the tiredness came back into his face, and with it something almost like gentleness.

"You did the right thing," he said. "Remember that, in a year, when you're eating regularly. You saved lives tonight."

"No," Pip said. "I don't think I did anything of the kind." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Can I ask you something? You knew all those words. Quoin. Frisket. Did you really grow up around books?"

"I did."

"Then how did you end up here?"

Brell capped his inkwell. For a moment he seemed about to answer, and then he didn't, and the silence he left behind was the truest thing anyone had said to Pip Halloway all night.

* * *

Word reached Corvane in his study at half past three, delivered by a captain with rain on her shoulders and the news written in her face before she spoke it.

"The cider house," she said. "Under the orchard, south row. The boy gave it clean—cellar, double floor, a press with a recent stone. We have it confirmed by two of the men who watched the coffin come through Second Gate; it went that direction."

Corvane set down his pen. He had been writing the final paragraph of the day's orders—the disposition of pickets along the tannery road—and the interruption cost him nothing but the end of a sentence, which he finished anyway, because unfinished sentences offended him even now.

"So," he said. "Everything converges."

"Shall I take the press tonight?"

"No." He rose and went to the window. Below, Vellamark lay black under a low ceiling of cloud, the river a dull seam of reflected lamplight, and far to the southeast—too far to see, but visible to him nonetheless—the orchard district slept with its traitors in it. "Tonight we take nothing. We encircle. Quietly, in the dark, before the fog comes off the water. Every lane, every footpath, every gap in the hedges. Nothing leaves those trees—not a person, not a page, not a rumour. At dawn the fire goes through the outer rows to seal the perimeter and drive them down into the cellar where we want them, and my men take the press intact, and the plate with it, and everyone whose hands are inky."

The captain hesitated. She was Ilse, newly promoted, competent, and she had served him long enough to know when a hesitation would be forgiven. "And Ashgrove?"

"Dawn stands. Same hour. Let them hear the drum from their little cellar while they're setting their type." Corvane found that he was smiling, and examined the smile with mild interest, the way one examines an unfamiliar tool. "Symbolism, Captain, is simply typography performed with fire. The lesson must arrive on the same page as the punishment, or the reader loses the argument halfway down."

"And the girl? Ashgrove's daughter?"

"She'll be there." He said it with complete certainty, and the certainty was itself a pleasure, cool and clean as a well-cut counter-punch in a letterform. "She has spent twelve years learning to be invisible, which means she has spent twelve years learning exactly the skills required to walk into my orchard tonight. People always bring their finest training to their ruin. It's almost touching. No—she'll be there, Captain, with the archivist and the soldier and the child, because everything they love is in that cellar, and frightened families gather. That is what families are *for*."

He returned to the table and added a final line to the orders, his pen moving without haste:

*At dawn, the fire.*

Outside, the first grey breath of fog was rising off the river, crawling up the embankments, sliding through the streets toward the southeast and the sleeping trees. Corvane watched it go with the satisfaction of a man watching his own ink flow evenly across a page.

In the orchard cellar, by a shielded lamp, hands were setting type.

He did not know that yet. But he would know it soon, and briefly, and then never again—and the fog slid on through the dark toward the trees, carrying the smell of the river and the coming fire, patient as print.
