## 18. Night in the Orchard

The orchard smelled of smoke that had not yet been lit.

Juniper noticed it at once—the wrongness of it, a smell like a memory of fire rather than fire itself, as though the trees were already grieving their own burning. The last row of apple trunks gave way to a sunken hollow where the old cider house had half-collapsed into the earth, its roof beams black with forty years of rain, and beneath those beams, hidden under straw and rot and the droppings of pigeons who had never known they guarded anything, waited the press.

Wren saw it first. The child let out a sound—not a word, nothing so formed—just a small broken note of recognition, and broke from Maren's side to run down the slope.

"Wren—" Juniper started after her, but Maren's hand found her arm in the dark, sure as a compass needle.

"Let her go," the archivist said quietly. Her face was turned toward the sound of small feet on wet grass. "That's her mother's place. Let her arrive first."

They came down more slowly, Davor limping badly now, his ribs wrapped in strips of Maren's spare shirt, his face still carrying the yellow-green bloom of the beating he had taken at the first gate. He had not spoken since the tanneries except to count—bridges, guards, lanterns, minutes—and his counting voice had a flatness to it that frightened Juniper more than any complaint would have. A man who stopped describing pain had simply folded it away somewhere she could not see.

Inside the cider house, Wren stood before the press with both hands pressed flat against its iron frame, the way a child touches the flank of a sleeping horse. It was an old hand-press, older than the one in her father's shop, its screw dark with oil someone had kept applying through all the silent years, its bed swept clean of dust. Beside it, shelves ran along the earthen wall: type in cases, paper wrapped in oilcloth, three lamps with full reservoirs, a whetstone, a knife. Someone had kept this room ready for years without knowing when—or whether—it would ever be needed.

"Who tended it?" Juniper asked softly.

Maren's fingers moved along the shelf edges, reading the wood like a page. "My brother," she said. "And after him, whoever he trusted. There have always been hands. That is what Corvane never understood." She turned her blind face toward Juniper, and in the dim light coming through the collapsed roof her eyes seemed almost ordinary, almost calm. "You can burn an orchard. You cannot burn the habit of tending."

Davor went at once to the doorway, because that was what he was now—he had made himself the hinge of every door they passed through—and looked out across the dark rows of trees toward the distant glow of Vellamark's walls. What he said next he said without turning around.

"They'll come before morning."

"You don't know that," Juniper said, though she did.

"I know how orders move. I know what a captured apprentice buys them." He flexed his ruined left hand, wincing. "Tomas told them enough. Maybe not the exact tree—but the district, certainly. They will seal the orchard roads within hours, and then they will come down the rows with torches and start at one end." He paused. "We should set type now. Print fast. Leave the rest to luck."

"No," said Wren.

It was the firmest word the child had spoken in days. She turned from the press, and her small face was streaked with dust and old tears, but her chin was up in a way Juniper had never seen.

"We do it right," Wren said. "Mama said names are only true if you set them carefully. If you rush them, they're just letters. She used to say—" the voice caught, steadied— "she used to say my father's name wrong once in the ledger and it took him two years to get his pay corrected. Two years. Because one letter was wrong." Her fists clenched at her sides. "So we do it *right*."

No one answered for a moment. Somewhere above them a night bird called, and far off, so far it might have been imagination, a bell tolled in the city—a watch bell, or perhaps a warning.

Then Maren smiled, that slow unfolding smile Juniper had come to think of as a lamp being lit in a window.

"The child is the archivist tonight," Maren said. "I'll read the list. She sets the names. Juniper composes the frame. Davor—" she tilted her head toward the door, "—you keep being the door."

* * *

They worked by lamplight, and the lamplight made a small gold country inside the ruin, bordered by darkness on every side.

Juniper opened the type cases and ran her fingers over the sorts and felt her father's hands teaching her, twelve years ago, in the shop on Chandler's Row: *Feel the nick, Junie. Every letter has a nick cut into its stem so the compositor knows by touch which way it faces. Even in the dark, even blind drunk, even weeping—you can find your way by the nick. Type forgives no carelessness and rewards no hurry.* Aldous Ashgrove's voice in her head, steady as a metronome. She thought of him in the Graykeep tonight, in some cell whose dimensions she refused to imagine, and she took that thought—that cold hard seed of him—and planted it in her chest where it could push her forward instead of pinning her down.

She built the forme first: borders, masthead, the columns of empty space waiting to be filled. Then Maren sat with the list she had carried in her head for eleven years—every name, every trade, every street of the disappeared—and began to recite.

"Hetta Brandt. Cooper's daughter, tanner by trade. Taken from the Salt Gate, ninth of Harvestmonth."

Wren knelt at the case, lips moving as she searched the compartments, hunting letters the way other children hunted eggs. Her fingers were quick and surprisingly certain. H. E. T. T. A.

"Wrong tray," Maren said gently, without seeing. "Small caps are your left."

Wren corrected herself. Set the name. Looked up. "Like this?"

"Read it back to me."

"*Hetta Brandt.*"

"Again."

"*Hetta Brandt.*"

"Good," Maren said. "Now she exists twice. Once in me and once in lead. Lead lasts longer than flesh does. Remember that, little wren. We are making things tonight that outlast everyone in this room."

Juniper locked each line into the chase with quads and furniture, her movements precise, economical, the work of twelve years flowing through her hands while her mind stood apart and watched the darkness at the ruined doorway where Davor stood guard. Twice he raised a fist—their signal—and twice they froze, lamps hooded, breath held, until his fist lowered again and the work resumed. Once it was a patrol lantern moving along the orchard road, unhurried, oblivious. Once it was only a fox. But the second time, Juniper noticed, the lantern had paused at the mouth of the lane. Paused, hung there a long moment, and moved on.

"They're mustering already," Davor murmured when it passed. "I can hear wheels on the north road. Heavy ones. Gun carriages, maybe. Or wagons for prisoners." He looked back at them, and the lamplight carved his bruised face into planes of shadow. "How many names?"

"Forty-one so far," Maren said.

"How many altogether?"

"One hundred and six," said Maren. "One hundred and six, and I will say every one of them before that press strikes a single sheet."

* * *

Hours passed. The candles burned down; they lit the second lamp, then the third. Wren's knees ached from kneeling on the stone and no one suggested she rest, because she would have refused, and everyone knew it. Somewhere past midnight she set a name and went still over it, her small shoulders rigid.

"This one," she whispered. "This is Mama's friend. Sylwen. Sylwen Oakes. She taught me letters. Before." Her voice wavered and held. "Before Mama said run."

"Spell it," Maren said softly. "Every letter is a candle you light for her."

Wren spelled it. Set it. Read it back three times. And then, unbidden, she did something that stopped Juniper's heart: she reached into the case, chose a small ornamental fleuron—a tiny leaf, of all things, a printer's leaf—and set it beside the name.

"For the orchard," Wren explained, daring anyone with her eyes to object.

Juniper looked at Maren. Maren's face had gone very still, the way it did when something touched her deepest. "Keep it," the archivist said at last, her voice roughened. "Keep it for every one of them. A leaf for every stolen tree. Let Corvane's own broadsheet be an orchard he cannot burn."

So it became their ritual. Name, leaf. Name, leaf. The forme filled slowly with the dead and the disappeared, each marked with the small green thought of a leaf, and Juniper worked the composing stick beside Wren, feeding her sorts, checking her spacing, correcting nothing harshly, and understood—with a clarity that felt almost like fever—that this was the thing her whole silent life had been building toward. Not the plate hidden in wedding invitations. Not the flight through tanneries. This: a child's fingers learning that letters are people, that setting a name is raising the dead by inches, that ink is a promise you make in public.

*I am done being quiet,* she thought, and the thought had no fear in it anymore, only weight, good weight, like a full forme locked tight in its chase.

Near the third hour, Maren reached the end of the list and sat back on her heels, exhausted, her hands trembling slightly in her lap.

"And the last line," Juniper said. She had planned this in the coffin cart, between the mourners, staring at the lid above her. "There has to be a last line."

Maren turned her face toward her, listening.

"Not just the accusation. Anyone can accuse. Corvane will call it lies, and half the city will believe him, because fear makes people stupid." Juniper knelt and began setting the words as she spoke them, her hands moving ahead of her voice. "But if a soldier signs it—if one of their own says *I was there, I saw, here is my name, judge me with the rest*—then there is no lie big enough to cover it." She finished the line and read it aloud:

"*Set and witnessed by Davor Rehn, late of the Third Garrison, who stood at the Salt Gate on the ninth of Harvestmonth and did nothing. He asks no forgiveness. Only that his name stand beside theirs.*"

Silence. Out in the dark, the gun carriages rolled and rolled.

Davor had come inside without anyone hearing him—he moved quietly for such a large man—and he stood over the forme reading it with his eyes, since he could not read it any other way. His jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice came out sanded down to bare wood.

"You didn't have to put in the part about doing nothing."

"Yes, I did," Juniper said. "It's the truest sentence on the page."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod at orders they intend to obey, and went back to the door.

* * *

They inked the press as the sky beyond the broken roof went the color of wet slate. Maren tested the rollers; Juniper dampened the first sheets; Wren fetched water from the rain barrel, hauling the bucket with both hands, refusing help. The press itself needed muscle, and that fell to Davor between his watches at the door—he pulled the great bar with his good arm, gritting against his cracked ribs, and each impression came off the bed crisp and black and perfect, better than Juniper dared hope from a press this old.

The first finished broadsheet lay drying on the stone. Juniper picked it up and read it entire by lamplight, top to bottom, and her eyes blurred halfway through, and she let them. One hundred and six names. One hundred and six leaves. One soldier's confession. At the bottom, in type her father had cut with his own hands years ago and given to the underground without ever asking what it would carry:

*Printed in Vellamark, in the orchard, in the dark, by people who refuse.*

"Maren," she said, and could not finish.

"I know," Maren said. "I felt you stop breathing. Keep breathing. We're not done."

But the east was graying at its hem now, and Davor stood at the doorway with his head cocked like a man listening to weather, and what he heard was worse than weather.

"Boots," he said. "Lots of boots. Coming up the north lane. And behind them—" his face changed, and Juniper saw fear cross it openly for the first time since the tanneries, "—they've brought pitch and faggots. They mean to burn the rows while we work."

Juniper looked at the stack of printed sheets—two hundred, perhaps, and the city needed thousands—and at the press that could not run faster, and at Wren asleep at last on Maren's shoulder with an ink-smudged cheek, and at the gray light rising like water.

"Then we print," she said, pulling the next blank sheet onto the bed, "until the smoke comes."
