## 19. Ink Before Dawn

The press in the orchard cellar was older than any of them—older than the magistrate, older than the checkpoints, older perhaps than the city's habit of looking away. It had been built in the years when Vellamark still believed ink was a public trust, and its iron frame wore that belief like a patina, every bolt polished by generations of hands that had refused to be quiet.

Juniper stood before it with ink to her elbows and her heart hammering somewhere behind her teeth.

Above them, through the grate in the cellar ceiling, she could hear the orchard moving—not the trees, though a wind had risen with the small hours and set the bare branches knocking like knuckles on a door. Soldiers. Boots on frozen ground, the creak of leather, the low bureaucratic murmur of men who had been told to surround a square of earth and were patient about it. The fires would come at dawn. Corvane had ordered it, Wren's contact in the market had confirmed it: burn the rows, burn the press-house above, burn whatever history the orchard district still held.

So they printed.

"Forme is locked," Maren said, her fingers flying over the type one last time, reading it the way other people read the faces of loved ones. She moved down the line of metal with both hands, left and right working in tandem, and Juniper had stopped wondering how she did it weeks ago. "Headline first. *The Names of the Vanished.* Then Corvane's order—the one he thought no copy of survived. Then the list."

"How many names tonight?" Juniper asked, though she knew.

Maren's hands stilled on the last line. "Forty-one," she said. "And mine will be the forty-second if we fail. I've memorized it too, just in case. It seems only practical."

Juniper laughed—a short, startled sound, half hysteria—and pulled the lever for the first proof.

* * *

Davor heard the press before he saw anything: a rhythmic iron heartbeat rising out of the ground beneath his boots, muffled by the cellar but persistent as water finding stone. He stood at the orchard gate with a soldier's coat buttoned to the throat and a soldier's rifle in his hands, and he understood, with the clarity that sometimes comes to men at the edge of ruin, that this was the exact shape of the night he had been walking toward for three years.

The night of the relocations, he had been posted at the tannery road. He had seen the carts go out full and come back empty. He had looked at the river instead of the carts, because looking at the carts would have required him to do something, and doing something would have required him to stop being a man who followed orders. Forty-one people had gone into the water or the ground or wherever Corvane sent the inconvenient, and Davor had stood at his post and counted his own heartbeats and told himself it was war, it was the times, it was above him.

Tonight he counted soldiers instead.

Four patrols ringed the orchard, twelve men each, spaced for a sweep that would begin when the signal fires went up. He could hold the gate against the first patrol for perhaps ten minutes—longer if they hesitated, and soldiers hesitated when one of their own stood in front of them with a levelled rifle and a face they knew. After that, arithmetic failed.

He checked the flint of the lantern at his feet. Unlit. Its only purpose now was to be lit at the wrong moment, if it came to that—to be thrown into the dry brush at the eastern fence so the fire started early, away from the cellar, and drew the patrol like moths. A coward's trick or a hero's, depending on who wrote the account. Depending on whether anyone survived to write an account at all.

From below, faint as a pulse, the press kept its rhythm. *Names,* he thought. *They're printing names.* His own among them—he had asked for that, demanded it, and Juniper Ashgrove had looked at him across the type case with those grey eyes that missed nothing and said, *Then you'd better live long enough to be ashamed in public.*

He grinned at the dark, alone at the gate, and found that his hands had finally stopped shaking.

* * *

Wren's job was the small type.

Her fingers were the smallest and the steadiest, and after two nights of practice she could set a name in eight-point lead faster than Juniper could read it aloud. She worked by lamplight at the composing stone, tweezers in hand, lifting each letter from its case and setting it in the stick the way her mother had taught her to set beads on a string—left to right, careful, careful, don't drop the thread.

*TABITHA HOLLIS.* Her mother's name, in mirror-writing on the stone. When the press printed it, it would come out right-way-round and true, and anyone in Vellamark who read the broadsheet would read her mother's name and know it had belonged to someone.

"Don't space it tight," Juniper murmured, passing behind her with a fresh tin of ink. "Give her room to breathe."

"It's not *her,*" Wren said. "It's letters."

"It's her," Juniper said, and did not argue further, which was one of the things Wren had decided she liked best about Juniper Ashgrove: she said a thing once, plainly, and then let you decide what to do with it.

Wren breathed, and loosened the spacing, and gave her mother room.

Somewhere above, a branch cracked under a boot. Everyone in the cellar froze—the archivist's head tilting like a bird's, Juniper's hand flat on the press, Davor's empty chair by the stairs where he wasn't, because he was up at the gate being brave and stupid in exactly equal measure. The boot passed. The wind knocked the branches again. The lamp flame leaned and straightened.

"Forty-two names now?" Maren asked quietly, from across the stone. She always knew where Wren was in the forme; she said she could feel whose name was where, though Wren suspected that was a kindness dressed up as magic.

"Forty-two," Wren confirmed, and set the final period herself, and felt it land in her chest like a coin dropped down a well.

* * *

By the hundredth impression, Juniper had found the rhythm that her father called *the conversation*—the dialogue between hand and machine, pull and release, the platen's kiss and the paper's surrender. You did not force an old press. You listened to it. Aldous had taught her that at nine years old, standing behind her on a stool much like this stool, his big scarred hands over her small ones: *Feel for the moment it wants to turn, Junie. Everything has a moment.*

She thought of him in the Graykeep and pulled harder.

Stacks of broadsheets rose along the cellar wall, wet ink gleaming in the lamplight, forty-two names multiplied into hundreds upon hundreds of sheets. Enough for the market squares. Enough for the Graykeep steps. Enough, if the wind was kind and the citizens of Vellamark were braver than they had been in twelve years, for every doorway in the city to wear a list of the dead like a candle in a window.

Maren moved among the stacks, counting by touch, squaring edges with her forearms. "Two hundred and sixty," she reported. "The ink's setting. We should begin thinking about leaving before we want to."

"We'll give it another hundred."

"Juniper." The old woman's voice was gentle and immovable, like the root of an oak. "The fire is not a rumor anymore. I can smell it."

And she could—Juniper realized that with a lurch. Not smoke yet, not exactly, but something in the air had changed, a thin acrid thread woven through the cold cellar damp. Torches gathering. Pitch being prepared. Dawn was an hour off, maybe less, and Corvane's men would not wait for sunrise out of politeness.

"One more hundred," she said anyway, because her father was in the Graykeep, because Wren's mother was under the river, because forty-one people had been erased by a man with a private press and a public office, and every sheet that came off this bed of type was a nail in the coffin of his certainty. "Then we run."

* * *

The first flare went up over the eastern wall at half past four—a red star climbing on a tail of sparks, blooming, dying. Davor watched it fall and did the arithmetic again and got a worse answer.

They were moving early.

Torches kindled along the tree line, a dozen points of orange light where there should have been darkness, and the patrol nearest the gate began to advance through the bare orchard rows at a walk—unhurried, professional, men who had done this before and expected no opposition worth the name. In the lead was a sergeant Davor recognized. Hask. Decent enough at cards. Had once given his rations to a beggar woman outside the tannery gate, back before the tannery gate was a place where people disappeared.

"Hold," Davor called, stepping out from the gatehouse shadow, rifle shouldered. His voice carried in the frozen air the way voices do over snow. "Gate's closed by my order."

Hask stopped. Raised a torch, squinted. "Ostrava? That you? What in the—" He lowered the torch slowly, and Davor watched understanding move across the man's face like weather. "Step aside, Davor."

"There are civilians inside."

"There's a *press* inside, is what there is. Magistrate's orders. Whole quadrant burns at dawn, and dawn's coming fast." Hask glanced back at his line of men, then forward again, and his voice dropped. "I've read the broadsheet, Ostrava. The one from the market last week. I know what's on it." A pause, terrible and human. "Which is exactly why I can't let it spread. Step aside. Please. I'll say you weren't here."

"No," Davor said.

Hask sighed, the sigh of a man who has drawn the bad card in a game he didn't choose, and raised his torch high, and the line of soldiers began to advance.

Davor fired the first shot into the ground six feet in front of them—a warning, deliberate, loud enough to wake the whole orchard and every conscience sleeping in Vellamark. The line halted. Somewhere to his left, a junior soldier's rifle came up nervously, and Davor tracked it, and time thinned to wire.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, to all forty-one of them, and to Hask, and to himself, three years younger and standing at the tannery road pretending the river was interesting. *This is me, looking at the carts.*

"Come on, then," he said aloud, and racked the bolt.

* * *

In the cellar, the shots came down through the earth like hail on a roof.

"He's engaging," Maren said. She had gone very still, head bowed, listening with her whole body. "One rifle. They're not returning fire yet—they're regrouping, I think, the footfalls are bunching." She turned her blind eyes toward Juniper, and in the lamplight they seemed almost sighted, fixed on some point beyond the walls. "We have minutes. Perhaps fewer than I said."

Juniper threw the lever one last time. The press spoke—*thump, hiss, sigh*—and the three-hundred-and-somethingth broadsheet slid onto the stack, complete, forty-two names legible as scripture.

"That's all of them. Bundle them. Wren—" She knelt, took the girl's shoulders. "You remember what Maren showed you?"

"The tannery drain, then the willow, then count thirty paces with the water on my left, then the low wall." Wren recited it flat and fast, the way children recite things they have decided to survive. "I carry the bundle. I don't stop for anything. Not even you."

"Not even me." Juniper's throat closed on the words, but she said them. "Go."

What happened next happened in pieces, and afterward Juniper could never have said in what order. Maren shouldering her pack and taking Wren's hand with the unerring ease of a woman who had navigated darkness all her life and feared nothing in it. The stairway door opening onto air that now, unmistakably, smelled of smoke. The press house above them beginning to crackle—or was that the trees, was that only wind—and the red glow through the grate that was no longer a torch-line but a fire, an actual fire, eating the eastern rows. Running. The bundle of broadsheets heavy against Wren's chest, tied with cord, and the girl running like a hare, sure-footed even in the dark because Maren's hand was on her shoulder and Maren did not need light.

Then the lamps went out.

Not blown out—doused, snuffed by a gust of superheated air that came down the stairwell ahead of the flames, and suddenly the cellar was black as the inside of a closed book.

"Wren! Maren!" Juniper's voice cracked in her own ears. She put her hands out and touched nothing. The smoke was coming. The exit was somewhere, and the walls were somewhere, and she had lived her whole life in this city and never felt so lost in it.

"Here," Maren's voice said, calm as a bell in fog. "Follow my voice, child. Left of the press. There's a bucket in your way—step wide. Now straight on. Twelve steps."

"Maren, you can't see—"

"I have never needed to see this room. I memorized it the hour we arrived—every beam, every barrel, every uneven flagstone. The dark is my country, Juniper. You are only visiting." A dry, fierce laugh. "Twelve steps. Come. Your girl is waiting at the drain, and she will not leave without you no matter what she promised, because she is her mother's daughter."

Juniper ran toward the voice with her eyes shut and her hands out, and Maren's voice laid the path before her step by step—the beam to duck, the barrel to skirt, the stair rail worn smooth—and it occurred to Juniper, absurdly, in the last seconds before the open air hit her face, that this was what her father had meant all those years about type. Every letter in its place. Every obstacle already known. Memory, set solid in lead, walked through by touch.

Everything has a moment. This one was hers.

* * *

At the gate, Davor's world had contracted to muzzle flash and torchlight and Hask's voice shouting *hold, HOLD, damn you* — because half the patrol had stopped shooting.

It happened the way it always happens, the way Davor had gambled it might: a boy of nineteen on the flank had picked up a discarded broadsheet from last week's scattering, had actually read it in the torchlight, and had begun, without seeming to know he was doing it, to lower his rifle. Then his neighbor saw. Then Hask saw. And the beautiful, terrible mathematics of conscience began propagating down the line faster than any order could travel against it.

"They're *names,*" the boy said. "Sergeant. My cousin's street is on this list."

"Fire is orders," Hask said hoarsely—but he was looking at the burning orchard, at the press house roof catching, at the column of orange climbing into the last of the night, and his rifle pointed at nothing.

Behind Davor, out of the smoke and the dark, three figures emerged at a dead run: a tall girl with ink-blackened hands, an old woman steady as a keel, and a small girl hugging a paper-wrapped bundle like it was warm. Alive. All alive. Davor felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the tannery road.

"The south fence," he gasped. "Go—it's clear, I pulled the patrol—"

"You're coming with us," Juniper said.

"In a minute." He turned back to the line of soldiers, most of whom had now fully lowered their weapons, several of whom were holding copies of the truth and looking at their sergeant, who was looking at the fire. Davor smiled at all of them, wolfish and exhausted. "Somebody has to tell the magistrate his own army read the news."

* * *

They ran south through the flooded tanneries with the orchard burning gold behind them, and Wren laughed as she ran—a wild, impossible sound—because the bundle was safe, and the names were safe, and the presses in the city proper would be waking within the hour.

Behind them, at the gate, a soldier's voice began, uncertainly, to read the list aloud.

Then a second voice joined it.
