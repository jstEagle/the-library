## 20. Paper on the Wind

Wren had never seen a dawn come up over water before.

In the orchard district, where she had lived the whole first span of her life, the sun rose through trees—filtered, broken into coins of light, always arriving late and apologetic. Here at the river's edge it came all at once, pouring flat and gold across the Vellamark, setting every window of the far shore burning, and turning the mist into something that looked less like fog and more like paper. Like sheets of it, drifting. As if the city itself were trying to tell her what was about to happen.

She stood in the doorway of the print shop with a bundle of broadsheets against her chest, still smelling of ink and lamp oil, and her arms shook.

Not from cold. She had been cold before; she knew cold the way she knew the back of her own hands, which were cracked and ink-stained now like a working printer's, though she was not yet nine. Her arms shook because inside this bundle were names. Eleven of them. And the eleventh name—the one at the very top of the list, in letters taller than the rest because Juniper had let Wren set them herself, one piece of type at a time, guiding her small fingers to each reversed and silver-lettered character—that name belonged to her mother.

*Ilset Thane.*

Wren said it silently, the way she had practiced all night while the men pressed and the women folded and the fires came closer on the far hills. She had learned it as a kind of prayer, except that prayers went upward to someone who might or might not be listening, and this name went outward, into the world, to everyone, whether they wanted to hear it or not.

That was the difference. That was everything.

"Wren." Juniper's voice behind her, low and rough from a night of breathing lamp fumes. "You remember which streets?"

Wren nodded, then remembered that Juniper had spent twelve years learning not to be noticed, and turned around anyway, and spoke out loud because those days were over. "The Fishmarket first. Then the Weavers' Stair. Then across by Maren's bridge when Davor opens it." She recited it like the multiplication tables her mother had taught her with apple slices. "And if anyone asks where I got the paper, I say I'm delivering wedding invitations for my aunt."

"Good." Juniper crouched down so they were eye to eye. In the gray-gold light her face looked carved out of tiredness, smudged under both eyes with ink where she had rubbed them, but her gaze was steady. "One more thing. When you put the papers down—in the market, on doorsteps, under doors—you don't run. You walk. Slow as Sunday."

"Why slow?"

"Because running says *I did something wrong.*" Juniper reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Wren's ear, the way her mother used to. "Walking says *this belongs to everyone.*"

Wren thought about that. She thought: *my mother ran.* She had pressed the plate into Wren's arms in the dark behind the tanneries and told her to run and not look back, and Wren had run, and she had never stopped being sorry for it even though there had been nothing else in the world to do.

Today she would walk.

* * *

The Fishmarket at dawn smelled of brine and old ice and the tarred rope they used to bind the day's catch, and it was nearly empty—which was what they had planned for—but the plan began to break apart almost immediately, because the fishwives were already there.

They arrived in twos and threes out of the morning mist, broad women in aprons, laying out their stalls with the wordless choreography of people who have done the same thing ten thousand mornings. Wren walked among them, slow as Sunday, sliding a broadsheet under the scale-weights at one stall, folding another into a fishmonger's empty crate. Nobody stopped her. Nobody even seemed to see her, and for once in her life invisibility felt like a gift instead of a grave.

Then the tallest of the fishwives, a woman with forearms like hams and a birthmark shaped like a boat on her cheek, paused in her arranging. Looked down at the paper lying on her scales. Wren froze three steps away, heart hammering, hand already reaching for the story about the aunt and the wedding invitations.

The woman picked it up. Read the great black headline—**THE NAMES OF THE DISAPPEARED**—and then read the smaller lines beneath, and her lips moved as she read, shaping each name carefully, and then, without any warning at all, she said the first name aloud.

"Tomas Ellery," she said, in the voice you use for weighing fish, ordinary as bread. "Tomas Ellery. Why, I knew his mother. Sold mackerel off this same row."

And then she said it again, louder, to nobody, to everybody. "Tomas Ellery!"

Another wife came over. Took the paper from her hands. Read. "Sela Voss," she read, and then, wonderingly, "they said Sela Voss was relocated. Relocated to where? To a hole in the Graykeep floor is where."

A third took it from her. A dockhand wandering past slowed, stopped, took it next. The paper moved from hand to hand through the growing market like a bucket passed along a fire line, and every pair of hands that received it read it, and every mouth that read it spoke, and by the time Wren slipped out of the market she could no longer count how many voices were saying the names, only that the sound followed her up the lane like weather. Like wind full of paper.

She had thought it would feel like triumph. It did not, quite. It felt like her mother's hands on her shoulders again, and the taste of apples, and eleven names going out into the world on legs that finally, finally walked instead of ran.

* * *

At the Weavers' Stair she found trouble, and found it wearing a red-and-gray uniform.

There were four soldiers at the foot of the stair, and they had gathered a knot of early-rising laundrywomen around them, and at the center of it all a sergeant with a voice like gravel in a sack was holding a broadsheet at arm's length and shouting.

"—sedition, this is. Printed filth. Whoever's handing these out will be found and flogged, and anyone caught with one—" he brandished it "—had better eat it fast."

The laundrywomen looked at him with the particular blankness of people who have survived many governments. Wren shrank against a wall with her remaining bundle, calculating. The stair was the fastest route down to Maren's bridge. Around it, the long way, lay two checkpoints and an hour she did not have, because the fires on the orchard hills were visible now even in daylight, thin gray threads rising from the rows where her mother had once worked, and every minute Corvane's men were climbing toward the press that was printing nothing anymore because Wren herself carried its last work against her chest.

She should go around. Juniper would say go around. Slow as Sunday, but around.

Then one of the laundrywomen—a tiny bent grandmother with a washbasket on her hip—did the most astonishing thing Wren had ever seen. She reached out with wet red hands and took the broadsheet out of the sergeant's grasp, smoothed it against her hip, held it up to the light, and began to read the names aloud in a cracked, unhurried voice, as if she were reading a grocery list, as if the sergeant were her nephew and late for dinner.

"Ilset Thane. Bram Osk. Ilset Thane. Dear me, sergeant, can you read? I'll teach you, my mother taught weavers' children letters for sixpence a season—Ilset Thane—"

"Give me that—"

"Ilset Thane," said the second washerwoman.

"Ilset Thane," said the third, and then the whole knot of them said it together, not shouted, just said, in the round patient voices of women who sing while they work, and the sound of it went up the stairwell of the Weavers' Stair and came echoing back down doubled, because voices do that in stone places, and the sergeant stood in the middle of it looking suddenly very young and very far from home.

His own soldiers were not looking at him. They were looking at the ground. And one of them—the youngest, a boy with down on his cheeks—was mouthing the name along with the women, silently, his lips moving: *Ilset Thane. Ilset Thane.*

Wren walked past all of them. Down the stair. Slow as Sunday. Nobody saw a child carrying wedding invitations for her aunt.

But at the bottom she looked back once, and saw the sergeant standing with the confiscated broadsheet still in his fist—and he had not thrown it down. He had opened it. He was reading it. His shoulders had a folded-in look, the look of a man discovering that the ground he has been standing on for years was never ground at all, only a roof, and a thin one.

* * *

Maren's bridge was chaos, and Davor was at the center of it, and Wren understood later that she had witnessed, without fully knowing it, the exact moment the magistrate's authority died in the city of Vellamark.

The bridge was one of the old stone ones, wide enough for carts, and it led to the Graykeep square, and by mid-morning the square was filling. Word of the broadsheets had run ahead of them like oil ahead of flame. People came from the Fishmarket and the Weavers' Quarter and the river stairs, and they came carrying the papers held high, and those who had none came anyway because you cannot hear eleven names spoken by a thousand mouths and stay home.

Davor stood at the bridge gate in his checkpoint uniform, and his face was swollen on one side where the tannery beating had left its marks, yellow-green now, healing into a map of everything he had paid. He had orders, Wren heard him telling the crowd, to hold the bridge. No gatherings. Disperse or be dispersed.

The crowd did not disperse. It did exactly what Juniper had taught her. It walked. Slowly, steadily, family by family, toward the gate, and it kept walking, and Davor stood in front of it with his hand on his baton and did not draw it, and Wren, wedged between two tall strangers, watched him make his choice in real time.

He stepped aside.

"Gate's open," he said, loud enough for his own squad to hear. "I'll not raise a hand over printed paper. Any man who wants to arrest me for it can write my name on his report. You'll find it in the broadsheet." A ragged sound went through the crowd—not quite laughter, not quite a cheer. "Third name from the bottom."

And that was when the other soldiers began to decide.

It happened slowly, the way a lockpick turns. A corporal near the far pylon grounded his halberd, butt-down, which is what soldiers do when a fight is over. Two more drifted to the railing, facing outward, guarding nothing, their backs to the square in a way that meant everything. The young one from the Weavers' Stair appeared—how he'd gotten there so fast Wren never learned—and stood beside Davor without a word, and Davor put one swollen hand briefly on his shoulder.

By the time the sun cleared the rooftops of the Graykeep itself, there was not a single soldier on that bridge facing the crowd.

Wren crossed with them. She had delivered her last bundle—pressed it into the hands of a ferryman's daughter with instructions she invented on the spot about a fictional aunt—so she climbed onto the low stone parapet where she could see, and what she saw made her breath catch and hold.

The square below the keep was full. Not angry-full, not riot-full. Something stranger than either. Hundreds of people stood shoulder to shoulder in the dawn-lit square, and from somewhere in the middle of them a voice rose, alone, reading: "*Bram Osk.*" And the crowd took it up like a bell being struck: "**Bram Osk.**" And then the lone voice again: "*Ansel Rood.*" And the crowd: "**Ansel Rood.**" Name after name after name, call and response, the oldest music there is, until the words rolled up the keep's gray walls and came back down and it seemed to Wren that the stones themselves had begun to speak.

She sat on the parapet with her legs dangling and her chin lifted and she said the names too, all of them, in order, because she had learned them from Maren in a cellar by candlelight and they were hers now, an inheritance heavier and lighter than anything else she owned. When they came to her mother's name she did not falter. She had promised Juniper she would not, and she had promised herself, and promises made in cellars are the strongest kind.

"*Ilset Thane,*" said the crowd, a thousand throats, and Wren felt the syllables pass through her chest like a tide going out and coming in.

That was when the keep's gates opened.

Everyone saw it. The great iron-studded doors swung inward and a rank of guardsmen emerged in Corvane's livery, and the crowd did not scatter—it tightened, drew breath, waited—and the captain at the front of the rank looked out over all those faces, and down at the broadsheets littering the cobblestones like snow, and up at the walls where his own sentries stood, and Wren saw him read the arithmetic of the morning in about four seconds.

Then he turned on his heel and marched his men back inside and closed the gates behind him.

From the crowd, one single laugh rose, then another, and then the whole square was laughing and crying and saying names, and somewhere in the middle of it all a bell began to ring—one of the market bells, rung by who knew whom—and Wren laughed too, high and helpless, because it was done. Not all of it, not yet, not the hardest parts, but this thing was done: the silence was broken everywhere at once, and it would never be whole again, and no one alive could gather up all that paper off all that wind.

* * *

She found Juniper at the edge of the square, standing very still in the shadow of a shuttered apothecary, watching. Wren crashed into her and Juniper caught her and held on, and neither of them said anything for a while, because there are moments when speaking is the smaller language.

When they finally drew apart, Juniper's eyes were shining and wrecked. "You walked?" she asked.

"Slow as Sunday."

"Good girl." Then Juniper's face changed—tightened—and Wren followed her gaze across the square to the Graykeep's iron doors, and understood before any words were said, because some things a child learns early and never forgets.

"The gates closed," Wren whispered. "But your father's still inside."

Juniper nodded once. Behind them the crowd was still chanting names, glorious and safe in its numbers, but Juniper was looking at the doors the way Wren had once looked at a locked tannery gate, and her jaw was set in a line Wren recognized from the night they set type by lamplight while the orchard burned.

"Aldous Ashgrove is behind those doors," Juniper said quietly, "and Corvane's guards have just decided not to die for him." She took Wren's hand. Hers was steady. "Stay close to me."

"Where are we going?"

"Through the door," said Juniper Ashgrove, who had spent twelve years perfecting silence, "to speak."
