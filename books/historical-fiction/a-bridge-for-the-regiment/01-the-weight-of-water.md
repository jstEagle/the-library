## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide came in first at the breakwater, the way it always did, sliding over the black rocks with a sound like a woman drawing breath before speech. Coral Voss heard it through the counting house window, through the smell of ink and lamp oil and old paper, and he did not look up. He had learned, in ten years, to hear the sea the way other men heard weather — as information only. The tide meant the fleet would put out within the hour. The fleet putting out meant the day's entries would need ruling and dating before the boats returned. Everything meant something else; that was the whole of bookkeeping, and very nearly the whole of his life.

His pen moved down the column without his watching it. *Debit: Maren Kell, boat share, four shillings six. Credit: powder, one tin.* He wrote in a hand so even that Director Brannock had once held a page to the light and said, half in wonder, *It's as if nobody wrote it at all.* Coral had taken it for the compliment it was meant to be. Invisibility, in Saltmere, was a trade like any other, and he had apprenticed to it young.

He was thirty-one years old and he could not remember the last time anyone had asked him a question about himself.

Through the window, past the harbor gate with its new chain, the water lay the color of hammered pewter, and on it the pearling fleet rode at anchor like sleeping birds — eleven boats, tarred hulls, patched sails, each one carrying eight divers and a tender and the accumulated weight of debts no diver could read. That was the arrangement, though no one called it an arrangement aloud. The company kept the books. The books kept the people. And Coral Voss kept the books.

* * *

He went down to the quay at midmorning because Brannock wanted the third boat's tally witnessed, and witnessing required his presence the way a signature requires a hand. The wind had come around from the east overnight and carried salt all the way up the streets, into the shutter-cracks of the houses, so that even the town smelled of the thing that took its living from the water and gave back little enough.

Sefa was already in the water.

He saw her before he saw the boat — saw the pale bloom of her towline breaking the surface as she walked the seabed below, forty feet down where the light went green and then grey, her fingers reading the oyster beds the way he read columns. She had been down four minutes by the rope's slackness. He knew how to tell a dive's length from a rope the way he knew how to tell a forged entry from a true one: by small hesitations in the line.

Tender-boy on the third boat was hauling her up now, hand over hand, and Coral stood at the quay's edge with his ledger-satchel against his hip and watched the water fold and boil and give her up. She broke surface gasping, threw her hair back with one motion, and gripped the gunwale while her chest fought for air. There was blood in her ears — he could see it in the way she tilted her head, working her jaw — and there was a shell in her fist, and when she opened her fingers the tender-boy made a sound like a struck bell.

A pearl. Even from the quay, even across twenty feet of bright morning water, Coral could see it was a pearl that would buy a man free.

She looked up. For a moment their eyes met across the gap between the drowned world and the dry one, and she raised the shell toward him — not offering it, never that; everything she brought up belonged to the company before it belonged to the morning — but showing him. As if he were worth showing things to. As if the man with the satchel were not the same man whose entries said she owed the company eleven pounds, nine shillings, and would owe it until she drowned or died of age, whichever came first.

Coral inclined his head. It was all he allowed himself.

She dove again, and he turned back to the counting house, and if his steps were slower going up than coming down, there was no one to mark it, because there was never anyone marking anything Coral Voss did.

* * *

The truth about Sefa Tamu — her true name, her birth island, the year of her sale — lived in the master ledger, folio nineteen, in an entry dated the year of the great fever, written in a hand that was not hers and not her seller's either. Coral knew this because he had written it. Not all of it. But enough.

She had been nine years old when her mother died of the fever, and the company had indentured her against her mother's outstanding debt, which was company practice and against the law both, the law being a thing that stopped at the harbor gate unless someone carried it out. Coral had been twenty-one then, three years orphaned, already fluent in the grammar of the books. He had been given the task of fair-copying the indenture roll and he had found, in the arithmetic, a way to make her mother's debt smaller than it was. Not gone. Smaller. A transposed figure here, an unentered payment there — kindness measured in shillings, invisible to everyone but God and the auditor who never came.

And then, two years later, braver and more foolish, he had altered the manumission entry itself — the record of the payment her freedom papers rested on — backdating a remittance so that the paper Sergeant Ives had sold her mother's cousin for three pounds would hold against any clerk's inspection. It held. It had held for seven years. She was free, on paper, and did not know it, because the paper saying so sat in a strongbox and the strongbox sat behind Brannock's desk and Brannock had reasons, none of them lawful, for keeping the whole fleet uncertain of what it was.

So she dove. Free or bound, the water did not care which, and neither, in practice, did the company. She dove because diving was the only trade she had been permitted to learn, and because the boats put out whether a woman was free or not, and because Coral Voss watched her from windows and quays and said nothing, ten years running, about the entries that kept her exactly where she was.

Some nights he told himself he had protected her. Some nights he believed it. Most nights he knew better. Kindness that lives only in a ledger is a coin no one can spend.

* * *

The afternoon passed in figures. He ruled the fleet's daily account: oyster shells opened, pearls graded, weights entered against each diver's name — not their names, he corrected himself, not truly; the ledger used the company names, the port names, the names given at intake or invented at the desk by clerks too hurried or too indifferent to ask. Sefa was *Sefa* because a clerk twelve years ago had liked the sound of it. Half the fleet answered to words that had been assigned to them like ration allotments. Their true names — island names, river names, names meaning *born-at-low-tide* and *she-who-listens* — those lived in the master ledger's front section, folios one through fourteen, in columns Coral had copied out once and burned the draft of in the yard brazier, and had dreamed about for a month after.

He knew every name in those folios. That was the secret shape of his life: the most invisible man in Saltmere was also the only one who knew everyone, truly, completely — their origins, their debts, their diseases entered as depreciation, in one case an infant entered as *equipment, minor*. He carried them all in his head the way the harbor carried its wrecks, silently, dangerously, in water nobody saw the bottom of.

At five the bell rang the fleet home, and at half past the divers came up the quay steps wringing out their hair, and the tally line formed outside the counting house door, and Coral sat at his table with the day-book open while Brannock took the shells and read the weights and spoke to each diver with the warm, coarse heartiness he wore like a coat for public hours.

"*Voss.*" Brannock's voice, from across the room. "Third boat's pearl. Where d'you want it entered?"

"Top grade," Coral said. "Her line."

Brannock grunted, weighed it, whistled. "Best stone off that boat since spring." He dropped it in the velvet tray and looked out the window at the quay, at the woman walking up the steps with a towel around her shoulders. "Shame about all that, eh? Talent like that, wasted on—" He stopped himself, smiled his public smile, patted the ledger. "Enter it."

*Wasted on a debtor,* he had been about to say. On a body the books owned. Coral dipped his pen and wrote *Top grade, third boat, Sefa, 1 pearl,* and the pen pressed harder than it needed to, and the ink blotted, and he corrected nothing.

* * *

That evening he climbed to his room above the sailmaker's loft with bread and a heel of cheese and his private notebook, the one not owned by the company, the one he kept sewn inside the lining of his winter coat. He lit the lamp, ate without tasting, and wrote what he always wrote: the day, the tide, the take. Then, beneath it, in a shorthand of his own devising that no clerk in the Empire could have read:

*T's pearl = freedom-price. If she ever learns the manumission is real, they'll void it rather than honor it. If she asks me, I will have to lie. Again.*

He sat with the pen above the page for a long time. Down the street someone was singing — one of the Esterly girls, practicing for the New Year, though New Year was months away and the song was older than the town. The tide turned below the breakwater with its long exhaled sigh, and the lamplight guttered, and Coral Voss, keeper of every name and owner of none, blew out the flame and sat in the dark being fluent in the only language he had left.

He did not know — could not, yet — that within the week the sea itself would change, that grey shapes would come up the shipping lane at dawn with gun-metal in their hulls and soldiers on their decks, and that the question of who owned whom would be taken out of Brannock's hands and Brannock's books entirely.

But that night the water lay quiet, and Sefa slept somewhere in the divers' row with salt still in her hair, and the ledger lay locked in its drawer holding every name in the town like a fist, and Coral listened to the tide come in over the breakwater and thought, as he had thought for ten years, *tomorrow I will tell her.*

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow.

The tide, which keeps every promise except that one, came in.
