## 2. Gunboats at Dawn

The guns woke us before the gulls did.

That was the first wrongness — the order of the morning inverted. For ten years I had measured my days by the sound of Saltmere's gulls quarreling over the fish offal on the quay, a noise so constant it had become a kind of silence to me, and beneath it the slower rhythm of the tide knocking against the harbor pilings like a patient creditor. But this morning there was thunder with no cloud in the sky, three flat concussions rolling across the water from beyond the headland, and the gulls went up in a single ragged sheet and did not come down again.

I was already awake. I had been awake since the fourth bell, which was my habit, though not by any virtue of discipline. I slept badly now; I had slept badly for ten years, since the night the company's boat came back without my father and Sergeant Ives sat me down in the counting house with a cup of rum I did not want and read aloud to me, in his careful clerk's voice, the words he had written about a man he had never seen drown. *Loss of footing while working the winch. Body not recovered.* I had been fourteen, and I had listened to that lie being read over my father's absence, and something in me had folded shut like a ledger closed at year's end, all the accounts carried forward, nothing settled.

So: awake, then, in my room above the counting house, listening to guns.

I dressed in the dark out of habit rather than fear, though fear arrived soon enough, the way cold water arrives through canvas — slowly, then everywhere. From my window the harbor lay gray and flat under a sky the color of unwashed linen, and beyond the headland I could see them: three hulls, low and dark, funnels breathing thin black smoke into the still air. Not merchantmen. The lines were wrong for merchantmen, too clean, too purposeful, the way a knife is purposeful compared to an oar. Gunboats. I knew the word the way everyone in Saltmere knew it, from stories of other ports, other requisitions further up the coast — places that had been spoken of afterward in the lowered voices people use for the dead and the bankrupted.

I stood at the window until the light improved enough to show me detail, and what detail showed me made my stomach go quiet and heavy, like a stone dropped into deep water. Marines on the foredecks. Ranks of them, small as painted figures, standing at ease in the way men stand when they are waiting to be told to stop standing at ease. And launch boats dropping from the sides of the nearest hull, six of them, eight, pulling for our quay in slow unison, oars lifting and falling with a rhythm that had no relationship to the tide or to anything human.

Saltmere had seen soldiers before. We were a port; ports see everything eventually. There had been a customs cutter stationed here two winters ago, and once, years back, a governor's inspection that Director Brannock had spent a week preparing for, airing the counting house's best ledgers and hiding its worst ones behind false spines on the record shelf. I had helped hide them. I had been sixteen and invisible even then, and Brannock had said, patting my shoulder with his soft ringed hand, *You have a talent for what doesn't get noticed, boy,* and I had taken it for praise because at sixteen you take whatever is offered.

But this was not a customs cutter. This was not an inspection. Launches do not carry inspectors in that number, and gunboats do not anchor in a pearl port unless someone in a capital city has signed a paper saying the port belongs to them now.

Downstairs, the counting house was already lit. That was the second wrongness. Brannock never arrived before nine; he arrived at nine exactly, in a coat brushed by his houseboy, smelling of bay rum and grievance. But at this hour, with the guns still echoing their last flat syllables off the headland, lamplight was already bleeding yellow through the counting house windows onto the wet cobbles, and through the glass I could see Brannock himself moving among the desks, and behind him his two senior clerks, Merrow and Pell, hauling the record shelf away from the wall.

He had known. He had known before the guns, and he had not told us.

I did not go in through the front. Ten years in that building had taught me its doors the way a diver knows the holes in a reef, and I came around the side through the passage where the diving weights were stored, past the smell of cold iron and brine, and entered by the records room, where I belonged, where no one would think to wonder why I was.

The master ledger stood open on the long table.

You must understand what that means. In ten years, the master ledger had left its locked case four times. It was brought out for audit, for the annual closing, and for the signing of indentures, and each time it was handled by Brannock personally or watched by him while others handled it, the way a man watches a snake he has grown fond of. Its pages recorded every contract, every advance against wages, every debt carried forward season upon season until the debt became the diver and the diver became the debt. And in its early pages — pages older than my employment, written in the cramped hand of a clerk named Aldous who died before I ever held a pen in that room — it recorded the true names. Not the harbor names the divers wore like borrowed coats. The true names. The villages. The islands some of them had been taken from as children. The prices paid for them, itemized with the same dry precision used for rope and lamp oil, because to the company a person had always been an entry, and entries were honest even when the hands that wrote them were not.

And beside those true names, in the margins, in initials only Brannock and I could decipher, the annotations. Which papers were forged. Which manumissions were genuine. Which freedoms existed only as ink, revocable by anyone holding the book.

Sefa's name was in that book. Her true name — Sefa Ilori, of a village on a coast she had been carried from at seven years old, too young to remember more than the color of the water. And beside it, in my own hand, disguised in the cipher I had taught myself from Brannock's example: *paper amended, 1893, C.V.*

I had altered her entry five years ago. Her mother had bought her freedom honestly, shell by shell, dive by dive, twenty years of the bottom of the sea traded for a paper that said Sefa belonged to herself — and Brannock, or one of his clerks before him, had quietly marked the payment short, a shortfall small enough to be arithmetic and large enough to be chains. I found it during an audit when I was nineteen, and I understood it completely, and I changed the figure. One numeral. The smallest forgery in the history of that book, and the only one I had ever committed for love instead of profit. If the master ledger fell into the wrong hands, that single numeral was enough to prove the paper false, to prove her free status a fiction, to put her back under the company's hand — or the army's.

The ledger lay open on the table in the lamplight, and I looked at it the way you look at a wound you have just discovered on your own body, with a strange calm that you know will not hold.

"Coral." Brannock's voice from the doorway, softer than usual, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Good. You're here."

"Director." I did not ask what was happening. Asking questions was how you revealed what you didn't know, and invisibility, I had learned, was mostly a matter of never revealing anything.

"They're requisitioning the port," Brannock said, as if reporting weather. He came into the records room and stood looking down at the open book, and his ringed hand rested on the page edge, and I saw that his fingers were trembling very slightly, which told me more than his voice did. "Naval administration, effective this morning. The fleet's being mobilized for the eastern campaign and they need our harbor, our stores, our boats." A pause. "Our men."

"Our divers," I said.

"Our assets," Brannock corrected, gently, the way he corrected everything, as though the whole world were merely careless with its vocabulary. "They'll want the rolls. Crew lists. Indentures." His eyes came up to mine, and for the first time in ten years I felt seen in that building — truly seen, catalogued, assessed — and I understood that whatever was coming, Brannock had already decided what role I would play in it, and had not thought it necessary to inform me. "When the officers arrive, you'll produce whatever they ask for. Promptly. Courteously. No flourishes."

"Yes, sir," I said, which was the answer I had given him ten thousand times, and the first time I gave it while meaning none of it.

* * *

They came ashore at half past seven, boots ringing on the quay planks in a cadence Saltmere had never heard before, and by eight the counting house was occupied.

I use the word precisely. They did not visit, inspect, or call. They occupied it, the way water occupies a hull — finding every space, filling it, making it theirs. A captain with a red-trimmed collar walked the length of the main room once, slowly, his gloved finger trailing along the desk edges and coming away clean, and nodded as if the building had passed some examination. Soldiers took positions at the doors. A lieutenant with a field desk and two clerks set up in the middle of the floor among our own furniture as though our furniture had always been theirs, and began unpacking boxes stamped with numbers instead of names.

Brannock met them at the door with his best coat and his worst smile, bowing from the neck, and I stood at my corner desk with my pen uncapped over a page of figures I was not reading, and I was invisible again, and for the first time in my life I hated it.

"And the company's records?" the captain asked. He had a voice like a file drawn slowly across iron. "The colonial office requires a full transfer of commercial documentation. Master registers, crew rolls, contracts."

"Of course, Captain. Of course." Brannock turned, and I watched him cross the floor to the record shelf, and I watched his hand — steady now, the trembling gone, because Brannock's body always recovered its composure faster than his face did — and I waited for the false spines. For the hesitation. For some signal, some flicker, some arrangement between us, because surely — surely — he would not simply hand them everything.

He lifted down the master ledger and carried it to the captain's field desk and laid it there with both hands, like a man presenting a gift.

"There is the master register," Brannock said. "Complete. Current to last quarter's closing. My chief clerk will assist your officers with the cipher notation — the company uses certain internal abbreviations."

Every hair on my arms rose. *My chief clerk.* Me. He was selling me along with the book, packaging the interpreter with the text, and doing it so smoothly that it sounded like deference.

The captain opened the ledger at random. His glove rested on the page, and I watched his eyes move down the columns — names, debts, origins — and I watched the moment when the reading slowed, when the officer's attention snagged on something in the margin, and I knew without seeing which entry it was. There are perhaps forty annotations in that book concerning men and women whose freedom exists only as ink, and any one of them, read correctly, was worth a conscription notice or worse.

"Comprehensive," the captain said, and there was something in his tone that had not been there before, the note of a man who has opened a drawer expecting linens and found money. He looked up, and his gaze swept the room, and it passed over me the way a lighthouse beam passes over a rock — touching, dismissing, returning. "Your chief clerk. Which one?"

Brannock's hand described a small arc across the room, and landed on me.

"That gentleman," Brannock said. "Coral Voss. Ten years in the company's service. There is nothing in these books he cannot explain."

The captain looked at me for three seconds. I counted them, because counting was the only thing my hands could do that would not betray me. In those three seconds I understood the shape of the morning completely, the way you understand a storm surge all at once, too late to sandbag anything: the army had the book. Every true name. Every forged paper. Every margin note in Brannock's cipher and mine. Sefa's entry, with my numeral sitting in it like a splinter, proof waiting to be read. And I had been introduced to the occupying power as the man who could make all of it legible.

"Mr. Voss," the captain said. "You'll report to Lieutenant Anselm within the hour. The register will be copied in full for military archive. Your director informs me you're thorough."

"He does," I heard myself say, and my voice came out level, clerk-flat, a voice from a man who had spent ten years practicing exactly this. "I am."

* * *

They worked through the morning. The lieutenant's clerks set up at the long table with the master ledger between them, and I stood at their shoulders translating cipher — *orig., orig. cert., am.*, the small honest-seeming abbreviations under which whole lives were filed — and answering questions in the tone of a man discussing freight rates, while inside me a single sentence repeated itself with the patience of surf:

*They have her name. They have her name. They have her name.*

Through the counting house windows I could see the harbor, and the fleet putting out despite the soldiers on the quay — the divers going down anyway, because pearls did not wait for politics and debts did not either, and among the boats I found hers by the way she stepped the mast, a motion I could have identified blind, in fog, in my sleep. She dove, and the sea took her, and I stood in a room full of soldiers guarding a book that could take her back forever, and I translated another abbreviation, and another, and my pen did not shake, because I had built it not to shake, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling: *ten years of silence, and this is what it bought. A front-row seat.*

At noon a runner brought the captain a dispatch, and after reading it he called Brannock aside, and I caught only fragments — *census, muster, able-bodied, by week's end* — and Brannock's face performed regret beautifully, and I went back to my desk and opened the day-book and wrote figures that meant nothing.

By evening the soldiers' camp on the headland had cookfires burning, and the gulls had finally come back down to argue over the offal, and the town moved carefully around the blue uniforms as around broken glass. And I sat late in the records room with the door locked and the master ledger's empty case open in front of me, and I did not yet know what I was going to do.

I only knew that dread had gotten ahead of understanding, the way the guns had gotten ahead of dawn — and that somewhere between the two, before the army finished copying its archive, before the census reached the fleet, there was a window, narrow as a breath, in which a man fluent in silence might learn to speak.
