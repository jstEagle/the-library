## 3. What the Ledger Knows

The counting house at night was a different country.

By day it belonged to the company — to Brannock's boots on the floorboards, to the scratch of other men's pens, to the smell of tar and cheap tobacco and the brine that came in on every clerk's coat from the quay. By day Coral Voss moved through it the way a fish moves through current, unremarked, part of the furniture of commerce. But after the lamps were trimmed and the soldiers' footsteps in the square faded to nothing, the room became his alone, and he could admit what he would never have said aloud: that he loved it. That he had loved it for ten years, this room of paper and quiet, because paper did not drown, and quiet did not ask questions.

He lit the lamp low, turning the wick down until the flame was no taller than a thumbnail. Light showed through windows; light got men hanged, or at least got them asked questions by soldiers who had nothing to do at two in the morning but be suspicious. The requisition had taken the master ledger into the garrison's keeping eleven days ago, but Coral had done what invisible men always do — he had made a copy of the index before anyone thought to stop him, and an index, to a man like Coral, was a skeleton key.

Tonight he meant to use it.

He spread his own working memoranda across the desk — not the master ledger itself, that lived now under Hale's eye in the occupied east wing, but the fair copies and duplicate entries that clerks like Coral were required to keep against loss by fire or flood. The army had not requisitioned those. The army did not know they existed. That was the whole architecture of Saltmere's bookkeeping: the company kept one truth for itself and another for the world, and Coral was the mortar between the bricks.

He began, as he always began, at the beginning. Not the first page — the first page was a fiction, a list of shareholders and pious sentiments about lawful trade — but the first *true* page, folio three, where the debt entries started. He read the way other men prayed, lips moving, finger tracing, and as he read he built the ledger again in his mind, beam by beam, until he could walk through its rooms with his eyes shut.

Folio three through nineteen: the original fleet indentures, written in old Maren's crabbed hand, each diver entered with a purchase price, a term of years, and a rate of interest calculated so that the debt grew faster than any human being could pay it. Coral knew the arithmetic the way a priest knows liturgy. A diver entered at forty pounds would owe sixty-eight by year's end, ninety by the second, and so on forever, the numbers swelling like a drowned body, bloating past any hope of retrieval. That was not a flaw in the bookkeeping. It *was* the bookkeeping.

Folio twenty through fifty-one: the renewals. Every five years, a diver's indenture was rolled over, the accumulated debt restated, the term extended, the original date struck through and rewritten so far back that some entries claimed debts contracted before the divers in question had been born. Sefa's mother appeared there — not under her true name, which had been Vashti, but under the trading name the company had given her, *Pearl*, as if the ocean herself had sold her daughter into the fleet. Coral remembered entering the renewal himself, eight years ago, his hand steady, Maren watching over his shoulder, and how afterward he had gone to the seawall and been sick into the harbor.

*You are fluent in silence,* his father had told him once, in the last winter of his life, meaning it as praise. *That is why you will outlive us all.*

His father had been wrong about the outliving. He had been right about everything else.

Coral turned pages. Folio fifty-two through seventy: transfers between vessels, deaths, desertions marked in red — *desertion* being the company's word for a man who swam away, though no diver ever swam away, because a diver's family stood surety for his debt and the company collected in flesh when coin failed. Folio seventy-one onward: the births. Children entered within days of drawing breath, their futures mortgaged before their eyes opened, assigned their mothers' debts divided by surviving kin. Coral had copied hundreds of these. He could recite them. Some nights, in the years since, he had tried not to, and failed, and lain awake listening to the harbor breathing against the pilings while names marched through his skull like a press-gang.

It was near the third hour when he reached the section he had been circling all night the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Folio eighty-eight. The Sefa entries.

There were four of them. He had known there were four; he had written three of them himself. But knowing a thing and seeing it laid open under lamplight were different countries too, and he sat very still for a long moment before he let himself read.

The first entry, dated twelve years back, recorded the purchase of a girl called Sefa from a broker out of the northern islands — price thirty-five pounds, term fifteen years, entered under the trading name *Coral*, which the company thought witty, naming the child for the reef she would work until the reef worked her. Coral had never asked whether the joke was Maren's or Brannock's. Some questions you did not ask even in your own head, though this one had lived in his head for a decade, laying its eggs.

The second entry, four years on: renewal, debt restated, term extended. His own handwriting, young still, careful.

The third entry was the one that mattered, and he made himself look at it the way a surgeon looks, without flinching, because flinching was for men whose hands could afford to shake. Dated six years back, it recorded a payment received — a pearl sale credited against Sefa's account, a credit large enough, per the entry, to reduce her balance below the redemption threshold, such that per Article Nine of the indenture standard, the remainder of her term was commuted to service-owed rather than person-held. In plain language: she was free. On paper she had been free for six years. Her manumission paper — the document she kept sewn into the lining of her diving jacket, the document she touched before every descent like a talisman — rested on this entry the way a house rests on its footings.

And the entry was false.

Not entirely false. That was the horror of it, the thing Coral had understood only slowly, over years, and understood completely tonight, sitting alone with the lamp turned down to a coal. The pearl sale was real. Sefa had brought up a shell that season worth real money — the Kestrel's luck, the fleet had called it, the kind of find that came once in a boat's lifetime. The credit was real. What was false was the figure. The actual sale had covered perhaps half her remaining debt. Half was not enough. Half left her bound for nine more years, diving until her lungs gave out or the bends took her, as they had taken her mother.

So Coral had altered the number. Sixteen years old, three months into his tenure as record-keeper, trusted alone with the book for exactly one afternoon while Maren attended Brannock ashore, and he had changed *eight pounds, four shillings* to *eighteen pounds, four shillings*, and then, because a forged entry that stood alone was a forged entry that got found, he had altered three other figures in the same folio to make the discrepancy look like clerical drift — the small honest errors that accumulate in any long ledger — and he had done it all in twenty minutes with hands that had not shaken until afterward, and then had shaken for a week.

He had told himself it was arithmetic. A girl freed early against a lucky pearl; the company lost a little margin; no one bled. He had told himself she need never know, because knowing would make her complicit in a fraud that could see her entry voided entirely and her re-entered at the brokers' price, and worse, it could see *him* flogged and worse, and worse than that, it could bring Hale's kind of attention down on the whole fleet. He had told himself a great many things. Tonight, with the lamp low and the folio open and his own handwriting staring back at him across six years like the face of a stranger he recognized, he finally heard the truth underneath all of them.

His kindness had left a trail.

Every forgery did. That was the first law of ledgers, older than ink: a false figure does not sit still. It leans on the figures around it. It propagates. The eighteen pounds he had invented rippled forward through every subsequent statement of her account — the annual balances, the tax assessments, the garrison census lists that the army had begun copying eleven days ago. Somewhere in the east wing, under a soldier's hand or Hale's own careful pen, someone was transcribing Sefa's account and finding in it a sum that had never truly been paid, a redemption that had never truly occurred, a free woman standing on paper that a boy had forged out of love and terror in a single stolen afternoon.

And when they checked it — when the inevitable audit reached folio eighty-eight, and it would, because Hale struck Coral as a man who audited his own dreams — they would not find a clerical error. Clerical errors drifted. This one pointed. It pointed like an arrow at the only clerk with access, the only clerk whose initials appeared discreetly in the corner of every folio he touched, *C.V.*, the signature of his own invisibility, which he had suddenly understood was also a signature of his guilt.

Coral closed the ledger and sat with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the grain of the wood under his palms, listening to the tide work at the harbor piles. Guilt was a thing he knew well; he kept it the way he kept everything, filed and ordered. But this was different. This was not the guilt of silence, which was diffuse, a fog. This was specific. This had a folio number.

*If they void the entry,* he thought, *she goes back on the books. Back to the brokers' valuation. Back to fifteen more years.* And the paper trail that proved her freedom fraudulent would lead, folio by folio, initial by initial, directly to him — and through him, if anyone pressed hard enough, to the truth about the other things he knew. About the drowning reports. About his father's entry, folio sixty-three, the one he had never dared reread.

The lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick and did not move.

*Then I must be the one who checks it first,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the cold clarity of a knot coming tight. *I must know every page Hale has seen, every flag he has set, before he comes asking. I must hold the whole book in my head so that whatever he knows, I know it sooner.*

He opened the ledger again and began, methodically, to memorize.

* * *

Sefa woke before the gulls, as she always did, in the room above her aunt's drying-shed where the air tasted of salt and split fish and the particular smoke of the seaweed fire her aunt kept burning against rheumatism.

She dressed in the dark by feel — jacket, and her hand went first, as it always went first, to the lining where the paper lived, the folded manumission with its official stamp and its words that had made her free when she was nineteen. She touched it the way other women touched saints' medals. Then she dressed.

The fleet was not putting out. That was the fourth day running, and the reason sat in the square in grey uniforms copying names out of company books, and nobody had said plainly why, because nobody said anything plainly anymore; the whole town had gone quiet the way water goes quiet over something large moving beneath it.

But a diver could not stop diving. Four days idle and her chest began to ache for the pressure of deep water the way other chests ached for air. So she went down alone at first light to the rocks below the fort, where the channel ran fast and cold and nobody fished, and she dove without gear beyond knife and basket, free-diving, the old way, her mother's way.

The cold closed over her like a door. Below, the light went green and then blue and then to that darkness where the pearl beds lay, and she hung there in the middle of the water column, weightless, neither rising nor sinking, and she thought — as she had been thinking for eleven days — about names.

The soldiers wanted names. She had watched them from the quay, pen after pen, taking down the fleet roster from the company books, and the books used trading names, everyone knew that — Pearl, Coral, Shell, the pretty mineral names the company gave its property — and the soldiers wrote them down without asking, and that was wrong in a way she could not yet articulate but felt in her ribs like pressure at depth. Her mother had been Vashti. Nobody in Saltmere's books would ever know that. When her mother died, the ledger had recorded the death of *Pearl*, and that erasure still angered Sefa the way a splinter buried too deep to dig angers the flesh, a small permanent wrongness.

Her own book-name was Coral. She had hated it as a child — named for the thing she was made to harvest, the way you'd name a ox *Plough*. And then, years later, she had met the clerk who shared it, this pale quiet boy who kept the books, and the name had become something else entirely, a private joke between them, a thread. *We match,* he had said once, mortified, having discovered the coincidence aloud by accident, and she had laughed until she had to sit down on a crate, because his face had gone the color of the paper he worked with.

She surfaced, breathed, dove again. Deeper. The pearl beds passed below her in the blue gloom, and she thought about Coral Voss and the strange look on his face yesterday when she'd asked him, straight out, what the soldiers were writing down. He had lied to her. She had known him ten years and he had never once lied to her — not because he was incapable of it but because he seemed constitutionally unable; lies appeared to cause him physical discomfort, like a man asked to breathe underwater. And yesterday he had said, *They're just verifying tonnage records, cargo manifests, nothing that touches the fleet* — and his voice had done the thing voices do when they carry a lie, gone smooth and over-careful, like ice over moving water.

She had let it pass. But she was a diver; she knew about things beneath surfaces, about the drag of hidden mass, about how the dangerous currents never showed at the top of the water.

Something was under this. Something big enough to bend a man like Coral, who bent for no one, out of his shape.

She broke the surface at last, gasping, the dawn fully come up now, red along the harbor mouth — *red sky at morning* — and she looked across the water at the counting house, at the thin line of smoke from its chimney standing straight in the windless cold, and she thought: he was up all night again. Fourth night. Whatever he was doing in there with his lamps and his paper, he was doing it against the clock, and he was doing it alone, and Coral Voss alone was the most unnatural sight Saltmere could offer, because if there was one law of the universe surer than tides it was that Coral Voss did everything alone.

*Well,* she thought, wringing out her hair on the rocks, watching the red dawn burn, *we'll see about that.*

* * *

On the fifth night, Coral reached the end of the book.

He had eaten nothing he could remember; the lamp had consumed oil he could not spare; his eyes ached and his right hand had stiffened into the shape of its own pen-hold. But he had done it. Folio by folio, entry by entry, he had rebuilt the master ledger inside his skull, and it sat there now entire, four hundred and eleven pages of bought and borrowed lives, and he tested it the way he tested all knowledge, silently, in order, and found no gaps.

Only then did he allow himself the last thing. He turned back to folio sixty-three and read his father's entry for the first time in six years.

*Drowned, 12th October, off the Kestrel's moorings. Body recovered. Wages owed to estate: nil.*

Body recovered. Except Coral had been fourteen, and had stood on the quay, and there had been no body, only the report of one, delivered to Brannock's office in Sergeant Ives's hand, and a sealed coffin, and his mother's face. He had been a child. He had swallowed it whole, the way children swallow. He was not a child now, and tonight, with the whole ledger living in his head and the trail of his own forgery winding through it toward discovery, the entry looked different. Everything looked different. The book he had served for ten years had resolved, under lamplight, into a single continuous machine for making true things false and false things binding, and his father's coffin was somewhere in its gears.

Coral put out the lamp. In the dark he sat still, listening to the tide, and made himself say the thing plainly, because plain speech was the one luxury his silence had never permitted him:

*They will audit her entry. They will trace my initials. I have days.*

And beneath that, quieter, in the voice of the boy he had been at sixteen with a wet pen in his hand: *And when they come — I will need help. From people I have spent ten years learning never to ask.*

Somewhere across the harbor, in the garrison's east wing, a light burned in Corporal-Adjutant Hale's window. Coral could see it from the counting house, steady through the pre-dawn grey, patient as a hook.

He began, carefully, tearing along the ruled lines, to destroy his notes.
