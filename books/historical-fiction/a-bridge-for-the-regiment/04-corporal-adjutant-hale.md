## 4. Corporal-Adjutant Hale

The counting house smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath it, the sea — that briny sweetness that clung to everything in Saltmere the way sin clings to a man who has stopped confessing. Corporal-Adjutant Elias Hale stood at the window of the director's office with his hands clasped behind his back and watched the harbor wake up, and he permitted himself, for exactly one minute, to feel satisfied.

Below him the requisition was proceeding in good order. Soldiers moved crates along the quay with the unhurried competence of men who had done this in six ports before this one. The gunboat *Vigilant* rode at anchor where its guns could reach both the fleet and the town, which was not a threat so much as a statement of arithmetic. And the pearl divers were going out on the morning tide as though nothing had changed, their boats sliding over water the color of hammered tin, because for them nothing had changed. That was the thing about people like these — they had been governed by paper long before the army arrived. The army had simply brought better paper.

Hale turned from the window and surveyed the office again. Director Brannock's chair was too large for the room, upholstered in cracked maroon leather, and it sat behind a desk of black island wood into which some previous incumbent had carved his initials and then tried, badly, to sand them out. On the desk lay the master ledger.

Hale had read it twice through since dawn. It was, he thought, a beautiful object — which was precisely what troubled him. Beautiful things in colonial ports were usually lies wearing good clothes. The binding was calfskin, the ruling was even, the hand that filled it was small and immaculate and utterly without personality, ten years of entries in an ink so consistent it might have been printed. Debts, wages, advances, interest, renewals. Every diver in the fleet accounted for in a script that never trembled.

He had spent four hours last night cross-referencing it against the garrison census, and he had found what he always found: almost nothing. Almost. It was the *almost* that had brought him here before the morning bell, and it was the *almost* that would, if God and the War Office were just, carry him out of this salt-stained purgatory and onto a staff appointment in the capital by spring.

He needed one clean prosecution. Not a massacre dressed as justice — those were cheap, and the new administration had grown squeamish about them. A clean one. Documents, testimony, a conviction that would survive review by lawyers who had never seen the sea. The trading company that ran Saltmere was famous enough to be worth breaking. Break it properly, with paper, and every ambitious man in the department would know Hale's name.

A knock. Two knocks, rather — soft, precise, apologetic even in their rhythm.

"Enter."

* * *

The clerk who came through the door was not what Hale had expected, though Hale could not have said what he had expected. He had imagined the company's record-keeper as Brannock's creature: fat, or sly, or both, a man with rings and a wet handshake. Instead a slight young man stood at attention just inside the doorway, hat in hand, with the stillness of furniture. Twenty-eight, perhaps thirty. Pale in the way of men who worked indoors in a town of brown-skinned sailors. Sandy hair going thin early at the temples. Hands inked faintly blue at the nails, the mark of the trade worn into the skin like a tattoo.

"Coral Voss," the man said. His voice was quiet and level. "You sent for me, sir."

"I did." Hale gestured to the chair across the desk and watched how the man took it. Voss sat down the way a man sits when he has practiced sitting in front of superiors for a decade — spine straight without stiffness, hands folded on his knee, eyes lowered to a point on the desk that was respectful but not servile. Everything correct. Everything calibrated.

"You've kept the company's books for how long?"

"Ten years, sir. Eleven in the autumn."

"And before that?"

"My father dove for the company. I grew up around the ledgers." A pause of exactly the right length. "I was good with figures."

Hale let the silence stretch. This was the first instrument in his kit and he played it now, letting it hum between them while he studied the man's face. Most people filled silences. Most people, given five seconds of quiet across a desk from a uniformed officer, began talking — about the weather, their loyalty, their innocence — and told you more in thirty unguarded words than in an hour of questions.

Coral Voss looked at the blotter and waited, patient as a stone in a stream.

"You'll understand," Hale said at last, "that the army's interest in your company's records is administrative. We require accurate rolls of the laboring population for conscription assessment and port security. Your director has been cooperative."

"Yes, sir."

"Which is why I'm puzzled." Hale opened the ledger to a ribboned page and turned it to face the clerk. "Your book is beautiful, Mr. Voss. Genuinely. I've handled regimental accounts kept by commissioned officers that weren't half so tidy. And yet." He laid beside it three sheets of the garrison census, copied out in his own hand overnight. "And yet there are discrepancies."

He watched the man's eyes move across the pages. They moved quickly — too quickly for ordinary reading, or perhaps exactly quickly enough for a man who had written every line himself and needed only to confirm what he already knew. Something passed over Voss's face and was gone. Not fear. Hale catalogued it and was annoyed to find he couldn't name it. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man finding a familiar room rearranged.

"Discrepancies, sir?"

"Forty-one names in your ledger do not appear in the colonial census of Saltmere. Sixty-three names in the census have no corresponding entry in your wage rolls. Now, fishing folk drift, I know that. Men come and go with the season. But forty-one is not drift. Forty-one is a pattern." He leaned back. "Explain it to me."

What happened next was small and it was perfect and it made the back of Hale's neck prickle like weather coming. Voss reached forward — asking permission with a tilt of the head first, another calibrated gesture — and turned the ledger toward himself, and began, in a low even voice, to explain.

"The census counts residents of the town proper, sir. The diving fleet berths on the spit and the hulks. Half the fleet sleeps aboard during the working season — your soldiers counted the town houses, not the anchorage. As for the forty-one—" he turned pages with a scholar's economy, "—these are seasonal contracts. Men signed to the company who winter elsewhere. Here—" a finger on an entry, "—Tomas Reyes, contracted March through November, winters with family upcoast. He'd appear in no census taken between December and February. Your census was taken in January, sir."

It was in January. Hale knew it was in January. The explanation was plausible, detailed, delivered without hesitation, and completely unverifiable before spring.

"And these," Hale said, tapping the sixty-three.

"Company pensioners and widows, mostly. Families whose breadwinner drowned. The company keeps them on the rolls for charity disbursement — half-rations, schooling allowance. Brannock calls it goodwill. I call it what it is, which is cheaper than scandal." For the first time something moved behind the man's eyes, something dry and bitter, gone as fast as it came. "But they earn no wages, so they're absent from the wage rolls."

Hale wrote nothing down. He wanted his hands free; writing made men perform. "You keep charity accounts separately?"

"In the same volume. Interest columns and relief columns share a page. It saves paper." A beat. "Paper is expensive here."

Everything the man said was reasonable. That was the trouble. In Hale's experience, the truth was usually awkward — it stumbled, it overshot, it volunteered too much or too little. This testimony flowed like water finding a channel it had cut long ago. Either Coral Voss was telling the truth, or he had rehearsed these answers against questions Hale hadn't yet thought to ask, which would mean he had anticipated the interview itself, which would mean—

Which would mean he understood exactly what the army wanted from these books, and had understood it before the gunboats cleared the headland.

"Tell me about the indentures," Hale said.

The change was nearly invisible. Nearly. The clerk's folded hands tightened by a degree — Hale saw the knuckles whiten and ease again inside a single breath — and his gaze came up from the desk and met Hale's for the first time.

Hale had looked into a great many eyes across interrogation tables, and he flattered himself that he could read them the way other men read tide tables. These eyes gave him almost nothing. They were grey-green, unremarkable, steady. But behind the steadiness, deep in, there was a light he recognized and distrusted: the light of a man doing arithmetic. Not the arithmetic of guilt. The arithmetic of assessment. *How much does he know? How much does he want? What will he trade?*

"The indentures are standard instruments, sir," Voss said. "Advance against wages, term of service, renewal clauses. All recorded."

"All legal?"

"All recorded, sir."

Hale smiled. It was a good smile; he'd practiced it in mirrors until it conveyed reasonableness without warmth. "Mr. Voss, I was not born on a ship. 'All recorded' is not 'all legal.' I have broken companies in three territories, and in every one of them the books were immaculate and the truth was in the margins. Somewhere in this beautiful book there is a margin where a man's true name became a debt he couldn't pay, or a free diver became property on paper. My work is finding that margin. Your work—" he tapped the ledger, "—is apparently making sure I can't. So let me ask plainly, man to keeper of records: if I sit in this chair every day for a month, what will I find?"

Silence. Outside, a gull screamed once and quit. Down on the quay, faintly, the sound of crates and boots.

Then Coral Voss did the one thing Hale had not predicted in any of his scenarios. He reached across the desk, took the ledger, and turned to a page near the middle — and held it out.

"This one, sir," he said quietly. "If you're looking for a margin, start here."

Hale took the book. The entry was ordinary: a diver named Ansel Koa, advance of eleven pounds, term renewed twice, balance outstanding. Ordinary, except that the second renewal was dated in a hand fractionally different from the rest — the same hand, but tired, the downstrokes heavier. Dated, if Hale's eye for such things served, in the same week the original contract should have expired.

"Why this one?"

"Because the renewal is irregular, sir, and I won't pretend otherwise. Koa was ill that season. The company extended his term without the statutory notice. It happens more than the directors admit. If you want a prosecution that survives review, that entry will bear weight." Voss paused, and added, with terrible mildness: "I assumed that was the sort of thing you wanted."

*He's giving me one.* Hale felt the pleasure of the hunt curdle into caution. A man who surrendered a discrepancy this smoothly was either a fool — and this man was not a fool — or he was feeding the dog a bone to keep it from the meat. Or. Or he was a man with his own reasons for wanting certain pages examined, and Hale had met that animal before, in himself, in every clerk and adjutant and junior officer who had ever decided that the system above him was rotten and that he alone knew where the rot began.

Dangerous men, those. Useful men, sometimes. Never innocent ones.

"That will be all for today, Mr. Voss."

The clerk rose, replaced his hat, and inclined his head. At the door he stopped — half a heartbeat, no more — and said, without turning fully around:

"The fleet goes out with the morning tide tomorrow, sir. Forty-one boats. If your census needs correcting, the anchorage is best counted at dusk, when they're all ashore." Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor, soft and even as a pulse.

* * *

Hale stood at the window a long time after, watching the harbor, and made himself honest the way he made himself honest every night of his life, in the privacy of his own skull where ambition could not overhear.

He did not trust Coral Voss. He did not distrust him either, which was worse. The man was a locked drawer walking around in a plain coat, and every answer he'd given had been true the way a chart is true — accurate, and drawn by someone who very much wants you to sail a particular course. Giving up Koa's entry. Suggesting the dusk count. Offering help, unprompted, to the man sent to break his employers. Either Voss was angling to save his own position by sacrificing the company's smallest sins, or he was steering the army toward something specific and away from something else, and Hale intended to find out which.

Because here was the thought he had been circling all morning, the one he set down now like a card he didn't want the table to see: nobody keeps ten years of perfect books out of love of tidiness. Perfection like that was a wall, and walls are built by people with something to hide — or something to protect. And a man who protected other men's secrets for a decade, silently, invisibly, asking nothing —

That man was not a servant of the company. That man was a vault. And somewhere in Saltmere, Hale was quite sure, someone held the key.

He returned to the desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him. At the top he wrote, in his small hard hand: *V., C. — cooperative. Too cooperative. Watch.* Below it he wrote the name *Ansel Koa*, and underlined it twice.

Then, because thoroughness was the whole of his religion, he turned back to the ledger and began reading it a third time — slowly this time, line by line, hunting not for errors but for the shape of the hand itself, for the places where the ink pressed harder, where the letters leaned, where a man's discipline had frayed for a single stroke. Somewhere in those ten thousand entries, the clerk's heart had beaten visibly at least once. Hale would find the beat.

Outside, the afternoon light went copper on the water, and far out past the headland the diving fleet came home over the darkening swell, boat after boat after boat, forty-one lanterns kindling one by one on the tide like a slow procession of small fires — and among them, Hale noted without knowing why he noted it, one boat that lingered longest on the water, as if its crew were in no hurry at all to be counted.
