## 5. Three Checkpoints

Coral Voss had never once, in ten years of keeping the company's books, drawn a map.

He drew them now. Not on company paper — he was done with company paper forever, though no one yet knew it but him — but on the backs of eleven old dive-rotation schedules, paper so water-stained and creased that no clerk would ever want it back. He worked at his own table in his own room above the cooperage, after midnight, with a single candle shielded behind a flour tin so that its light fell only on the page and nowhere else. The tin threw a hard shadow up the wall, a dark rectangle like a door standing closed, and Coral found himself glancing at it from time to time as though someone might open it.

No one would. That was the first thing he made himself understand, sitting there with his pen dry until he needed it: nobody watched Coral Voss. Ten years of invisibility had been his affliction, his quiet grief; now, tonight, for the first time, he held it in his hands and turned it over and saw what it actually was.

It was a tool.

The fear had come the morning the gunboats did, whole and animal, a cold weight under his ribs. But fear was a solvent; it dissolved everything into urgency. What he needed was structure. And structure was the one language he spoke better than anyone alive in Saltmere.

So instead of asking *how do I save them*, which was a question with no edges, he asked the question he asked of every account that refused to balance: *what are the entries?*

There were three.

* * *

He began at the harbor gate because everything began there.

The gate was not really a gate — Saltmere had never had walls worth the name — but the army had made one anyway out of chain and tarred rope and two repurposed bollards, stretched across the throat of the quay where the fish-market road met the stones. A sentry box of new-cut timber, still bleeding sap at the joints, stood beside it. Two soldiers at all hours, four during the change of watch. They checked cargo going out, not people coming in; Saltmere's exports were pearls, dried fish, and guano, and the army wanted to know about every sack that left the rock.

Coral walked past it twice a day on legitimate business and had done so since he was fourteen years old, and so he had the particular knowledge that comes only from long habit: he knew it without knowing it, the way he knew the number of stairs to his room. Now he forced himself to know it deliberately.

He stood Tuesday noon at the mackerel stall across the road with a basket he had no use for, and he counted. Sentry rotation: forty minutes. The soldiers were garrison men, bored, cold in the wind that came up the harbor mouth; they searched sacks when an officer was visible and waved things through when one wasn't. They opened crates if the crate rattled wrong. They never, ever opened anything that looked heavy and dull and expected — and nothing was more expected at the harbor gate than diving weights.

Forty-one boats. Better than three hundred lead weights going down to the beds every working tide and coming back up. A bag of dive weights passing through that gate was less remarkable than a barrel of salt fish. It was furniture. It was part of the shape of the harbor itself.

Paper could be sewn between two flat leads inside a canvas bag, oiled cloth around it against the damp, and no hand would ever find it unless it went in looking specifically for sewn seams. And who would search a weight bag? Weights were worthless. Weights were the opposite of smuggling.

But — and here Coral made himself write it plainly, pen scratching in the candle-shadow — the gate solved nothing by itself. Whatever left the harbor gate still stood in Saltmere. It had passed from one part of the town into another part of the same town. The ledger's copies were useless while they remained inside the cordon, and Hale's cordon, Coral had already established, ran not along the shoreline but along a line drawn on a requisition map: everything seaward of the customs house was military ground. Everything landward, inland, over the headland road toward the government station at Point Ansell —

That was magistrate territory. Neutral ground. A naval magistrate with writ powers sat at Point Ansell because Point Ansell had been a naval station before Saltmere ever mattered, and Director Brannock's friends in the colonial office had never managed to have the arrangement dissolved, though Coral had processed three separate letters over the years attempting exactly that. Brannock feared that magistrate. Coral had filed the fear, in the way he filed everything, in neat chronological order.

Between the harbor gate and Point Ansell lay two obstacles. He wrote their names at the top of two fresh schedule-sheets and looked at them for a long time.

*The Customs House.*

*The Fort Bridge.*

* * *

On Wednesday he carried a genuine company errand to the customs house — a declaration of bonded stores, dull and true, the best kind of cover — and let his eyes do what clerks' eyes do, which is inventory.

The customs house had been a modest building before the requisition: one room, one surveyor, a stove that smoked. Now it was a checkpoint in fact if not in name. Soldiers had installed themselves in the annex, and every piece of cargo moving inland from the quay passed across the long surveyor's bench, where a customs man named Pell examined it with the weary thoroughness of a man paid either way and hoping only to be blamed for nothing.

Coral watched Pell work for an hour, pretending to wait on a signature.

Pell was honest. That was the trouble. A dishonest examiner could be bought, delayed, distracted; Coral had spent ten years among the company's small corruptions and knew their grammar fluently. But Pell opened things. Pell weighed things against manifests. Pell ran his thumb along the seams of flour sacks. He was slow and fair and utterly incurious, which meant he could not be bribed, but he could be *burdened*. There is a species of honesty that depends on the finite capacity of one tired man's attention, and Coral had spent a decade learning exactly how much attention any official could spare.

The customs house took everything inland and weighed it. So the answer was not to hide the pages from the weighing. The answer was to make the pages weigh what the manifest said they weighed.

He thought about it the way he thought about a false total: you didn't alter the suspicious figure, you built an honest-looking column around it. Cargo that crossed Pell's bench needed papers, and papers were prepared — Coral suddenly understood, with a sensation like a key turning — papers were prepared by clerks. By *the* clerk. Every manifest leaving Saltmere's counting house bore his own initials at the bottom, C.V., in his own cramped hand, and no soldier or surveyor in the world had reason to doubt them. His invisibility was not merely personal. It was institutional. It was printed on every document that moved through that room.

If the copied pages traveled inside a shipment whose paperwork was flawless, Pell would weigh it, match it, stamp it, and wave it through, because that was his job and his job was boring and the war — if it could be called a war — was somewhere else.

One risk: random inspection. He had seen Pell pull aside two crates in an hour, maybe. One in twenty. So whatever carried the pages must survive being opened. Nothing hidden in a false bottom; nothing sewn into linings that showed when garments were shaken out. The pages should live inside something so ordinary that opening it confirmed rather than contradicted its innocence. He did not decide yet what that thing was. He only wrote, beneath the word *Customs*, a single line:

*Must be true in every particular except one.*

Then he sat with the sentence a moment, hearing how it described not just a shipment but a life, and blew the candle out, and lay in the dark listening to the cooperage hammers gone quiet and the tide working at the pilings below his floor.

* * *

The fort bridge he saved for last, because the fort bridge frightened him, and he had learned by Thursday that the useful thing to do with fear was to measure it.

The bridge was old — older than the company, older than the colony's flag, some said, though Coral had found no record to confirm it and distrusted all sayings. It crossed the tidal narrows on stone piers at the neck of the headland, the only route off the rock that did not require a boat, and it joined Saltmere's island to the mainland road that ran, nine miles later, into Point Ansell. At its far end stood the fort itself, half-garrisoned now, and at the bridge's midpoint the army had posted the third checkpoint: a plank barrier, a brazier, two sentries and a corporal, checking everything that left Saltmere for the mainland.

Everything. This was the choke. The gate watched exports; the customs house watched cargo; the bridge watched *everything*, foot traffic and cart traffic, letters and luggage, going out. Hale was not a fool. Coral had understood that in the interview, in the terrible gentle way Hale had said *you would know, wouldn't you, Mr. Voss* — Hale had drawn his cordon the way a careful bookkeeper draws a closing line. Three stations, each covering the other's blindness. Anything crossing that bridge with evidence in it would need to pass a soldier standing in the wind with nothing to do but look.

And yet.

Coral went out on Friday under cover of delivering the fort's laundry accounts — the garrison contracted washing through the company, another entry bearing his initials — and he stood at the bridge's near end in the iron-colored afternoon and watched the crossing for two hours until his fingers went numb.

He saw what he needed to see, and then he saw something more.

Traffic over the bridge was thinning as the requisition strangled the town, but it was not stopping, and most of it was the army's own. Supply carts went out light and came back heavy. Dispatch riders crossed at a trot. And every evening, without fail, the garrison's own waste went over the bridge in a mule-drawn cart — kitchen slops, ash from the braziers, broken stores, the great unglamorous tide of refuse that two hundred men generate whether or not there is a war. The rubbish cart crossed at dusk. The sentries did not search it. Coral watched three evenings' worth of crossings before he admitted why the sight snagged at him: they did not search it because searching it was beneath them, and because it smelled, and because everyone knew what was in it.

Everyone knew. That was the vulnerability. Not ignorance — *certainty*. Hale's cordon was a set of books, and every set of books has an account that no one audits because everyone is sure of it.

He stood in the wind and felt the old fear come back, and this time, instead of dissolving, it settled, and cooled, and took shape. Three checkpoints. Gate, customs house, bridge. Each one passable. None of them passable by luck — luck was a currency he did not possess and could not counterfeit. Passable by preparation, by patience, by using precisely the qualities that had made him invisible: his thoroughness, his dullness, his ten years of being the man nobody imagined doing anything at all.

He did not pretend to himself that it would be enough. He wrote the risks down that night, all of them, in his small even hand, because writing a thing down was how he kept it from growing in the dark. *The gate: ninety seconds of exposure.* *The customs house: one in twenty.* *The bridge: certainty itself is the guard.* And beneath those, the largest risk of all, the one he circled and could not solve: *It cannot be one man carrying one thing. It must be several hands, none of whom knows the whole route.* Because Hale would interview people — Coral had watched him do it — and a man who knew everything could lose everything at once.

Which meant he could tell no one. Not all of it. Perhaps not any of it.

He thought of Sefa then, the way he always did at the edge of a decision, the way a compass needle finds its north no matter how you shake the case. If she knew, she would want in — Sefa did not stand behind doors, Sefa walked through them — and her wanting in would double the risk to her and halve his ability to protect her, and he could not, *could not*, weigh those two things honestly without discovering which one he truly served. So he would not weigh them. He would carry this alone until carrying it alone became impossible, and he would hate himself quietly, and that was simply the price.

Coral looked at his three sheets of dive-rotation paper, covered now in his cramped mapping hand, and did the last thing a careful man does with dangerous arithmetic: he fed them, one by one, into the stove, and watched the flame take the ink, and stirred the ash.

He kept nothing. It was all in his head, where he had kept ten years of other people's debts, and where it had never once leaked.

Three checkpoints. Gate, customs, bridge. He dressed in the dark, took his coat, and went down the stairs into the blue hour before dawn, because the counting house opened early now that the army owned it, and Corporal-Adjutant Hale liked his clerks punctual, and the first rule of moving unseen through a guarded place is to be exactly where you are expected to be, doing exactly what you are known to do.

He was halfway down the cooperage stairs when he heard the voices from the quay below — soldiers, more of them than usual, and the flat clank of a rifle bolt — and stopped, in the shadow of the doorway, to watch a file of men marching a fourth figure down the frozen strand toward the harbor gate.

The figure stumbled. The soldiers caught his arms. Even at that distance, in that light, Coral knew the rolling, seaman's walk of the stumble, and his stomach dropped through ten years of ledgers all at once, because it was Sergeant Ives they were dragging toward the gate — and Ives, head lolling drunkenly upward, was looking directly at Coral's window.
