## 6. Sefa's Question

The soldiers had been in the counting house for nine days, and Sefa Vaelu could feel it in the water before she understood it on land.

She felt everything in the water first. That was her mother's blood in her — her mother who had dived until the sea took her breath and kept it, who had taught Sefa that a body moving through saltwater reads the world more truly than any eye above it. The tide had been wrong all week. Not dangerous-wrong, not storm-wrong, but *held* — as if the harbor itself were waiting, the way a diver waits between breaths, lungs full, everything suspended.

On the ninth morning the fleet put out anyway, because debts did not care about tides, and Sefa went down into the green half-dark off Gull Rock with her knife and her basket and her heart beating slow the way it always did when she slipped beneath the surface. Forty feet down the light came apart into shifting coins. She worked the oyster beds by touch, fingers reading shell and stone, and she let the sea do what it always did: take her name away from her. Down there she was not Vaelu, debtor of the Saltmere Pearl & Trading Company, entry forty-one in a book she had never been allowed to see. Down there she was only hands and breath.

It was when she surfaced that she saw them — two soldiers on the quay beside the company shed, one holding the ledger open while the other wrote, their pens moving in the same rhythm, copying names.

She treaded water and watched them the way she watched sharks. From below, still, patient, counting.

* * *

They brought the catch in at noon, and the whole fleet passed the counting house door on the way to the curing tables, because Brannock had arranged it that way years ago — every diver walking past the ledger, past the clerk who recorded weights against debts, so that no one ever forgot which side of the book they stood on. For ten years that clerk had been Coral Voss, thin and quiet as a shadow under a door, his pen scratching, his eyes never rising above the paper. Ten years of noon after noon, Sefa walking past him smelling of brine and shell, and never once had he looked at her longer than the space of an entry.

Except that he had. She had known it the way you know a current under calm water — invisible but definite, pulling somewhere. He thought he was careful. Everyone in Saltmere thought Coral Voss was careful. But Sefa had spent ten years reading the sea, and the sea teaches you that stillness is its own kind of motion.

Today, when she passed the counting house door, the soldiers were gone and Coral was alone at the high desk, and he was not writing. He was sitting with both hands flat on the ledger as if holding it shut, staring at nothing, and when her bare feet crossed the threshold his head came up fast — too fast, like a man caught at something — and his eyes went past her shoulder to where the soldiers had sat.

She set her basket down on the trestle by the door. Deliberately. The oysters clacked together like teeth.

"Coral."

"Forty-one," he said, automatic, reaching for his pen. "Vaelu, Sefa. Twelve pounds four ounces by yesterday's tally, debt carried forward—"

"Why are they copying names?"

His pen stopped above the page. A bead of ink gathered at the nib, swelled, and hung there without falling, and Sefa thought: *there*, there is your whole answer hanging from the end of that pen.

"The army takes inventories," he said. "Requisition means inventory. They're counting what the company holds."

"Boats are inventory. Shell is inventory." She came further into the room. It smelled the way it always had — ink, candle tallow, dried kelp, the ghost of her father's tobacco from before — but underneath it now was something new, soldier-smell, boot leather and cold iron. "People are not inventory."

"No," Coral said quietly. "They're not."

"Then why is Corporal-Adjutant Hale's clerk writing down that my grandmother was born on a island the company burned the charts of? Mereani told me this morning. A soldier asked her where her people were from. Asked her mother-tongue. Wrote it down like she was a crate being marked for shipping."

The ink bead fell. It struck the ledger page and spread, a small black flower blooming on the column of numbers, and Coral moved to blot it and then didn't, as if he'd forgotten what blots meant, what clean pages meant, and that frightened Sefa more than anything the soldiers had done. Ten years she had watched this man keep accounts where not one drop of ink was ever out of place.

"It's the census," he said. "New territories get censused. Names, origins, next of kin. It goes into files. It stays in files."

"And then what?"

"Nothing." Too quickly. "Then nothing."

* * *

She made him work through the afternoon first. That was her advantage and she knew it: he could lie to her across a desk, but he could not lie to her across a curing table while they shucked oysters side by side, because his hands would tell her what his mouth denied. So she said nothing more, just took her place at the table where the company's divers worked the afternoon shift, and waited, and when the bell rang for the weighing Coral came out with his slate as he always did, and Brannock was absent — Brannock had been absent a great deal since the gunboats came, closeted with Hale behind closed doors — and so it was Coral himself who walked the line of divers with the scale, recording weights against debts.

He came to her last, as he always did. She had never asked him why. Today she watched his hands.

"Twelve pounds even," he said, and his voice was steady, and his fingers were not. They fumbled the counterweight. Coral Voss, who could total three columns in his head faster than any man on the coast, dropped a brass weight into the dirt.

"You've got soldier-shake," Sefa said.

"I have cold hands. It's December."

"It's not cold. I've been in the water all morning." She wiped her own hands on her apron and held one out, palm up, an offering and a challenge both. "Feel. Then tell me whose hands are colder."

He looked at her hand and did not take it, and the not-taking was worse than anything. Around them the other divers were drifting toward the cookshed and the evening fire, and the quay was emptying, and the light was going long and gold across the harbor where the gunboats rode at anchor like sleeping animals, dark-hulled, smokeless, patient.

"Sefa." He crouched to pick up the weight. When he straightened he was doing the thing he did — squaring the slate against his forearm, becoming the clerk, becoming furniture. "You should go home. It's getting late."

"I've been coming to this quay since I was six years old. I helped bury my father's bones in the water off Gull Rock. My mother's mother dove these beds when Brannock was still wetting his breeches." She stepped closer, close enough to smell the ink on him again, close enough to see the fine tremor in his jaw where he clenched it. "Do not tell me to go home. Tell me why the soldiers copy names."

"They just do."

"Wrong answer. Try again."

"Sefa—"

"Mereani," she said. "Her indenture has eleven years left on it. Eleven years, Coral, and she's fifty-three and her chest is bad from the diving. If a man came with a list and a boat and said *you, you're wanted*, what does her paper mean then? Whose law stands behind her paper now — Brannock's or theirs?"

Something moved through his face then, quick and deep, a fish crossing under ice, and she knew — she knew the way she knew a bed of shell under sand, by the shape of the disturbance — that she had struck near the truth. Not on it. Near it. There was something underneath his fear that was older than the soldiers, something that had to do with Mereani, with papers, with—

With her.

The thought arrived whole, the way large things arrive in deep water: suddenly, silently, displacing everything.

"What does my paper say?" she asked.

His breath stopped. Actually stopped; she saw his chest go still.

"My paper," she said slowly. "Entry forty-one. You read me the debt figure every month for ten years. You never once read me the rest of it. What does the rest of it say, Coral?"

"Debt carried forward," he recited, and his voice had gone flat and clerical and terrible. "Terms of indenture per original agreement. Standard terms."

"There's nothing standard about my terms and we both know it. My father signed papers in a language he couldn't read and died before the ink was dry, and somehow, some year — I never knew which year — my debt stopped growing. It shrank. It shrank like no debt in this fleet has ever shrunk. I thought—" Her voice caught and she hated it, hated the crack in it, and pushed on. "I thought the company was being decent. Can you imagine? I was grateful. To Brannock. I prayed for that man's soul."

"Sefa—"

"What did you do?"

The question came out smaller than she intended. Smaller and truer. Because it wasn't really a question about ledgers anymore, and she saw him understand that, saw it hit him in the place where he kept whatever it was he kept — ten years of lowered eyes, ten years of never looking longer than an entry allowed.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Liar," she said, softly, and the word landed between them like a stone in still water, and she watched the ripples go out through him. "Ten years, Coral. Ten years you have told me the weather and the tide and the weight of my catch and never once lied to me about a single one of them, because you never once spoke to me long enough to try. And now you're lying, and you're bad at it, and I want to know why."

* * *

He walked away from her then.

Not far. Three steps toward the counting house door, then a stop, one hand rising to press against the doorframe the way a man presses against a rail in rough seas. The evening was coming down violet and cold over the harbor. Somewhere beyond the point, the fleet's boys were singing the hauling song, ragged and young, and the sound carried flat across the water.

"If I tell you something," Coral said, without turning around, "it puts you in danger. Do you understand that? Not inconvenience. Danger. There are men on those ships who look at a list of names and see cargo. And there is a man in the counting house — Hale — who looks at a list of names and sees a way up. Out. Transfer. Promotion. He needs one prosecution, Sefa. One clean one. One name he can prove was never properly bound, so he can make an example, show the fleet what happens when paper fails."

Sefa felt the ground tilt under her, though her feet had not moved. "Paper failing."

"The census isn't a census." Now he turned, and his face in the dying light was stripped of everything clerical; what was left underneath was young and frightened and ten years tired. "It's a muster. They're building conscription lists out of the company books. Every diver with an irregular entry — every forged indenture, every debt inflated past legality, every name in this ledger that can't stand in daylight — those are the names going on Hale's list. The irregular ones. The breakable ones. The ones nobody will fight for, because fighting for them means admitting the paper was rotten from the start."

"Mereani."

"Mereani. Old Tam. The brothers from the wrecked lugger. Half the fleet, if he reads the entries the way I read them." His hand was still flat against the doorframe. "And yours. Yours most of all, maybe. Because your entry is—"

He stopped.

"Is what?" She crossed the distance between them, and this time when she held out her hand it was not a challenge; it was the only thing she had. "Coral. Is what?"

For a long moment he looked at her hand, and she watched him weigh it — she could see the scales working behind his eyes, the same scales he used on oysters and debts and ten years of silence — and then he took it. His fingers were freezing. Cold as deep water, cold as a drowned thing, and shaking.

"Your entry is wrong," he said, barely above the sound of the harbor. "It has been wrong since the winter you were seventeen. Someone altered it. The date, the sum, the witness mark — altered so cleanly that no auditor ever caught it, and I know because I audited it myself, over and over, hoping I'd imagined it. Your freedom paper rests on a forgery, Sefa. And I know it's a forgery because—" His voice broke and reassembled itself. "Because I'm the one who made it."

The world went very quiet and very bright, the way it does thirty feet down when the light comes down a shaft and everything hangs suspended.

"Six years ago," she said. Numbly. Doing arithmetic. "I was seventeen six years ago. That's when my debt started shrinking. That's when—"

"That's when old Tam's boat came back short and Brannock was going to add the loss to your account, roll it in the way he rolls everything in, and you'd have been bound another fifteen years and I—" He was holding her hand too tight now, or she was holding his; she honestly could not have said which. "I couldn't watch it happen again. Your father. I watched your father's account get rolled and rolled until the sea was kinder to him than the company was, and I said nothing, I was fourteen and I said nothing, and I swore—"

"You altered the master ledger." She was not asking. She was standing in the wreckage of six years of prayers for Brannock's soul, and her voice came out of it strange and level. "You altered it, and then you sat across from me every month for six years and read me a number that was a lie, and let me thank God for Brannock's mercy."

"Yes."

"You let me be *grateful* to him."

"Yes."

She pulled her hand free. Not violently — that was the terrible part, she would remember; she pulled it free gently, the way you take your hand back from a sleeping person, and the gentleness hurt him visibly, and she found she was glad it hurt him, and then ashamed of being glad, and all of it happened in the space of one wave breaking on the harbor stones.

"Why?" she said. "Not the forging. I'd have forged it too. Why the silence, Coral? Six years. You could have told me. You could have told me and no one else, and I'd have carried it with you—"

"Because a secret shared is a secret with a shape," he said, and now he sounded like himself again, like the ledger-keeper, precise even while drowning. "And Hale is here hunting for shapes. Because if you'd known, you'd have looked at Brannock differently, and Brannock watches everyone, and if Brannock had seen it in your face he'd have gone back through six years of entries and found mine, because mine is the only one that leads to a clerk's desk instead of a director's chair. Because telling you the truth was the one thing in the world that would have made everything true cost *more*." He laughed, short and awful. "That's the arithmetic I did. Every month. For six years. Silence was cheaper."

"Cheaper for whom?"

The question stopped him the way a harpoon stops a fish. She watched him reach for an answer — for *for everyone*, for *for you*, for any of the ten clever deflections he must have rehearsed against this day without ever admitting he was rehearsing — and watched him find none of them that would hold weight.

"For me," he said finally. "It was cheaper for me. Telling you meant risking you looking at me the way you're looking at me now. And I couldn't—" His jaw worked. "I couldn't survive you looking at me that way, so I made sure you never had reason to look at all. That's the truth. That's all of it. I was a coward dressed up as a protector, and I did the sums, and the sums always came out with me safe and you ignorant, and I called it love because I didn't have the courage to call it what it was."

The word hung between them on the emptying quay. *Love.* He had never said it. In ten years he had never come within a hundred fathoms of saying it, and now he had thrown it down like a man throwing down a weapon before a search, *here, take this too, since you're taking everything.*

Sefa looked out at the harbor. The gunboats had lit their lanterns, rows of small gold eyes in the dusk. Beyond them the fort bridge stretched black across the channel, its lamps burning, the boundary between the company's rock and the magistrate's mainland, the line every story in Saltmere eventually came to. The hauling song had ended. The fleet's fires glowed along the beach like a scattered constellation laid low.

"Hale has my name on a list," she said at last.

"He'll have it copied fair by Friday."

"And Mereani's. And Tam's."

"Yes."

"And you're going to sit at that desk with your cold hands and your steady pen and watch it happen." It was not a question, and it was not forgiveness, and it was not even accusation, quite. It was a measurement. She measured him the way she measured water before a dive — depth, current, what lived in it, whether she could hold her breath long enough.

And Coral Voss, who had lied badly for the first time in ten years and then stopped lying altogether, looked back at her with something new in his face — something that was not the clerk and not the coward and not the boy who had forged a freedom with a borrowed pen, but someone standing up out of all three.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

Behind him, through the counting house window, a lamp was moving — not Coral's lamp, not in its usual corner, but crossing the room where no lamp should be, and a shadow with it, tall and unhurried, wearing the silhouette of a peaked cap. Someone was in the counting house after hours, going through the shelves, and whoever it was had heard, or had not heard, and either way the night had just changed.

Sefa saw Coral see it. She saw him go still, all the way through, the stillness of a diver before the descent.

"Go home," he breathed. "Now. By the beach road. Don't run."

"Coral—"

"*Go.*"

She went — but at the corner of the shed she stopped, one hand on the wet timber, and looked back. He was already at the counting house door, squared shoulders, hand on the latch, a small figure setting itself deliberately in the path of whatever was moving inside with the lamp. Above the harbor the first star of evening came out over the fort bridge, thin and cold, and Sefa stood in the dark and realized she had just been given, after ten years, the only thing she had ever actually wanted from him:

A reason to be afraid for him.
