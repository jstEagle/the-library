## 7. Brannock's Bargain

The summons came the way weather comes to Saltmere — not as a surprise, but with a weight you feel in your knees before your mind consents to believe it.

A boy in company livery, one of the director's own runners, appeared at my desk in the counting house annex just past the midday bell. He didn't speak. He set a card on my ledger blotter, square and heavy, cream stock with a black border, and waited until I looked up. Then he turned and left, his footsteps quick on the boards, as though proximity to me might cost him something.

The card said only: *Seven o'clock. Come alone. — B.*

I sat for a long moment with my pen still wet above an entry I would later have to re-ink, because the nib had rested too long and bled a small black bloom into the paper. Ten years I had worked under Director Brannock's roof, and he had never once sent me a card. He spoke to me across the width of the counting room, or through his secretary, or not at all. I was furniture that could calculate. Furniture does not receive invitations.

But furniture is precisely what a man consults when he wants to know what the room has heard.

I finished the column. I blotted it. I locked my pens in the drawer the way I had done ten thousand times before, because whatever else the evening held, tomorrow there would be ledgers, and ledgers do not forgive a scribe who abandons his habits.

* * *

Brannock's house stood above the harbor on the last street before the cliff path, a tall grey house with white shutters and a garden that the salt wind had beaten into submission years ago. In summer the hydrangeas went blue as drowned things; in December they were brown stalks rattling in the wind like bundles of old pens. A lamp burned in the study window. It had burned there every night of my working life, so far as I knew — the steady yellow eye of the company, watching its fleet come home.

A maid let me in without a word. The hall smelled of beeswax and coal smoke and, beneath it, the sea, because in Saltmere everything smells of the sea eventually; it gets into plaster and timber and prayer books alike.

He was standing at the window when I entered the study, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor where the gunboats rode at anchor with their riding lights doubled in the black water. He did not turn when the door closed behind me. For a while neither of us spoke, and I understood that this too was part of it — Brannock liked a man to stand in his silence long enough to remember who owned the silence.

"Coral Voss," he said at last, to the window rather than to me. "Ten years in my counting house. Do you know what I paid for you?"

"I was indentured at fourteen, sir. My father's debt passed to me."

"Your father's debt." He said it the way a man reads a title off a deed. "Two hundred and forty pounds, less salvage fees. Your father cost this company a boat and eleven fathoms of good line, and we recovered neither, and then we kept his son anyway, because his son could write a fair hand and never asked for anything." Now he turned. He was a big man going soft at the edges, silver-haired, with a face built for boardrooms in colder cities — a face designed to be believed by men who never came here. "Do you know why I keep you, Voss? Not the hand."

"No, sir."

"Because you are quiet. Because you see everything and repeat nothing. In twenty-six years of trade I have employed clever men and loyal men and fast men, and every one of them eventually wanted something loud. You have never wanted anything loud in your life." He crossed to the sideboard and poured two glasses of brandy, which he had never done before either. "Tonight I need that quality of yours more than I have ever needed it."

I took the glass he offered. I did not drink. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere down the hill a soldier's boot heels rang on stone, unhurried, the garrison walking its rounds through our town as though it had always owned it.

"You've seen the soldiers in the books," Brannock said.

"I've seen them copying, sir. Three days now."

"And you've seen what they copy." He drank, watching me over the rim. "Names. Origins. Terms of service. Every diver in the fleet, laid out in ink like specimens in a case. And now a corporal-adjutant with a lawyer's eyes sits in my counting house reading those pages as though they were scripture, looking for sins." He set the glass down. "You know the fleet better than any man alive, Voss. You wrote half those entries yourself. So tell me plainly: how many of them will hold water?"

It was a question with a hook in it, and we both knew it. I looked at my brandy and gave the answer I had been giving myself all week, in the dark, before sleep.

"Some will. Some won't."

"How many?"

"Sir — if I say a number, it becomes my number. It goes into your mouth wearing my voice. I'd rather you asked me something smaller."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Interest. The look of a man finding unexpected weight in a tool he thought he knew. "Then I'll ask you something smaller. I'll ask you about pages."

* * *

He laid it out the way he laid out everything: calmly, in order, with numbers.

Hale had flagged sheets. Of course he had; Coral had watched him do it, the adjutant's pencil hovering and touching down like a heron striking minnows. Brannock knew the count. Brannock always knew the count.

"There are nineteen pages that concern us," he said. "Nineteen pages out of four hundred and sixty. Nineteen pages, and the company dies, or the company lives. If Hale carries those names to the magistrate, every diver on that list becomes government property — conscripted into the naval labor battalions or shipped north to the hulks, and this town loses its fleet inside a season, and I lose everything my grandfather floated on these waters. If the pages go into the stove instead, Hale finds a clean book, finds nothing to prosecute, writes his report, and goes away hungry. Saltmere keeps its fleet. The divers keep their work. Everyone stays exactly where God put them." He spread his soft white hands. "That is the whole arithmetic, Voss. Nineteen pages against four hundred and forty-one. Weigh it."

"The divers aren't weights," I said. "They're names."

"They're entries." His voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Entries that were made before you could grow a beard, by men whose names you never learned, under laws nobody on this rock ever voted for. You didn't forge their freedom, Voss, and you can't forge it now with a bonfire. All you can do is decide whether you drown with them or stand on the quay when it's over." He picked up his glass again, and his tone changed — dropped lower, warmer, almost confiding, and this was the true voice, the one I had heard him use perhaps twice in ten years, the voice he saved for men he was buying. "Here is what I am offering. Passage papers. Not company papers — real ones. Clean name, clean trade, passage to Port Calloway or anywhere the mail boats run, money enough to arrive like a gentleman instead of a clerk. You could be gone inside the month, before this garrison finishes counting its spoils. You would owe nothing to anyone. No ledger, no debt, no master. You understand what I'm describing?"

I understood. I understood so completely that for one full breath I could not breathe at all.

No ledger. I had been entered in a book since before I could read one. My father's drowning had become a debit; my mother's grief had become a credit against it; my childhood had become amortization. To be *unentered* — to walk onto a boat carrying nothing but a paper with my own name written free upon it —

"Sefa Toma," Brannock said.

The name fell into the room like a coin into deep water.

"She's on the list," he went on, watching me with terrible kindness. "Page eleven. Her entry has a flaw in it — you'll know the one I mean, since I'm told your hand appears in the margin of a certain amendment from some years back. If the pages burn, she stays a diver, poor but free of Hale's attention. If the pages sail north, she sails with them, because the navy doesn't distinguish between a forged indenture and a true one; to a magistrate a false entry means a false person, and false persons get processed." He swirled his brandy. "Unless, of course, a certain record-keeper happened to be holding a second set of papers. Two passages instead of one. A man may save whom he chooses, Voss. That is the only freedom anyone in this world actually gets — the choosing. Everything else is weather."

And there it was. Not a bribe, quite. Something older and filthier than a bribe: an invitation to become him. To sit in a tall house above a drowning town and choose, from a list of names, the few I loved, and call the rest weather.

I made myself look at the window. The gunboats lay black on the black water, patient as stones. Somewhere beyond them the diving fleet rocked at its moorings, lamps lit early because the winter nights came fast, and on those decks men and women were mending nets and boiling kettles and arguing about football and doing all the ordinary holy things people do, not one of them knowing that their entire existence had been reduced to nineteen pages and a stove.

"You want an answer tonight," I said.

"I want an answer by Friday," Brannock said. "I'm told Hale completes his inventory Thursday. After that, the choice stops being ours." He came around the desk and stood close, close enough that I could smell the brandy and the bay rum and, underneath, the cold iron smell of a man who had spent thirty years signing other people's lives. He put his hand on my shoulder — the first time in ten years he had touched me — and it weighed like a capstan bar. "You're the best thing this company ever produced, Voss, and I include the pearls. Don't let sentiment sink you. Sentiment is just debt with better manners."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the harbor road, because I needed the wind.

The tide was out and the mudflats breathed their low mineral breath, and the moored hulls knocked together in the dark like slow heartbeats. I passed the net-sheds where a lamp still burned — Sefa's shed; she was always the last to leave — and I saw her silhouette move against the oiled paper of the window, arms raised, shaking out a net so that it opened against the light like a great dark wing. Free, in that moment, of everything but the work and the lamplight. Page eleven. She didn't know she was page eleven. She had never once in her life thought of herself as an entry, and that, I realized, standing in the cold with my collar up and Brannock's offer sitting in my chest like swallowed lead — that was the whole difference between us. He saw the book. She saw the water. And I had spent ten years pretending I could stand with one foot in each and lose nothing.

Two passages. He would even honor it; that was the obscenity of it — Brannock was corrupt, but he was honest about his corruption, and a deal struck with him would hold. Sefa safe. Myself safe. Gone inside the month, south to Port Calloway, where no one would know my name or my father's debt or the precise pressure of my nib. I could live. I could live comfortably. All it required was that I stand in the counting house on Friday morning and feed nineteen pages into the stove while the fleet put out to sea above the very people those pages were sworn to free, and watch the smoke rise over the water, and call it weather.

And Hale would find a clean book. And Hale would go away. And nothing would ever change — not the terms, not the trucks, not the company store that sold a man back his own wages at interest, not the law that read my father's death as a rounding error. The town would keep its fleet the way a slave keeps his chains: carefully, because they're the only property he's permitted.

I stopped at the seawall and looked back at the harbor, and I found — this is the truest thing I can tell you about that night — that I felt no heroism in myself at all. Only a cold, clarifying anger, the kind that comes when temptation finally shows its face and you discover the face belongs to someone you know. Brannock had fed me my supper for ten years. He had taught me my letters' worth of silence, entry by entry. He was offering me the rest of my life at the price of everyone else's, and he had done the arithmetic so gently, so reasonably, that a weaker man — or a wearier one — would have signed before the brandy was finished.

Friday, he had said. Answer by Friday.

I stood there until my feet went numb, doing sums of my own.

Because here is what Brannock did not know — could not know, could not imagine, any more than a man can imagine the interior of his own lock. He thought the ledger was a single object, a book, a thing that lived in the counting house and died in the counting house. He thought destroying pages destroyed truths. But I had copied shipping manifests in three languages. I had reconciled accounts against customs rolls and parish registers and the memory of my own hand. I knew that a truth, properly recorded, is the one cargo that cannot be seized at a checkpoint, because it travels in the only hold no soldier has ever searched.

Four hundred and forty-one pages would burn exactly as easily as nineteen. The question was never which pages to save.

It was how to carry the whole book out from under the eye of the colonial army — past the harbor gate, past the customs house, past the fort bridge — before Thursday's inventory closed the account forever.

Down in the net-shed, the lamp went out. I turned up my collar and started home through the wind, already counting.
