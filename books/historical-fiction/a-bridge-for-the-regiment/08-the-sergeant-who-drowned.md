## 8. The Sergeant Who Drowned

The rum in Saltmere was called widow's milk, and Tobias Ives had been nursing it for eleven years, which made him, by his own rough arithmetic, the widower of everything he had ever been.

He drank at the Anchor & Chain because it was the only house on the rock that would still serve him, and he drank there at the far end of the bar, near the window where the harbor light swept past every eight seconds like a slow accusation. Eight seconds. He had learned to measure his life in eight-second intervals now, the way other men measured it in birthdays. The light came around, touched the wet stones outside, and went away again, and if a man was careful, if a man kept his eyes on his glass, he could pretend that each pass of the beam was not asking him the same question.

*What did you write in the report, Sergeant?*

He had written that Elias Voss, diver, aged forty-one, had drowned as a result of his own misjudgment — that he had descended without proper weighting, that the current off the western shelf had carried him, that no fault attached to the company or its equipment. He had written it in his neat sergeant's hand, the hand that had once copied garrison orders so cleanly the major had praised them aloud, and he had signed it, and Brannock had paid him thirty pounds for the signature, and the sea had kept the rest.

That was before the disgrace. Before the affair with the paymaster's wife that was only the excuse — the real reason was that a man who would sell one signature would sell anything, and everyone in the garrison knew it even if no one said it, and Ives had been quietly, comprehensively ruined years before the drinking gave them all a convenient reason.

Now the soldiers had come, and the town smelled different — gun oil and horse dung and young men's sweat laid over the old smells of brine and tar and the sweet rot of the drying racks. Ives watched them from his window seat with the professional eye he could not put down. They were sloppy at the harbor gate. Sloppy at the customs house. A man who had run supply convoys through two colonial campaigns could see six ways through their checkpoints before breakfast, and this thought had begun, over the past week, to arrange itself into something that felt dangerously like hope.

Because Ives had something. He had kept it for eleven years in a tin dispatch box under the loose hearthstone of his rented room, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: the original draft of the drowning report, in his own hand, before Brannock's clerk had made him recopy the clean version. The draft with the crossed-out line. The line that had said, in plain soldier's ink: *Diver's line cut from above. Cut is clean, blade-made, witnessed by self.*

Brannock had stood over him while he rewrote it. Had poured him a drink first, the way you'd steady a horse before the knife. *Elias Voss asked too many questions about the indenture ledgers, Sergeant. A man who drowns asks no questions at all. Think of your pension.* And then, when the pension hadn't been enough, the thirty pounds, counted out in sovereigns that Ives could still feel the weight of, cold and round and endless, in the palm of his hand whenever the harbor light came around.

He had never spent those sovereigns. That was the joke he told himself, alone, at the end of the bar. Eleven years sober of spending them and drunk of everything else.

* * *

It was the boy's name on the requisition lists that undid him.

Ives still had friends enough among the company clerks to see what passed through the counting house — disgrace bought a certain terrible transparency; nobody bothered hiding papers from a man they'd already written off. And when the army requisitioned Saltmere and demanded the master ledger, Ives had gone down to the quay in the grey morning and heard the soldiers reading out names, and one name had gone through him like a boat-hook.

Voss. Coral Voss. Record-keeper.

He had watched the boy — not a boy anymore, a man of middle height with ink-stained fingers and a face that gave away nothing, a face like a closed ledger itself — standing aside while Director Brannock handed over the book. Watched him bow his head slightly, invisibly, the way a man does when he is counting his exits. And Ives had recognized the posture because he had invented it. It was the posture of a man carrying something heavier than anyone can see.

The father had stood the same way. Elias Voss, who read at night by borrowed lamplight, who asked the wrong questions in a voice too soft to punish, who had found out — Ives never learned how much — what the company did with the names in its books. What an indenture really meant when the paper said seven years and the arithmetic always came out longer. Elias Voss had stood quietly at the edge of things, adding up the truth, and the truth had weighed exactly enough to drown him.

And here was the son, keeping the same books, wearing the same silence, and the soldiers were copying names out of the ledger with the flat efficiency of men making a laundry list, and Ives sat in the Anchor & Chain with his third rum and understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the bottom of a bottle or the end of a career, that he was going to have to talk to the boy.

He rehearsed it for three days. That was the coward's way and he knew it — a sergeant who had shouted orders over gunfire, rehearsing sentences in a tavern mirror — but grief does not make a man brave, it only makes him precise about his cowardice. Three days of watching Coral Voss come and go from the counting house. Three days of the harbor light sweeping its eight-second question. On the fourth evening, with the wind backing around to the north and the first real cold of winter coming off the water like a held breath let out, Ives put down coin for a fourth rum he did not want, picked up a second full glass, and crossed the room.

* * *

Coral Voss took his supper at a corner table in the Anchor & Chain because the tavern keeper's wife fed the clerks cheaply and asked nothing. He sat with his back to the wall, Ives noted. Always the wall. Elias used to do that too.

"Sergeant," Coral said, before Ives had spoken a word, not looking up from his plate. "They stopped giving you a table by rank some years ago, I think."

"Eleven years ago." Ives set the second glass down on the table between them. "This is yours. You'll want it, or you'll want me gone, and either way it costs me fourpence I can ill afford."

Coral looked up then. His eyes were grey, the exact color of the winter sea, and they went from the glass to Ives's face and stayed there, weighing. Ten years of the counting house had given the boy a way of looking at a thing that was worse than suspicion: it was assessment. Ives felt himself entered, itemized, valued, and written down, and he thought, absurdly, *so that's what it feels like from the other side of the desk.*

"Say what you've come to say," Coral said quietly. "You've been working up to it for three days. You drink faster when you're deciding something."

"You watch me."

"I watch everyone. It's the work."

Ives sat down across from him. The bench creaked. Around them the tavern did what taverns do — murmured, laughed, broke glass somewhere behind the bar — and the ordinary noise of it folded around their table like weather.

"Your father," Ives began, and stopped, because the word had come out of his chest like a rib. He started again. "Elias Voss. Diver. Drowned off the western shelf, the eleventh of March, eighteen eighty-eight."

"I know the date." No inflection at all. Ice on deep water.

"The official report says his weighting was improper. Says the current took him. Says no fault attaches." Ives turned his own glass slowly on the scarred wood, watching the rum turn with it. "I wrote that report."

Something moved in Coral's face — not surprise. Something older and more tired than surprise, arriving late, the way news arrives in port towns. "You're telling me you're the man who—"

"I'm telling you I'm the man who wrote down a lie and was paid thirty pounds for the handwriting." Ives's voice stayed level, which surprised him; he had expected it to break. "There was a line in my first draft. My own hand, before they made me copy it clean. It said the diver's line was cut from above. Blade-cut. Witnessed by myself. Your father didn't slip and he wasn't careless and the current had nothing to do with it. He was cut loose, Mr. Voss, because he'd been asking questions about the indenture ledgers, and Director Brannock decided a drowned man asks no questions."

The tavern noise went on. Somewhere a soldier sang something in a language none of the drinkers knew. Coral Voss sat absolutely still, and Ives watched ten years arrive in the boy's face all at once — not shock but confirmation, the terrible relief of a wound finally being acknowledged by a doctor after a decade of pretending it wasn't there.

"He talked about it," Coral said finally, very low. "Before he went out that last morning. He told my mother the books didn't add up. He said—" The grey eyes closed briefly. "He said a ledger is just a confession that hasn't been read yet. He actually laughed when he said it."

"Aye. That sounds like him. I met him twice. Both times he made me feel like I was being gently audited." Ives drained half his rum. "Mr. Voss, I'm not here to unburden myself. God knows a confession comforts the confessor and nobody else. I'm here because I still have the original draft. The one with the cut line in it. It's been under a hearthstone for eleven years, wrapped in oilcloth, and it will outlast us both. It's evidence. Real evidence — witness attestation in a serving sergeant's hand, predating the falsified filed report. It's the kind of paper a naval magistrate could hang Brannock's whole enterprise with."

Coral's breath changed. Just barely. But Ives had commanded men in the field; he could read the small weather of a human body, and he saw the boy's mind begin, instantly, furiously, to calculate — routes, checkpoints, risk — and he understood in that moment exactly what the son had become and what he was already planning, and his heart went out to him and broke a little further for him, both at once.

"And the price?" Coral said. His voice was steady. Too steady.

"Passage off this rock." Ives said it plainly because dressing it up would insult them both. "When whatever you're going to do is done — and you are going to do something, Mr. Voss, I can see it sitting behind your eyes like cargo waiting on a tide — I go with you, or I go on whatever ship leaves afterward, and I never come back. I don't want money. I've got money I can't spend. I want to be a hundred miles from Saltmere when the reckoning comes, because I'm not fit to stand and face it and we both know why."

"No," Coral said. "You're not asking to flee it. You're asking to be absent from it. There's a difference, and I suspect you know the difference, and I suspect that difference is why you can't sleep."

Ives looked at him for a long moment. The harbor light swept past the window — eight seconds — and came around again.

"You're your father's son," he said roughly. "God help you."

"Everyone says that," Coral answered, and for the first time something almost gentle moved in his face. "Nobody ever says it like a warning." He turned the untouched glass of rum in a slow circle, thinking. Outside, the wind rose another notch and the first spits of true winter sleet ticked against the dark glass. "The original stays where it is until I send for it. If anyone follows you home, if anyone has seen you talking to me tonight, it burns. Do you understand? Whatever else happens, that paper must not fall into Hale's hands, because Hale doesn't want justice, he wants a prosecution, and there's a difference there too, and it's a difference men die inside of."

"Hale." Ives tasted the name. "The adjutant. Yes. I've seen him work the room. He interviews like a man measuring for a coffin — polite the whole time, and always the measurements coming out in his favor." He hesitated. "Mr. Voss. One more thing, and then I'll leave you your supper."

"Say it."

"Whatever's in that master ledger they took — whoever's name is written in it however it's written — your father died because he tried to make the books tell the truth from the outside. You're going to try to make them tell it from the inside. That's braver and it's also more dangerous, because Brannock never feared the divers and he never feared the magistrate. The only thing that man has ever feared is his own record-keeper." Ives stood, joints cracking like ship's rope, and put on his coat. "Keep that in mind. The most dangerous man in Saltmere isn't the one holding the gun. It's the quiet one holding the pen who's decided to stop writing lies."

He left the fourpence on the table, more than the rum was worth, and walked out into the sleet without looking back, and behind him he heard — faint, almost imagined — the sound of Coral Voss finally picking up the glass and drinking it down in one swallow, the way a man drinks when he has just agreed to something that will cost him everything.

* * *

The sleet had turned to proper snow by the time Ives reached his rooms above the cooperage. He lit no lamp. His hands knew the way in the dark: the loose hearthstone, the tin dispatch box, the oilcloth bundle he did not unwrap because he did not need to. His fingers rested on it the way other men's fingers rested on a Bible when they swore.

"Elias," he said aloud to the empty room, to the dark, to the sea he could hear working at the pilings below even through snow and stone. "Your boy's going to do it. Your boy's going to open the books."

The harbor light swept across the window. Eight seconds. Then eight seconds more. And for the first time in eleven years, Tobias Ives slept without dreaming of the western shelf — though he woke once, near dawn, certain he had heard, beneath the wind, the sound of a pen moving quickly across good paper, page after page, like a man copying out his soul before the tide came in.

Three streets away, in the counting house, that was nearly exactly what was happening — and by morning, Corporal-Adjutant Hale would notice that someone else had noticed it too.
