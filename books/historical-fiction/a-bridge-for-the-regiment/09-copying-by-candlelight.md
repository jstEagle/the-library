## 9. Copying by Candlelight

The first night, I did not copy anything at all. I only sat with the ledger open on my knees and listened to the wind worry the shutters of the counting house, as if the building itself were trying to warn me.

Saltmere in winter is a town holding its breath. The pearl fleet was lashed down for the season, hulls knocking against the quay like restless animals, and the soldiers had settled into their occupation with the patience of men who expected to stay until spring. Every evening at six the harbor gate closed, and every morning it opened again, and between those hours Saltmere belonged to itself the way a held breath belongs to the lungs that hold it — temporarily, and under strain.

I had told myself for three days that there was nothing to be done. The master ledger sat under Corporal-Adjutant Hale's eye now; Director Brannock had surrendered it without so much as loosening his cravat, and what was written there was written. True names. Ports of origin. The terms of indentures that were not indentures at all but slavery wearing a clerk's collar. Sefa's name among them, or rather not her name — the name the company gave her when she was eleven years old and worth less on paper than a diving boat.

And beneath her entry, in my own hand, in ink ten years dry: an alteration so small that only God and I could find it.

I had made that forgery when I was nineteen and did not yet understand what I understood now — that kindness leaves a trail. That every mercy done in secret writes itself down somewhere, even if only in the memory of the man who did it, and memory can be interrogated.

So I sat with the ledger — no. Let me be precise, because precision was my trade before it became my salvation. I sat with *a* ledger. Brannock kept a working copy in the back room of the counting house, a fair record updated quarterly, and Hale's men had taken the master without ever asking whether the company was honest enough to keep only one book. Soldiers assume theft is simple. They had not yet spent ten years learning how complicated a merchant can make a lie.

The working copy lay open on my knees by candlelight, and I listened to the wind, and I thought about what it costs to become visible.

* * *

On the second night I began to copy.

I chose the back room because it was mine in every way that mattered. Ten years of carrying ledgers through that door had taught the floorboards my weight, and they kept their opinions to themselves. The window faced the alley where the night soil cart passed at four, and the chimney drew poorly, which meant no one would wonder why a room smelled of smoke and tallow when the counting house proper was cold and dark and honest.

I ruled my pages first. This mattered more than anything else I did that night. If you copy a ledger, you must copy its bones — the ruling, the margins, the particular cramped width of the columns — because a forgery fails at the edges before it fails anywhere else. Hale had struck me, in our one interview, as a man who read books by their structure the way a diver reads water by its swells. He would notice a margin. He might never notice a name.

I cut my paper to match. I mixed my ink — walnut and lampblack, a shade browner than the company's iron gall, but iron gall darkens with age and mine would darken with the same lie if I aged it properly by the fire. Then I set the working copy beside my fresh pages and began.

*Entry 114. Maelis, Toma. Origin: Kessu Harbor. Term: seven years from Michaelmas 1893. Valuation: £14.*

A man. I had seen him dive. I had watched him come up bleeding from the ears once, and entered the cost of his poultice into the accounts as *equipment, miscellaneous*. Fourteen pounds. That was what Toma Maelis weighed, on paper, in the year of our Lord 1898.

I copied him faithfully onto my page. I altered nothing. That was the discipline, and I want it understood: whatever I became that winter, I began as nothing but a mirror. A copy that alters too early is a confession. A copy that alters nothing is invisible, and invisibility was the only country I had ever held title to.

By the third bell my wrist ached and my eyes swam and I had covered eleven pages. I learned things in those eleven pages that I had known and never *known* — that Hale had already been through this working copy before me. There were pencil ghosts in the margins, faint as breath on glass, erased but not erased well enough for a man who spent his life reading paper. A dot beside entry 108. A hairline stroke beside 112, 113, 114. He was flagging. He was choosing. Somewhere in the garrison a list was growing, name by name, and the pencil marks said he had already decided who would fill out his quota of conscripts and who would be put ashore at the nearest colonial port with nothing but a bill of lading for a past.

I sat very still and let the candle gutter, and I understood that I was not copying a ledger anymore. I was copying a hunting map, and I was learning to read it from the wrong side.

* * *

The third night, Sefa nearly caught me.

It was my own fault, if fault requires a cause, and I suppose it does. I had grown careless with time. Copying does something to the clock; the pen eats hours whole and leaves no bones. I meant to stop at two bells and I stopped at nearly four, and at four the night soil cart came down the alley, and with it — because the world is built of small cruelties arranged with comic precision — came Sefa.

She worked the winter quay, mending nets and re-lashing the diving weights, because a diver who cannot dive in winter will find work with her hands or go mad, and Sefa had never gone mad in her life and did not intend to start. She rose before dawn to take the best patch of light on the seawall. And she came down the alley at four in the morning because the net store was locked and she had left her grandmother's knife inside it, and the night soil man let her in through the yard, and the yard door opened into —

— the alley. Only the alley. But the back room's shutter was loose, had been loose for a year, and my candle threw a bar of light into the dark like a hand reaching out, and I heard her footsteps stop.

I blew out the flame so fast that hot wax spattered my knuckles, and I stood in absolute blackness listening to my own heart behave badly.

"Coral?" Her voice came through the shutter, low and amused and wide awake. "I can see your shadow breathing."

There are men who can lie smoothly under pressure. I had spent ten years among them. I am not one of them; I have never been one of them; my gift had always been that no one ever asked me a question twice. She waited, and I could hear her smile in the silence, and after a moment I unhooked the shutter and swung the window open and the cold came in like a slap and took the last of the smoke smell with it, mostly.

"It's four in the morning," she said. She was standing in the yard with a knife in her fist and frost in her black hair, and she looked up at me the way she looked at everything — directly, without apology, as though the world owed her a straight answer and she had come to collect. "You're burning candles at four in the morning in a locked office. Either you're embezzling, or you're in love with somebody's wife, and I know for a fact you've never spoken to a married woman in your life."

"I'm doing accounts," I said.

"Nobody does accounts by candlelight in December." She climbed onto the rain barrel and put her forearms on the sill, and suddenly her face was a foot from mine, lit by nothing, close enough that I could see the salt dried at her temples. Close enough that honesty became a physical danger, like deep water. "Let me in."

"Sefa—"

"Let me in, Coral, or I'll start shouting your name at the top of my voice and let the sentries ask the questions."

So I let her in.

* * *

Here is what I confessed to her, sitting on two crates while she perched on the desk itself with her knife across her knees like a magistrate: everything except the true thing.

I told her about the requisition. I told her that the master ledger held every diver's birth name and origin port, that indentures were supposed to be voluntary contracts and half the entries in the book recorded people who had never signed anything, that the army now possessed all of it. I told her about the pencil marks — I showed her, actually, tilting the working copy toward the shuttered window where the gray was just beginning, and she found the ghost-marks herself within a minute, because Sefa reads water and paper with the same eye.

"That's beside my uncle's entry," she said quietly. "Entry 131. Raho."

"I know."

"And beside Maelis's."

"I know."

She was quiet for a long moment. Frost had begun to feather the inside of the glass, and the room was so cold we could see each other's breath, and somewhere out in the harbor a mooring line creaked against a post, patient as everything else that waits for spring.

"You're copying it," she said at last. Not a question. She had read the ruled pages stacked at my elbow, matched them against the original, and drawn the conclusion I had hoped she'd miss and known she wouldn't. "Why? What good is a copy?"

"Because," I said, and then I had to stop, because the answer was also a plan, and plans spoken aloud become witnesses' testimony, and I had not yet decided how much witness I wanted her to be.

"Because," she repeated.

"Because a copy can travel where the original can't. Because if something happens to the book — fire, misfiling, loss — there should be another one. Because—" and here I lied to her, badly, the first bad lie of my adult life, "—because someone should know what's written, in case anyone is ever asked."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the sky was turning the color of old pewter, and the first sounds of the waking quay drifted through the shutter — a hatch cover, a gull, boots on stone. In the gray light her face was unreadable, and I realized I had never once in ten years been unable to read it, and that this was what it felt like to be on the other side of me.

"You're a terrible liar," she said finally. "You always have been. Your mouth does a thing, right here—" she touched the corner of her own lips, "—when you're not saying the true thing. You've done it since you were fifteen and told Mr. Brannock you couldn't swim."

"Why would I lie about swimming?"

"Exactly. That's the point." She slid off the desk and stood, rolling her shoulders, and for a moment she was a silhouette against the graying window, all line and intention. "You're doing something dangerous, and you're doing it alone, and you think that's noble because you've been invisible so long you've mistaken it for virtue."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." One word, flat as a cleat. Then, softer: "If you're going to drown, Coral Voss, do it in daylight where someone can pull you out. Don't do it by candlelight in a locked room. That's not courage. That's just drowning with extra steps."

She went out through the yard door with her knife in her pocket, and I sat among my false pages and my cooling candle and understood that she had taken something with her — not the secret, which was still safe, but the comfort of it. Secrets carried alone weigh what feathers weigh. Secrets shared, even badly, even half-honestly, weigh what lead weighs. And yet I found, examining myself as I extinguished the stub and stacked the pages in their oilcloth, that I did not regret it. There is a kind of intimacy that has nothing to do with touch — the intimacy of shared risk, of two people knowing the same ice and hearing it crack together. She knew now. Not everything. Never everything. But the ice was ours.

* * *

The fourth night I finished.

I worked faster than wisdom recommends, because the pencil ghosts had told me something else on the third night, something I had turned over and over while pretending to mend nets on the quay like any other idle winter man: the flags clustered thickest around the newest entries. Pages forty onward. The indentures signed — or unsigned — in the last five years, the ones with living claimants, the ones easiest to prosecute and hardest to dispute. Hale wanted clean cases. He needed one prosecution, and he was going to build it out of the freshest ink in the book.

Which meant the oldest entries — the faded ones, the ones in dead clerks' hands, the ones whose claimants had died or vanished — were, for the moment, below his horizon.

Sefa's entry was twenty-two years old. Forged in my hand ten years ago. Buried in the deep sediment of the book, under pencil dust and erasure crumbs, unflagged.

For now.

At half past three in the morning I wrote the last entry — *Entry 217. Voss, Coral. Clerk. Salary: £40 per annum* — and copied even that, my own small self, my own small price, and set down my pen, and flexed my ruined hand, and looked at what I had made: a second ledger, complete, faithful, unaltered, smelling faintly of walnut smoke and lying in every fiber of its being. It would pass a casual inspection. It might even pass Hale's. Paper wants to believe in itself; that is the whole tragedy of records, and the whole hope of them.

I wrapped it in oilcloth, sewed the seams with sail twine, and weighed the packet in my hand. Three pounds, maybe a little more. Heavy as a heart. Light as a diving weight.

Then I heard the latch on the front door of the counting house lift — softly, expertly, at an hour when no honest key turned — and the cold draft came down the hall carrying the smell of wet wool and pipe tobacco, and boots crossed the floorboards toward the back room with the unhurried tread of a man who already knows what he will find and is merely confirming the arithmetic.

I put out the candle. There was nowhere to go. The window faced the yard where Sefa's footprints were still printed in the frost, and the door opened behind me, and the light of a lantern fell across the walls where forty pages of evidence hung drying on lines like fish.

"Well," said Sergeant Ives, and closed the door gently behind him. "Don't trouble yourself hiding, son. I've come about a different book entirely."
