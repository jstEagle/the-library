## 10. First Checkpoint: The Harbor Gate

The needle went through the canvas with a sound like a small bone breaking.

Coral had stolen it from the garrison mending pile three days earlier — a blunt sailmaker's needle, curved at the tip, meant for patching jibs — and he had stolen the waxed thread too, and the thimble that sat loose on his clerk's finger because his hands had never grown calluses in the way the army expected of them. He worked by the light of a shuttered lamp in the back room of Sefa's mother's house, where the smell of salt fish and tar had soaked into the walls over two generations, and where no soldier had yet thought to look, because soldiers did not look at the homes of divers. Soldiers looked at counting houses. Soldiers looked at ledgers. That was the whole architecture of Coral's hope: that the army's gaze was heavy and predictable, and that he had spent ten years learning exactly where it fell.

The diving-weight bag lay open across his knees like a gutted animal.

It was a good bag — Sefa's own, patched at the mouth where the rope had worn through, stiff with brine and blacklead dust. Inside it, folded into a packet the width of a prayer book and wrapped first in oilcloth and then in a diver's spare undershirt, were forty-one pages of copied ledger. Not the whole book; the whole book would have taken a wagon and a miracle. But the forty-one pages held everything that mattered: the true names, the villages of origin, the forged indentures, the interest rates that turned a loan of thirty shillings into a bondage of decades. And on page nineteen, in an entry dated nine years past, in ink Coral could still smell in his sleep, the alteration that proved Sefa's freedom paper rested on a lie — a lie told by her father's hand, witnessed by none, made lawful only because Coral himself had let it stand and then smoothed its edges until even Brannock believed it.

If Hale read page nineteen, Sefa would be seized before she finished her next dive. If Hale never read page nineteen, and the magistrate did, she would be free forever. The same page. Two futures folded together like wet linen, and no way to know which would unfold until the paper crossed the fort bridge.

So the pages had to cross. And they could not cross in Coral's hands.

He had understood this by the third night of copying, lying awake in his room above the counting house while the garrison's sentries coughed in the yard below. Hale watched him now. Not constantly — Hale was too careful for constant watching — but often enough. A question here. A request to see the fair-copy pages there. The adjutant's pale eyes moving over Coral's face the way Coral's own eyes moved over columns of figures, looking for the one entry that refused to reconcile. Coral was, he knew, already suspected. He simply could not yet be proven guilty, and between those two states ran a narrow ledge of days, and he meant to walk it all the way to the bridge.

But Sefa could walk through the harbor gate any morning she liked. Divers came and went; that was the nature of divers. Their names were in the ledger, yes, but their bodies belonged to the water, and the water had never been requisitioned. A woman carrying her weight bag down to the boats was as unremarkable as weather.

Which meant the most dangerous thing Coral had ever done was to ask her.

* * *

She had not said yes quickly.

He had come to her at the tide pools below the eastern breakwater, where the low water left basins of trapped sea, warm and glass-clear, and where they had talked as children because no adult ever bothered walking out that far. She was sitting on the rock with her feet in a pool, drying them, her hair still stiff from the morning's dive. He told her everything except the feeling underneath everything — the copying, the checkpoints, the plan for the gate. His voice stayed level. Ten years of reading figures aloud to suspicious men had given him that.

When he finished, Sefa looked at the water for a long time.

"You want me to carry it," she said. "Because they watch you and not me."

"Because they watch me and not you," he agreed.

"And if they find it." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes were the flat grey-green of deep water under cloud, eyes that had looked down at pearl beds sixty feet below the surface and learned not to flinch at anything. "What happens to the woman carrying stolen pages sewn into her own weight bag? Do I hang? Or is it just prison for someone like me?"

"They won't search a weight bag thoroughly," Coral said. "It's lead shot and canvas. It's heavy and filthy."

"That's not what I asked, Coral."

The honesty of it took his breath away. He had prepared arguments — the timing of the sentry rotations, the habits of the gate guards, the fact that searches at the harbor gate focused on cargo manifests and crates because crates carried value and women carrying dive gear carried nothing worth stealing. He had prepared all of it, and now he found he could not use any of it, because she had asked him a true question and ten years of silence had finally taught him what a lie cost.

"If they find it," he said quietly, "you will be arrested, and I will say the pages are mine and that you knew nothing."

"They'd believe that?"

"No," Coral said. "But they'd have to write it down, and writing things down is the only kind of honesty the army has. It might buy you enough confusion to run."

Sefa laughed — one short breath through her nose, no warmth in it. "Run where? We live on a rock, Coral. Everyone's always running somewhere, and the rock stays."

Then she was quiet again, working the hem of her shift between her fingers, and Coral sat with his hands flat on the cold rock and waited, because waiting was the other thing ten years of ledgers had taught him. Figures settled if you didn't shake them. People sometimes did too.

"Page nineteen," Sefa said at last, so softly he almost missed it under the wash of the tide. "Is my name on it?"

Coral's chest closed like a fist. "Yes."

"My true name. Where my mother came from. What my father owed." She nodded slowly, as if confirming something she had known since childhood and had simply never said aloud, the way one does not say aloud that winter comes every year. "And the paper. My freedom paper. Is it real or isn't it?"

"It's real," Coral said. "I checked every seal and signature. It's real *because*—" He stopped.

"Because you made it real," Sefa finished for him, and there was no gratitude in her voice, and no anger either, only a terrible clear attention, like the moment before a diver drops from the gunwale into dark water. "You've known for years what I am and what I'm not, and you kept the books on it, and you never once told me."

"I was protecting—"

"Don't." One word, quiet as a closing door. "Not tonight. Tonight I'll carry your pages. Because if those soldiers get hold of our names we're all conscripted or shipped, and being free and ignorant hasn't done me much good anyway." She stood, wrung out the tail of her shift, and looked down at him with the grey-green eyes. "But after this is done — after whatever happens happens — you and I are going to have a conversation, Coral Voss, and you are going to tell me everything you ever decided on my behalf without asking. Every entry. Every year."

"Yes," Coral said, because there was nothing else.

She picked up the weight bag and slung it over her shoulder, testing the rope. "Bring the needle tomorrow evening," she said. "My mother sleeps like the dead and you sew like a dying man. You'll need the hours."

* * *

He needed four of them.

The trick, he had realized, was not to hide the packet *in* the bag but to make it part of the bag. A lump in the lead shot would show under a probing hand; a flat packet against the canvas wall would not, if the canvas was doubled and restitched along its original seam line. So Coral unpicked eleven inches of the bag's bottom seam with a heated needle, laid the oilcloth packet flat in the void between outer canvas and inner lining, and sewed it shut again with stitches copied exactly from the original ones — same length, same angle, same slight irregularity where the original sewer's hand had tired. It was forgery of a different kind than the ledgers, but it was the same skill at heart: making a false thing look worn and true. His father had been a diver. Coral had never touched the trade. But that night, pricking his thumb for the fourth time and sucking the blood away in the dark, he thought he understood something of it — the patience, the faith that the work must be perfect because the water forgave nothing.

Sefa sat opposite him the whole four hours, sharpening her diving knife on a whetstone in slow, rhythmic strokes. She did not speak, and neither did he. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a patrol's boots rang on the cobbles and faded. When the last stitch went in, Coral ran his palm over the seam until his skin memorized it, and found he could not feel where the old sewing ended and the new began.

Sefa put down the knife, took the bag, weighed it in her hand, and looked at him.

"Well," she said. "Now either we're both brave or we're both fools."

"There's very little difference," Coral said. "I've recorded the careers of both kinds."

That almost got a smile. Almost.

* * *

Morning came up grey and cold, the harbor breathing mist, the fishing boats crusted with rime along their gunwales. The fleet was putting out late that day — the second boat, the *Maren*, had a cracked mast band and the company's shipwright was drunker than usual — so the quay was crowded with divers waiting, stamping their feet, their breath hanging over them like the smoke of a drowned village. Perfect cover. A hundred people at the gate meant a hundred faces for the sentries to sort through, and sorting was not what garrison soldiers did best before breakfast.

Coral walked the first stretch with her. This was deliberate: a clerk accompanying a diver toward the gate drew less notice than a clerk alone, because everyone knew clerks were harmless, and it let him see the gate before she reached it. Then, fifty yards out, they would separate naturally — she toward the boats, he back toward the counting house — and the packet would go forward without him, which was the entire point.

The harbor gate had been a customs arch once, stone pillars and an iron lantern bracket, and now it was two sawhorses, a trestle table, and four soldiers of the 3rd Colonial with a corporal who wore his greatcoat buttoned wrong. A fifth soldier lounged against the pillar with a bayonet-scabbarded rifle and the particular boredom of a man who has searched six hundred identical baskets and found nothing but fish. Beyond the gate, the mist. Beyond the mist, the boats. Beyond the boats, if everything held, the customs house, the fort bridge, the magistrate, the future.

"Gate duty rotates at noon," Coral murmured, eyes forward. "The fat one with the wrong buttons is new. New ones over-search. Walk slow, look bored, and if they open the bag, let them. Lead shot makes its own argument."

"Lead shot," Sefa said, "and eleven inches of treason."

"Eleven inches of truth," Coral said. "It only becomes treason when they catch it."

She huffed — nearly a laugh — and then they were at the sawhorses, and Coral peeled away leftward as arranged, angling toward the fish stalls where he had no business whatsoever, and looked back only once, and hated himself for looking back only once, because a man who looks back twice is a man with something to hide and the fat corporal was watching the crowd the way new men do, hungrily, wanting to find something, needing to justify the greatcoat.

Sefa joined the knot of divers funneling through the gate. From the fish stall Coral could see her plainly: the weight bag over her shoulder, her stride unhurried, her face set in the mild blankness of a woman thinking about cold water and wages. Around her, baskets of shellfish, coils of rope, a boy hauling a crate of gull eggs. The trestle table corporal waved through three divers without touching anything. Then Sefa.

Then the bored soldier by the pillar pushed off the wall.

Later, Coral would reconstruct those ninety seconds a thousand times, and each reconstruction would take longer than the event itself. The soldier — young, chapped-faced, gloves too big — stepped into Sefa's path and said something Coral couldn't hear over the gulls. Sefa stopped. Set the bag down. The soldier crouched, opened the mouth of the bag, and plunged his gloved arm in to the shoulder.

Coral's heart did not speed; it stopped altogether, and in the ringing silence he became aware of absurd details: a gull screaming somewhere over the breakwater; the smell of the fish stall, brine and ice; his own hands, resting on a barrel of mackerel, perfectly steady, because ten years of keeping his face still while Brannock lied to men's faces about their debts had trained even his body to outlast his terror.

The soldier's arm moved inside the bag. Lead shot shifted with a sound like slow rain. His fingers would be down near the bottom now. Eleven inches of oilcloth and canvas. A stitched seam that would pass a glance and might not pass a grip — if he gripped, if he pressed his palm flat and felt the wrong thickness, the wrong give—

The soldier frowned.

His arm stopped moving. His head tilted. Through the fish stall's noise Coral saw his lips move — *what's in here* or something like it — and saw Sefa answer with the flat patience of a woman asked a stupid question: she said one word, and gestured at the bag with both hands, the universal gesture of divers everywhere: *weight, you idiot, it goes on the rope, it sinks me, touch it yourself if you don't believe me.*

And the corporal at the table — the new one, the hungry one — called out.

Coral's stomach dropped through the quay into the harbour. But the corporal was not calling about Sefa. He was calling the boy with the crate of gull eggs, waving him over, because a crate was a manifest item and manifests were what corporals understood. The boy wheeled his crate to the table. Papers were produced. The fat corporal's hunger transferred instantly and totally to the crate, to the manifest, to the possibility of undeclared eggs.

And the young soldier by the pillar, hearing the commotion, half-turned his head —

— and his arm, still buried in the bag, moved with it, pulled sideways, and his fingers slid along the bottom seam and pressed.

Coral did not breathe. The whole harbor seemed to hold its air — the mist, the gulls, the mackerel under his hands, all suspended — and in that suspension the young soldier's brow creased, and his fingers pressed once more, feeling canvas, feeling shot, feeling nothing but the honest dead weight of a diver's bag, because the packet lay against the *outer* curve of the seam where his wrist couldn't turn, and Coral had measured that turn, had thought about wrists, had spent four hours thinking about nothing else in the world but the geometry of a stranger's arm inside a bag.

The soldier withdrew his hand, wiped it on his greatcoat with a grimace at the blacklead dust, muttered something apologetic, and waved her through.

Sefa shouldered the bag. She did not hurry — she had promised him she wouldn't, and Sefa kept promises like a keel keeps water — but Coral, watching, saw the thing she allowed herself as she passed the last sawhorse: one long exhale, white in the cold air, indistinguishable from any diver's breath. Then the mist took her, then the shape of the boats took the mist, and the packet — forty-one pages, page nineteen among them, two futures folded together — was beyond the gate and out of his hands for the first time in ten years.

Coral bought a mackerel he did not want, paid with exact coins, and walked slowly back toward the counting house with his heart beating again, huge and stupid in his chest, like a man released.

It was done. First checkpoint passed. Two remained: the customs house at midweek, and then the fort bridge, and the bridge was guarded by soldiers who answered to the magistrate's writ and not to Hale — in theory. In theory, the whole plan was sound. In theory, the ledger had never contained forged indentures at all.

He was halfway up the cliff road, the town falling away behind him all slate roofs and rigging, when he heard the boots behind him — two men, unhurried, military — and Sergeant Ives's voice at his shoulder, low and sour with drink:

"Walk, don't turn. Hale's got the copy clerks locked in the records room since dawn, and he's counting pages, lad. Counting them one by one."
