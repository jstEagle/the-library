## 11. Hale Closes In

Corporal-Adjutant Elias Hale had been told once, by a major whose name he no longer bothered to remember, that rage was a tool for men who had already lost the argument. He had taken the lesson the way he took all lessons — silently, completely, and without gratitude — and he had built a career on the far side of it. Rage was loud. Rage announced itself, filled a room, gave the watched man something to watch instead of the watcher. Patience was quieter. Patience sat in a chair with a cold cup of tea and waited for the world to make its mistake.

Saltmere, in his estimation, was a town made almost entirely of mistakes. It had simply not yet been asked to account for them.

He rose before the garrison did, as was his habit, and worked by the grey light that came through the counting house windows off the harbour water. The requisitioned room still smelled of the company — tallow, ink, old tea, and beneath it the faint mineral breath of the sea that got into everything on this rock, into ledgers and uniforms and lungs alike. The soldiers called the smell nothing at all; they had stopped noticing it within a week. Hale noticed everything. That was the whole of his profession.

The master ledger lay open before him, and he disliked it with a purity of feeling he otherwise reserved for very few objects in this world. It was a handsome book. Too handsome. Bound in oxblood calf with brass corners, its pages ruled in a clerk's flawless hand, every figure aligned as if set down by a machine rather than a man. Ten years of accounts, debts, wages, and — buried in the columns like stones in a riverbed — the true names, origins, and terms of indenture of every diver in the Saltmere fleet. Names that had been written down so they could be owned.

Hale did not care about the owning. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, because self-clarity was the discipline underneath all his other disciplines. He was not a reformer. Reformers were sentimentalists with better handwriting. What he cared about was the discrepancy — the single clean thread that, pulled hard enough, would unravel the whole garment, and give him the prosecution that would move him off this wet granite and onto a mainland posting where ambition could actually breathe.

The discrepancy had come to him three nights ago, in the manner most truths came: not dramatically, but arithmetically.

* * *

He had been counting pages.

It was work beneath his rank and beneath his intelligence, which was precisely why he trusted it. Clever frauds were built to defeat clever examinations. They were not built to defeat a man with a ruler, a pencil stub, and no interest whatsoever in being impressed.

The ledger's index — and there was an index, which told Hale the book had been maintained by someone who took a craftsman's pride even in concealment — listed the folios by section: Debts, Wages, Indentures, Shipping, Correspondence Abstracts. Each section's foliation ran unbroken, one through two hundred and forty-six across the full volume. Except that when Hale laid his rule against the actual leaves, section four began at folio ninety-one and ended at folio one hundred and thirty-eight — forty-eight leaves — where the arithmetic of the surrounding sections demanded fifty.

Two pages. Missing, or replaced. And the replacement, if replacement there was, had been done by a hand good enough to fool the eye and not quite good enough to fool a ruler pressed flat against paper stock a hair thinner than its neighbours.

Hale had sat back in his chair and looked out at the harbour for a long time, feeling the particular cold satisfaction that other men felt from brandy. Two pages. Somewhere in those forty-eight leaves — or in the two that were gone — lived whatever the trading company of Saltmere had decided the record must not remember.

Now, in the pre-dawn grey, he turned the suspicious leaves again. The indentures in this run were orderly: names, ports of origin, terms, witnesses. One entry near the middle caught him, not because it was wrong but because it was *smooth*. A woman's indenture — Sefa, of the Vailima fleet, entered under a term of seven years begun nine years prior — with a witness signature that matched no other signature in the volume. Every other entry in the section carried the same two or three company witnesses cycling like tired actors through their parts. This one stood alone, signed in a small, controlled hand: *T. Brannock, Dir.*

Directors did not witness indentures. Directors had clerks to witness indentures. A director's signature here was either vanity, forgery, or a patch over a hole.

And beneath the entry, in the margin, in pencil so faint it might have been erased and re-laid a dozen times, a single mark Hale had learned to read in customs houses along the coast: a small tick, the private shorthand of a clerk flagging his own work for review.

Someone in this building knew exactly what those two pages had held. Someone had made the substitution, or knew who had, or — the possibility Hale found most interesting — had copied the originals before they vanished.

* * *

At eight o'clock the company's clerks came to work, because the army now required them to come to work, and Hale received them one at a time in the small room off the counting house that had formerly served for the storage of damaged goods.

He began with the least of them and worked upward, as was proper. A boy of perhaps sixteen named Prewitt, all elbows and terror, who counted cargo chits and could not stop looking at Hale's hands. An older man, Merrick, with gout and a wheeze, who had kept the shipping abstracts since before the war scare and answered every question with a small cough and a smaller lie. Two more whose names Hale recorded without interest.

To each he asked the same gentle questions. How long in the company's service. Who kept the master ledger. Who was permitted to remove it from the room. Whether folios were ever re-copied, re-bound, repaired. Whether any pages had been damaged — water, fire, vermin — and replaced.

Water damage, he suggested mildly, was common in port towns. No blame attached. The army merely wished to reconstruct the historical record.

Merrick coughed. Prewitt stared at his own knuckles. Nobody had ever heard of a page being replaced in the history of the company, an institution that had operated on this rock for thirty-one years and had surely, in thirty-one years, spilled something.

Hale thanked each of them warmly and wrote their hesitations down afterward, timing them from memory, because hesitation had a duration and duration had a meaning.

It was on the fourth interview that the shape of the thing came clear to him — not the answer, but the silhouette of the answer, the way one sometimes saw a diver's shadow on the seafloor long before the man surfaced.

The fourth clerk was the quiet one.

Coral Voss. Hale had noticed him the first day of the occupation, in the way a man notices the load-bearing wall of a building he intends to renovate. Not the head clerk — that title belonged to a fat and frightened man named Quill whom Brannock hid behind like a barricade — but the one who actually touched the books. Ten years in the company's service, having entered it, according to the personnel abstracts, at some unremarkable age after some unremarkable local tragedy. He moved through the counting house like a draught through a keyhole: present everywhere, visible nowhere. When soldiers demanded files, it was Voss who produced them, already ordered, already correct, before the request had fully finished being made. When Hale had tested the room's knowledge of its own records in the first week, asking questions designed to embarrass, every answer that came back clean and complete had come back in Voss's level, apologetic voice.

The others, in their interviews, had all done the same thing when Hale mentioned the missing folios: they had looked, briefly, at the door. Not at the ledger. At the door, as if checking whether someone might be listening.

Hale understood doors. He filed the observation.

Now Voss sat across the table from him, hands folded, and Hale performed the courtesy of pretending this was an ordinary interview.

'You've kept the master ledger these ten years,' Hale said. 'Or kept it true, at least.'

'I've kept it,' Voss said. 'Kept is a broad word.'

'Is it? Broad how?'

Voss considered. He had a way of considering that most men would have mistaken for slowness. Hale did not mistake it. Slowness happened in the face; this was happening somewhere further back, behind the eyes, in a room with no windows.

'A ledger doesn't keep itself,' Voss said. 'But it isn't mine. I mend the binding. I cut new quills. I enter what I'm given to enter.'

'You never enter what you're not given?'

'No.'

'The hand in the indenture section is yours, though. Folio ninety-one through one hundred and thirty-eight.' Hale let the numbers sit in the air like coins placed on a table. 'A very fine hand. Consistent over ten years. Most men's writing drifts. Yours doesn't. That's discipline.'

'Thank you,' said Voss, in the tone of a man declining a compliment.

'Folio one hundred and twelve has a different feel to it. Thinner stock.' Hale watched the hands. The hands did not move. 'Possibly water damage, historically. Possibly replacement. You'd know better than I.'

'Would I?'

'You cut the quills. You mended the binding. If any page in that book was replaced in the last ten years, Mr. Voss, you either did it, watched it done, or found the evidence of it the next morning. There is no fourth possibility. Someone had to rule the lines, match the ink, press the leaf. That craft lives in this room, and the room contains you and five fools.'

For the first time, something crossed the quiet man's face. Not guilt. Hale catalogued it carefully. It was closer to grief, or to the memory of grief — the expression of a man who has just heard, from a distance, a door close on a room he cannot re-enter.

'Some pages,' Voss said slowly, 'were replaced before my time. Water damage, in the early years. Mr. Quill would have the repair log, if such a log exists.'

'If such a log exists,' Hale agreed pleasantly. 'Does it?'

'I've never seen it.'

There it was — the first flat lie of the interview, delivered so smoothly that a lesser examiner would have banked it as truth. Hale had watched Voss answer twenty-three honest answers by then, and the honest ones all shared a texture: a half-beat of retrieval, the eyes going slightly left as the true record was consulted in that windowless room behind them. The lie had come instantly, pre-built, worn smooth with use.

*I've never seen it.* As if a man who mended bindings had never once gone looking for the record of mended bindings.

Hale closed his notebook. 'Thank you, Mr. Voss. You've been very helpful.'

'Have I?' said Voss, and rose, and bowed his head slightly, and went out through the door that everyone else had glanced at, and did not glance at it himself, because he already knew precisely what was on the other side and how long it would take to cross.

* * *

That evening Hale walked the harbour wall alone, as was his custom, and let the wind do its blunt work on his thoughts.

He did not yet have proof. He laid the fact out before himself honestly, because dishonesty to oneself was the first rot, and he had seen what it did to officers who practiced it — Major What-was-his-name, retired to a card room in the capital, still calling his failures strategy. What Hale had was a page count, a thin leaf, a director's improbable signature, a marginal tick, six hesitations, and one seamless lie. Against that: nothing admissible, nothing a magistrate would read past the first paragraph.

But proof, Hale had learned, was not usually discovered. It was *cultivated*. You found the pressure point and you leaned, patiently, in the right direction, and you waited for the wood to speak.

The pressure point, he suspected, was not Voss himself. Quiet men who lied seamlessly had usually rehearsed against interrogation, and rehearsed liars were expensive to crack. No — the pressure point would be whoever else knew. Frauds were social animals. They needed hands. A page did not walk out of a bound volume on its own; someone had carried it, someone had hidden it, someone had perhaps been promised something. Six clerks, one director, one garrison, and a town full of divers whose names lived in that book like flies in amber.

He would begin, tomorrow, with the repair logs Quill claimed did not exist. He would go gently. He would ask about paper stock invoices, of all the dull things in the world, and watch which name made the fat man sweat.

And there was another thread, thinner, tugging at the corner of his attention. His sergeant had reported that at the harbour gate, three days ago, a routine search of a diving-weight bag had turned up nothing — nothing except that the bag's owner, a woman of the fleet, had gone white to the lips and stayed white for an hour after. Soldiers reported such things the way dogs reported smells: accurately, and without comprehension.

Weights, Hale thought. Ballast. The one cargo on this rock that nobody weighed, because everyone assumed it was only what it appeared to be.

He stood at the rail of the harbour wall until full dark, watching the riding lamps of the fleet bob on the black water, each one a name in a ledger, each ledger a weapon depending on who held it.

Then he turned back toward the counting house, unhurried, patient, and in his neat hand, on the last line of his day-book, beneath the date, he wrote:

*Voss. Watch the quiet one.*

Across the parade ground, in a lit window above the barracks stores, Sergeant Ives — newly volunteered for customs duty, newly sober, newly strange — poured the contents of a flask into the harbour and did not see the adjutant watching him do it.

Hale smiled, without warmth, and made a second note.
