## 12. The Price of Names

The morning they took Tavita, the water was the color of pewter, and Sefa knew before anyone spoke.

She knew it in her body, the way divers know weather before it comes — a pressure behind the eyes, a flattening of the swell. The fleet stood at its moorings in a long gray line, forty boats with their sails furled and their pearl-shell baskets stacked on the quays like sleeping animals, and along the harbor road marched six soldiers with a corporal at their head carrying a single sheet of paper as if it were a live coal.

They went to Tavita's boat first because Tavita's was first. That was all his name meant to the ledger: *first*. First entered, first assessed, first owed. Sefa had dived beside him for nine years. She had pulled him up when his air ran out too soon off the Middle Ground; she had sewn the tear in his shoulder with sailmaker's thread because there was no doctor on the rock who would touch a diver's flesh. And now a corporal with a clean jaw and a voice like a man reading a shipping manifest put that one sheet of paper in front of him and said words Sefa did not fully hear, though she understood every one of them: conscripted, requisitioned, His Majesty's service, labor due against debt.

Tavita looked at the paper the way he would have looked at a shark fin cutting the surface between him and shore. Not surprised. Only measuring the distance.

"I don't read," he said.

"Then I'll read it for you," said the corporal, and he did, in a flat careful voice, while gulls screamed over the fish-gutting tables and the whole fleet stood in silence on the quay, forty boats' worth of divers and boat-hands and basket-menders watching a man learn that his name had been used against him by strangers who had never once seen him wet.

* * *

Sefa did not cry. Crying was salt spent badly. Her grandmother had taught her that before her grandmother's own lungs had filled at last, down there where the shell beds opened like grey flowers, and Sefa had held to it through every funeral the sea had given her. But something moved in her chest that morning that was not grief exactly and not rage exactly, but the two of them married together and grown heavy, like an anchor stone she could neither lift nor set down.

She found Tavita afterward sitting on the gunwale of his own boat, which the soldiers had left — they had taken only him, not yet his vessel, though she understood with a cold sliding certainty that the vessel would follow the man, as the man had followed the name.

"They can't do this," Tavita said. It was not a question. He said it the way you say a prayer you have stopped believing in.

"The book says you owe them," Sefa said. "The book says it for all of us."

"Then burn the book."

She almost laughed. Almost. "And what does the burning change? They've copied it already. Coral told me—" She stopped. The name came out of her mouth before she could weigh it, and once it was out she could not take it back, so she finished differently than she'd intended. "Coral Voss says the army has copies now. Burning changes nothing."

"Coral Voss." Tavita turned the name over like a coin he suspected of being shaved. "The company's shadow. The little clerk who watches us from the counting-house window and never says one word for us in ten years."

"He's not—" she began, and heard herself, and stopped again. Ten years of watching Coral Voss stand in doorways with his ledgers and his lowered eyes, ten years of telling herself that his silence was different from Brannock's silence, kinder, more crowded, a silence full of things unsaid rather than things uncared about. And what proof did she have? What proof had she ever asked for? She had accepted his quietness the way the reef accepts the tide, without questioning where all that water went.

"He counts," Tavita said simply. "Whatever else he is, he counts, and everything he counts comes out against us."

Sefa sat down beside him on the gunwale, and the two of them watched the soldiers walk back along the quay toward the fort with their paper, and the harbor lay flat and gray and patient as a held breath.

"They'll come for another tomorrow," she said. "And another after that. Debt-labor, they call it, but it's the old trade wearing a new coat. Take a man's name out of a book and you can take the man."

"So what do we do?" And here was the thing about Tavita that nine years of shared water had taught her: he did not ask *can we do anything*. He asked *what*, as if doing were already decided and only the shape of it remained.

Sefa looked down the line of boats. Forty hulls. Two hundred divers, boat-hands, mend-alls, gutters, menders of nets — the whole breathing economy of Saltmere's water. The army wanted pearls; the war across the water had made shell and pearl worth more than they had ever been, and Hale's requisitions said so plainly enough for anyone with ears. Without the fleet, the counting house earned nothing. Without the counting house, the ledger was paper.

"The boats don't go out," she said.

Tavita looked at her.

"We're slow," she said. "We're sick. We mourn. We wait for fair winds that don't come. Every day the fleet sits at anchor is a day the company bleeds money, and money is the only blood those men understand. We don't fight them. We don't raise a hand or a voice. We just — stop."

"And the ones they've already taken?"

"We get them back," she said, and heard how it sounded, how it rang against the harbor walls, too large and too bright, a promise made of nothing but wanting. She said it again anyway. "We get them back."

* * *

By noon the word had gone through the fleet the way word travels among people who cannot afford written things: mouth to ear, hand to shoulder, a look passed across a fish market like contraband. *No one puts out tomorrow. No one puts out the day after.* Old Maren, whose hands were so ruined by rope that she could no longer close them fully, gripped Sefa's wrist with those same ruined hands and said, "About time someone counted us instead." Rangi the netmender spat into the water when Sefa told him, which among his people was an oath. Even the boys who crewed the small boats — boys who earned nothing but shares and bruises — nodded with the terrible solemnity of the young agreeing to a war they have not yet learned the price of.

Sefa walked the quay from end to end twice that afternoon, and everywhere she went she felt it gathering, that thing larger than herself she had called into being with a sentence. She had organized dives before. She had organized the sharing-out when a boat's master died and his crew had no claim. But this was different. This was the first time in her life she had ever asked two hundred people to be disobedient together, and she felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders like a diving stone, familiar and enormous.

That was when she saw Coral.

He was at the counting-house window, of course, where he always was — a pale smudge behind glass, half-hidden by the ledger stacks, the way he had been half-hidden by everything for as long as she had known him. But today he was not looking down at his books. Today he was looking at her, and even across the distance of the street she could read his face, because she had spent ten years learning to read water and Coral Voss's face had always been a kind of water to her: still on top, moving underneath.

He looked frightened. Not for himself. She knew the difference. For her.

And beneath the fear — and this was the part that caught in her chest like a hook — beneath it, something that might have been warning, or might have been plea, pressed against the glass of the window he never opened for anyone.

She turned her back on him and kept walking.

* * *

He found her at dusk, of course. He was nothing if not patient, Coral Voss; he had the patience of men who spend their lives waiting for other people to finish speaking. She was checking the lashings on her own boat, running her palm along the gunwale seam in the blue hour when the harbor smelled of tar and cooling fish, and he appeared at the head of the slipway the way he appeared everywhere: quietly, apologetically, as if existence itself were a discourtesy he hoped she would forgive.

"You shouldn't be seen with me," she said without turning. "Not now."

"No," he agreed. He didn't move.

She straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs, and faced him. In the failing light he looked younger than his years and older than his face, both at once, a trick he had managed since boyhood. His arms hung at his sides like a man who has rehearsed a speech and forgotten all of it.

"Say it, then," she said. "Whatever you climbed down from your window to say. Say it and go."

"Sefa." Her name in his mouth always sounded strange, careful, like a foreign word he was afraid of breaking. "The slowdown. You have to stop it."

She laughed — one short bark, no humor in it, all the salt of the day in that single sound. "I have to. There it is. Ten years, Coral, and the company's shadow finally speaks, and his first word to me is *you have to*."

"It gives Hale what he needs." He came a step closer, and his voice dropped low and fast, the flood-tide under the calm. "Don't you see? He wants you to refuse. A refusal is evidence. A work stoppage is mutiny by their law, sedition by their statutes — he can arrest organizers instead of debtors, break the fleet one name at a time, and every name will be *justified* in the record. Right now you are inconvenient. Refuse, and you become guilty. There is a difference, Sefa, and it is the whole difference, and he built his career on knowing it."

"And what would you have us do instead? Dive?" She stepped toward him now, and the anger that had been banked all day came up off her like heat off a stone. "Dive and deliver and smile, and hand Tavita's share to the men who took him? Wait quietly while they read names out of that book until the book is empty and the water is fished by strangers? That's your strategy? Silence?"

"Yes," he said. "Silence. Because silence keeps people safe."

The words landed between them like a dropped knife.

"Safe," she repeated. Very quietly. Somewhere behind her a mooring halyard slapped its mast in the evening wind, counting time. "Whose safety do you buy with yours, Coral Voss? Not mine. Not Tavita's. You stood at that window all day and watched them take a man off his own deck, and your safety bought him nothing."

"That's not—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere she had never heard it go, down past the clerk's carefulness, down to something rawer. "You think I don't know what my silence costs? You think I keep these books because I love them? I keep them because keeping them is the only reason they haven't taken me, and being un-taken is the only position from which I can—"

"Can what?" she demanded. "What have you done, Coral, from your safe position? Name one thing. One thing in ten years that any of us could point to."

He opened his mouth.

And closed it.

And she watched — she would remember this all her life, the way divers remember the exact second the light goes out at depth — she watched the thing he had been about to say die in his throat, swallowed back down into whatever deep place he kept everything, and his face closed like a door.

"There's a copy of the census schedule coming from the fort tomorrow," he said instead, flat and quiet. "Hale will use it to check the fleet rolls. If your name shows a discrepancy — if anything about your entry draws attention — it won't be a question of debt. It will be fraud, and fraud goes to the magistrate, and the magistrate's answer to fraud is not labor. Do you understand me? Stop the slowdown. Please. For me, if not for yourself."

"For you." She tasted the words and found them thin. "Everything you ask is always for you, isn't it? Or is it for you to give away later, the way you give away everything else — slowly, secretly, in ways nobody can see or thank you for?"

His face flickered. Just once. Like light on the bottom, refraction, something true passing under something false.

"What do you mean by that?" he said.

But she didn't know yet — that was the torment of it, the hook of it. She had spoken from instinct, from ten years of watching a man carry weights she couldn't see, and instinct is not knowledge. "I don't know," she said honestly, and hated the honesty, and gave it anyway. "But I know you're hiding something, Coral Voss. You've been hiding something since before the soldiers came. And whatever it is, I hope to the deep water it's worth what it's costing the rest of us."

She picked up her palm and her mending rag and turned back to her boat, and after a long moment she heard his footsteps go up the slipway, slow, uneven, the steps of a man carrying something heavier than books.

* * *

That night the fleet kept faith. Lanterns burned low on forty decks, and no engine turned, no sail slid from its brails, and in the morning when the company's overseer came down to the quay bawling about lost tides he found two hundred people mending nets that did not need mending, slowly, in no hurry whatsoever, and not one of them would meet his eye.

Sefa sat on her gunwale and watched the counting house. Its windows were dark. Behind them, she knew, Coral Voss sat with his ledgers in his lamplight, deciding — and she found she could not guess which way the decision would fall, and that not-knowing frightened her more than the soldiers did.

Because she had seen his face when she asked what his silence had ever bought them. She had seen the door close. And doors that close that carefully, she knew, are doors with something behind them.

Across the dark harbor, a lantern appeared in the counting-house window — one flame, lifted high, then shaded again, quick as a signal.

Or a confession.
