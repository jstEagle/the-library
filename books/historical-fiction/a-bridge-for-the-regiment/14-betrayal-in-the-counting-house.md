## 14. Betrayal in the Counting House

The winter light came into the counting house at a slant, the way it always had, falling through the salt-hazed windows in long grey bars that lay across the floor like the shadows of bars that were not there. Coral Voss had spent ten years learning to move within those bars without disturbing them. He knew exactly how many steps it took to cross from the ink cabinet to the copying desk — eleven, if he kept his heels down — and he knew which floorboards spoke and which held their tongues, and he had long ago concluded that this knowledge was the whole of his inheritance from his father: how to pass through a room as though the room would not remember him.

He was grinding ink when Brannock came for him.

He heard the director's boots first — Brannock wore good boots, English boots, and they announced him the way a bell announces a church service, from several rooms away. Coral did not look up. In ten years he had learned that looking up invited conversation, and conversation invited questions, and questions were small knives that could, over time, cut a man out of his own life.

But Brannock stopped at his desk. That alone was wrong. Directors did not stop at clerks' desks. They summoned; they were not drawn.

"Voss." The voice was low, pitched under the hearing of the two soldiers who stood by the door with their bayoneted rifles stacked like firewood. "A word. My office. Now."

Coral capped the ink. Wiped his fingers on the rag he kept folded in eight precise quarters. Rose. Eleven steps to the door, and Brannock watching him take them with an expression Coral could not read — or rather, could read too well, because it was the expression of a man doing arithmetic, and Coral knew better than anyone alive what it meant when Director Brannock did arithmetic.

The office smelled of brandy and cold smoke. The shutters were half-closed against the wind off the harbor, and through the gap Coral could see the fleet at anchor, the pearling luggers riding low and patient, their sails furled like sleeping birds. Sefa's boat was third from the quay. He let his eyes find it and leave it, the way a man touches a talisman in his pocket.

Brannock closed the door. Did not sit behind his desk but leaned against its front edge, arms folded, which was worse. Behind the desk was authority. In front of it was only a man.

"You've been busy," Brannock said.

"Sir?"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip on wet canvas. Then, immediately, the warmth came back into his voice, the unctuous merchant's warmth Coral had watched him deploy on naval inspectors and company auditors for a decade. "Don't insult us both, Voss. I've been counting pages."

Coral kept his face still. It was the one skill he had practiced more than any other, stillness of the face, and he deployed it now with everything he had, which was considerable, which had always been considerable, and which he suddenly understood might be about to stop being worth anything at all.

"The army requisitioned this office," Brannock went on, conversationally, as though discussing freight rates. "They requisitioned my ledgers. And Corporal-Adjutant Hale has been sitting in my back room for three weeks with two corporals and a census list, going through them line by line like a man picking lice out of a seam." He paused. "Three weeks, Voss. Do you know what he found?"

* * *

Coral had known, of course. He had known since the night he finished the fair copy — four nights by candlelight, his wrist aching, his eyes swimming, every flagged page noted and memorized. He had known that Hale's red marks were closing around the forged indentures like a noose finding the shape of a neck. But there is a difference between knowing a tide is coming and feeling the water close over your head.

"He found," Brannock said, answering his own question, "that the master ledger is eleven pages lighter than the index says it should be. Or rather — that the ledger in his possession is complete, and yet certain entries have been amended, and the amendments are dated in ink that wasn't manufactured until after the dates they carry. He is a thorough man, our Corporal-Adjutant. Not clever, perhaps. But thorough. Thorough men are the most dangerous kind. Clever ones can be bought with something interesting. Thorough ones just keep digging."

Coral said nothing. Outside, a gull screamed once and was answered by another, farther off.

"Hale came to me yesterday evening," Brannock said. "Sat where you're standing, more or less. And he laid it all out on my desk. The Voss woman's entry — your Sefa, though I suppose she isn't yours, is she, whatever you think you've hidden — the manumission paper, the altered debt column, the whole architecture of it. He'd traced it back nine years. He said—" and here Brannock smiled, a thin, unhappy smile, the smile of a man handing over something heavy, "—he said the hand that made those alterations was professional. Practiced. The hand of someone who had done such work many times, for many entries. And then he asked me, very politely, who in my establishment had that hand."

The stove ticked in the corner. Coral counted the ticks. Four. Five.

"And what did you tell him, sir?" His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it.

"I told him," Brannock said, "that I would need a night to consider the matter."

And Coral understood, then, with the particular clarity of a man who has stepped onto a bridge and felt the first plank give, what was happening in this room. It was not an accusation. It was not even, quite, a betrayal — not yet. It was a market opening. Brannock was showing him the price of things.

"You're offering me escape papers," Coral said quietly. "In exchange for the pages."

Brannock's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "You always were quick. Yes. A steamer out of Saltmere on the twelfth, papers in a clean name, money enough to start over somewhere the company has no reach. In exchange, the originals of everything dangerous come to me tonight, and tomorrow morning I hand Hale a clerk — some clerk — who will hang for the forgeries. There's a boy in the customs house, half-witted, whose hand resembles the amendments well enough if you don't look hard. Nobody will look hard. Nobody ever looks hard at people like that, Voss. You of all people should know."

"And the divers." Coral made himself say it plainly. "Hale wants them conscripted. Deported. Their indentures are illegal and he means to make them legal by force instead."

"The divers," Brannock said, "are an entry in a book. You taught me that, in a way. Ten years of entries in your neat hand, and never once did I see you weep over one." He pushed off the desk and crossed to the window, and for a moment Coral saw his silhouette against the harbour light, and thought — absurdly, treacherously — that he looked tired. Old. "I have a wife in Meridian and a son at university and thirty-one years invested in this company. You have a ledger and a girl. Weigh it, Voss. You're the only man in this town qualified to weigh anything honestly."

* * *

Coral walked back through the counting house past the stacked rifles and the bored soldiers, and sat at his desk, and ground more ink that did not need grinding, and understood that the floor had gone out from under him.

That was the sensation, precisely. For ten years he had built his safety the way he had built everything else — invisibly, patiently, line by line. He was the quiet one. The indispensable one. No one summoned him; no one suspected him; the soldiers had occupied the building for weeks and looked through him as though he were furniture, which was exactly as he had arranged it. Invisibility was not an accident of his nature. It was a fortress, and he had mortared every stone himself.

And now the fortress was open ground. Hale had his name — not proof, perhaps, not yet, but a name is a beginning, and beginnings are all a thorough man needs. Brannock had offered him a bargain priced in other people's lives. And somewhere in the garrison, on a desk under a lamp, lay a page-count discrepancy that would grow, day by day, into a case.

He calculated, because calculation was all he had left. If Brannock handed him over, Brannock implicated himself — no director survives the prosecution of his own clerk. So Brannock's offer was real, but it was also desperate, and desperate men revise their terms. The steamer left on the twelfth. Today was the eighth. Which meant that whatever Coral intended to do — surrender, flee, or finish what he had started — he had four days in which Hale's suspicion and Brannock's fear would converge on him from opposite ends of the same street.

Four days. He had told himself weeks. He had planned in weeks — one more checkpoint to map, one more cargo to prepare, the fort bridge timed for the New Year garrison drills when the sentries doubled and dozed. Weeks had been a luxury. Weeks were over.

And beneath the arithmetic, colder than the arithmetic, another understanding settled into him like water into a boot: they had taken his name from him. Not his true name, which nobody in Saltmere had spoken in years anyway, but the name he had made of silence and diligence — the invisible man, the furniture, the clerk no one sees. Brannock had seen him. Hale was hunting him. From this morning forward, every room he entered would contain at least one person who knew exactly where to look.

He thought of Sefa on her boat, third from the quay, mending net in the flat afternoon light. He thought of her question weeks ago — *why do the soldiers copy names?* — and the lie he had given her, badly, and the way she had looked at him afterward, filing the lie away the way she filed everything, in the deep cold water of herself.

If he ran on the twelfth, she stayed bound. If he gave Brannock the pages, she stayed bound and some half-witted boy in the customs house hanged in his place. If he did nothing, Hale came for him within days, and the master ledger stayed under the adjutant's desk with every true name in it — every name the divers had carried across the sea and buried, every origin, every illegal indenture — ready to be read aloud as a list of the condemned.

There was a fourth thing. He had known there was a fourth thing since the night he began copying. It required the master ledger itself, not the copy sewn into a diving-weight bag, not the memorized structure, but the book, the physical book with Brannock's seal and the company's watermark, delivered across the fort bridge into magistrate territory before anyone knew it was gone.

It required theft. It required days he did not have. It required, possibly, a disgraced sergeant who owed him a truth about a drowned man.

The light in the windows was failing. Somewhere below, on the quay, the fleet's bells rang the change of watch, and Coral sat very still among the grey bars of light and felt, for the first time in ten years, entirely visible — and found, to his surprise, that beneath the terror there was something almost like relief.

He rose, capped the ink, took his coat from the peg.

Behind him, in the doorway of the counting house, Sergeant Ives was leaning against the frame with a bottle in one pocket and an expression like a man arriving to collect a debt.

"Clerk," Ives said. "We should talk about your father."
