## 16. The Original

Three days before the turn of the century, the garrison began to decorate its own captivity.

They strung lanterns along the barracks eaves — paper globes, red and gold, shipped up from the southern ports at ruinous expense — and by evening the fort's grey walls wore them like a woman trying on her daughter's festival clothes. The soldiers sang in the yard. Someone had procured a barrel of the director's best rum, and Director Brannock himself, pale and sweating since his interview with Hale, had signed the requisition for it with a hand that shook only slightly. The century was ending. Even an occupation wants to celebrate.

Coral Voss watched the lantern-light from the counting-house window and did the arithmetic that had kept him alive for ten years.

Hale had his name. Brannock had traded it — Coral had heard the shape of it through the counting-house wall two nights ago, Brannock's voice going high and conciliatory, *the clerk, Adjutant, the quiet one, he keeps the true books, ask him about the true books* — and after that there was no longer any question of weeks. Days, perhaps. Perhaps only hours, once Hale finished verifying what Brannock had sold him. The fair copy Coral had spent four nights making lay hidden in the false bottom of the ink cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was no longer enough. A copy, however perfect, was only ever testimony. Testimony could be disputed, dismissed, burned. What the naval magistrate needed — what would survive Hale's lawyers and the company's friends in the colonial office — was the original itself. The book with the company's own seal in its wax, the book whose pages had been initialed in Brannock's own hand across eleven years of illegal indentures, drowned divers written off as *equipment loss*, true names entered and struck through like small murders.

The book lay, at this moment, under the adjutant's desk.

Coral had watched Hale carry it there himself, eleven days ago, after the page-count discrepancy. He had watched the adjutant kneel — Hale, kneeling, an image Coral had not expected ever to see — and slide the ledger into a canvas dispatch case, and set the case flat beneath the desk, and place his own boot-heel upon it while he worked, as though the weight of his authority could be transferred through leather and pine into paper. Hale was not a careless man. But he was a man preparing for a transfer, and transfers meant inventories, and inventories meant the ledger would eventually be crated and shipped to the colonial office, where it would vanish into the archive the way Coral himself had vanished into the counting house. After that, no power on the coast could find it again.

So it had to be tonight, or one of the two nights remaining, while the garrison drank and sang and the lanterns made every sentry's shadow into a festival.

* * *

He had spent the afternoon being ordinary. This was the discipline of invisibility, and he performed it now with the devotion of a man saying last rites. He inked the day's entries in the duplicate ledger. He sharpened four pencils to identical points. He answered Brannock's questions in a voice pitched to boredom, and when Brannock's eyes slid away from him — guilt does that, Coral had learned; the guilty cannot hold the gaze of the people they have sold — Coral looked at the wall calendar and counted backward from the thirty-first.

At four o'clock the garrison cook sent over a plate of fried plantain for the counting-house staff, a New Year courtesy, and Coral ate his portion slowly while Brannock bolted his and left for the fort, where the officers were toasting the Queen's century. At five the light began to fail. At six the first lanterns came on, and the singing started, ragged and cheerful, and Coral sat alone in the counting house with a cold lamp and listened to his own heartbeat the way a diver listens for the bottom.

He had rehearsed the theft so many times that the rehearsal had worn a groove in him, and now he simply stepped into the groove.

First: the fair copy. He had made it not from memory alone but from the original's very grain — he had spent four nights with the book open under his hands, and his hands knew it the way Sefa's knew a shell's weight in dark water. He had matched the paper by steaming and pressing sheets from the company's own reserve stock, aged with cold tea and dried flat under the ledger press. He had matched the ink — the company's iron-gall, gone brown at the edges of the older entries — by mixing his own and letting it oxidize in the stoppered bottle for a week. He had matched the binding by cannibalizing a dead ledger from 1887, same binder, same stitch pattern, and he had sewn the sections himself by lamplight with a sailmaker's needle until his fingertips were pricked grey. He had even matched the errors: the smudge where a clerk in '93 had dragged his sleeve through wet ink; the torn corner on the August '96 page; the way the '94 entries ran slightly downhill, because the clerk who made them had been drunk for a season and Coral had never corrected him, had only recorded.

He had made a book that would pass for the original to anyone who had not lived inside the original for ten years.

Which meant it would pass for the original to Hale, who had lived inside it for eleven days.

Second: the carrying. The oilcloth bundle was too wide for his coat, so he had not tried his coat. Instead he had spent two evenings with the diving-weight bags that hung in the counting-house storeroom — the same kind of bag in which Sefa had once carried his copied pages through the harbor gate, though she did not know yet how much of her own freedom had traveled that road — and he had chosen one with a split seam at the bottom, and restitched it with waxed thread in a pattern that would hold a book and still look like a repair. The bag went over his shoulder now, heavy with its false ballast, and the weight of it against his hip felt like a hand.

Third: the route. The counting house stood forty paces from the fort's side door, the one the clerks used to deliver the daily returns. Forty paces, a door, a corridor, the adjutant's office. Hale would be at the officers' dinner — Coral had seen the seating list cross the counting house that morning, and Hale's name was third from the top, because ambition always sits near the head of the table. The office would be locked. Locks had never been Coral's difficulty; locks were simply another kind of record, and records could be read. He had the key pattern memorized since November, pressed into wax from the cleaning woman's ring while she gossiped in the yard.

What he could not rehearse was the moment after. The moment when the original came up out of the dark under the desk and into his hands, and the ten years of silence came up with it, and there was no groove left to step into because he had never done this before, would only ever do it once, and the once would either be nothing — the invisible man passing through the world's blind spot one last time — or it would be the loudest thing he had ever done, louder than any name read aloud on any quay.

* * *

The fort at night, decorated, was worse than the fort at night, armed.

The lanterns turned the corridors into something festive and wrong, red light pooling on whitewash, and the singing came through the walls muffled and rhythmic, like surf. Coral moved through it with the weight bag on his shoulder and a clipboard of blank requisition forms under his arm — the universal camouflage of clerks, the paper equivalent of a servant's downcast eyes. Two soldiers passed him near the mess and one said, "Evening, sir," and Coral said, "Evening," in the flat voice that made everyone look through him, and the soldier's eyes did exactly that, sliding off, snagging on nothing, moving on.

*Sir.* He almost laughed. Ten years of being furniture, and the furniture was carrying the whole fleet's freedom in a diving bag.

The side door was unlocked — festival laxity, or perhaps Hale's own confidence, because Hale had doubled the guard at the harbor gate and the customs house, the two checkpoints Coral had mapped in the autumn, and had left the fort itself feeling safe as a heart inside its ribs. The corridor beyond was empty. His own footsteps sounded enormous to him and he knew they were not; he knew, with the precision of a man who had spent a decade measuring silence, exactly how much noise a body makes in a stone corridor, and it was almost none.

The adjutant's office door. The wax impression had given him the key's secret months ago, and the lock turned with a small brass click that the singing swallowed whole.

He went in and closed the door behind him and stood in the dark, and let his eyes adjust, and let his heart slow, because a fast heart makes the hands fast and fast hands die.

The office smelled of Hale: boot-black and pencil shavings and the faint carbolic of the garrison soap, and under it, faintly, the sea-damp smell of old paper. The desk sat where it always sat, square to the window, and Coral did not go to it at once. He went first to the window and looked out at the yard, where the lanterns swung and a dozen soldiers stood in a ring around a fiddler, stamping the cold out of their boots, and he noted the sentry at the far corner — facing the gate, facing away — and the second sentry's post, empty, its man presumably among the dancers. Then he crossed to the desk.

He knelt. The absurdity of it — Hale kneeling to hide the book, Coral kneeling to take it, the two of them mirrored across eleven days like the same prayer said in opposite languages — did not escape him, and he let it steady him. Under the desk, against the back panel where the knee of a seated man would never strike it, the canvas dispatch case. He drew it out. It was heavier than he remembered. He set it on his knees in the dark and opened the buckles, and there it was: the master ledger, the company's black soul, its spine cracked white from eleven years of Brannock's thumb.

He did not open it. That was the discipline. Opening it here, in this room, under this roof, was the one thing his rehearsals had forbidden, because a man who opens the book starts to read it, and a man who reads it stops moving. He knew every page of it already. He had it in his hands the way Sefa had the sea, without needing to look.

He lifted the fair copy from the diving bag. It came out wrapped in oilcloth and he unwrapped it and held it a moment — his own work, his own forgery, the twin he had built stitch by stitch in a season of candle-nights — and then he slid it into the dispatch case in place of the original, buckled the straps with the same tension Hale used (he had watched the man's thumbs a hundred times; everything was watching, everything had always been watching), and set the case back against the desk's back panel, in the same position, rotated the same degree, its canvas dust-shadow undisturbed because he had thought of dust too, and brushed the floor with a clerk's soft duster before he knelt, and left no knee-prints in anything.

Then the original went into the diving bag, wrapped in oilcloth, and the bag went over his shoulder, and the weight of it was different now, and he understood that the difference was not in the bag.

He stood in Hale's dark office for the length of one full breath, and in that breath he let himself feel it — the whole enormous thing of it, the point of no return, the moment the invisible man stepped into the light whether anyone saw him or not. His father's name was in the bag. Sefa's true name was in the bag, and the forged entry that had bought her, and the eleven years of struck-through names that Hale would have used to fill a regiment with the drowned's orphans. It was all in the bag, and the bag was on his shoulder, and the singing in the yard had reached some chorus the soldiers all knew, and their voices rose together, and Coral Voss walked out of the adjutant's office and pulled the door shut behind him and locked it, gently, the way you close a book you have finally finished.

* * *

The corridor. Forty paces to the side door, and he made thirty-eight of them before the world ended quietly.

Boots. Not the dancers' stamping — real boots, unhurried, coming around the corner behind him, and Coral did not turn, did not quicken, because quickening is the one thing a guilty body does that innocence never does. He kept walking, clipboard under his arm, weight bag on his shoulder, a clerk going home on festival night.

"Voss."

Hale's voice. Hale's voice, in the corridor, on the night of the officers' dinner, and Coral turned with the smoothness of ten years' practice and found the adjutant standing in the lantern-light with his coat still on and a drop of wine on his cuff, and his eyes — clear, patient, the eyes of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice — resting on the diving bag.

"Adjutant." Coral's voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "The returns are finished. I was taking them—"

"I left the dinner." Hale came closer, unhurried. "Brannock was toasting the century. He was toasting rather a great deal, and saying rather a great deal, and I found I preferred the cold." He stopped an arm's length away, and his gaze went down to the bag, and stayed there, and Coral felt the ninety seconds of the harbor gate come back to him, the terror compressed, the soldier's hand in the ballast — except there had been no hand then, only the almost, and this was not an almost. This was Hale.

"Heavy bag," Hale said, "for a man carrying blank requisitions."

Coral heard himself say: "Ledger paper, sir. The duplicate volumes for the year-end audit. Director's orders — he wants them crated before the holiday."

It was a good lie. It was the best lie of his life, delivered in the flattest of his flat voices, and it would have worked on anyone in the world except the one man standing in front of him, because Hale had spent eleven days learning this ledger and eleven days learning its keeper, and hunters learn.

"Year-end audit," Hale repeated softly. "Crated." He looked at Coral for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes that was not suspicion exactly — suspicion had been there for weeks — something more like the moment a chess player sees the shape of the whole game at once and understands he has been in it longer than he knew. "You know," he said, "when I interviewed you in October, I wrote in my notes: *this one is either nothing at all, or everything.* I have been unable, for two months, to decide which."

"And which is it, sir?"

Hale almost smiled. "I'm on my way to my office," he said, "to fetch the master ledger. I mean to crate it tonight, in fact — the same instinct as your director's, it seems. The century turns, and things must be secured." He paused. "Walk with me as far as the corner, Voss. I find I like company in the cold."

And Coral Voss, with the original of the company's black soul heavy against his hip, walked beside the one man in Saltmere with the right and the reason to open that bag, one step, two, the lanterns swinging their red light across the whitewash, the singing rising in the yard, the side door forty paces gone behind him and the night beyond it holding the only road left in the world — and Hale said, without turning his head, in a voice as level and quiet as Coral's own:

"Tell me, Voss. When you made your copy of the ledger — and do stop looking at the bag, you'll give us both away — how did you manage the '96 watermark? I've had three experts on it since Tuesday, and none of them can say the book under my desk isn't the original. Which means either you are the finest forger on this coast, or the book I've been guarding for eleven days is worthless, and I confess I no longer care which. I only want to know how you did it."

The corridor stretched out ahead of them, red and gold, all the way to the dark.
