## 18. The Magistrate's Door

The road down from the fort bridge was the longest short distance of my life.

I had crossed it a hundred times as a boy — running errands for the counting house, carrying Brannock's letters to the telegraph office on the far side of the headland — and it had never once felt like this: like walking on the skin of a drum that someone was about to strike. Behind me Saltmere lay in its winter dark, the harbor lamps doubled and trebled in the still water, and somewhere among those lights Hale would be discovering, at this very hour or within the hour after, that the book beneath his desk was paper I had made and ink I had laid down with a copyist's patience over four candlelit nights. A fair copy. The forgery of my life. It would hold him for perhaps two days if he was careless, one day if he was not.

I did not run. Running draws the eye the way blood draws sharks. I walked, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest under my coat, its weight so familiar now that when I pressed my hand to my breastbone I could not always tell which heartbeat was mine and which was the book's. Ten years of entries. Ten years of true names written down by men who never once wrote their own honestly. My father's drowning report was in there, in Ives's careful clerk's hand, the falsified depth and the invented tangle of line. Sefa's indenture was in there, resting on the alteration I had made when we were both too young to understand what kindness costs. Every diver in the fleet was in there, named truly — Amara Kessiah from the Windward Islands, Tomas Reyes whose mother sold mussels on the north quay, old Beno who had forgotten more of the sea than the company ever learned — bound to Brannock's ledgers by signatures most of them could not read.

I carried them all through the cold, and the cold carried me.

* * *

The naval magistrate's station stood apart from the town the way an honest man stands apart from a crowd — slightly back, slightly apart, lit by its own lamps. It had been a merchant's house once, before the navy claimed it; you could still see the ghost of its former life in the fanlight above the door, the painted monogram of some shipping family half-scraped away, and in the garden wall where fig trees grew out of season, sheltered from the salt wind. Two marines flanked the door, greatcoated, their breath smoking in the lamplight, and beyond them, through the windows, I could see the warm yellow of occupied rooms: a clerk at a desk, another man moving with the unhurried authority of someone whose word ends arguments.

I stopped at the foot of the steps and made myself breathe.

This was the moment I had built the whole winter toward, and now that it stood in front of me, ordinary and lamp-lit and smelling faintly of woodsmoke, I found I was afraid in a way I had not been at any checkpoint. At the harbor gate I had feared discovery. Here I feared something worse: reception. That I would lay ten years of truth on this desk and be told, politely, in the language of procedure, that it changed nothing. That the company had friends. That names were only names until someone powerful agreed to read them.

"Name and business," said the nearer marine. He was young. His voice had not finished settling.

"Coral Voss," I said, and heard how strange it sounded, giving my name freely, in the open air, to a stranger with a rifle. "Record-keeper for the Saltmere Pearl and Trading Company. I have evidence to place before the magistrate concerning illegal indentures held by the company. I'm asking to make a deposition."

The young marine looked at his companion, and his companion looked at me, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind coming off the water and the creak of the fig branches in the walled garden.

"Wait here."

* * *

They kept me waiting forty minutes in the entry hall, which I understood to be a test, or a habit, or simply the way power breathes — slowly, and on its own schedule. I stood because sitting felt like surrendering my spine. The oilcloth bundle rode against my chest. Once, when a marine crossed the hall, I turned slightly so that my body stayed between him and the shape of the book under my coat. Old habit. The invisible man's choreography.

The hall itself told me things, the way rooms always do if you have spent a decade reading ledgers. The naval notices pinned by the door were dated within the week — fresh authority, recently arrived, not yet grown comfortable. The duty roster chalked on a slate showed three names struck through and rewritten, suggesting sickness or reassignment, a garrison stretched thin at year's end. And on the clerk's desk just inside, half-covered by a blotter, I saw the corner of a printed circular bearing the crest of the Pearl and Trading Company — a pearl in an open oyster shell, the same crest that had stared down at me from Brannock's office wall for ten years.

The company had friends here too. Or at least acquaintances. Or at least correspondence.

When the inner door opened at last, it was not the magistrate but a lieutenant — young, precise, with the particular neatness of a man who has never had to carry anything heavier than a pen — who looked me over and said, "You're the company man."

"I was the company man," I said. "Until tonight."

"That's what they all say." But he stepped aside. "Magistrate Ondine will see you. You'll speak when spoken to. You'll hand over whatever you're carrying when asked, and not before." He paused, and something almost human moved behind the precision. "And Mr. Voss — whatever this is, it had better be worth waking him at this hour on the last night of the century."

* * *

Magistrate Ondine received me in a room that smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular staleness of papers too long shut up with anxious men. He was older than I had imagined — sixty, perhaps, with a face like a coastline seen from a height, all erosion and persistence — and he sat behind a desk that was nearly bare, which told me he had either just arrived or just cleared it, and I guessed the latter. A single lamp burned between us. He did not offer me a chair.

"You are Coral Voss," he said. Not a question. "Company record-keeper. Ten years in the Saltmere counting house." He let that sit. "You'll forgive me if I observe that men in your position rarely come to me. Men in your position are usually the reason others cannot."

"Yes, sir."

"And yet here you are, on the thirty-first of December, past nine o'clock, telling my lieutenant you carry evidence of illegal indentures." He folded his hands. "Show me."

I unwrapped the oilcloth on his desk, and the ledger came into the lamplight, and I watched his face as he took it in — the size of it, the wear of it, the way the spine had been mended twice with different thread, the way the corners were soft as cloth from a decade of handling. He knew books. Whatever else he was, he knew what a working ledger looked like, and this one looked exactly like what it was: the true heart of a trading concern, opened every day for ten years.

"Master indenture register," I said. "Saltmere fleet. One hundred and fourteen divers entered across twelve years. For each: the name the company used, the name the person was born to, their port of origin, the terms of their contract, and the payments recorded against their debt." I turned to the first flagged page. "Except that the debts were constructed to be unpayable. Divers were charged for their own gear, their own food, the repair of boats they didn't damage, fines levied without hearing. When a debt approached payment, new charges were entered. I know because I entered some of them myself, under Director Brannock's instruction, and I can show you my hand beside his on every altered page."

Ondine's fingers rested on the cover but did not open it. "Altered pages," he repeated. "You're telling me the evidence is contaminated by your own admission."

"I'm telling you the contamination is documented, sir. That's better than clean evidence. Clean evidence can be called forged. This can be proven — entry by entry, date by date, in comparison against the customs manifests, the paymaster's duplicates, the parish registers at Saint Brendan's where the divers' true names appear in baptismal records. I've spent four nights preparing a concordance. It's in my coat, along with my sworn statement." I drew out the packet I had sewn together in the counting house while Sefa slept, and set it beside the ledger. "I am not bringing you a story, Magistrate. I am bringing you a cross-reference."

For the first time he looked at me — really looked, the way Hale had looked at me in the interview room, but with a different hunger. Hale had hunted discrepancies. This man was measuring whether I would survive cross-examination.

"The army holds the original registers," he said. "Requisitioned in November. Corporal-Adjutant Hale's garrison has custody."

"The army holds a ledger," I said. "As of tonight, it holds my fair copy. This is the master. I took it from under Adjutant Hale's desk during the New Year preparations, when the garrison's attention was on the fireworks stores." I heard how it sounded and did not soften it. "I stole it. I'll sign anything you like saying so. But I'd ask you to consider why a record-keeper with a protected position, a salary, and every reason to keep silent chose theft over silence. The answer is in the book. Page ninety-one. Sefa Maren, entered as debt-bound though her freedom was purchased six years ago — the purchase concealed by an entry I altered myself when I was nineteen years old and thought I was saving her. She has dived as a bonded woman for six years on the strength of my forgery. There are eleven others like her. Some of the entries aren't even forgeries. Some of these people were simply taken."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere outside, across the water, Saltmere's harbor bell rang the three-quarter hour, and I thought of Sefa on the quay, watching the road I had taken, keeping watch the way she had promised with her eyes if not her words — because we had not yet rebuilt enough trust for promises, only for this.

Ondine opened the ledger at last.

He read the way I imagine a surgeon operates: quickly, precisely, without sentiment, turning pages with two fingers, stopping at the entries I had marked, his lips moving once or twice around a name. When he reached page ninety-one he stopped for a long time. I did not look at the page. I looked at his hands, and I saw them go still in the way hands go still when they touch something that will require them to act.

"This name," he said. "Maren. There's a Maren woman who dives the deep beds off the point. The fleet speaks well of her."

"She's the finest diver in Saltmere," I said, "and she has been legally free since 1893."

He closed the book. He did not push it back toward me, and I noted that — noted it the way I noted everything now, the habit of a lifetime of small observations finally aimed at something that mattered. He left his hand flat on the cover, as if holding the book down against a wind only he could feel.

"Mr. Voss," he said, "you should understand what you've walked into. The Pearl and Trading Company holds supply contracts with three government departments. Its director dines with the colonial secretary. Its pearls pass through assayers who are paid in part from the same fund that pays my salary. If I accept this ledger into evidence, I begin a process that will not stay contained. It will reach people who will write letters about me, and about you, and those letters will be read by men who owe the company favors." He leaned back. "I am not telling you this to discourage you. I am telling you because you deserve to know the weight of what you're asking me to lift, and because I want to see whether you flinch."

"I've been flinching for ten years, sir," I said. "I'm done. What I want to know is whether you flinch."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might live in a man whose face had eroded past easy expression. "Hale's requisition gives me jurisdiction to inspect company records for irregularities," he said, half to himself. "If the master register shows the army's copy to be incomplete or tampered with, I can demand production of all subsidiary documents. Customs manifests. Paymaster duplicates. The parish registers you mention." His fingers tapped the cover, once, twice — the rhythm of a man counting clauses. "It would take a writ. Writs take time. Time is the one thing you may not have, if Hale discovers his ledger is paper."

"He'll discover it tomorrow," I said. "Perhaps tonight. He counts everything. It's what makes him dangerous."

"Then we should hope he counts slowly on New Year's Eve." Ondine rose, and for the first time offered me the chair, an odd little courtesy that undid me more than any kindness of the winter had — because it meant he believed me, and belief was a door I had been knocking on since November. "My clerk will take your deposition tonight, every word, before witnesses. Your concordance and statement enter the record under seal. In the morning — the first morning of the new century, God willing and the tides permitting — I will issue the writ and send it to Saltmere under marine escort." He looked at me over the lamp. "You understand that once your name enters that deposition, it cannot be withdrawn. Brannock will know. Hale will know. Whatever protection your invisibility bought you is spent the moment you sign."

"I signed my name on the first page of the statement," I said. "In full. Coral Ambrose Voss. I haven't written it out like that in ten years. It felt—" I searched for the word and found it. "Loud."

Ondine almost smiled again. "Good," he said. "Truth should be loud. It's the lie that needs to whisper."

* * *

The deposition took until nearly midnight. The clerk — a narrow, quick-handed man named Pell — wrote as I spoke, and I spoke for two hours, and the strangest thing was how easily it came. Ten years of silence had not rusted my voice; it had only been storing everything up. I gave them the structure of the ledger, the system of constructed debt, the dates of Brannock's instructions, the names of the clerks who had helped, the method of my copying, the route through the checkpoints, Ives's confession and his sacrifice on the fort bridge — Ives, who was at this hour in a cell under the garrison, arrested so that I could walk, and I made sure his name went into the record three times, spelled correctly, because a man who buys back his soul in small coin deserves at least to have the transaction witnessed.

Pell read it back to me. I signed. The nib scratched, and the sound of it was the loudest thing I had ever heard, louder than gunfire, louder than the sea, the small dry scratch of a name entering the world's accounting.

"There," Pell said, blotting it. "That's law now."

Outside, the sky over the harbor had begun to gather itself for midnight. I could hear the garrison's fireworks crews working on the far mole, the clank of mortar tubes being set, and the town below was lighting lamps in every window — the old custom, lights at the dying of the year, so the drowned could find their way home if they wished. I thought of my father out in that black water, and of Ives in his cell, and of Sefa standing watch on the quay, and I stood at the magistrate's window with my empty hands — empty for the first time in weeks, the ledger gone into Ondine's strongbox, its weight transferred but not lifted — and I waited for the year to die.

Ondine came to stand beside me. He had changed into his dress uniform, I noticed; whatever hesitation lived in him, he had decided to meet the new century in the clothes of his office rather than his dressing gown.

"My aide departs for the capital at dawn with dispatches," he said quietly. "Copies of everything. If Saltmere goes wrong — if Hale resists, if Brannock's friends move faster than my writ — the record survives in duplicate." He glanced at me. "You taught me that tonight, Mr. Voss. Never keep only one copy of the truth."

"Then you learned faster than I did," I said. "It took me ten years."

Below us, across the water, the first rocket went up — early, some impatient gunner's error or a fisherman's celebration — and burst green over the harbor, and its light ran across the water and up the walls of the counting house where I had spent my invisible decade, and for one suspended moment the whole town stood lit in that color, the quay and the fleet and the fort and the long road from the bridge, every window, every face turned upward.

Then Ondine's clerk appeared in the doorway, hat already on, breathless from the stairs.

"Sir — signal from the fort. Marine picket reports riders left the garrison twenty minutes ago, riding hard for the customs road. Adjutant Hale's own escort. They're carrying dispatches." Pell swallowed. "Addressed to the colonial secretary. Requesting authorization to seize all records in the magistrate's custody — dated tomorrow morning. He's asking permission to come for your strongbox, sir. Before the writ can issue."

The green light faded off the water. Ondine's face, in the lamplight, was the coastline again — eroded, persistent, unmoving.

"Then," he said, taking his coat from the chair, "we had best make sure the writ leaves this house before his does. Wake the escort, Mr. Pell. It seems the century won't wait for midnight after all."
