## 20. Reckoning

The marines came ashore at half past seven in the morning, and Saltmere woke to the sound of boots that did not belong to Hale's garrison.

Coral heard them from the counting house window — two longboats grinding up the shingle, forty men in blue with white crossbelts, an officer reading from a paper while gulls screamed over his head as though they had been paid to drown him out. The writs were nailed to the customs house door one by one, four sheets of heavy paper that lifted and fell in the wind like things trying to escape.

He had imagined this moment for eleven days — since the night he crossed the fort bridge with the master ledger pressed against his chest, since Ives turned to face the sentry and said *go on then, lad, don't look back* — and he had imagined it would feel like something breaking open. A door. A dam. A fist unclenching after ten years.

It did not feel like that. It felt like a Tuesday.

That was the first lesson of reckoning, Coral thought: it arrives dressed as administration. No trumpets. No tide of justice sweeping the quay. Just a naval magistrate's clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a list, and marines who did not know any of their names, and the same grey light on the same grey water.

* * *

Brannock was taken at nine o'clock, in his own office, behind his own desk.

Coral was not meant to be there. The marines had sealed the counting house at dawn and posted a corporal at the door, but the corporal was young and Coral still moved through buildings the way water moved through pilings — unnoticed, assumed, everywhere and nowhere. He let himself in through the records room, the way he had done ten thousand mornings, and stood among the shelves where the company's whole history slept in calfskin and string, and watched through the inner doorway as three marines read the warrant aloud.

Director Ambrose Brannock did not shout. Coral had expected shouting, or bargaining — Brannock had bargained his way through thirty years of salt and scandal, trading favors like a man shelling peas — but he only sat very straight in his chair with his hands flat on the blotter, listening to the charges the way he might listen to a quarterly report. Conspiracy to falsify instruments of indenture. Unlawful detention of persons. Fraud upon the Crown. The words fell into the room like stones into deep water, and Brannock's face gave back nothing but ripples.

When they took his arms, he looked past the marines and found Coral standing in the shadows of the records room, and for one long moment the two of them regarded each other across the office like men meeting on a bridge neither could retreat from.

There was no hatred in Brannock's eyes. That was the worst of it. There was only a kind of tired accounting — the look of a merchant totaling a loss he had known was coming, entering it neatly in the final column. His mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, Coral read the shape of it easily enough. He had spent ten years reading Brannock's lips through glass partitions and half-closed doors.

*You were mine,* the mouth said. Or perhaps: *I made you.*

Perhaps both. Both were true, and both were lies, and Coral found he had no answer worth shaping with his own lips. He simply stood there among the ledgers and let the director see him clearly for the first time — not the quiet one, not the invisible clerk, not the instrument — but a man counting.

They walked Brannock down the stairs with his coat still buttoned wrong, and out onto the quay, and the town came out to watch. They always did. Coral stayed at the window and watched too, and saw Sefa in the crowd near the fish sheds, her arms crossed, her chin high, not cheering as some of the divers were cheering but not turning away either. Watching the count being made. Making sure it was made honestly.

That, at least, they had never needed words to share.

* * *

The readings began at noon on the quay.

Magistrate Aldous Renwick — a spare, dry man with a voice like a file drawn slowly across iron — had chosen the quay deliberately, everyone understood that. Not the courthouse, with its benches for respectable citizens. The quay, where the fleet moored, where the divers landed their catch, where the company's tally board hung with its columns of debt that never shortened no matter how many pearls came up out of the dark. Renwick stood at the foot of the customs house steps with the master ledger open on a table before him — *his* table now, though Coral knew every stain and scratch of that wood — and began to read names.

Not ledger names. True names.

That was the thing Coral had fought eleven days to make happen, the thing that had cost him Ives, cost him his position, cost him whatever sheltered life he had built inside these walls, and hearing it done was stranger than all of it. Because a ledger entry said *Sefa, indentured diver, debt outstanding 14 pounds 6 shillings, origin: island registry, status: bound.* But Renwick's voice, pitched to carry across the water, said:

"Sefa Anahera Tuiwai. Indenture entered the ninth of March, eighteen ninety-one. Instrument examined. Instrument voided. Sefa Anahera Tuiwai is free."

And the name went out over the harbor the way a pearl goes up from the bottom — slowly, shining, pulled against its whole long weight by a thin line held in someone's steady hands.

The fleet had stopped work. Of course it had. Every boat was tied up, every diver on the stones, and they came forward one at a time or in small clumps when their names were called, and each time the little ceremony repeated itself: the reading, the examination — a marine handed up a sheaf of instruments and Renwick scanned them with his eyeglasses — the pronouncement, the voiding stamp brought down on the page with a sound like a knuckle rapped once on a door.

Free. Free. Free.

Coral stood at the edge of the crowd, well back, where the fish sheds threw their shadow, and told himself firmly that this was exactly where he belonged and exactly where he intended to stay. He had delivered the ledger; the magistrate's clerks had taken his testimony over three days in a room that smelled of lamp oil; his part was finished, and the proper posture for a man who had betrayed his employer was invisibility until the machinery ground fully to rest. Old habit. Old armor.

But the names kept coming, and the armor kept not working.

Because he knew what was under each name. That was the terrible intimacy of it. When Renwick read *"Matai Okoro, entered 1889"*, Coral saw the actual page — the smudge where the clerk before him had blotted the ink, the way Matai's age had been written *nineteen* in a hand that had first written *sixteen* and thought better of it, because nineteen was old enough to sign and sixteen was not. When he read *"Pelenato Vaeau, debt carried forward"*, Coral remembered carrying that debt forward himself, year after year, adding the interest Brannock dictated, watching the number grow like a barnacle on a hull no storm could scrape clean. He had written the chains. He had written them so beautifully. Even now, even knowing what the entries were, part of him ached at how clean his figures had been.

A woman beside him — old Mrs. Ferrow, who sold rope — muttered, "About time," and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and Coral realized his own eyes were burning and did nothing about it, because no one looked at Coral Voss, and today that was the only mercy left to him.

Then the reading reached the middle of the afternoon, and Renwick paused over a page, and Coral felt the pause in his chest before he understood it.

"Sefa Anahera Tuiwai." Her full name again, spoken a second time, because hers appeared twice in the master ledger — once as the bound girl of 1891, and once, six years later, in an entry Coral had made himself, alone, by lamplight, with his heart going like a hammer in his ears. "Second instrument. Manumission, dated the fourth of November, eighteen ninety-seven. Payment recorded: forty pounds."

Renwick looked up from the page. Looked out at the crowd. Found her, somehow, in all those faces — the small straight figure with her arms crossed.

"This instrument has been examined," he said, and here his dry voice did something almost gentle, "and found to be irregular. The payment recorded was never made. The signature certifying it belongs to a clerk of this company acting without authority." A rustle went through the crowd. "However. The underlying indenture has already been voided this day upon evidence of fraud in its original execution. The second instrument is therefore set aside entirely, and with it any question of debt. Sefa Anahera Tuiwai stands free, and stands free of the first date, not the last."

*Free of the first date.* As if the seven years had been returned to her. As if paper could do that.

Coral watched Sefa receive it. She did not cheer. She bowed her head once to the magistrate, formal as a salute, and then she turned — he saw it happen, saw her head come around, saw her eyes move along the crowd with a specific direction in them, toward the fish sheds, toward the shadows, toward him —

— and Coral stepped back, out of the light, and was gone before her gaze arrived. Cowardice or courtesy, he could not have said which. Perhaps by evening he would know. Perhaps he would never know, and that too would be a price, and he would pay it.

* * *

They voided forty-one indentures that day. Coral counted. Counting was the only prayer he had ever learned.

By dusk the tally board beside the fleet had been taken down by marines and thrown on a driftwood fire, and the company's flag over the counting house was struck, and someone — Coral suspected old Matai, who had waited twenty years and was owed this exact small pleasure — had nailed a fresh board in its place with a single sheet of paper on it, and on the paper, in a fair round hand copied from the magistrate's own list, were all forty-one true names, spelled properly, dated properly, belonging to no debt at all.

People stood before it in the falling light the way they stood before altars. Touching the corners of it. Reading their neighbors' names aloud to neighbors who couldn't read, so that the paper said itself over and over into the darkening air, a litany without a church.

Coral stood among them. That was all. He stood among them instead of behind the glass, instead of behind the counter, instead of behind the columns of figures that had been the walls of his whole adult life, and the strangeness of it was physical — like walking on a leg newly out of a splint, the ground arriving differently than expected. For ten years he had been the man who counted others. Now he stood in the counted crowd, one body among bodies, and no one deferred to him, and no one feared him, and no one needed anything from him at all, and the freedom of that was so close to loneliness that he could not tell the difference except by holding both feelings up to the light and looking very carefully.

Vindication, he decided, was an overrated word. It sounded like trumpets. What actually happened was that a stamp came down on wet paper forty-one times, and a fire burned a tally board, and the cold came off the harbor as it always did, and your father was still dead, and Ives was still in the fort cell awaiting his own reckoning, and the woman you loved had learned you had lied to her for years out of pity, and none of the stamps changed any of it. Justice, it turned out, did not mend things. It only stopped the wound from being made again. Mending was slower work, and quieter, and nobody wrote warrants for it.

An hour after full dark, a marine found him at the edge of the quay — Coral did not trouble to wonder anymore how people kept finding him; invisibility, apparently, was another thing the winter had spent.

"Lieutenant's compliments. Magistrate wants you tomorrow, nine sharp." The marine hesitated. "Not a charge, mind. He says — " the young man squinted at a folded paper, got it wrong, gave up, and paraphrased — "he says the company's books aren't the only books, and there's a matter of a manumission entry signed *C. Voss*, and whether Voss wants to say anything about it official-like, or leave it buried. His words. Buried."

Coral looked out at the water. Somewhere beyond the point, past the fort bridge where Ives had stood and turned and given himself up so a clerk could walk free, the black sea breathed against the rocks, patient as everything else.

An entry signed C. Voss. A forgery that had bought seven years of freedom for a diver who hadn't asked for it that way, and who had every right now to let it be dragged into the light and weighed, or to leave it buried with the rest of the drowned year.

Tomorrow he would stand in front of the magistrate again and answer for his own handwriting, among all the other reckonings. And whatever he chose to say, there was one person whose answer mattered more than the law's, and she had been looking for him on that quay, and he had run.

He turned toward the town, toward the lane above the fish sheds where a lamp sometimes burned late in a window he knew better than his own prayers, and started walking.

Time to stop hiding among the counted. Time to be counted himself.
