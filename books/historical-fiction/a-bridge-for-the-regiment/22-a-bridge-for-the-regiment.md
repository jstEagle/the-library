## 22. A Bridge for the Regiment

The school stands where the counting house used to be.

I chose the site deliberately, though I have never said so aloud. The old building was sold for salvage after the trials — its ledgers pulped, its iron safe broken open with hammers and left in the yard like a whale's skull — and for two years the lot sat empty, a rectangle of weeds and broken slate that the gulls used for a marketplace. When the money finally came through from the magistrate's fund, I asked for this ground and no other. There is a particular justice in teaching children to write on the floorboards where fear was once filed alphabetically.

It is a small school. Nine desks of salt-whitened pine, a stove that smokes when the wind comes out of the northeast, one window that looks down the hill toward the harbor. On the wall behind my own desk hangs no portrait of any governor or admiral, only a framed page of plain paper on which I have written, in letters large enough to read from the back bench: *Every record can be questioned. Every name can be wrong. Ask who wrote it, and why.*

The children think it is a strange motto. They are right. It is not a motto at all; it is a confession.

* * *

This morning's lesson is forgery.

I set before each of them a facsimile — I copied them myself over the winter, eleven sheets reproducing the hand of a company clerk named Merrick, may he rot in whatever mild purgatory his crimes earned him — and I ask them to find what is false. They bend over the pages like divers over the side of a boat, and I walk among them and say nothing. That is the method. I learned it from the silence of ten years, and I teach its opposite by means of it: I hold my tongue so they will not.

A girl named Petra — yes, I let her parents keep the name, though they chose it after reading the trial transcripts, which pleases me more than I can politely say — raises her hand first, as she always does.

"The ink is different," she says. "On the third line. It goes on thicker, like the pen was pressed harder."

"Why would a clerk press harder on one word?" I ask the room.

"Because he was writing something new," says Tam, the ferryman's son, who is twelve and has hands like knots of rope. "Over something old."

"Or because he was frightened," Petra adds. "My mother says your hand shakes when you're frightened, and a shaking hand presses too hard."

I look at her for a long moment. She looks back. She does not know — none of them know — that I once altered a single entry in a master ledger to buy a woman's freedom, and that the alteration was perfect in every particular except the pressure of the pen, which betrayed my fear the way a pulse betrays a lie. I have never told them. But every year I choose facsimiles with flaws of exactly that kind, and every year one of them finds it, and every year I go home and stand at my window looking at the harbor lights until Sefa asks me what I'm looking at, and I say, *the future,* and she laughs at me, and I let her.

"Good," I tell them. "Now the harder question. The entry you've found is false. What do you do with it?"

They argue. Some say burn it. Some say show it to everyone in the square. Tam says take it to the magistrate, and Petra says the magistrate might be bought, and the argument goes on past the bell, and I let it, because the bell belongs to me and I ring it late on forgery days.

What do you do with a false record? You do not destroy it, because destruction is also an edit, and the man who edits history owns it. You do not parade it, because a truth shouted once is a noise, and noises fade. You copy it, you witness it, you carry it across whatever bridges stand between you and the people empowered to act — and then you make sure the carrying is remembered longer than the lie.

That is the whole curriculum, if you want it in one sentence. The rest is penmanship.

* * *

At midday I walk down to the harbor, as I do most days, though there is no reason for it anymore except the walking itself. Saltmere has changed and has not changed. The company's colors no longer fly over the customs house — the customs house is a chandlery now, run by a widow named Kess who gives me coffee I don't drink and gossip I do — but the stones are the same stones, and the water is the same water, and the gulls still scream their ancient complaints about all of it.

The fleet puts out in the afternoon. The diving boats go out with the tide as they always did, but the men and women aboard them go out differently now, and you can see it from shore if you know what to look for. They stand straighter in the boats. They argue about prices openly, loudly, laughing. There is a divers' cooperative with a charter and a seal and a ledger of its own — a ledger kept, I am proud to say, by Tam's older sister, who was in my first class and audits it twice a year against three independent copies, because I taught her paranoia as a civic virtue.

Sefa dives still. Not for pearls — the pearl beds were part of the voided indentures, and the cooperative leases them now on terms she helped write — but because she says the water is where she thinks, and a person should think where she thinks best. I watched her go under today from the end of the quay. The water took her the way it takes light, without ceremony, and I stood there like a man of sixty instead of fifty-odd, doing what I have done for thirty years: watching Sefa and the sea negotiate, and being grateful to both.

She surfaced with her hair plastered flat and her fist raised, which means she found what she was looking for, whatever it was, and climbed the ladder grinning saltwater. "You'll ruin your eyes staring," she said.

"I run a school," I said. "My eyes were ruined years ago."

"By candlelight and other people's secrets." She wrung out her hair beside me, close enough that I could smell the cold under the salt. Thirty years, and she has never once let me forget what I kept from her, nor let me believe the keeping was unforgivable. That balance is the marriage, if you want to know. Most days it holds like good masonry.

* * *

The bridge.

You cannot live in Saltmere and not cross the fort bridge; it is the town's one throat, the single span between the garrison rock and the magistrate's jurisdiction beyond. In the old days we crossed it quickly and with our eyes down. Now there is a plaque.

It was put up eight years ago, paid for by subscription — the divers gave most of it, which is fitting, since they gave most of what was needed on the night itself — and it is not beautiful. Municipal bronze, municipal lettering, bolted to the stone abutment where every cart going over rattles it loose by a hair and the councilman's man comes out twice a year to tighten it. It reads:

**SERGEANT WILLIAM IVES  
OF THE 14TH COLONIAL REGIMENT  
WHO PAID THE TOLL OF TRUTH  
ON THIS BRIDGE  
IN THE LAST HOUR OF THE OLD CENTURY**

Ives spent four months in the garrison cells for what he did that night — obstructing a sentry, striking an officer, drunk and disorderly; the charge sheet was long and, in its way, honest — and Hale himself, to his lasting credit, testified at the hearing that Sergeant Ives's conduct, however irregular, had been in service of lawful process. The sentence was reduced. Ives lost his rank and his pension and gained, by his own account, the first clean sleep of his adult life.

He did not stay. He said Saltmere had too much water in it for a man who'd made his living drowning things on paper. He went east, took work as a wharf watchman, wrote me a letter every Christmas for eleven years, and then stopped writing, and when the letters stopped I knew without being told. I have the letters still, in a locked drawer with the things that matter. His hand was terrible. His spelling was worse. In the last one he wrote that he hoped the school was teaching the children *to count true*, which is how he would have put it, and I have stolen the phrase and use it every day, and I have never once credited him aloud, because he would have called me a sentimental fool, and he would have been right.

Children coming over the bridge touch the plaque. I have seen them do it — a little pat as they pass, the way you'd touch a doorpost of a house where something holy happened. They don't entirely know who Ives was. The details have softened into story. But the gesture survives the story, and maybe that is enough, and maybe it is better than enough: a whole town of people touching bronze in memory of a drunk who burned a false drowning report and stood in front of a sentry's lantern so a quiet clerk could cross with a stolen book under his coat.

If there is a better monument to the human race than that, I have not found it, and I have been looking for forty years.

* * *

Which brings me to the book.

I have started it seven times. I know because the false starts are in the drawer with Ives's letters, each one abandoned a few pages in, each one begun in some voice that wasn't mine — the memoirist's measured boom, the reformer's righteous heat, once, God help me, the novelist's lyricism, which lasted half a page before I heard Sefa's laughter in my head and burned it in the stove. The trouble was always the same. I had spent thirty years writing other people's names accurately and my own name never, and a man who has practiced invisibility that long does not simply step into the light because a chapter plan tells him to. The hand shakes. The ink goes on too thick.

But I am older now, and the shaking has become a kind of honesty in itself.

So tonight, after supper, after Sefa has gone up with the lamp and left me the fire and the small hours as she always does — she knows, she has always known, and she has never once hurried me — I sit down at my desk with a blank book, good paper, sewn binding, the kind that lasts. On the first page, where a book announces what it is, I write:

*A Bridge for the Regiment. Being a true account of the Saltmere ledgers, the winter of 1899–1900, and the people in them, set down by one who was there.*

And beneath it, where the author's name goes:

*Coral Voss.*

Only it isn't. It never was.

I have written that name ten thousand times — in receipt books, in crew manifests, on the school's charter, on the marriage lines. And every time, some small cold clerk in the back of my skull has signed it the way I signed everything for the company: correctly, legibly, and lying. My father's name was Voss, that much is true. But my mother came off a transport out of the eastern islands with a debt already attached to her passage, and the company clerks — it is all in the ledgers, I found the entries years ago, in the same hand as everything else, Merrick's careful, frightened hand — the clerks did not bother recording her name. They recorded her as *native woman, Voss household, indenture transferred.* I was born between those words. Coral, because of the reef; Voss, because the law required a surname and his was the one on the paper. The rest of what I am — the language she sang over my cradle, the names of her people, the shape of the prayer she taught me before she died — went unrecorded, and therefore, in the eyes of every book in Saltmere, never happened.

For thirty years I told myself the omission was the company's crime and not mine to repair. For thirty years that was true, and it was also a hiding place, and I have taught two hundred children to tell the difference between those two things.

So underneath my name — the borrowed one, the working one, the one Sefa married and my students call through the schoolroom door — I write the other one. Her name for me. The one she spoke into a squalling infant's ear on a dock nobody bothered to document, the one that survived thirty years of silence because it lived in one memory and one only, mine, carried the way Ives carried his guilt and I carried the ledger: across checkpoints, through winters, against all sense.

I write it slowly. The ink goes on too thick.

Then I sit back, and look at it, and find that the sky has gone grey at the edge of the window, and that my hand is steady now, and that the word on the page looks neither strange nor triumphant. It just looks true. Which is, I have come to believe, the only style worth mastering.

Tomorrow I will begin the second page, and the third, and I will get the names right — Sefa's, Ives's, Hale's, even Brannock's, even Merrick's, because a true account spares no one and flatters no one, least of all its author. I will write the parts that shame me. I will write the forged entry and the trembling pen. I will send the fair copy to the cooperative's archive and keep the original here, and when I die, the school will inherit both, and some child not yet born will open this book and question it, as I have taught her to, and check my arithmetic, and find — I pray — that every figure holds.

The fire is low. Upstairs, the floor creaks once as Sefa turns over, and settles.

Down in the harbor, the tide is turning, and somewhere past the fort bridge, in the dark, the water is remembering every name ever spoken over it, and keeping them all.
