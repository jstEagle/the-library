## 1. What Fits in One Trunk

The trunk lived under the bed, and Coral Okafor opened it every Sunday whether she needed to or not.

This was not sentiment. She would have said so plainly if anyone had asked, and no one ever asked, because no one ever came. It was practicality. A keeper's life was a life of transfers—three years here, four years there, a light on a rock, a light on a spit, a cottage that smelled of someone else's cooking when you arrived and never quite of your own by the time you left. Tomás had been moved seven times in nineteen years of service. She had learned early that the difference between a home and a posting was nothing more than how long you allowed yourself to believe otherwise.

So: the trunk. Every Sunday, after the lamp was trimmed and the log written up in her careful hand, she knelt on the cold flagstones of the bedroom and took inventory.

She began with the linens, because they were easiest. Two blankets, wool, grey. One quilt her mother had stitched before the crossing from Lagos to Liverpool, the stitches uneven now with age, some of them gone entirely, so that the quilt held together mostly out of stubbornness—which Coral considered a family trait and respected accordingly. The linens went in first, folded tight, because soft things cushioned hard things. That was a rule. She had rules for everything in the trunk. Rules were what stood between packing and mourning.

Then the books. There were only six, and she knew their weights the way other women knew the weights of their children. *A Treatise on the Illumination of Sea Coasts*, Tomás's, swollen with damp. A Bible she never read but could not leave behind, for reasons she did not examine. Two novels bought from the circulating library cart at Penzance in 1931 and never returned—the fine paid twice over in guilt. A slim volume of poems in Portuguese that had belonged to Tomás's mother, which Coral could not understand a word of and read anyway, aloud, sometimes, just to hear the shape of the language he'd dreamed in. And a tide almanac, current year, useless the moment December turned.

The kitchen things came next. One iron pan. One kettle. Four plates, though she needed one; three had come with her into the marriage and she had never decided which to part with, and now there was no one to decide with. Cutlery wrapped in cloth. A tin of salt, because salt kept, and because a trunk without salt seemed to her an admission of despair.

And then the bottom layer. The photographs, wrapped in oilcloth. Her mother's face, her brothers' faces, faces she had begun to hold wrong in memory, so that she checked them monthly like accounts. The certificate of Tomás's commendation, the one for the night in '29 when he'd gone down the tower steps into a flooding gallery and kept the mechanism turning with his bare hands until dawn. And beneath all of it, flat against the wood, the letters—but we will not speak of those yet. Even in the privacy of her own skull, Coral rationed herself.

She packed the trunk completely. That was the ritual's cruelty and its comfort both: she did not merely look at these things, she packed them, properly, as though the boat were coming tomorrow, as though the requisition officer were already climbing the path with his clipboard and his rubber stamp. She arranged every item according to the system she'd refined across twenty years of departures. Then she unpacked it again, item by item, back to its place in the house, and latched the empty trunk, and slid it under the bed.

Packed and unpacked. Ready and not going. It made no sense, and it was the most sensible thing she did all week.

* * *

On this particular Sunday—the second of September, wind rising out of the southwest in long grey shoulders—the typewriter stopped her.

It sat on the table under the front window of the cottage, where Tomás had left it, where it had sat for two years and four months while she dusted around it and never once touched it. An Underwood No. 5, black enamel gone chalky with salt-spray, the carriage seized, the keys standing at slightly different heights like teeth after a fight. He'd been typing something when he died. Not at the moment of dying—he'd had time enough for that, a whole winter of it, coughing his lungs loose in the bedroom while she boiled water and burned peat and pretended that nursing was the same as saving. But near the end he'd still worked at the machine, ten minutes at a stretch, two fingers hunting the letters, pausing to cough, resuming.

She had never read what he wrote. The page had still been in the platen when they carried him down to the boat, and afterward she couldn't bring herself to lift the carriage and take it out. Taking it out meant the sentence ended unfinished forever. Leaving it meant—what? That somewhere in the rusted works of that machine, Tomás Okafor was still mid-thought. Still about to say something.

Salt had gotten into everything by now. She could see it from across the room, a white bloom along the type bars like frost. In another year the thing would be solid, fused, a paperweight shaped like a voice.

*Practicality,* she told herself. *You should clean it. Oil it. Or sell it, or give it to Dara for his boy to pull apart.*

She put the kettle on instead.

* * *

The orders could come any day. Everyone knew it; the mainland papers had said as much since spring. Automation, they called it. Acetylene burners on timers, unattended lights, a relief crew visiting once a month to change tanks and scrape barnacles. *Economy measures.* The word *keeper* going the way of the word *lamplighter*, into the drawer where obsolete trades sleep. There were perhaps forty manned lighthouses left on the coast, and the Commission had published its intentions with the bland confidence of men who had never spent a storm night listening to a building argue for its life.

Coral had done the arithmetic a hundred times. Widow of a keeper, no children, fifty-one years old. They would pension her off with a paragraph and a railway warrant. She would pack the trunk—for true, this time—and be carried to the mainland, to some boarding house in Falmouth or Plymouth where landladies measured your comings and goings and called you *Mrs. Okafor* in voices like receipts. She would live another thirty years among people who had never heard the fog signal answer the fog.

Two years ago this prospect had felt like mercy. Two years ago, in the weeks after the funeral, she had wanted nothing so much as to be taken off this rock, away from the gallery where he'd worked and the stairs he'd climbed and the bed where the coughing had finally stopped. She had packed the trunk nightly then, kneeling in the dark, wanting the island gone from under her like a splinter.

But grief is a tide, and it goes out further than you think, and what it leaves exposed is not what you expected. What she found, when the first raw season passed, was this: she was good at the work. Better than good. The mechanism sang under her hands the way it had sung under his. She read weather in the color of the water before the barometer admitted anything. She kept the log so clean that the district inspector, a man who praised nothing, had written *exemplary* in the margin and then crossed it out, embarrassed, leaving only the ink ghost of it.

And there was no one else. That was the plain fact under all the other facts. If the light went automatic tomorrow, the last person who understood this rock—not the chart of it, the *thing* of it, its moods, its lies, the way it hid a reef in clear water and showed safe passage over a wreck—would be a woman in a boarding house remembering.

She was not ready to be remembered. She was barely ready to be here.

* * *

Sunday afternoon brought the wind up to a proper blow, and Coral climbed the ninety-two steps to the lantern room because the glass wanted wiping regardless of the weather, and because the climb was the closest thing she had to conversation with her husband. Ninety-two steps, each one a small argument with gravity, each one a step he had taken ten thousand times. At step sixty she always paused, at the landing where the wall held the old brass hook for the oil can, and let her breath come back, and said nothing out loud, and felt him there anyway, the way you feel a warm handprint on a cold rail.

From the gallery the sea was running slate-green with white manes on it, and the supply boat was late.

That registered slowly, the way important things do. The *Merry Alice* ran fortnightly, weather permitting, and the fortnight was up Friday, and Friday had been too rough, and Saturday, and today the swell looked murderous but passable. Mrs. Quill would be short of flour. Dara would be short of patience. The whole island—a rock, honestly, half a mile end to end, eleven souls counting her and not counting gulls—lived its small economy around that boat the way the cottages lived around the light.

She wiped the glass in overlapping strokes, inside and out where she could reach, and polished the lens with the chamois until the great bull's-eye rings threw their slow wheel of light across the darkening water. The lens was Heinrich's work—or rather, Heinrich's teaching, Tomás's hands. She thought of that every time she cleaned it: a German engineer and a Portuguese-English keeper, head bent together over the clockwork in 1923, arguing happily in broken English and diagrams drawn in the condensation, while she made tea for two men who spoke to each other better than either spoke to anyone.

Before. Everything worth remembering was filed under *before*.

She came down the steps slower than she went up. Age, she told the steps. The steps didn't argue.

* * *

The kettle had gone cold, so she lit the burner again, and while it heated she allowed herself the evening's one indulgence: she set out two cups.

Not three. Never three. Two—one for her, one for the space across the table, filled or unfilled depending on her courage that day. Tonight she filled both, because the wind was moaning in the chimney in a register Tomás had liked, low and conversational, and because the alternative to the second cup was thinking about the third one, the one she never set out, the one that had appeared in her inventory every Sunday for two years without ever being packed.

Four plates. Two cups. Three—

No. She drank her tea.

The wind strengthened through the evening. By full dark it was a proper September gale, the kind that made the tower hum a note below hearing, and Coral sat by the window with the lamp burning behind her and watched the sea try its old tricks on the rocks. She should have gone to bed. Instead she found herself doing the thing she forbade herself on windy nights, which was reading the water for a boat.

There. Or not there. A shadow where no shadow should be, out past the eastern skerry, moving against the swell rather than with it. A fishing boat could make that passage in this sea if its skipper were mad or desperate, and Dara was neither, and the *Merry Alice* was a steamer that kept well offshore.

The shadow vanished behind the skerry and did not reappear. Probably a wave. Probably her eyes, fifty-one years old and salt-scoured, reporting fictions. She finished the tea in the second cup, cold, out of discipline, and washed both cups and set them upside down on the rack, side by side, and told herself the day was closed.

But she lay awake a long time in the narrow bed, listening to the gale walk its rounds of the tower, and the thought that kept surfacing, patient as a buoy, was this: the supply boat was due, and supply boats sometimes carried more than flour. Inspectors. Requisition officers. Men with clipboards and rubber stamps and questions.

And underneath that thought, older and quieter, folded flat against the bottom of her mind the way certain letters were folded flat against the bottom of a certain trunk:

*When the man comes, whatever he asks—you will lie, Coral. You will lie beautifully, or you will ruin everything.*

She slept eventually. The light burned all night, steady as a promise, over water that held its secrets.
