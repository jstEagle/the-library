## 2. The Man with the Census Book

The supply boat came on a Tuesday, which was wrong, because the supply boat always came on Thursdays, and Coral Okafor had learned long ago that the things that arrived off-schedule were the things that mattered.

She saw it from the lamp room first—a gray hull pushing through a gray sea, no flag she could read at that distance, and behind it the usual gulls wheeling like torn paper. She counted the crates stacked on the deck. Paraffin, flour, tea if she was lucky, the glass mantles she'd ordered in September. And one man standing apart from the boatmen, holding a leather case against his chest the way a vicar holds a Bible in weather he doesn't trust.

She went down the ninety-one steps and put the kettle on before she put her coat on. That order of operations was deliberate. A woman who answers the door already pouring is a woman with nothing to hide and nowhere to go, and Coral had spent eleven months being exactly that woman—visible in the correct amounts, useful in the correct ways, forgettable everywhere else.

The boat bumped the jetty at half past ten. The man came ashore dry-shod, lifted across by the boatmen like something that mustn't be dropped, and stood a moment looking up at the tower while the wind tried his hat. He was younger than she expected. Forty, maybe, with the kind of face that would still be mistaken for thirty when he was sixty—the smooth, unweathered face of a man who worked indoors. His coat was good wool, city-cut, buttoned to the throat. The case was brown calfskin, scuffed only at one corner.

He climbed the path to the cottage and knocked twice, evenly, as though knocking were a procedure he'd been taught.

"Mrs. Okafor." Not a question. He knew her name before she gave it, and she filed that away where she filed everything now—in the drawer that had no bottom.

"You've the advantage of me."

"Elias Brandt." He produced a folded card from an inner pocket, held it out without quite offering it, and withdrew it again to consult it himself, which told her the card was not for her but for him—a script written down so he wouldn't deviate. "National Registration. I'm conducting the census for this district. The offshore lights were missed in the autumn enumeration." He said it as though the omission were the island's fault. "It will take twenty minutes. Perhaps thirty."

"The supply boat doesn't wait."

"I've asked it to."

*Of course you have.* "Then you'd better come in before the tea goes cold," she said, and stepped back from her own doorway, letting him into the house she had arranged like a sentence she'd rehearsed: Tomás's chair square to the hearth, Tomás's chronometer centered on the shelf, Tomás's typewriter under its oilcloth in the corner like furniture covered in a house someone has died in. Everything placed, nothing dusted more than necessary. Grief, properly staged, looks like tidiness.

He did not sit at first. He stood in the middle of the room with his case held against him and turned slowly, the way a buyer turns in a shop, and she watched his eyes do what eyes did—they snagged on the typewriter, stayed there half a second longer than on anything else, and moved on.

"Your husband?" he asked.

"Died last February. Eleven months ago."

"And the light? You keep it alone?"

"The Board pays me to keep it alone."

He looked at her then, directly, and she saw the assessment happen—her height, her hands, the color of her skin, the accent she had flattened years ago into something unplaceable. She had been looked at that way her whole life, in ports and offices and church pews, the arithmetic of *what is she* running behind people's eyes. It no longer stung. It was simply weather.

"Mrs. Okafor," he said, "I'll need particulars for each soul on the island. Name, age, occupation, place of birth, place of residence during the war."

"There are nine of us."

"Nine." He opened the case at last. Inside was a ledger bound in green cloth, a bottle of ink, a pen with a nib she could see was fine as a needle, and—she noted this without appearing to note it—a second book, slimmer, dark covers, kept beneath the first.

He caught her noticing. He smiled, mild as milk. "Names and addresses," he said. "That's all this is."

They began with Mrs. Quill at the shop, because Brandt wanted to work from the jetty inward, and Coral walked him through the village in a wind that smelled of iron. She gave him the island the way she gave supply boats their manifests—accurate, minimal, nothing volunteered. Dara Gillhook, fisherman, born here, here through the war, nets and engine trouble and a lame left arm from a winch. The Coyle brothers at the sheep station, silent men, harmless. Old Pethick, deaf as a post, who had fired at a German plane in 1916 with a fowling piece and told the story to anyone who stood still, including, at length, Brandt, who wrote it all down.

Coral watched him write. He had a fast hand, small and regular, and he did not look up while he wrote, which meant she couldn't tell what he chose to record and what he let fall. When she mentioned that Pethick's rifle had been confiscated by the coastguard in '15 and never returned, the pen paused—one beat, two—and continued.

At Mrs. Quill's they drank tea out of saucers and Brandt asked questions that sounded like kindness. Was the shop well stocked. Did the boat come often enough in winter. Whether anyone on the island was poorly, whether anyone needed to be on the mainland for treatment, whether any children had been born or died since the last enumeration. Mrs. Quill answered everything with the cheerful imprecision of a woman who has sold goods off the books for forty years, and Coral, watching, felt something almost like pride. The island closed around its business the way a whelk closes around its meat. You could knock all day.

But Brandt wasn't knocking. He was waiting. Coral understood that by the time they walked back up the hill, his stride easy beside hers, his case swinging lightly at his side. He had the patience of a man being paid by the year. He let silences run long and left gaps in conversation the way other men left baited hooks, and twice she nearly stepped into one—once about the wireless, once about whether the islanders had seen any vessels in the winter darkness that might have been running without lights.

"Might have been fish," she said.

"Might have been," he agreed pleasantly, and wrote something down anyway.

* * *

In the cottage he set the green ledger on the kitchen table, squared it, uncapped the ink, and turned to a page already ruled with her name at the top. That was the detail she disliked most. Her name, written in a hand she recognized as his, before he'd ever knocked.

"Coral Winifred Okafor. Born?"

"Cardiff. Eighteen ninety-four."

"Widow of Tomás Duarte Okafor, principal keeper." He didn't make it a question either. "Occupation: keeper of the light, provisional capacity." The pen scratched. "Place of residence during the war—1914 to 1918?"

"Here. This island. I've lived on this island nineteen years."

"All of the war years here."

"Every one. You can check the logbooks. Every entry's in my husband's hand until 1917 and mine after."

His pen stopped. "Why his until 1917 and yours after?"

Because Heinrich left in 1914 and my husband's heart broke a little more every year after and his hand shook by 1917, she thought, and said: "Rheumatism. Cold goes to the joints, in a tower like this."

Brandt nodded and wrote it down, and she understood that he had gotten something from her just then, though she couldn't say what. That was the trick of him. He took things the way the tide takes sand off a beach—grain by grain, invisibly, until one morning the shape of everything is different.

He ran through the rest quickly. Age, health, dependents—none—religion—she said none and he wrote it without blinking—languages.

And then he laid his pen down, crosswise on the blotter, which she would learn later was something he did when he wanted his hands empty and visible, and said:

"Your husband trained as an engineer before the war. Under a German firm."

The kettle was just coming to the boil in the next room and Coral listened to it the way you listen to a clock when you need somewhere to put your attention. *Don't fill the silence. Silence is free. Words cost.*

"He apprenticed with a firm that built optics," she said. "Lens grinding. Fresnel work. That was before I met him."

"And the engineer he studied under—what was his name?"

"It was a firm, not a man."

"A firm employs men, Mrs. Okafor." He said it gently. Everything about him was gentle; that was what made her hands cold inside her gloves. "I ask because we have records—shipping manifests, patent filings, correspondence between British harbormasters and Continental firms before the war. Your husband's name appears in some of them. In personal correspondence." He picked the pen up again, turned it once between his fingers. "In letters. Written in German."

"They were technical letters," Coral said. "Blueprints don't have a language. Neither does arithmetic. My husband learned a trade and learned the words for the tools. Half the words in a galley kitchen are French. Nobody asks the cook whom she's loyal to."

"Nobody does," Brandt agreed. "Where did *you* learn German?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, the wind had dropped the way it sometimes did at noon, a held breath between systems, and she could hear the surf on the far rocks and the gulls and her own blood.

There had been a plan for this. There in the drawer with no bottom, there had been a plan: *the harbor classes, Cardiff, before my marriage.* It was even partly true—two winters of evening classes in a room above a chandler's, a textbook with a cracked spine, conjugating verbs while the gas lamps hissed. She had kept that truth oiled and ready for years, the way Tomás kept the spare wicks.

But Brandt sat with his pen crosswise and his face open and patient, and something in her—the part of her that had drunk tea in three languages at one table while two men argued happily about prisms over the noise of the sea—something in her refused, at the last moment, to hand Heinrich over as a lie. Even as a small one. Even to save herself.

So instead she reached past the plan and pulled out the other truth, the duller one, and hoped it would hold.

"My husband practiced," she said. "Evenings, mostly. Reading aloud, working through the old manuals—he said if he stopped using it he'd lose it. I sat with him. You can't sit across a table from a man for nineteen years and not soak up the rain." She shrugged, and made the shrug careless. "I know the words for *lamp* and *gear train* and *more tea*. I couldn't order dinner in it. I couldn't read a newspaper."

It was a better answer than the harbor classes. It was warm where the classes were official. But she saw it land wrong the moment it left her—saw Brandt's stillness, the particular quality of his attention sharpening rather than relaxing, a man leaning in at a sound in the night.

"Reading aloud," he repeated. "To each other. In German."

"In the evenings. There was no wireless then, Mr. Brandt, and the foghorn gets tedious."

He wrote. He wrote for a long time, much longer than the answer deserved, and she watched the nib travel and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: whatever she gave him, he would weigh it. He had come to the island with her name already in his book, and every word she said was being fitted against something he already carried.

"Your husband's correspondent," he said, not looking up. "The engineer. Would you describe him as a friend of the family?"

"He was my husband's teacher. Teachers send Christmas cards. It's not conspiracy, it's gratitude."

"When did the correspondence end?"

The kettle screamed in the next room, merciful, and Coral rose to take it off the heat, buying herself the length of the room and back. When she returned with the pot he was watching her hands, so she made them steady.

"Letters stopped arriving," she said, "when wars start, Mr. Brandt. That's generally how it goes."

"Some letters continue," he said quietly. "Some people keep writing. Through neutral countries. Through Sweden, through Switzerland. The postal censors see a great deal of it." He poured his own tea, and his hand did not shake, and his voice stayed in the register of a man discussing the price of coal. "If such a letter ever came to this island—to you, or to Mrs. Quill, or to any of the nine souls I'm counting—it would be your duty to surrender it to the authorities. You understand that."

"I understand that reading other people's post is against the law too," she said. "Last I checked, both laws were still on the books."

Something crossed his face then—quick, gone before she could name it. Not offense. Closer to respect, and that frightened her more than anything else he'd done, because respect meant he found her worth the effort.

He finished his tea standing. At the door he paused, case in hand, and looked back at the oilcloth-covered shape in the corner.

"May I?" he asked, and without waiting lifted the cloth from the typewriter.

Salt air had gotten into it over the years despite the oilcloth; rust bloomed along the carriage rail like orange lichen. Brandt looked at it the way Mrs. Quill looked at flour—measuring, pricing.

"Underwood," he said. "American. They're good machines." He replaced the cloth, tucking its edge in neatly, and she thought: *he knows typewriters. Why does a census man know typewriters?*

"Thank you for the tea, Mrs. Okafor. I'll be on the mainland through Thursday. If anything occurs to you—any detail about the war years, the correspondence, names—" He produced a card at last and set it on the shelf beside the chronometer, squaring its edges to the wood. "Anything at all occurs to you, the mainland office holds my report until Friday."

Then he was gone, down the path, careful on the wet stone, and the boat took him away toward the mainland with the paraffin and the flour and the glass mantles, and Coral stood at the window until the gray hull dissolved into the gray water and could not have said which of them was being released.

* * *

Elias Brandt wrote up his notes on the crossing, braced against the wheelhouse bulkhead while the boat punched into the chop.

The green ledger held the official count. Nine souls, one light, occupations and ages and places of birth, all of it clean and complete and worthless. Nine souls was nothing. Any clerk could count nine souls.

The dark book under the green one held the rest.

*Okafor, Coral W., b. Cardiff 1894. Widow of T. D. Okafor, principal keeper, d. Feb. 1936. Resides alone, offshore light. Languages: English, Welsh (?), German—denies fluency, admits conversational exposure via husband. Husband apprenticed Continental optical firm pre-war; identity of instructor withheld (see file ref. K-114, harbormaster corr. 1909–13, letters in German signed "H."). Subject composed. Answers prompt but not forthcoming. Concealment assessed: moderate to significant.*

He read that last line twice and then, because no one was watching, added a word and struck it out again.

The trouble with Coral Okafor was that she lied well and badly at the same time. The lie itself—the husband practicing aloud evenings, the wife soaking up words across a table—was excellent. It was warm, it was plausible, it explained everything and implicated no one, and he had heard four variations of it in six months of coastal interviews, which was precisely why it failed. Liars converged on that story the way travelers converge on the same inn. But her delivery had a flaw in it, a hairline crack right through the glaze: she had hesitated a quarter-second before choosing it. He'd seen her reach for the prepared answer—there had been a flicker, a settling of the mouth, the micro-pause of a woman selecting from stock—and then, at the last instant, she had substituted something else. Something truer. People only improvised when the script offended them, and she had taken offense on behalf of someone. The teacher, presumably. *H.*

He flipped back through the dark book. Persons of interest, offshore district: a retired schoolmaster at Harrow Point who corresponded with his sister in Dresden; a scrap merchant whose ledgers didn't add; now the widow keeping a light at the edge of the map. Most of his files would die in a cabinet somewhere, weighed by no one, meaning nothing. He had begun to suspect that was the point of the work—not the finding, but the filing. A nation making lists so it could feel held.

Except this one. This one had a typewriter under a cloth and rust on its carriage, and a name in a harbormaster's file, and a woman who defended a German she claimed barely to know.

He looked up. The island was dissolving behind them into rain. He thought about the way she'd stood at her own stove with her back straight as the tower, pouring tea for the man who'd come to catch her, and he thought about the instruction he'd been given at the district office in October, delivered over sandwiches: *We're not after spies, Brandt, we're after reassurance. Count them, sort them, keep the files current.* Keep the files current. As if the country were a lighthouse and fear were the mechanism turning it.

The boatman handed him tea in a tin mug, thick enough to stand a spoon in.

"Keeper's a good woman," the boatman said, apropos of nothing. "Kept the light single-handed all winter, her and nobody."

"I'm sure she did," Elias said, and wrote nothing down, and marked, in the place where notes would have gone, that he had chosen not to.

The rain came aboard sideways. He closed the dark book, and then reopened it once, briefly, and beneath *concealment assessed* added a single line in his small regular hand:

*Return in spring. Ask about the daughter.*

He didn't know yet why he wrote *daughter*, or where the word had come from—only that the file reference K-114 contained one letter dated March 1914, its last page missing, cut cleanly away, and that in eleven months of reading other people's mail he had learned to notice the shape of what was removed.

The island sank out of sight. Elias drank his tea and watched the water, and somewhere behind him, in a tower going dark against the weather, a woman was climbing ninety-one steps to light a lamp for strangers in boats she'd never meet.

He envied her, a little. Then he put the feeling away with the book, and buttoned his coat, and let the mainland take him back.
