## 3. Letters Under the Lens Plate

The storm took three days to pass, and on the fourth morning Coral climbed the ninety-one steps to the lamp room with oil and a rag and the particular dread of a woman who has decided to do something she has been avoiding for a year.

She told herself it was maintenance. The lens needed cleaning after the gale; salt crusted the glass in a fine white bloom, and the clockwork that turned the apparatus had developed a stutter in its rotation, a hesitation every fourth second that Tomás would have heard from the bottom of the stairs. She carried the tools up because they needed carrying, and she did not think about the letter, and she thought about nothing else.

The lens was Tomás's cathedral. He had kept it the way other men kept their mothers' graves — swept, tended, spoken to. Even now she caught herself addressing it silently: *hold together, old girl.* A first-order Fresnel lens is a beehive of crystal, six hundred prisms stacked in rings around the flame, each one ground to a tolerance thinner than a coin's edge, and when the sun came through it at certain hours the whole lamp room filled with scattered light, rainbows sliding across the walls like something alive. Tomás used to say you could read a book by the fragments. He used to say a great many things.

Coral set down her rag. She checked the gallery door — bolted. She checked the stairwell — empty. Then she knelt at the base of the pedestal, where the brass housing met the iron column, and pressed the hidden catch that only two living people knew about.

The plate swung open.

Inside lay an oilcloth packet, dry as bone, exactly where she had sealed it fourteen months ago. She did not take it out immediately. She looked at her own hands hovering over it, hands that had signed her husband's death certificate with a steadiness the doctor had praised, hands that packed and unpacked a trunk until the leather hinges wore soft. Her hands were shaking now, over paper.

*You're being foolish,* she told herself. *It's just ink.*

But ink was the most dangerous substance on this island. Ink was what the census man wrote with. Ink was what turned neighbors into informants and names into files. She had spent a year becoming no one — the widow who kept the light, who asked for nothing, whose German died with her husband as far as any record showed. And all that time, under the brightest thing for forty miles, sat a folder of evidence that could hang her.

She took it out anyway. She had always known she would.

* * *

The packet held eleven letters. She had counted them a hundred times without reading them again, counting being a form of touching. Now she carried them down to the kitchen, built the fire up against the damp, and laid them out on the scrubbed table in order of date, oldest first, like laying out instruments before surgery.

The earliest was from 1923, written in a hand so upright and patient it made her smile even now. Heinrich Vogel wrote English the way he did everything — precisely, formally, with occasional small catastrophes of idiom. *Dear Thomas,* the first letter began. *I hope this finds you well and your wife also. I have been transferred to the works at Bremerhaven but I think often of the island and of the winter we nearly froze and of your stubbornness regarding the governor mechanism, which I still maintain was wrong, although the light never failed, so perhaps the sea is the final engineer and we are both apprentices.*

Tomás Okafor had come to the light in 1919, a young keeper with strong shoulders and a stammer he only lost when talking about machinery. Heinrich arrived in 1921, seconded from the German firm that had manufactured the lens, sent to oversee its installation and staying nine months past his orders because the installation kept going wrong and then going right in ways he couldn't bear to leave unexplained. For those nine months the two men worked sixteen-hour days and argued constantly and became, in the way men sometimes do through shared labor rather than shared words, inseparable.

Coral had been twenty-four that first winter. She remembered Heinrich as a tall stooped figure in a coat too thin for the Atlantic, forever cold, forever delighted. He taught Tomás the deep grammar of the lens — why the central drum mattered more than all the outer rings, how to lap a bearing with rouge and patience, how to listen to the clockwork the way a doctor listens to a chest. Tomás taught him to fish, badly; taught him to drink tea instead of coffee, reluctantly; taught him the island's name-days and which families could be trusted in weather. And on the long nights between their shifts, while Coral mended nets or read aloud, the two of them talked about building things — bridges, mostly, though neither of them ever built one. That was the joke. Two lighthouse men dreaming of bridges. *A bridge needs two shores,* Heinrich said once, gesturing with his pipe at the black water. *That is the whole difficulty. Everyone wants to stand on their own side and wave.*

When the firm recalled him in 1922, Tomás walked him down to the landing stage in fog so thick they navigated by shouting, and the last thing Coral saw of Heinrich Vogel was his hat blowing off into the water and Tomás laughing so hard he had to sit down on the wet stone.

The letters came after, two or three a year, then more often. They were full of machinery and weather and small domestic news — Heinrich married in 1926, a girl named Marta who "forgives my correspondence the way one forgives an old dog"; a daughter born in 1928, Greta, whom he described in a letter Tomás read four times aloud: *She watched the lamp for one hour without blinking. I believe she is an engineer already. God help us all.*

Then 1930, and the letters changed. Not in affection — in weight. *Times here are becoming strange,* Heinrich wrote that autumn. *Men march in the streets wearing anger like uniforms. Do not worry for me. But keep our letters safe, Thomas. Some day the world may be embarrassed by them and some day after that it will be proud again.*

By 1934 the letters were coded in plain sight — talk of weather, of shipping, of mutual acquaintances — and Coral understood without being told that the friendship itself had become contraband. The last letter before the war came in the spring of 1939's predecessor year, and then silence, eighteen months of it, until the war made silence official.

And then, fourteen months ago, three weeks after they buried Tomás — heart, sudden, at the foot of the stairs he'd climbed ten thousand times — a letter had come inside a double envelope addressed in a stranger's hand, posted from a neutral port, containing a single sheet that Coral had read once and then sealed away because reading it felt like pressing on a wound to see if it still bled.

She pressed on it now.

*Dear Coral,* it began. No preamble. His formal English had broken down entirely, which frightened her more than anything else in it.

*I do not know if this reaches you or if you are permitted to answer. I will write as if you can hear me. Tomás was my brother in every way that matters and you were his heart and I have no right to ask anything of either of you and I am asking everything.*

*Marta is dead. Two years now. Greta is seventeen and they have taken notice of me — my work, my letters abroad, my daughter's parentage, which is not what the new law wants a child to be. There is a list. I am on it, and children of men on lists inherit the list.*

*I cannot protect her. I have found a way to send her out by fishing boat when the season allows — there is a captain who runs quiet cargo and owes me a debt older than this government. When the storms break, she goes. I am sending her to you because you are the only address left in the world that I trust and because Tomás, if he were alive, would not hesitate one second, and I know grief does not pause for logic but I am asking you to let him answer for himself.*

*Keep her. Hide her. Teach her the light if you wish — she will learn faster than I did. I cannot say where I will be. I cannot say goodbye properly because proper goodbyes are dangerous now. Tell her about the bridge. Tell her her father stood on his shore and waved.*

*Your friend in everything,
Heinrich*

Coral sat at the table for a long time. The fire ticked. Outside, the aftermath sea ran long and green, the kind of swell that meant more weather queuing behind it.

She had read the letter once, fourteen months ago, and put it under the lens, and told herself the storms would never break, that no boat would come, that the whole thing was the desperate arithmetic of a drowning man — *if I save the letter, I haven't refused.* She had become expert at that arithmetic. It was the same sum she performed nightly with the trunk: pack it, and packing becomes preparation, and preparation becomes intention, and intention never has to become action because tomorrow she'll unpack it again.

The letter was a promise someone else had made on her behalf. Dead Tomás answering for himself. She had buried him; how could he accept a child?

But he had accepted Heinrich. That was the thing she kept circling. Twenty years of letters proved it. *My brother in every way that matters.* She got up, went to the parlor, and took down the box of Tomás's effects she was supposed to be sorting for the requisition officer — papers, mostly, which the officer would want to see, which the censor would certainly want to see. At the bottom, folded into a notebook of tide calculations, was a single typed page, half-finished, the ribbon faded:

*Of all the things the sea has given me, the strangest is a brother who speaks another language and stands on another shore. H. says a bridge needs two ends. I begin to think he was describing us.*

She put it back. She put the letters back in their packet. She climbed the ninety-one steps with steady legs and knelt at the pedestal and slid the oilcloth into its slot and closed the plate until the catch clicked, soft as a held breath.

Then she stayed there a moment, her palm flat on warm brass, and did the arithmetic one final time, honestly, without the trick of it.

If a boat came, and a girl stepped off it, and Coral turned her away — that was a choice too. Refusal was also an action. She had known this for fourteen months and had filed it under *later*, and *later*, she understood now, standing above the sea that had already taken one husband, was not a place. Later was a country across the water, and boats were leaving for it every day, and one of them might already be loading.

Above her head the great lens turned, throwing its white blade out across the grey, six hundred pieces of glass from a German factory carrying light to fishermen who would never know or care where it was made. It had always struck her as the loneliest kind of bridge — one end anchored here, the other end nowhere, shining at the dark on faith alone.

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, picked up the rag, and began, methodically, to clean the glass.

She had just finished the third ring of prisms when she heard it: an engine, low and wrong for the supply boat, throttling down somewhere off the northern point where no honest vessel came.

Coral went very still among the rainbows.

Through the lantern glass she could see it — a dark hull running without lights toward the lee of the rocks, riding low, riding quiet.

Storms didn't break for another month.

Someone had come early.
