## 4. Inventory of a Marriage

The requisition officer wanted three lists. Effects of value to the Crown; effects of sentimental value only; effects of no value whatsoever. Coral had copied the categories onto the back of an old tide table and pinned it above Tomás's desk, and for four days she had done nothing but look at it while the gulls argued on the roof.

It was the third list that defeated her.

The first was easy enough. The brass sextant. The chronometer, still keeping perfect time in its case like a heart in a jar. Two pairs of sea boots, the good binoculars, the barometer he'd polished every Sunday until you could read your face in it. She wrote these in her careful schoolroom hand and felt nothing, or told herself she felt nothing, which amounted to the same posture.

The second list was shorter than anyone would have guessed. His wedding ring. That was all. A marriage of eleven years, and when she asked herself what she could not let the government have, the answer came back one circle of gold, thin as wire now from years of rope work.

Everything else fell into the third category by default, and that was where the trouble began. Because the third category — *no value whatsoever* — contained his coat with the mended elbow. It contained his pipe, gone bitter with tar. It contained forty-one empty ink bottles he'd never thrown away because, he said, a man who threw away bottles was a man preparing to stop writing, and he did not intend to stop. It contained, folded into the drawer beneath the typewriter, ninety pages of foolscap covered in his slanted, urgent hand, half of them typed over clean, half of them not.

She had known the pages were there. She had known for two years. Knowing a thing is in a drawer is different from taking it out.

On the fifth morning, with rain coming sideways off the Atlantic and the light turning above her in its slow patient rotation, Coral took the pages out and carried them to the kitchen table and sat with them the way you sit with something that might be sleeping or might be dead.

*For my brother,* the first page said. *Who taught me that a light is a promise, and my wife, who taught me that a promise can be kept alone.*

Her own name. Eleven years gone and he could still reach up out of the paper and take her hand.

She poured tea she didn't drink and began at the beginning.

He had written it as a memoir, or what passes for a memoir in a man who lived thirty-eight years on one rock. *I was born in the same room in which I expect to die, this being the ambition of most islanders.* She laughed out loud at that, once, sharp, and the laugh frightened her. The prose was better than she'd expected. Tomás had been shy with his voice in life, deferring always to Heinrich's fluency, to her own quicker tongue, but on the page he was direct and dry and occasionally very funny, and she thought: *you were going to give me this. You were writing me a book and you didn't tell me.*

Then page nineteen, and the laugh died in her throat.

*People ask why a German engineer and an island keeper became friends, as if friendship required a customs declaration. Heinrich came in '23 to refit the lens and stayed eight months because the weather would not permit him to leave and then because he could not bear to go. We argued about optics in four languages, two of them invented. I called him brother before either of us had sisters or brothers living, and if the word embarrassed him he never showed it. When the war comes again — and men like Heinrich know it will come, they can hear it in the factories — I want it written down somewhere that I loved a German once, and he loved this light, and neither of those facts cancels the other.*

Nineteen twenty-three. She had been new to the island then, married less than a year, suspicious of the tall foreigner with the precise hands who drank his tea black and pronounced lighthouse as *leuchtturm* under his breath when he thought no one was listening. By spring she had stopped being suspicious. By the time the refit was done she had learned more German from him than Tomás ever had — kitchen German, weather German, the German for *hold that steady* and *not so tight* and *come and see the moon on the water.*

And after the war began its long slow gathering, after Heinrich went home in '34 with promises to write, the letters had come through her, addressed to both of them but written in English, deliberately, plainly, in the flat unremarkable English of a man who knew his mail might be read. She had hidden them inside the lens housing because it was the one place on earth no stranger would open without her standing beside him. She had not known Tomás was writing back at length. She had not known he was building a document.

*Evidence,* she said aloud, and the word sat on the table among the teacups looking exactly as dangerous as it was.

Because here was the arithmetic of 1937. A memoir naming a German national as brother, written in a keeper's hand, found in a keeper's house on an island the Admiralty already considered strategically tender — that was not memory anymore. That was material. It could hang him posthumously. It could hang her presently. It could hang whoever else got named in the remaining seventy pages.

Or it could save them. She turned the thought over slowly, the way Tomás used to turn a fouled fitting over, looking for the thread. If the day ever came when someone in a uniform asked her to prove where her loyalties lay, here were ninety pages proving it: proof that the German connection was peacetime, professional, human; proof that the letters were engineering and family news, not codes and rendezvous; proof that love between nations had existed once and might exist again. A censor would call it treason. A tribunal, a fair one, might call it testimony.

If there was a fair tribunal. If the reader was fair. If the wind blew the right way.

She read the whole thing twice that afternoon while the rain worked at the windows. Then she did what she had not let herself do in two years: she sat down at Tomás's typewriter, put a fresh sheet in, and typed the inventory lists properly for the officer, and when she reached the third category she paused with her fingers over the keys for a long moment, listening to the light turn above her, and typed:

*Assorted papers. Personal correspondence. Value: private.*

Not *none*. Not *sentimental*. *Private*. A word with a locked door in it. Let some requisition clerk chew on that.

Then she took the memoir downstairs to the lens housing, and Heinrich's last letter out from behind the counterweight where it lived, and laid them together in the space between the red glass panels where only a keeper's hands could reach, and closed it up, and stood for a moment with her palm flat against the warm brass as though taking a pulse.

Memory as burden, she understood. She had carried it two years. But evidence — evidence needed a reader worth trusting, and she did not yet know who that would be.

* * *

Elias Brandt came back nine days later, on the supply boat, in weather too fair to blame for delay. He stood at the jetty with his census case under his arm and his collar up against a wind that smelled of nothing but clean cold sea, and Coral met him on the path with her hands in her pockets and her face arranged into the mild blankness she wore for officials.

"Mrs. Okafor. I'm verifying figures." He said it the way a man says a line he has rehearsed and half disbelieves. "Head Office wants confirmation before the district rolls are consolidated."

"You counted six souls," she said. "You'll find six."

"Islanders move. Fishermen especially." He fell into step beside her on the climb, matching her pace without appearing to try, which she noted and resented. "Dara Quill was logged at his mother's house in April. He sleeps at the harbour now. Small things like that rot a record from inside."

"Then you're a gardener as well as a counter."

Something moved across his face — she would have called it surprise, on a less controlled man. "I hadn't thought of it that way."

"Most men don't think of their work at all. They just do it and describe it afterwards however flatters them."

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that he had heard the warning in it and filed it. He was not a stupid man. She had hoped he would be.

At the cottage she made tea because not making tea would itself be a statement, and statements were currency and she was learning to hoard. He sat in Tomás's chair without knowing he was sitting in it, and she watched his hands — pale, precise, a clerk's hands, but steady — and thought of the other pair of hands that had held that census book's cousin, the engineer's logbook, in this same room, in this same light, thirteen years ago. Hands were honest. Elias Brandt's wanted to be honest. She put that observation in her own private inventory and gave it no value whatsoever, yet.

"The figures," he said, opening the book across his knee. "Six residents. One dwelling occupied, one store, one light maintained by —" he glanced up — "yourself. Sole keeper."

"Sole keeper since March of '35."

"That's unusual. A woman holding a light alone."

"It's unusual a government noticed."

His pen hovered. Then, quietly, he wrote it down — *sole keeper, since March '35* — and she realised he was recording not the anomaly but her answer to it, and that the difference between those two things was the whole of his job.

"The requisition officer's been," he said. Not quite a question.

"He has. Lists are typed and ready."

"I'll need to see them. Property figures cross-reference with the census." He said it smoothly, but she caught the seam in the sentence, the place where a second purpose was sewn into the first. Cross-referencing property meant reading what she'd written about Tomás's papers. Reading the papers meant asking about them. And asking about them was the question he had actually come to ask, wearing verification as a coat.

She fetched the folder and set it on the table between them, equidistant, like a chessboard.

He read quickly, pen ticking items. Sextant. Chronometer. Barometer. Ring. Then the third list, and his pen slowed, and she poured his tea with both hands so she would have an excuse not to watch him.

"Assorted papers," he read. "Personal correspondence." He looked up. "Correspondence with whom?"

"Family."

"You wrote on your schedule that you have none living."

"His family, then." She nodded at the chair he sat in, at the typewriter rusting gently under its cloth. "A man writes to people. Mine is a small household but it was never empty."

The silence stretched. Outside, the gull dropped a crab on the rocks, once, twice, until it broke.

"Mrs. Okafor." He closed the folder but did not slide it back. "When we spoke in September, I asked where you learned German. You told me you studied it from a grammar book during the last war, for the intercept work." He set the book flat on his knee. "There was no intercept station within two hundred miles of this island. I checked."

The fire ticked. Above them the light turned, and turned, and turned.

"So you see my difficulty," he went on, and his voice had lost none of its mildness, which somehow made it worse. "I count things for a living. And there is a German word in my book next to your name, and a grammar-book story next to it that does not add up, and I am obliged to send the sum to Head Office whether or not I believe it. What I am *not* obliged to do," and here he paused, and looked at the typewriter under its cloth, and looked back at her, "is send it today."

"Mr. Brandt."

"Elias." Almost a wince. "Please. My mother was from Dresden. I'd rather you didn't make me a *Mr. Brandt* today."

That landed harder than any interrogation could have. She sat down slowly opposite him, and for a moment neither of them spoke, two careful people across a table with a folder between them, each waiting to see which of them would break form first.

"Why tell me that?" she said finally.

"Because you lie well," he said simply, "and I've met few enough people who do, and I think you're lying to protect something, not to harm anything. Those are different kinds of lies. The department doesn't distinguish. I'm trying to."

"And if what I'm protecting would look like harm to the department?"

"Then we'd both be wiser if it stayed in a drawer."

She held his eyes and did not move, and was aware of the lens housing below them, one floor down, holding its paper secret in the dark like a heart holding blood.

"There are no German letters in this house," she said.

"No," he agreed, standing, taking up his case. "Of course not. I'll note the property figures as verified." At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "For the record, Mrs. Okafor — the storms this winter are forecast to be bad ones. Supply runs may be irregular. If a person needed something brought to the island, quietly, this would be the season it could arrive unnoticed. Or leave." He opened the door onto the grey light. "Good day. Six souls. I'll confirm the six."

Then he was gone down the path, coat collar up, a thin dark figure against a sea the colour of gunmetal, and Coral stood in the doorway watching until the boat took him small and then took him entirely.

*This would be the season it could arrive unnoticed. Or leave.*

She stood a long time with the wind pulling at her hair, doing arithmetic she had forbidden herself: Greta's letter, dated June, promised for *when the storms break*. The forecast storms, arriving early. A census man from Dresden's city, telling her, in the language of clerical caution, that doors swing both ways.

She went inside and took Heinrich's letter from the lens housing and read the last paragraph again, the way she had read it forty times, the way she would read it forty more:

*Coral — if anything happens to me, Greta will come to you. Not ask. Come. Keep her as you kept my friendship: without declaring it. I know what that costs. I will owe it to you in a currency I hope we both live to see devalued — gratitude, and news, and the names of birds.*

Below the window, far out, a fishing boat was working the swell toward the harbour, riding low, running a light that wasn't Dara's.

Coral put the letter away, and put the kettle on, and set out two cups — then stopped, and looked at them, and set out a third.
