## 5. The Storm Season Opens

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the gulls had gone inland.

Coral had learned to read the island before she'd learned to read Tomás's handwriting, and the two skills were not so different—both required attention to small marks, both punished carelessness. The gulls leaving was the first sentence of a long warning. The second was the sea itself, which had turned a bruised green-black under the overcast and was stacking swells against the northern reef with a patience that frightened her more than violence would have.

She spent Wednesday doing what her husband had called *paying the storm its respects*. Fresh wicks trimmed and set in the oil room. The spare lens plate wrapped in flannel and stowed high above the tide line. Water carried up in every vessel she owned, because the pump would fail before the wind peaked—it always did—and thirst made people stupid faster than fear did. She checked the davits on the landing stage twice, though no boat would use them for days.

The typewriter sat in the window of the cottage, taking the light.

She had meant to cover it. She had meant to cover it for eleven months now, since the day they'd carried Tomás down the cliff path and she had come back to an empty house where his last unfinished page still stood in the platen like a hand raised halfway to a question. But covering it felt like closing a coffin, and not covering it felt like waiting, and she had discovered that she preferred waiting. It was a dishonest comfort. She took those wherever she could find them.

By dusk the wind had arrived—not the storm itself but its herald, a long northeast moan that set the wire stays on the tower singing in three thin notes. Coral ate standing at the window, watching the light sweep out over the water. Four seconds bright, one second dark. Four and one. Tomás used to say you could set a heart by it.

At nine o'clock, the light stuttered.

It was not the dramatic failure of stories—a great blackness swallowing the sea. It was smaller than that, and worse. A hesitation. The beam dragged, caught, dragged again, as if the mechanism had developed a limp. Coral was out the door before she decided to go, oilskin over her nightdress, boots unlaced because there was no time to lace them.

* * *

The lamp room at the top of seventy-one iron steps was a different country in a gale. In fair weather it was a glass crown with a view of heaven. Tonight it was a drum being beaten from the outside. The wind came through every seam in flat, hammering gusts, and the whole tower leaned into each blow with a groan that Coral felt in her back teeth, and then sprang back, and leaned again.

The clockwork drive was fouled. She saw it at once: the governor weights swinging wrong, short-stroked, something jamming the escapement that ought to click the rotation forward four seconds at a time. Salt crust, most likely. Salt got everywhere; salt was the island's true resident and she only its tenant.

She got the lamp turned down to a low flame first—that was drilled into her, that was Tomás's voice in her ear even now, *flame low before hands in the works, Cora-lie, a fire up here is the end of everything*—and then she knelt on the brass grating with a wrench and a rag and put her hands into the cold heart of the machine.

The trouble was worse than salt. A pin had sheared somewhere in the weight train, and its broken half had dropped into the gear below and lodged there, and every rotation ground it a little deeper. She could see a sliver of steel wedged against a tooth. She worked the rag around it, worked the pick she kept on her belt, swore words Tomás would have charged her a kiss for, and freed it—and the mechanism lurched back to life with such sudden enthusiasm that the winding crank spun backward and took her left hand with it.

She heard the bone before she felt it. A small dry sound, like a twig stepped on in the next room.

Then the pain arrived, whole and white, and Coral Okafor knelt on the grating of her own lighthouse with two broken fingers and a lamp that had to be relit and turned up inside ninety seconds or the dark would go out over the water like a lie.

Ninety seconds. She knew the number because Tomás had timed it once, laughing, timing himself: *a ship coming out of the channel sees nothing for four seconds at a time, Cora-lie, and if we're dark for five minutes that's forty ships' worth of nothing.*

She got up. She did not decide to get up; the body simply refused the floor, the way it refuses drowning. With her right hand she turned the flame back up, watching the mantles catch, watching the great lens gather the light and throw it outward in its patient wheel. Four seconds bright. One second dark. The beam went out over the water and the water received it, and somewhere out in all that black a fishing fleet was running for shelter, and every man aboard would look up and see it and not know her name.

Her hand was swelling inside her glove. She climbed down the seventy-one steps one-handed, which is to say she fell the last dozen and lay at the bottom listening to the storm try to unscrew the roof of the world, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *if I die here, the light will burn until the oil runs out, and that is something.*

She did not die. She bound the fingers herself with a splint made from a pencil and strips of the flannel meant for the spare lens, because there was no doctor nearer than the mainland and no boat that could have reached the mainland anyway. Then she sat in Tomás's chair with her ruined hand in her lap and let herself shake, quietly, the way you shake when the performance is over and the audience has gone home.

Outside, the storm settled in for the night like an old enemy who knows he is owed several rounds.

* * *

Someone knocked at dawn.

Coral came to the door with the poker in her good hand, because the years had taught her that much, and found nobody—only a crate of herring packed in wet sacking, sitting on the step with a stone on the lid to keep the gulls honest. No note. Dara's knots, though. Nobody on the island tied a knot like Dara; it was a scandal among proper sailors, all loops and no logic, and it held forever.

She stood in the doorway a long moment with the wind pulling her hair sideways, looking down the cliff path. Halfway along it, a figure in oilskins was already receding toward the village through the horizontal rain, walking with that rolling, deliberate gait, not hurrying, not looking back, a person performing the fiction of having merely passed by.

Coral took the fish inside and fried two of them for her breakfast and ate them with one hand and cried a little without deciding to, the way the body does things the mind hasn't authorized.

* * *

The storm lasted three days, and the island changed shape around her.

Not geographically. Geographically it lost a fence and gained a pond where the low field had been. But in some other register—the one she had no instrument to read—the island drew closer. On the second morning there was bread on the step, still warm, wrapped in a cloth Mrs. Quill used for her own counter loaves; Coral knew the cloth, knew the faded blue stripes, knew the shop it had come from twenty years ago. On the second evening, a jar of dripping and a twist of paper holding tea. On the third day, when the wind finally backed around and began to lose its argument with the land, she opened the door to find firewood stacked under the lean-to—split, dry, stacked with a care nobody spends on their own pile—and beside it a pair of men's gloves, enormous, tarred at the palms, left as casually as if they'd blown there.

She asked about it, once. She rowed—no, there was no rowing yet; she walked to the village when the path first allowed it, splinted hand tucked in her coat, and bought flour from Mrs. Quill and said, lightly, *people have been very kind, leaving things*, and Mrs. Quill weighed her flour with great attention and said, *have they? The wind brings strange things ashore,* and looked at Coral's bandaged hand for exactly as long as it takes to count to four—the light's own rhythm, Coral thought absurdly—and said nothing else about it.

That was the arrangement, then. That was how it would be done. Kindness delivered like contraband, denied by both parties, deniable to any census taker, requisition officer, or God. Coral understood it from the inside, because it was exactly how she kept Heinrich's letters: things that could not be spoken of aloud were still real, and still passed hand to hand, and still fed people.

Dara appeared at the landing stage that afternoon while she was inspecting the davits, appearing the way Dara always appeared, out of weather that should have kept anyone indoors, hands in pockets, face like a plank.

"Heard your light went lame Tuesday night," Dara said.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody tells me anything on this rock." Dara spat expertly to leeward. "I'm out in it, is how I know. Went dark maybe two minutes. Felt like longer."

"It was ninety seconds," Coral said, and heard how that sounded, and wished she hadn't said it.

Dara looked at her. Dara had a way of looking that made you feel inventoried—not catalogued like Elias Brandt's census book, itemized and judged, but taken stock of in the older sense: *what does this creature need to last the winter.* "Hand's broke," Dara said. Not a question.

"Two fingers. It'll knit."

"Winding crank?"

Coral said nothing, which was answering.

"Should've let it stay dark," Dara said. "Two minutes. No skipper alive holds a course on two minutes' dark in that blow—they'd have hove to and cursed you and lived." A pause. Wind walked across the water and shook the spray off the rocks. "But you wouldn't, would you. You'd break your own hand first."

"You'd have done the same."

"I'd have done the same," Dara agreed, with no pleasure in it whatsoever, and looked out at the grey line of the horizon where the fishing grounds were, where boats were already putting out again because storms end and debts don't. "There's a winter coming, keeper. A real one. Old Quill says the birds went early and the berries are heavy, and old Quill's never wrong about anything except prices." Dara shifted, uncomfortable suddenly, a person approaching the edge of what could be said. "If the season gets bad—if boats can't run regular—you've provisions laid in? Enough?"

"Enough."

"Liar," Dara said, not unkindly. "There's a locker in the old bothy past the head. Don't ask what's in it. Don't thank anybody. That's how it works."

And Dara was gone down the staging, into the boat, out onto the water, moving with the swells instead of against them, a person entirely at home in a world that wanted Coral dead.

* * *

That night she climbed the tower again, because the light needed keeping and her hand needed proving. The splinted fingers screamed on every rung and she let them; pain was just the body's bookkeeping, and she had accounts to settle.

In the lamp room the storm's leftovers still ran high, but the glass held its long view: the whole dark Atlantic turning under the beam, and beyond it, faint as breath on a mirror, the lights of the mainland strung along the coast. Somewhere behind one of those lights was a census office with Elias Brandt's neat columns in it. Somewhere further, across a colder sea, was a man who had taught her husband everything, writing letters she hid inside the machinery of the light itself.

She wound the clockwork carefully, one-handed, cranking with her palm flat so the mechanism couldn't take her again. The weights rose. The escapement clicked. Four seconds bright, one second dark, out and out and out over the water.

And as she stood there, wrist aching, watching her light walk the waves, she saw something that was not a wave.

A light answering. Far out, low on the water, small and steady and wrong—wrong in color, greenish, and wrong in rhythm, flashing where no marked channel flashed, in a stretch of open sea where the charts showed nothing but weather. It showed for perhaps a minute. Then the dark took it, and the swells rolled through where it had been, and it did not come back.

Signals happened, sometimes. Naval exercises, she told herself. A trawler's distress lantern seen at an angle. The eye playing tricks after three days of storm.

But her hand, splinted and swollen against the cold brass rail, remembered something her mind had not yet said aloud: that signals travel in both directions, and that someone out there on the black water might be flashing a question toward this island—to this light—awaiting an answer she did not yet know she had promised.

She stayed at the rail until the cold drove her down, and the question followed her all the way, blinking patiently in the dark behind her eyes.
