## 6. A Question Too Many

He came on a Tuesday, which was wrong. The supply boat ran Thursdays, weather permitting, and nothing about Elias Brandt had ever depended on permission from the weather.

Coral saw him from the gallery while she polished the storm panes—a figure in a grey coat picking his way up the causeway path with his census satchel slung crosswise, the way a man carries something he expects to be robbed of. The wind was moderate for November, which meant only that it didn't try to take her off the rail when she leaned out. She watched him come the whole way up without once pretending not to.

That was the first difference. Last month he had knocked like a guest.

She went down before he reached the door, so that opening it would be her idea. "Mr. Brandt."

"Mrs. Okafor." He took off his hat, and the wind immediately tried to take that instead, and they both pretended not to notice him wrestling it. "I'm verifying figures for the district office. Household composition, occupations, country of origin." A pause exactly long enough to be heard. "Languages spoken."

"You verified all that in September."

"The district office has a new form." He held up the satchel as evidence. It looked heavier than paper. "May I come in? There's a gale due from the southwest and I'd rather not conduct official business in it."

It was true about the gale—she'd smelled it since dawn, a green metallic tang under the salt—and it was also true that refusing him would look like refusal, which was its own entry on whatever new form the district office had invented. She stood aside.

Inside, the cottage was small enough that every object testified. She had spent the morning, without admitting it to herself, straightening: Tomás's chair pushed square to the table, his typewriter covered, his oilskin hung by the door where a dead man's coat looks less like grief than like storage. She put the kettle on because her hands wanted work.

Brandt did not sit. He stood at the table with his ledger open, copying figures she had already given him once, in her own careful hand, onto a fresh line. His pen moved quickly. He was left-handed and wrote with his wrist curled over, hiding what he wrote even as he wrote it, and Coral thought: *a habit of a man who sits across from people*. Then thought: *stop thinking like that.*

"Occupation," he said. "Keeper of the light."

"Sole keeper. As you know."

"As I know." He made the note anyway. "And your late husband. Keeper also."

"Yes."

"And before the war—he trained under a German engineer. That's in the light's service record. Heinrich Voss, optical engineer, seconded from Bremerhaven, 1924 to 1929." He said the dates the way other men said scripture. "The record notes he lived in this cottage for six months during the lens refit."

Coral set two cups on the tray. Her hands were steady; she made a study of them being steady. "Heinrich Voss taught my husband how to hold a light together. That was twenty years ago and it was peacetime, and if the Empire can't tell the difference between 1926 and now, then the Empire is filling out forms faster than it's reading them."

The kettle began its low climb toward boiling. Brandt watched her pour.

"That isn't what I asked," he said quietly.

*There it is.* She had known since the causeway, since the satchel carried crosswise, since *languages spoken*. She had known since September, if she were honest, since the moment his finger stopped on her census page and tapped twice beside the word *none* where languages should have been, and she had watched him decide—not to believe her, but to wait.

"What did you ask?" she said.

"I asked where you learned German."

"And I told you in September. Tomás taught me. He learned it from Voss and passed along what was useful—lamp terminology, mostly. Words for glass and gears. My German is a mechanic's German. It couldn't order dinner."

"It could," Brandt said, "if you wanted it to."

The kettle screamed. She lifted it and poured water over the leaves in silence, letting it speak for her, counting seconds—one, two, three, four—because Mrs. Quill's tea went bitter past four and some standards survived everything. When she looked up, Brandt had closed the ledger.

"Mrs. Okafor." His voice had changed register, dropped below the official. "I'm going to say something now that I am not authorized to say, and after I say it, one of us will have made a serious mistake, and I've spent three weeks deciding it shouldn't be me."

"Then don't say it."

"There are letters."

The clock ticked. Out beyond the point, the sea had begun to organize itself for the gale, a long low mutter against the rocks like a jury filing in.

"I don't know what you mean," Coral said.

"Correspondence in German. Received irregularly, likely via fishing boats rather than the mail packet, which is why no postal censor has seen them. Filed—" he paused, and for the first time looked faintly embarrassed— "the file speculates as to where. The speculation involves the lamp mechanism itself, which tells me the person writing the file has never actually been inside a lighthouse, because anyone who had would know that a lens housing is the first place any keeper would look, and also the last place anyone else would dare put a hand."

Coral said nothing. In the glass of the window behind him, the sky was going the color of wet slate, and she thought, absurdly, of Greta at the window, and shut the thought with a door-slam loud enough that she almost flinched.

"If such letters existed," Brandt went on, "their existence would constitute communication with a national of an enemy state. Retention would be an offense. Concealment, a worse one. The penalty scales with the content, and the content is unknown, which means the penalty is whatever the tribunal decides, and tribunals this winter have not been deciding generously." He laid his pen down on the ledger, parallel to its edge, precise as a surgeon setting instruments. "You understand me so far."

"I understand you're threatening me in my own kitchen."

"No," he said. "I'm about to stop threatening you. That's why I came back."

She waited. The tea steamed between them, going amber, going dark.

"My instructions," he said, "were to confirm the correspondence and file a full report. Seizure follows. Arrest may follow that—the file uses the word *detention*, which is a word chosen by someone who wants arrest to sound temporary." He looked at the window, at the coming storm, anywhere but at her, and when he spoke again his voice was dry as the ledgers he lived inside. "My instructions also assume you'll deny everything, and my report will note that denial in the face of evidence indicates consciousness of guilt, and the machinery will do what machinery does. That is the road we're on, Mrs. Okafor, if you keep driving down it. So here is the fork."

He turned the ledger around and slid it across the table toward her. It lay closed between the teacups like a card played face-down.

"These notes represent four months of work. Two trips in foul weather. A file that will make my name at the district office—I'm thirty-four years old and I carry a bag and count fishermen, and men like me do not get offered names often." His jaw worked once. "If you tell me the truth—all of it, now, today—I will lose these pages. All of them. An accident of the crossing, ink run, paper spoiled, my error entirely, noted and reprimanded. And whatever truth you tell me stays in this room, because a report built on ruined notes is a report nobody reads."

The wind arrived then, the front edge of the gale, and the chimney drew hard and the fire flattened and recovered. Coral stood very still.

Because here was the thing he could not know: he was offering her the exact trade she had already refused once, alone, at midnight, with the letter in her hand and the stove door open and the flames breathing up at her like something alive. *Burn it,* she had told herself. *Burn it and be invisible again.* And she had closed the stove door instead, because the letter was Heinrich's handwriting and Heinrich's desperation and the last living proof that the world had once contained a kitchen in Bremen with three people laughing in it, and she could not make herself feed that to a fire.

Now a stranger stood in Tomás's kitchen offering to unmake the danger, and the price was the same: give up the letter, give up the truth, give up whatever it protected.

"No," she said.

Brandt blinked. "No?"

"No." She sat down, finally, across from him, and wrapped her hands around her cup though the tea was still too hot. "You want the truth as payment. But the truth isn't mine to sell, Mr. Brandt, and it isn't only mine to tell. If I gave it to you, I'd be spending something that belongs to other people—people who trusted me with more than paper. People who trusted me with their lives." *One life,* she corrected herself silently. *One life, arriving on a boat you mustn't imagine.* "So you can write your report. You can spell my name correctly—it's Okafor, hyphenated on some forms, Coral Wynne Okafor—and you can send your men with their seizure warrant, and they can stand in my lamp room and wonder where to put their hands. But I won't buy safety with someone else's blood. I did not learn that from a book. I learned it keeping a light."

Brandt stared at her. Something moved through his face—not anger, not triumph, something more like a man watching a door close on a room he'd convinced himself he didn't want to enter.

"You'd rather be arrested," he said slowly, "than inform."

"I'd rather be *left alone*. Since that appears to be off the table—" she pushed the teacup toward him, an inch, deliberately, "—drink your tea. It's going over bitter."

He looked at the cup as if it might also require cataloguing. Then, to her genuine surprise, he pulled out the chair and sat.

Not just any chair. She realized it as he settled into it—Tomás's chair, the one with the spindled back worn pale where a shoulder had leaned for eleven years, the one she had squared that morning to make the room look orderly. He didn't know. Of course he didn't know. But she watched him sit down into her husband's place with the ledger abandoned and his hands wrapping the cup, and something in her chest performed a maneuver she had no words for.

They drank tea for a while without talking. The gale found its rhythm against the north wall, gust and pause, gust and pause, like something enormous learning to breathe.

"Voss," Brandt said at last, quietly, not looking up. "What kind of man was he."

Coral studied him. "Why do you want to know that?"

"Because the file has a photograph of him from 1918 and it's a photograph of an enemy, and I've been carrying it around so long it's started to look like a fact." He turned the cup in his hands. "And because you talk about him the way people talk about the dead they loved, and I'd like to know if I'm wrong about that too."

The question was dangerous in a way his earlier ones hadn't been—the others probed at evidence, and this one probed at her. But perhaps that was why she answered.

"He was patient," she said. "He believed machines could be honest even when people weren't. He used to say a lens doesn't care whose flag is on the boat it guides home—that light is one of the only promises a country can make that doesn't require a border." She smiled despite herself, at the memory of Heinrich standing in this very kitchen with his sleeves rolled, drawing lens prisms in spilled flour because there was no paper. "He called Tomás *Bruder*. Not figuratively. It was how he signed his letters."

"Was," Brandt repeated. His head came up. "*Was* how he signed his letters."

But Coral only rose and began stacking the cups, and the click of porcelain on porcelain was a door closing, gentle and total.

He understood. To his credit—or whatever word applied—he didn't push. He gathered his satchel, wound his scarf, and at the threshold paused with the wind already pulling at him.

"For the record," he said, "your tea was excellent. And for no record at all: the district office rechecks files quarterly. Whatever an accident of the crossing might have destroyed, it would need to stay destroyed through March."

* * *

The ferry back was the worst crossing of Elias Brandt's professional life, which was saying something, because he had taken this crossing in September with a file that kept him awake in his lodgings for a week afterward.

He stood at the stern rail under canvas while grey water heaved and the island shrank behind him, and he conducted, as he always did, an inventory. It was how he kept himself honest—or he had believed it was, until this afternoon. *Inventory:* one ledger, unopened since the kitchen. One offer, refused. One report, unwritten. One career, measured this morning in promotions-to-come, and measured tonight in pages he could not bring himself to take out of the satchel.

She had refused. In fourteen months of this work he had interviewed smugglers and deserters and a schoolteacher who read foreign newspapers aloud, and every single one of them, when the fork came, had taken the road that led away from the tribunal. Fear was reliable. Fear was the load-bearing wall of the entire apparatus. And this woman—alone on a rock, widowed, transferable on a department's whim, with everything to lose and no one to help her carry it—had looked at the open door of escape and said *no* on behalf of people she wouldn't name.

Either she was the best liar he had ever met, or she was innocent of anything except the offense of having loved across a border. And Elias Brandt, who had built four months of methodical work on the certainty of the first, sat down on a wet bench in the ferry's lee and confronted, with the same rigor, the growing probability of the second.

*Light is one of the only promises a country can make that doesn't require a border.*

He took out his notebook—the working one, the field copy—and uncapped his pen, and wrote the sentence down before he could decide whether he was documenting evidence or collecting contraband.

At the district office the next morning, he typed his interim report. *Subject cooperative. Correspondence unverified. Further inquiries pending seasonal conditions.* Every word defensible. Every omission deniable. He initialed the bottom, dated it, and carried it to his superior's desk, and was halfway back to his own before a voice stopped him.

"Brandt."

Deputy Controller Marsh stood in his doorway holding the report, reading it with the expression of a man tasting soup for salt. Marsh had a small face and large patience and had spent the war in rooms Elias did not have clearance to know the names of.

"Unverified," Marsh said. "Pending. Seasonal conditions." He let each word sit in the air like a specimen pinned. "Four months ago you requested this island specifically, Brandt. Cited anomalies in the household census. Wrote me two memoranda—one of them eight pages—on the probability of concealed foreign correspondence at the Okafor light. Now you cross in a gale, spend ninety minutes ashore, and come back with *pending*." He folded the report once, precisely. "Tell me what changed."

"The subject," Elias said, and heard his own voice hold steady, barely, "is not what the file assumes."

"No," Marsh agreed pleasantly. "She isn't. Files rarely are." He set the report on his desk, unhurried, and the smile he gave was the worst thing in the room. "Which is why in future, the file gets checked from more directions than one. I've assigned a secondary inquiry. Nothing personal, Brandt—you know how the office loves thoroughness." He returned to his papers. "Close the door."

Elias walked back to his desk through rows of clerks bent over their lists, and sat, and did not move for a long time. *A secondary inquiry.* Someone else would go to the island now. Someone with no tea drunk, no fork offered, no memory of a woman choosing the tribunal over betrayal with both hands around a cooling cup. Someone who would search properly—who would know to look where a keeper would look.

He opened his drawer and took out the requisition forms, and began, with the steadiest hand he owned, to fill in a request for reassignment: *return engagement, island station, follow-up verification, earliest available sailing.*

Above the district office, November pressed its grey weight down on the harbor, and somewhere out in that grey, past the shoals and the patrol routes and the narrowing storm windows, a light burned that he was no longer certain he'd been sent to extinguish—or sent, at all, for the reasons he'd been told.
