## 7. Greta at the Window

The fog came in on the ninth of November without warning, the way grief does.

Coral had watched the barometer all morning, and the needle had been steady, almost smug. Then she'd gone down to wind the clock and come back up to find the whole world erased. The sea was gone. The rocks were gone. Even the gallery rail, six feet from her face, had become a rumor. She stood in the lantern room with her hand flat against the cold glass and felt the island shrink to a coal of light in a wet black cloth.

Fog meant the fog signal. She climbed the iron stairs to the horn house and set the mechanism breathing—four seconds of sound, eight of silence, four again—and the island began to moan into the whiteness like something wounded but determined to go on announcing itself.

She had learned, in three years alone, that a lighthouse is mostly a machine for refusing silence.

She went back down through the tower, past Tomás's typewriter under its oilcloth shroud, past the kitchen where the kettle sat cold because she hadn't bothered, and stopped at the window over the landing stage. The glass was beaded so thickly that the view beyond it moved like something alive. She wiped a circle clear with her sleeve.

Nothing. Nothing. Then—a shape.

It came out of the fog the way fish come up under ice: suddenly, all at once, as if it had been there the whole time and only now consented to exist. A fishing boat, small, patched, riding low. It bumped against the landing stage with a sound like a knuckle rapped on a door.

Coral's heart did not leap. It dropped—the way it drops when you hear your own name spoken by a stranger in a dark street.

*Not yet,* she thought. *The storms haven't broken. He said when the storms break.*

But Heinrich had also written, in that last letter folded inside the lens housing where no requisition officer would ever think to look: *If she comes early, do not turn her away. If she comes early, it is because staying away has become worse than the sea.*

Coral took the lamp key off its hook. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Three years of winding, polishing, logging, enduring, and here at last was the moment her careful invisible life might crack open like an egg—and her hands were steady as a surgeon's.

She went down the outside stairs into the fog.

* * *

The girl was already on the stage when Coral reached it, standing with one hand on the wet iron rail, swaying slightly, as though the ground were still moving beneath her. She was perhaps nineteen. Her coat was wool, good once, now soaked black with spray, and her hair had escaped its pins entirely and hung in ropes. She carried nothing. No bag, no bundle. Whatever she owned had been left behind or lost, and her empty hands hung at her sides like things she no longer knew how to use.

Up close, in the yellow cone of Coral's hand torch, the resemblance was not subtle. It was a verdict.

The same deep-set grey eyes. The same jaw that looked drawn by someone in a hurry who nonetheless cared about the result. Coral had studied Heinrich Brandt's face across a galley table for two years of peacetime, had watched him laugh with Tomás over engine diagrams, had seen him weep exactly once, silently, the night before he sailed home. She knew that face the way you know a hymn.

"You're Greta," Coral said.

The girl flinched at the name as if struck. Then she said, in English broken but carefully assembled, word by word, like a child stacking blocks in wind:

"Heinrich is my father. You are—" she stopped, searched, failed, and pressed on with the only certainty she had, "—the wife of Tomás."

"I am."

"Then I am—" Again the search. This time she found it, and her voice cracked on it. "I am expected."

"Yes," Coral said. "You are expected."

Greta's knees went then. Not dramatically—she simply stopped agreeing to stand, and Coral caught her under the arms, and the two of them stood locked together on the landing stage in the fog while somewhere out in the white the boat that had brought her coughed twice and faded away, its engine note thinning until the fog swallowed it and there was only the horn, moaning its four-and-eight, four-and-eight, like a heart that would not stop.

"Inside," Coral said. "Now. Quickly."

* * *

She got the girl up the stairs and into the kitchen, stripped the wet coat off her, wrapped her in Tomás's old watch blanket—the one that smelled, Coral had never been able to stop noticing, faintly of his pipe—and pushed her into the chair by the stove. Greta's lips were blue-grey. Her hands shook so badly she couldn't hold the tea Coral pressed into them, so Coral held the cup to her mouth herself, tilting it, patient, the way you'd feed a fledgling.

"Slow," Coral said. "Slowly."

Greta drank. Color began the slow work of returning to her face. And Coral, kneeling there with her arm around a stranger's shoulders, felt something move through her chest that she had no shelf for. Tenderness, yes—but fierce, sudden, unreasonable, arriving like weather. She had spent three years being precise about her feelings, rationing them the way the government rationed everything else, and this thing would not be rationed. It simply was, enormous and inconvenient, kneeling on a cold floor holding a cup of tea to a German girl's mouth.

Because that was what the girl was. Coral made herself say it plainly, inside her own head, the way you check a knot. A German national. Arriving by unregistered boat. In wartime. Onto an island with one inhabitant whose file—if she had a file—was already suspect enough that a census man had asked her, twice, where she had learned German.

Elias Brandt. She thought of his careful handwriting in the census book, his eyes that noticed too much, and felt the first true cold of the evening slide under her skin.

"Your father," she said aloud. "Is he—"

Greta understood the unfinished question. Everyone understood unfinished questions now; they were the grammar of the age. She shook her head quickly. "He lives. He works. They let him work, because he knows the lights." Her hands tightened around the refilled cup. "But they watch him. Because of me. Because I—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the whole enterprise of her existence, "—I say wrong things. In class. I wrote a letter." A ghost of Heinrich's stubborn jaw set itself. "One letter. They came to the school. So my father bought me a place on a boat, and the boat was late, and the storms—" She stopped. Looked at the window, where the fog pressed like a palm against the glass. "The boat could not wait longer. So I come early. I am sorry. I know I am early."

"You are not early," Coral heard herself say. "You are alive. That is never early."

Then the horn fell silent mid-breath.

Four seconds of sound, eight of silence—that was the contract. But this silence stretched. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Coral set down the cup and stood, every nerve she owned pulled taut, because she knew that silence. She knew it the way sailors know the particular wrongness of a sea that has stopped breaking.

An engine. Running quiet, running slow, running *searching*.

Through the wiped circle of window, a shape resolved: a grey patrol boat, low-slung, its searchlight sweeping the fog in long blind strokes, feeling along the coast the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth.

"Greta." Coral kept her voice level, and was distantly proud of it. "Can you climb stairs? Quickly?"

"Yes."

"Then climb."

* * *

They went up through the tower in the dark, Coral ahead, counting steps by feel, the patrol boat's light strobing through the stairwell windows in pale bars that slid across their faces like the fingers of something patting down the island for contraband. At the service door to the lantern room Coral paused, one hand on the latch, and made a decision she would remember making for the rest of her life.

There was nowhere to hide the girl. There was only the light itself.

"In there," she whispered, pointing to the narrow space behind the lens assembly, between the great glass drum and the curved iron wall, where the counterweights ran and the shadow was total. "Whatever happens. Whatever you hear. You do not move, and you do not breathe loud. Can you do that?"

Greta looked at her—soaked, shaking, nineteen years old and three hundred miles from everyone who loved her—and nodded once, the exact economical nod Coral had seen her father use a thousand times.

"Yes," she said. "I am expected," and slipped into the dark behind the lens like water finding its level.

Coral closed the service door, crossed to the main entrance, and opened it just as boots hit the gallery outside.

Two men. An officer with rain sheeting off his cap brim, and behind him a rating with a rifle slung and miserable. The officer's torch beam hit Coral full in the face and she did not raise a hand against it. She had learned that from Tomás: never shield your eyes. Guilty people shield their eyes.

"Keeper Okafor?" The beam wavered. "Alone on station?"

"Since my husband died. You know that. It's in your own records—I've signed the returns myself."

"Fog tonight. Unusual traffic possible." The beam swung past her shoulder into the tower, crawled down the spiral stairs, lingered on the oilcloth-shrouded typewriter below. "We're checking every light on this stretch. Anyone come ashore here? Any boat?"

"The fog signal's been sounding since six o'clock," Coral said. "You'll have heard it. If a boat had tried the landing stage in this, I'd have heard it over the horn. I hear very little else these days."

It was true, and it was calm, and it was also a lie by omission so large she could have sheltered inside it. She felt the lie settle over her like a second coat. Somewhere four feet behind the lens drum, a girl was controlling her breathing in the dark, and Coral's heartbeat didn't even show in her voice. She marveled at herself, mildly, the way you marvel at a stranger.

The officer stepped past her onto the lantern room floor. His torch swept the mechanism, the log desk, the chart drawer left half open. It passed within an arm's length of the service door. It moved on.

"Log book," he said.

Coral handed it over. He read by torchlight, slowly, letting the silence do its work. She let it do its work right back. Outside, the patrol boat's searchlight swung its blind white arm across the fog, once, twice, and each pass threw the lens assembly's shadow huge and turning across the lantern room walls—a great slow wheel of darkness rotating over them both, and behind that darkness, a heartbeat.

"Nothing logged since the supply boat in October," the officer said at last.

"Nothing's come. Nothing's gone. That's the arithmetic of this place."

He snapped the log shut and handed it back, and for a moment his eyes rested on her—not suspicious, exactly. Weighing. The way Elias Brandt's eyes weighed. She wondered, with a lurch, whether the two men compared notes, whether her name traveled back and forth across the water in folders, whether somewhere in a mainland office there was a page with her name on it growing annotations like mold.

"Keep your light burning, Keeper," he said. "Visibility's nil. There may be small craft about."

"There always may," Coral said.

He turned to go, then paused at the door. "That horn," he said. "It stopped while we were coming in. Irregular."

"Condensation in the valve. It does that in fog. I'll have it seen to."

He nodded, unconvinced or convinced, impossible to tell, and his boots receded down the gallery stairs, and the boat's engine note faded into the fog, and the horn—Coral went straight up and restarted it—resumed its four-and-eight, four-and-eight, like a heart allowed to beat again.

* * *

She waited ten minutes. Then twenty. Then she opened the service door and said, into the dark, "It's done. Come out."

Greta unfolded herself from behind the lens, stiff with cold and the effort of stillness, and stood blinking in the lamplight among the brass and glass. She looked around the lantern room slowly—at the great lens turning its patient turn, at the log desk, at the chart of the coast pinned above it with its pencil annotations in two different hands, one dead, one living.

"This is where my father worked," she said quietly. "He drew this lens. The new one. Nineteen twenty-six. He kept the drawing on our wall my whole life." She touched the lens housing with two fingers, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "I used to think it was a story he told. The island. The other engineer. The keeper's wife who argued about poetry."

"He argued worse than me," Coral said. "Tomás. He called your father *Bruder*. Brother. He put it in writing." She stopped. Swallowed. "In a memoir he never finished. Half-typed. It's downstairs."

Greta turned and looked at her fully then, and Coral saw that the girl was crying without any fuss about it, tears simply present on her face like rain, as if crying were a function of the body that continued regardless of circumstances, like the light turning overhead.

"He said," Greta managed, "if anything happened to him, I should find you. He said, *the keeper's wife keeps more than the light.*"

Downstairs, faintly, the kettle Coral had finally put on began to sing.

And through the fogged window, far out in the white dark, a light appeared that should not have been there—a single green flash, short-short-long, repeated. Signal. Someone out in that fog, in a small boat, signaling toward the island. Toward her.

Toward the girl standing in the lamp room with her father's eyes.

Coral's blood went cold and hot at once. The boat that brought Greta had gone. But someone was out there—someone who knew the code, someone waiting for an answer she did not know how to give.

"Greta," she said slowly. "Do you know what that means?"

The girl leaned toward the window, read the flashes, and went white.

"It is Dara's cousin," she whispered. "The fisherman. He says—" she translated the rhythm with her lips before her voice caught up, "—*patrols doubled. Two days. Maybe less.*"
