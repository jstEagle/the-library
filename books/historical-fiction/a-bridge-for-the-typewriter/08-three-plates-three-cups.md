## 8. Three Plates, Three Cups

The patrols doubled. That was the arithmetic of the morning, and Coral carried it down the ninety-one steps of the tower like a stone in her chest.

Greta came behind her, one hand on the rail, learning the count of the stairs the way her father had once learned them—by feel, by failure, by the bruise on the hip where the turn caught you if you miscounted. Coral heard her stumble at sixty and did not stop, did not offer a hand. Heinrich's daughter would not thank her for softness. The Okafor women had never had any to spare, and neither, from all accounts, had the Brandts.

"Seventy-one," Coral said. "The step is cracked at seventy-one. Mind it going up, mind it worse coming down."

Greta said something in German—low, half to herself—and Coral caught only the number. Counting too. Good. A keeper's child already.

In the kitchen, Coral put water on to boil and set about making the morning real again, because the morning had to be made real or it would dissolve into the thing they were both thinking about: two days, maybe less, and a girl under her roof who could not exist on any official paper.

"You will learn the light," Coral said, as though teaching her were the most ordinary work in the world. "If I am to keep you hidden, you will be useful while you are hidden. I don't house passengers."

"I understand some," Greta said carefully. Her English came slow and square-built, each word set down like brickwork. "My father—he speak English with me. When I am small."

"He spoke it here, in this kitchen. At that table." Coral nodded at it. "He sat where you're sitting and ate my burnt bread and told Tomás his bread was worse."

Something moved across Greta's face—a photograph developing. "There is a story. Papa tell it. About the bread, and the gull who took it."

"The gull came through the window," Coral said. "Tomás swore the bird was sent by the mainland to prove we couldn't feed ourselves. He wrote two pages of complaint to the Commissioners about that gull." She heard her own voice do something it hadn't done in months—curl at the edges, warm itself. She shut it away. "The wicks first. Come here."

She walked Greta through it in the cold morning light, when the lamp slept: how the mantles were handled like moth wings, how a fingerprint on hot glass meant a dark spot on the horizon for every ship that passed. How the clockwork that turned the lens wanted winding to the minute, twice daily, no forgiveness. Greta listened the way her father had listened—with her whole body leaning toward the mechanism, hands hovering but not touching until permission came.

"May I?"

"Wipe your hands first."

Greta wiped them on her borrowed sweater—Mrs. Quill's grey wool, three sizes wrong—and touched the lens housing with two fingers, gentle as touching a sleeping animal's nose.

"Papa say this light has his hand in it," she said. "The gear ratios. He and Tomás change them, 1926. Before, the turn is—" she searched, found nothing, drew a circle in the air with her fist jerking, "—not smooth. Like old man walking. After, smooth."

"We re-did the escapement the winter of '26," Coral said. "Tomás talked of nothing else for four months. I heard about gear teeth at breakfast, dinner, and bed." She turned the great wheel a fraction and let the lens glide through its arc, and watched Greta watch it—the same hunger in the face, the same lit-up attention. It was like looking at a ghost that had been fed and warmed and given dry socks. It was unbearable. It was the best thing she had seen since Tomás died.

"The rhythm," Coral went on, because if she stopped she would have to sit down. "Sunset, light on. Midnight, check the mantles. Dawn, light off, clean the glass, wind the clockwork, log the weather. The light doesn't care about your feelings, the light doesn't care about wars, the light cares only that someone shows up. You show up. That's the whole trade."

"That is also what he say," Greta said quietly. "Show up. He write it in every letter."

Coral kept her hands moving among the cups and didn't answer that.

* * *

The typewriter sat on the shelf where it had sat for fourteen months, wearing its salt-spray rust like a skin disease. Tomás had bought it secondhand in 1931 with money he'd meant to spend on a new coat, and he'd typed on it every evening of his life after—the log book's fair copies, letters, complaints about gulls, and finally, in the last year, the memoir that stalled at page forty-something and died with him mid-sentence.

Coral dusted around it. That was all she had ever done. Dusting around a thing was a form of prayer, and she had not dared a deeper one.

It was Greta who stopped in front of it on the second evening, head tilted, the way her father had tilted his head at the old escapement.

"It is rusted?"

"It's rusted," Coral agreed, from the stove.

"May I look?"

"It's not a secret. It's just dead."

But Greta was already lifting it down, and Coral watched her carry it to the table with both arms and set it down on folded sacking, and watched her examine it under the lamp with a jeweller's patience—no, not a jeweller's. An engineer's. The patience of someone who assumes the machine wants to live and is only waiting to be asked.

"There is no key for the ribbon spool?" Greta muttered, mostly to herself, and then she was hunting through the tin of odds and ends by the window—Coral's tin, Tomás's tin before hers—and coming up with a bent hairpin and a flat piece of clockspring and holding them up like a surgeon selecting instruments.

"You can't fix that," Coral said. "Salt gets into these things. Salt is patient. Salt always wins."

Greta looked up at her, and for a moment the face was pure Heinrich, twenty-five years younger, sitting in this same kitchen telling Tomás that no mechanism was dead, only discouraged. *Salt is patient,* young Heinrich had said, *but I am German. We outlast everything, including your opinions.*

"Papa fix typewriter on Ushant once," Greta said. "French keeper's machine, very bad, full of salt. He take it apart on the table, three days. The keeper cry when it type again." She smiled a small, fierce smile. "I am his daughter."

Coral gave up. There was no sense giving up gracefully, so she gave up the way she did everything—by putting the kettle on and pretending she wasn't watching every second.

For three evenings the table belonged to the disassembled typewriter, its parts laid out on white cloth in rows like an anatomical drawing, each piece rubbed with vinegar and fine oil, freed of rust with wire and patience. Greta worked with her sleeves pushed up, humming something low and tuneless, and Coral worked around her—cooking, logging, pretending indifference—and every night Coral went to bed with the strange sensation of a house breathing differently than it had the day before.

On the fourth night, near midnight, Coral came down from the lamp room to find Greta still at the table, the machine whole again, black and gleaming faintly, and a single sheet of paper in the roller.

"Listen," Greta said. Her eyes were shining. "I test only."

Her fingers found the keys—not fast, but sure, the way her hands had been sure with every screw and spring—and the carriage shot back with a bright mechanical ring that Coral had not heard in fourteen months and had believed, without admitting it, she would never hear again.

*Der Leuchtturm braucht keine Namen*, the paper said. Then, beneath, in Greta's careful English: *The lighthouse needs no names.*

Coral stood in her own kitchen with her hand pressed flat against her breastbone.

"It types," she said, and her voice came out rough. "Well. Salt lost, then."

"Salt always loses," Greta said, "against somebody who show up." And she grinned—at herself, at the pun, at the whole ridiculous world—and Coral laughed before she could stop herself, a short bark of a laugh that startled them both, and then they were laughing together in the lamplit kitchen at midnight, quietly, hysterically, because the alternative was to think about patrols and two days maybe less.

The sheet stayed in the roller. Neither of them mentioned it. But in the morning Coral noticed that Greta had rolled a fresh sheet in, squared and ready, and left the machine sitting there open like a door.

* * *

On the fifth morning Coral counted out the tea.

That was how it happened. Nothing dramatic. She opened the tin, took the caddy, and spooned leaves into the pot—one measure, two measures—and then her hand went back for a third before her mind caught up with her hand.

She stood at the dresser with the spoon suspended and looked at the three cups already set out on the dresser top. Not two. Three. And she had no memory of setting the third one out; it was simply there, in its place, as though it had always lived in that row between her good cup and Tomás's chipped blue one.

When had that happened? Last night? The night before? Had the third cup crept into the procession while she wasn't looking, the way roots creep under a wall?

Greta came in from checking the fog signal stores—she had appointed herself to it, or perhaps Coral had, the distinction long since dissolved—and stopped in the doorway. She looked at the three cups. She looked at Coral standing frozen over the teapot.

"Is wrong?" Greta asked. "I take mine after, if—"

"No." Coral set the spoon down harder than necessary. "No, it's—it's right. Sit down."

They drank tea at seven in the morning with the third cup steaming between them, and Coral studied the table instead of the girl, the grain of the wood, the rings Tomás's mug had left, and thought: this is how it happens. This is exactly how it happens. You pack a trunk for eleven years, you measure every possession against departure, you keep your soul down to carry-on weight—and then some engineer's child walks out of the fog and sets a cup on your dresser and you don't even notice yourself becoming a household again.

Belonging had arrived the way weather arrived. Uninvited. Unannounced. Impossible to send back.

And welcome. God help her, welcome.

"I make list," Greta said into the silence, pushing a paper across the table. In pencil, in her square brick-built English mixed with German, was written: *things for crossing — oilskin (Mrs. Quill bring), bread, fresh water 2 gal., chart?, the small compass??*

Coral read it twice. The plan sat there in pencil, two days maybe less, Dara's boat, the neutral port, the whole fragile scheme reduced to a shopping list—and at the bottom of the list, unremarked, Greta had written a line and half crossed it out. Coral could just make out the struck words: *typewriter cannot—*

"You were going to write that the typewriter can't come," Coral said.

Greta flushed. "Is stupid. Is heavy. Is your husband's."

"It is heavy." Coral folded the list neatly along its lines, the way she folded everything, and put it under the sugar tin. "We'll see what fits in the trunk."

She said it lightly. She did not feel light. Because she had understood something in that moment, sitting between two cups and one that had appeared by itself: that the trunk she had packed and unpacked for eleven years—the trunk that held the whole measurable weight of her life—was no longer the same trunk. Something had been added to it that weighed nothing and could not be lifted by anyone else. And something else had been taken out that she had not known she was carrying.

From the shelf, the typewriter sat gleaming, ready, a fresh sheet squared in its jaws.

Outside, the barometer was falling faster than it should for the hour, and far out beyond the point, where the sea met a sky the colour of wet slate, Coral saw—or imagined she saw—the shape of a boat that had no business being there, running dark against the swell.

Two days, maybe less.

She reached for the log book and began, in her straight keeper's hand, to write down the weather.
