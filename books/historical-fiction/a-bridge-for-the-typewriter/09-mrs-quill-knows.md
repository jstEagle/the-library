## 9. Mrs. Quill Knows

The flour gave her away. Coral understood that later, turning it over the way she turned a wick in its socket, examining all sides of a thing before she trusted it to burn.

She had gone down to Mrs. Quill's shop on Tuesday, as she did every second week, and asked for two stone of flour instead of one. She had caught herself mid-sentence—*two* stone, spoken plain as daylight—and had watched Mrs. Quill's hands pause over the paper sack, just for a moment, the scoop hovering above the bin like a gull deciding whether to land.

"One stone," Coral said quickly. "And a tin of condensed milk."

Mrs. Quill scooped the single stone. Weighed it. Tied the sack with the same precise three turns of string she'd used for twenty years. But when she slid it across the counter, she looked up—not at Coral, but at the space just past Coral's shoulder, the way country people look when they are seeing something larger than what stands in front of them.

"Storm's coming," she said. "Thursday, by my knee."

"Your knee has been wrong twice this month."

"My knee has been right nine times." She rang the milk into the ledger without writing it down first, which meant it was going on credit, which meant she was not going to press for payment soon. "You'll want kindling too. I've got good birch, dry as a sermon."

Coral hesitated. The old reflex—the trunk-reflex, the counting-reflex—told her to take only what was needed, to leave no pattern on any ledger anywhere. But Greta drank milk the way young things drink anything: constantly, without ceremony, as if the world owed it to her. And the girl was always cold. Two weeks in the lamp room's back alcove, wrapped in Tomás's spare blanket, shivering through the fogs even with the stove going, and still she said *nicht kalt, nicht kalt*, not cold, not cold, with blue lips.

"The birch," Coral said. "And another tin of milk."

Mrs. Quill wrote nothing at all this time.

* * *

The thing about an island of forty souls was that there were no strangers, only degrees of acquaintance. Coral had spent eleven months practicing the art of being barely known—a widow keeping a light, unremarkable, self-sufficient to the point of rudeness. It had worked because nobody had reason to look closely. Islands run on economy; you got exactly as much attention as your needs required, and Coral had arranged her needs to require nothing.

Greta had broken the arithmetic in four days.

The girl came out of the fog on Dara's cousin's boat with Heinrich's name in her mouth and salt frozen in her hair, and from the moment Coral hid her in the lamp room, the island began, slowly and without any single person saying anything, to notice. Not the truth itself—Coral believed, mostly, that the truth was still sealed—but the shape of the truth. The way a seal under snow shows you where it breathes.

There was the extra milk. There was the fact that Coral now took eggs every week instead of fortnightly. That she'd bought wool—a full pound of it, grey, good for mittens or a sweater—and Mrs. Quill knew Coral Okafor knitted nothing but her own socks, same pattern, eleven years running. There was the smoke from the lighthouse cottage chimney at hours when Coral was visibly elsewhere. There was the laundry: a small garment, a woman's but slight, hung inside the lamp room once when the wind shifted, visible from the harbour wall for perhaps ten minutes.

Ten minutes. On an island, that was a biography.

Coral lay awake after the flour trip and catalogued her exposures the way she'd once catalogued the light's defects. She made lists against the ceiling: milk, wool, eggs, smoke, the garment. Then she made a second list, harder to face: who knew what, and who might say.

Dara knew—had to know; his cousin's boat had landed the girl, whatever story had been told to cover it. Dara's silence could be relied upon the way you rely on a mooring: not because it loves you, but because it is set deep and holds by nature. Mrs. Quill knew something, though whether she'd assembled the whole truth or only its silhouette, Coral couldn't tell. The other families—the Perrys, the old man Sowle who netted eels in autumn—they saw everything and reported nothing to anyone except each other, and their gossip moved at the speed of weather, which meant it would reach the mainland eventually, distorted but recognizable, like a flare seen across water.

And somewhere out there was Elias Brandt's census book, with his neat entries and his questions about German.

Coral turned onto her side. Below her, through the floorboards, came the small sounds of Greta sleeping: the rustle of blanket, the half-word in a language Coral was beginning to hear in dreams. *Vater.* Father. The girl said it in her sleep the way sailors name ports they may never reach again.

* * *

It happened without announcement, the way most true things on the island happened.

Coral came down from the light on a grey morning with rain coming sideways and found a parcel on her doorstep, wrapped in oilcloth and weighted with a stone so it wouldn't blow away.

Inside: a coat. A woman's coat, heavy wool, dark green, well-made and slightly worn at the cuffs—the kind of coat a person owned who had more coats than she needed. Underneath it, boots, size six, hobnailed, nearly new. And beneath the boots, folded flat so it wouldn't show through the wrapping, a sheaf of ration cards.

Coral stood in the doorway with the rain driving past her ankles and counted the cards twice. Sugar, tea, meat, bread—two weeks' worth, maybe three, in denominations that had been carefully chosen so no single shopkeeper anywhere would remember selling them. Someone had been hoarding. Someone had been hoarding deliberately, quietly, for some time, and had decided this morning to convert the hoard into a message.

There was no note. Of course there was no note. A note would have been a rope tying them together, and the whole grammar of the gift was that it touched nothing, accused nothing, promised nothing. It simply sat on her step like weather.

Coral carried it upstairs and set it on the table, and Greta came to stand beside it, dripping comb-water, her eyes going from the coat to Coral's face.

"For me?" she said. In English now—her English arrived in pieces, nouns first, verbs limping after. "For me, this?"

"It appears so."

"Who?"

"That," Coral said, "is the question."

But even as she said it, she knew. Nobody else on the island bought flour by weight and remembered the weights. Nobody else kept a ledger in their head the way Mrs. Quill kept hers, cross-referencing households the way the Admiralty cross-referenced tonnage. Two stone of flour where one had been sold for eleven years. Milk doubled. Wool purchased by a woman who knitted nothing. Mrs. Quill hadn't deduced the truth from suspicion; she'd deduced it from arithmetic, which was worse, because arithmetic could not be lied to, and neither could Mrs. Quill.

And having deduced it, she had done nothing. Said nothing. Had rung up a single stone of flour with a steady hand and sent Coral home unmarked—and then had opened her own cupboard and given away her own rations and her own coat, and left it all anonymous, so that if the soldiers ever came to her door and asked, *have you aided the keeper of the light in harboring an enemy's daughter*, she could say with a clean heart that she had not, that she found a parcel on her step once and assumed it belonged to whoever lost it.

Coral sat down slowly in Tomás's chair.

She had thought, all these months, that she was alone inside her secret—that she was a fortress with one defender, holding a wall that would come down the moment anyone brought a proper ladder. She had imagined betrayal, discovery, denunciation. She had never imagined this: the wall quietly reinforced from the outside, by hands that refused to be named, in defiance of laws that none of them had voted for.

Greta lifted the coat from the table and put her arms into it, turning at the shoulders the way girls do, wanting the mirror in the coat to be true. The green suited her. It swallowed her a little—she was narrower than the coat's intended owner—but she stood in it with her chin up, and for a moment she looked so much like photographs Coral had seen of Heinrich in 1923, standing proud in front of the lens housing with his tools fanned out like a surgeon's, that Coral had to look away at the window.

"Gut," Greta said. "Good." She smoothed the lapels with both hands. Then, quieter, watching Coral in the glass of the window: "They know. Your people. They know I am here."

"Yes."

"And they—" Greta searched for the word, abandoned English, drew a shape in the air with one finger: a circle, enclosing. "They close around. Like a wall. Yes?"

"Like a wall," Coral agreed.

Greta considered this. Then she said, in her careful, borrowed English: "In Hamburg, when the shops closed to us, nobody closed *around*. Only away." She hung the coat on the hook by the door with great precision, arranging the collar, and Coral understood that the girl was filing this morning away somewhere deep, in the place where people who survive keep the evidence that the world is not entirely one thing.

* * *

After that, the conspiracy grew the way frost grows: invisibly, everywhere at once.

A bag of kindling appeared, split fine, stacked against the cottage wall. No note. Coral didn't ask. Kindling pointed toward Sowle, whose eel-smoking shed produced offcuts of the same pale wood, but Sowle was seventy-three and spoke maybe forty words a season, and confronting him would only cost him words he'd need later for denial.

Eggs began arriving with double yolks, which was statistically impossible unless someone was selecting for it. The fish Dara left on the slab by the gate grew consistently larger than the fish he sold at market, and once there was a lobster in among them, which on this island was practically a love letter. When Coral tried to pay him, pressing coins into his palm at the harbour, Dara looked at the money the way a man looks at an insult he's decided not to catch.

"You're paid by the Commissioners to keep the light," he said. "I'm paid by the sea to catch fish. Neither of us gets to choose where the surplus goes." He picked up his creel and walked off, then stopped, half-turned. "Rough Thursday coming. Knee weather. You'll want your storm glass topped."

He meant the paraffin. He meant: I know the light matters more than ever now, and I am telling you I will see you have fuel. Coral stood on the harbour stones with the rejected coins warming in her fist and felt something open in her chest that she had boarded shut eleven months ago.

That evening she climbed to the lamp room and found Greta at the typewriter.

The girl had taken Tomás's machine apart. That was the sight that met Coral at the door: the typewriter in components across the table, laid out on a cloth in rows, each piece cleaned and ordered, Greta bent over the escapement with a jeweller's loupe pressed to her eye and a pair of fine pliers in her fingers. Salt rust flaked away in orange dust. The keys, black-rusted since the funeral, gleamed.

"Heinrich taught me," Greta said, not looking up. "Machines speak the same language everywhere. This part"—she held up a small toothed wheel—"is the heart. It was sick. Now it is not." She set it down and looked at Coral over the loupe, and her eyes were Heinrich's eyes exactly, patient and bright. "Tomorrow it types. You will see."

Coral stood in the doorway of her own lamp room, in the home she had measured for eleven months in terms of what could be packed and carried away, and watched a German girl rebuild her dead husband's voice, and understood that the trunk had already lost. Whatever the orders said, whenever they came, she was no longer a woman preparing to leave. She was a woman being surrounded.

* * *

On Thursday the storm came as promised, and with it, the test.

Coral saw the patrol boat first—out past the point, sweeping slow arcs along the channel where the fishing boats ran on clear days. Grey hull, the lazy menace of its wake. It was checking the island passage, probably routine, probably nothing, but routine had a way of becoming otherwise, and Greta was downstairs drying her hair by the stove wearing a green coat that fit her like an accusation.

Coral went down the stairs two at a time.

"Coat off. Blanket on. If anyone comes, you're a niece from the mainland, arrived before the weather, too seasick for conversation. Practice looking seasick."

"I do not need practice," Greta said gravely, pulling the blanket around her shoulders. "I have crossed in Dara's cousin's boat."

The knock, when it came an hour later, was not the patrol's. It was Mrs. Quill.

She stood on the step with rain beaded on her shawl and a covered basket in her arms, and behind her, at intervals down the lane, other figures: Mrs. Perry with a jar under each arm, old Sowle with his hat literally in his hands, Dara leaning against the wall with the studied indifference of a man guarding a door by appearing to lean on it. The storm moaned offshore. Nobody spoke.

Mrs. Quill looked past Coral into the cottage, the same way she'd looked in the shop—seeing the larger thing—and then back at Coral's face.

"Bread's still warm," she said. "And you'll be needing to top your storm glass tonight, with that coming." She stepped forward, and Coral, moving on instinct older than caution, stepped aside.

Mrs. Quill entered the kitchen of the most surveilled widow on the coast, set her basket on the table next to the dismembered and reassembled typewriter, and looked directly at Greta—at the blanket, the wet hair, the girl's spine straightening under inspection the way it must have straightened in Hamburg doorways—and said, in the tone she used for discussing the price of mackerel:

"She'll want thicker stockings. I'll send some up with the kindling." She turned to Coral. "Two stone next time, and don't you dare go back to one. Growing girl."

Then she unpinned her shawl, took down the teapot, and began to make tea for four, in a house she had never entered, while the storm came in over the water and the patrol boat swept its grey arc past the point and away.

Coral stood in the middle of her own kitchen and let it happen. The cups came down—Tomás's mother's china, the good cups, the ones she'd wrapped in wool for the trunk—and Mrs. Quill poured, and Sowle came in out of the rain mumbling something about eels, and Mrs. Perry's jars turned out to be damson jam, and Dara finally abandoned his post at the wall and filled the doorway, blocking most of the light and all of the view from outside, which Coral suspected was the point.

Nobody said the word *hide*. Nobody said *German*, or *patrol*, or *if they come for her*. The conspiracy conducted itself entirely in stockings and jam and warm bread and the placement of large men in doorways, and it was, Coral realized, holding her cup too tightly, more formidable than anything Elias Brandt's superiors could send against it. Governments moved by memo. Islands moved by flour.

Greta sat very still in the green coat, watching all of it, and when Mrs. Quill pushed the bread toward her and said, "Eat up, child, you're all collarbone," Greta's eyes filled so suddenly and so completely that she had to look down at her plate, and Mrs. Quill continued pouring tea with the immovable composure of a woman who had decided years ago exactly what she would and would not see.

* * *

They stayed an hour. When the storm's leading edge hit—rain flattening against the windows, the light above them beginning its long rotation into the dark—Mrs. Quill rose, repinned her shawl, and restored the kitchen to order as if she'd been doing it all her life.

At the door, she paused.

"There'll be harder nights than this," she said quietly, to the doorframe rather than to Coral. "Nights when they come asking. My husband was a conscientious objector in the last war—know that about me, since we're knowing things. I buried him with a white feather they'd sent him in his breast pocket, because he wouldn't give them the satisfaction of throwing it away." She settled the shawl. "I'm done letting other people tell me who my enemies are. Good night, keeper."

She went out into the rain. One by one, the others followed, down the lane to their own doors, until only Dara remained, and then only for the time it took him to say, "Fuel delivery Saturday, before dawn. Have your tank key ready," and vanish into the weather.

Coral bolted the door and stood with her hand on it, listening to the storm assemble itself offshore.

Behind her, Greta had gone to the typewriter. As Coral turned, the girl inserted a fresh sheet with the deliberation of a priest preparing an altar, positioned her fingers, and typed—slowly, huntingly, but cleanly, the repaired mechanism ticking like something alive:

**THE MACHINE LIVES. THANK THE WOMAN WITH THE BREAD.**

Coral read it twice. Then she pulled a chair to the table beside the girl's, and reached past her, and began to type beneath, one careful letter at a time, her hands remembering the choreography of keys the way feet remember stairs:

**SHE IS NOT A WOMAN WITH THE BREAD. SHE IS A WALL THAT WALKS. AND SO ARE THE OTHERS. WE ARE NOT ALONE ANYMORE.**

Greta read it. Her mouth made a small, wondering shape.

Outside, the wind rose to a register Coral had heard only twice before in her life—a sound like the whole Atlantic clearing its throat—and the light above them wheeled on into it, faithful and blind, sweeping its beam across a sea that was already, far out past the point, carrying something toward them that neither of them could see yet: a boat, a file, a flag, or a man with a census book, coming back across the water.
