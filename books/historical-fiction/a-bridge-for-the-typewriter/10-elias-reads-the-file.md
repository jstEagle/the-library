## 10. Elias Reads the File

The ferry from the island took nine hours in a following sea, and Elias Brandt spent all of them on deck with his collar up, watching the light shrink behind him until it was only a rhythm—dark, bright, dark—and then not even that.

He told himself he had come back with nothing. That was nearly true. He had come back with a census ledger containing forty-one names, three of them seasonal, one disputed (the Quill woman's lodger, who may or may not have existed before the winter), and a marginal note about Coral Okafor that read, in his own careful hand: *Widow. Competent. Possible foreign correspondence — unverified.*

Possible foreign correspondence, unverified. Six words. He had written them on the boat out, in the first hour, when the wind was still at his back and the whole exercise still felt like an exercise. By the fourth hour he had taken out his pencil to strike them and by the fifth he had put the pencil away again without striking them, because a man who struck notes was a man who had something to hide, and a man who had nothing to hide had no reason to strike anything.

That was the arithmetic of the work now. Everyone understood it. You did not need to be guilty; you needed only to be interesting.

The mainland had gone greyer since autumn. The harbor office flew its flag at an angle that suggested the halyard had frozen rather than any particular enthusiasm, and inside, the corridors smelled of wet wool and coal gas and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by many worried men. Elias checked in at the recording desk, signed the ledger with the date backward—he always did this, a small private rebellion or perhaps a small private error he'd chosen to call rebellion—and carried his field case up two flights to the annex where the regional files were kept.

His superior was named Hargreaves. Hargreaves had been a customs man before the reorganization, and he had brought with him the customs man's great gift: he could look at a person the way he once looked at a crate, and know without opening it whether the manifest was honest.

"Brandt." Hargreaves didn't rise. He was standing at the window with a cup of something he wasn't drinking. "The island."

"The island," Elias agreed.

"How many souls?"

"Forty-one. Thirty-eight resident year-round."

"And your impressions?"

Elias had prepared for this question on the ferry. He had prepared three versions of it, actually—a full version, a medium version, and the version built entirely of weather and fishing yields. What came out of his mouth was none of them.

"Poor," he said. "Isolated. Self-sufficient past the point of politeness. They'll give you trouble if you ever try to requisition their boats, but they're no threat to anyone."

"Hm." Hargreaves turned from the window. "And the keeper. Okafor. The widow."

The name in the other man's mouth did something unpleasant to the air. Elias was aware, suddenly and acutely, of the file already sitting on Hargreaves's desk—the island file, the regional file, whatever it was called this month—and of himself standing next to his own contribution to it like a man standing beside a confession he hadn't written yet.

"Diligent," Elias said. "She keeps a better light than the manual requires. Her logbooks are exemplary."

"I didn't ask about her logbooks."

"No," Elias said. "You didn't."

Hargreaves looked at him for a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he sat down, opened a drawer, and slid a folder across the desk—not toward Elias, exactly, but into the space between them, the way you might set down a card you weren't sure you wanted to play.

"Your preliminary report," Hargreaves said. "Type it up properly. I want it filed by Friday." A pause, calibrated. "Include everything you observed."

*Everything.* Elias picked up the folder because there was nothing else to do with his hands, and left.

* * *

His rooming house was four streets from the harbor office, under a roof that leaked in a manner the landlady described as *character*. Elias climbed to his room, lit the stove, hung his coat to dry, and sat at the small desk with the folder unopened in front of him while the kettle worked itself toward a whistle he would ignore.

He knew what the report had to say. He had known since the boat. The honest version went like this:

*Coral Okafor, widow, keeper of the light since the death of her husband Tomás Okafor, 1934. Subject maintains correspondence in German. Subject denied knowledge of the language when questioned, then corrected herself. Letters concealed within the optical apparatus of the lamp—location disclosed only under direct questioning, and even then reluctantly. Correspondent believed to be Heinrich Vogel, engineer, formerly resident on the island in a civilian capacity, pre-war. Vogel taught the late husband his trade. Their relationship appears to have been familial in character. Subject's loyalty indeterminate but her conduct, over three years of solitary service, has been beyond reproach. She keeps the light. In winter. Alone.*

And then the last line, the one the form didn't have a box for: *This recorder requests guidance.*

He had typed the first draft of the dishonest version too, on the ferry, before he'd made himself stop. The dishonest version was shorter. It said: widow, competent, no irregularities. It said nothing about German, nothing about letters, nothing about the way she had poured tea for him—the enemy, the intruder, the man with the census book—in her dead husband's chair, and how she had done it not out of fear but out of something that looked unbearably like courtesy, as if hospitality were a discipline she practiced against her own instincts.

The kettle screamed. He made tea he didn't want and drank it anyway.

Here was the problem, laid out as plainly as he could lay it: he had spent six weeks learning to see the way Hargreaves saw, and now he could not stop. He had looked at Coral Okafor's kitchen and catalogued the wear patterns. He had looked at her ledgers and noted the steadiness of the hand, the absence of tremor, the discipline of it. He had watched her lie about the German and had thought, with something adjacent to admiration: *she lies the way she keeps the light. Reliably. On schedule. Against all weather.*

And he had understood, standing in her doorway with his notebook, that she was protecting someone. Not herself—you did not hide letters inside a lens housing to protect yourself; you hid them there because they were precious, and precious things belonging to widows were, almost always, other people.

So now the watcher sat at his desk with two reports in his head and discovered that the difference between them was not truth versus falsehood. Both versions were true. That was the trap of the new arithmetic: the file did not record what you believed. It recorded what you were willing to write down, and what you wrote down followed a person forever, through every drawer and every reorganization, arriving years later in rooms you couldn't imagine, read aloud by strangers to other strangers, while the subject stood somewhere with her hands at her sides.

He thought of her hands at her sides. He thought of her saying, evenly, *I don't recall receiving correspondence,* and the half-second pause before it, which he had timed out of professional habit and now could not time out of his memory.

Elias pulled a fresh sheet into the typewriter. The machine was a bureau issue, heavy, indifferent, the ribbon old enough that the capitals came out faint. He began to type the report.

He typed the weather. He typed the population figures. He typed the state of the pier and the disposition of boats and the fact that Mrs. Quill's lodger did, upon verification, exist, and was named Fennimore, and was bedridden, and was nobody's anything. He typed about Dara O'Leary's vessel, seaworthy, licensed, crewed by one, suspicious only in that Dara had sold him mackerel at an honest price and seemed embarrassed about it.

Then he reached the section marked PERSONS OF INTEREST, and stopped.

The stove ticked. Rain moved across the window in long diagonal strokes. Somewhere below, the landlady was arguing with a delivery boy about herring.

*Coral Okafor,* he typed. And then, after a long moment: *Keeper of the light. Diligent beyond requirement. No irregularities of conduct observed.*

It was a lie of omission, which was the most respectable kind. It would hold. It might even be true, if truth were a thing you could decide by deciding.

He fed the sheet into the folder, and his eye caught the edge of something else in there—paper he hadn't put in. Hargreaves's folder. Of course; Hargreaves had handed him the wrong one, or the right one deliberately, or had simply let him take it, because customs men did not make mistakes with paper. Ever.

Elias sat very still for a moment. Then he opened it.

* * *

The file was thin. Thin files were either good news or very bad news, and the header told him which.

**ISLAND 4 — OKAFOR, C. (KEEPER) — CROSS-REF: VOGEL, H.**

Vogel. Spelled correctly, cross-referenced, dated. His own marginal note—*possible foreign correspondence, unverified*—was reproduced in the file in someone else's typing, which meant his field notes had been transcribed before he'd even left the island, which meant someone on the supply boat had copied his ledger, or Hargreaves had requested the transcription of every recorder's notes as a matter of course, which amounted to the same thing: Elias had never been writing a private notebook. He had been writing, all along, directly into the machine.

He turned pages. His own observations, laundered into officialese. Then the older material, and this was worse, much worse: a shipping manifest from 1926 listing one **H. Vogel, engineer, nationality German**, disembarked at the island, contracted for maintenance of the optic. Photographs from some civic archive—two men at the base of the tower, arms shaded against sun, one of them unmistakably Tomás Okafor, young, squinting, laughing at something off-frame; the other tall, fair, standing close in the easy way of colleagues who have become more than colleagues. On the reverse, in pencil: *T.O. & H.V., lens renewal.*

A friendship, documented. Filed. Cross-referenced. Sitting in a drawer in a grey building waiting, apparently, for him.

And beneath it, the newest sheet, initialed by Hargreaves himself:

*Subject C. Okafor retains possible correspondence with alien-national contact (Vogel). Vogel currently believed residing Hamburg. Assessment: low operational significance at present. Recommend continued observation. Note: field recorder E. Brandt displays atypical personal investment in subject; recommend verification of recorder's own associations before further island deployment.*

*Atypical personal investment.* Elias read the phrase three times. It was precise, bureaucratic, and devastating. Hargreaves had watched him stand in that office defending the competence of a lighthouse keeper and had weighed him, like a crate, and found the manifest wanting.

He was not the watcher anymore. He had not been the watcher for some time—perhaps since the tea, perhaps since the chair, perhaps since the moment on the ferry when he had taken out his pencil to strike six words and put it away again. The file knew it before he'd admitted it to himself. Files were like that. They knew you first.

He should have felt afraid. Some part of him registered that fear was the correct response, that a man flagged in his own department's paperwork ought to tread carefully, submit the clean report, request a nice inland district, count farmers, grow old. The sensible door was right there, open, lit.

But the file also contained this, tucked between the manifest and the assessment, a single line in Hargreaves's hand, undated, almost casual:

*Vogel correspondence suggests family in Hamburg. Daughter, b. 1919. Unconfirmed whether subject remains in contact re: same.*

Daughter, b. 1919. Seventeen, eighteen years old. Unconfirmed whether subject remains in contact.

Elias thought of the lens housing. Of the letter hidden where only a keeper would ever look. Of Coral Okafor lying about German with the reliability of a light against weather, and asking for nothing, and giving him tea in her husband's chair as though courtesy could hold a door shut.

People did not hide letters in lenses for correspondence that mattered nothing. People did not pause for half a second over questions that had no answers worth protecting. Whatever was coming across that water—if it was coming—would come in winter, through storms, past patrols that had doubled since autumn, and it would arrive at a tower where a woman lived alone measuring her life in what fit in one trunk.

He closed Hargreaves's file and returned it exactly as he'd found it, squared to the millimeter, because if he was being watched then he would be watched doing nothing remarkable at all.

Then he took out his own report—the clean one, the omission—and signed it. Let it go into the drawers. Let it say what it said. If the day came when the omission mattered, he would stand in whatever room required him and add, aloud, everything he had left out, and take whatever followed.

There was one more piece of paper to write tonight. This one was simple. He had filled out the form a dozen times for other men; the fields were familiar as his own name.

**REQUEST FOR REASSIGNMENT — RECORDER E. BRANDT**

*District:* Island 4 and associated coastal stations.

*Reason:* Continuity of observation. Recorder is uniquely acquainted with population; relationships of trust established; follow-up verification recommended per regional directive. Recorder notes that the island's records contain discrepancies best resolved in person, and that the approaching storm season makes spring verification impractical. Requests earliest available posting.

He read it over. Every word of it was true, in the way that mattered to forms, and every word of it pointed somewhere Hargreaves did not want him to go—which meant, he suspected, that Hargreaves would grant it, because a customs man's second instinct, after weighing crates, was to keep the suspect where he could see him.

Let him watch, then. Elias would be on the island, watching back, and between the two of them—one of them counting, one of them keeping—there might be just enough overlap of vision to keep a light burning and a girl alive if a fishing boat ever came through fog with a name in its mouth.

He sealed the request, set it atop the report, and banked the stove. Outside, the rain had turned to sleet, ticking against the glass like something small and urgent trying to get in—or out.

On the ferry over, he had watched the island's light fall astern until it was only a rhythm: dark, bright, dark. Lying in the dark of his room, he understood at last why he could not stop seeing it.

It had never been a signal aimed at ships.

It was a promise, kept hourly, by a woman who had decided—years ago, alone, without witnesses—that some things would continue regardless of weather, regardless of orders, regardless of what fit in one trunk.

He slept badly, and in the morning he booked passage on the first supply boat of the season, and found the clerk looking at the sailing list with a small frown.

"You'll want to know," the clerk said, tapping the manifest with a pencil. "Patrols doubled on that run since the new year. And there's a storm system coming down from the north that the forecasters are calling—" he consulted the wire slip pinned to the board, and his frown deepened— "well. They're calling it early."

Elias took the ticket.

*Early,* he thought. Storms were not supposed to be early. Boats were not supposed to be early. Nothing that crossed water in winter, carrying what it carried, was supposed to arrive ahead of anyone's plan.

He packed his case that night with the ticket on top, and did not sleep at all.
