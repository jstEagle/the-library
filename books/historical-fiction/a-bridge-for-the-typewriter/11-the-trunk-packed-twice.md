## 11. The Trunk Packed Twice

The orders came on the second Wednesday of November, folded inside a government envelope that had crossed the water with the mail boat and sat in Mrs. Quill's shop for two days before anyone remembered the lighthouse existed.

Coral knew what it was before she opened it. She had learned to read official paper the way sailors read cloud—the weight of the envelope, the stiffness of the fold, the way the sender's stamp was printed rather than handwritten. Nothing handwritten ever carried bad news so efficiently as print.

She stood at the counter of Quill's shop with the envelope in her hands while Mrs. Quill weighed flour behind her with exaggerated care, the scoop rising and falling like a woman pretending not to watch.

"You'll want to sit down for it, I expect," Mrs. Quill said.

"I've been sitting down for it for eleven years," Coral said. "Since Tomás died. Every envelope's either the transfer or it isn't."

She opened it there, because waiting would have been worse, and read it standing, her thumb pressing hard enough on the counter to whiten the nail.

*By direction of the Commissioners of Northern Lights: be advised that the Okafor Light is scheduled for automation effective the first of February next. An acetylene apparatus with sun-valve will be installed by contracted engineers, weather permitting. The position of principal keeper is accordingly superseded, and the present holder will be transferred to the mainland station at Harrow Point no later than the thirty-first of January, where she will assume duties as assistant keeper under Principal Keeper R. Mowbray.*

There was more—forms to complete, an inventory schedule, a requisition officer who would come to assess fixtures—but Coral had stopped reading after *no later than the thirty-first of January*. The words sat on the page like stones laid across a path.

"Well," said Mrs. Quill. She set down the scoop. "They've finally done it."

"They've finally done it."

"Tea?"

"No." Coral folded the letter along its crease, precisely, the way she did everything now, and put it in her pocket. "No, thank you. I've got packing to do."

Outside the wind had come around to the north, and the gulls were flying low over the harbour wall, which meant weather within three days. She walked home along the causeway road with her collar up and the letter against her ribs like something broken set in plaster.

The light was to be automated. The light was to burn without her.

She had imagined this moment so many times—in the long watches, in the winters when the island shrank to the tower and the cottage and the sky—that she expected to feel nothing at all when it came, only confirmation. Instead what she felt was the specific vertigo of a floor giving way: not grief exactly, but the sudden absence of the thing she had been bracing against. For eleven years the threat of departure had been the frame she built her days inside. Pack light. Keep ready. Love nothing you can't carry. Now the frame had been taken away and she was left standing in the open, holding a letter, and the island looked different—larger, closer, realer—without the possibility of leaving it.

And Greta was upstairs asleep.

That was the thought that undid her, halfway up the cliff path, wind pushing at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades. Not the light, not the transfer, not even Tomás's ghost in every fixture the requisition officer would inventory. Greta Heinrichsdottir, nineteen years old, was sleeping in the cot in the loft room of the keeper's cottage, her wet boots stuffed with newspaper by the stove, her father's name sewn into the collar of the coat Coral had given her—and the crossing out was planned for the last week of January, when Dara said the patrols thinned and the fishing fleets worked the western banks and a boat could slip through to the neutral coast if the weather broke kindly.

The thirty-first of January.

The same week.

* * *

The trunk lived under the bed, and had lived under beds since 1926, when Coral had packed it for her wedding journey and discovered, somewhere in the first year of marriage, that a life measured in postings was best kept always half-ready to move.

It was a good trunk. Brass corners gone green at the seams, leather straps she'd replaced twice, a lock whose key she wore on the same ring as the keys to the lamp room. Inside it, in the order she'd maintained for over a decade, lived everything Coral Okafor intended to survive on: two dresses besides the one she wore, both dark, both mended at the cuffs. A wool coat. Her mother's shawl, moth-holed but sound. Photographs in a tin—her parents, her wedding, Tomás laughing on the gallery rail with his hat blown back. Letters from her sister Ada, tied in bundles by year, the later bundles thinning out until they stopped altogether in 1934 and Coral stopped writing back and neither of them mentioned it. Sewing kit. Bible, unread but carried. The silver hairbrush with three teeth missing. Papers: certificates, Tomás's death notice, her own marriage lines, the deed to nothing.

She had unpacked and repacked this trunk perhaps two hundred times. It was a ritual the way the lighting of the lamp was a ritual—not superstition exactly, but maintenance. As long as the trunk stayed packed, she told herself, leaving would cost nothing. As long as she wanted nothing that wouldn't fit, nothing could take root where it couldn't be pulled up. She had learned that lesson early, watching her mother lose a house and a garden and a pew in a church to a landlord's letter, and she had built her whole life on the arithmetic of it: *what fits in one trunk is what you're allowed to keep.*

Greta's things did not fit in one trunk. Greta's things fit in one oilcloth parcel the size of a loaf, because that was all a girl could carry when she stepped off a fishing boat at night and swam the last hundred yards in November water to avoid the patrol launch's searchlight.

Coral climbed the cottage stairs and stood a moment outside the loft room before going in.

The girl was asleep in the shape of someone used to sleeping badly—curled small, one hand under her cheek, the other hanging off the cot's edge as if reaching for something in the dark. Her hair had dried crooked from the last rain. On the chair beside the cot lay the mending she'd been doing in the evenings: Coral's spare coat, taken in at the shoulders with stitches so fine and mechanical they might have been done by a machine, because that was what German girls learned, Greta had explained, shrugging, *my father says I sew like an engineer, which is not a compliment among the aunts.* And beneath the coat, wrapped in its own cloth, the typewriter ribbon Greta had rewound and re-inked herself, working by lamplight with fingers black to the knuckle, humming something Coral didn't recognize.

In the four weeks since the fog had brought her ashore, Greta had become part of the cottage the way damp becomes part of a wall—not visible everywhere, but structural. She knew the light's rhythms now. She could strip and clean the lens assembly without being shown twice. She had fixed the typewriter, which Tomás's salt air had been killing slowly for a decade, and typed on it at night, letters to her father that couldn't be sent, exercises in a language she was teaching herself out of a primer and Coral's careful dictation. She called Coral *Frau Okafor* still, formally, every time, but she said it the way you say the name of someone you've decided to trust, and last Tuesday, unprompted, she had begun calling the lamp room *upstairs* instead of *the tower*, as if the whole building were one house and they all lived in it.

The thirty-first of January. Coral's transfer date. And Dara's window—the last dark-of-the-moon tides before the spring patrols tightened—sat squarely inside that same fortnight, and no one could say yet which would come first, the boat or the removal van, the break in the weather or the requisition officer with his inventory book.

If she went to Harrow Point, Greta crossed alone. That was the plain arithmetic underneath everything. Dara would take the girl regardless—he'd sworn it with the recklessness of a man who has decided a thing and doesn't want to discuss it—but a single girl of nineteen, alone on a fishing boat with forged papers Coral had been coaching her through, crossing forty miles of winter sea to a coast where nobody waited, nobody vouched, nobody had wired ahead to a cousin of a cousin with a spare room? Coral had made crossings of that kind herself, in miniature, and she knew what they cost, and she knew what the sea did to boats that went out unlucky.

Whereas if Coral stayed—

If she stayed, she disobeyed a direct order of the Commissioners of Northern Lights, in wartime, with a file on her somewhere in a government office that already contained the word *German* written in a census taker's careful hand. She'd be dismissed without pension. Possibly worse. Possibly summoned, questioned, the letters found, the girl found, the word *traitor* doing its work faster than any court could correct it.

She went downstairs and took the trunk out from under the bed.

* * *

Packing was easier than thinking, so she packed.

The dresses first, folded along their old creases so the fabric fell into its grooves like water finding a channel. Then the coat. The shawl, wrapped round the photographs so the tin couldn't rattle. The bundles of Ada's letters—she held the last bundle a moment, the one from the year they'd stopped, and then put it in, because the trunk was not the place for decisions about Ada. Sewing kit. Bible. Hairbrush. Papers in their oilskin wallet, weighted at the bottom where nothing could crush them.

She knelt on the floorboards and worked with the economy of long practice, and it was restful, it was almost narcotic, this fitting of a life into measured space. This was the oldest skill she had. Before keeping a light, before keeping a husband through his last illness, before keeping a secret the size of a girl—she had known how to pack. Everything else in her life had been improvisation. This was craft.

The trunk closed. She buckled the straps, top and sides, and sat back on her heels, and looked at it.

Then she heard the typewriter.

One key struck, tentatively, from the loft room—a single bright metallic note through the floor, the sound of Greta waking and reaching for the machine the way other people reached for company. Then another key. Then a pause long enough that Coral knew she'd heard the trunk buckling below, and was waiting, politely, not to intrude.

Coral put her hand flat on the trunk lid.

Here was the thing she had never let herself think in the four weeks since the fog, because thinking it while there was still time to pretend otherwise would have made it true: she did not want to go to Harrow Point. She did not want the assistant keeper's cot in a stranger's station, or Principal Keeper Mowbray, whoever he was, or the mainland with its streets and its notices and its neighbors who watched. Eleven years of readiness had been built on the assumption that the island was exile and the mainland was home, and somewhere in these four weeks—with three cups on the dresser and a girl's stitching in the hem of her curtain and her dead husband's typewriter alive again in the loft—the assumption had quietly died, and nobody had informed her.

She had spent her whole life keeping the trunk packed so that leaving would cost nothing. And now leaving was going to cost everything, and the trunk didn't matter at all.

Coral unbuckled the straps.

She lifted the lid and looked at her life lying in its neat layers, and then she took it out again, piece by piece, and did not put it back. Not the dresses—they went onto the chair, to be aired. Not the shawl—it went back round her own shoulders, because the cottage was cold and she was done rationing her own comfort against a departure she now chose not to make. The photographs she kept out and set on the dresser face-up, all of them, including the one of Tomás laughing on the gallery rail, which she hadn't displayed in eleven years because displaying it meant admitting the wall was hers.

The letters from Ada she left in the trunk, but she left them there lightly, the way you leave a door unlocked—she would write to Ada again. Soon. There would need to be a great many letters before spring.

What remained in the trunk, once she'd finished, was almost nothing: the Bible, the sewing kit, the papers. The things you keep because a person ought to keep them, not because a person is running. The trunk sat open and mostly empty under the window, and it looked, Coral thought, like a boat that had finally come in and unloaded.

From the loft, the typewriter began properly now—rhythmic, confident, a sentence being born. Greta typing her unsentable letters to her father. Or perhaps, Coral thought, feeling something move in her chest that was not quite hope and too warm to be fear, perhaps tonight it was something else. Perhaps the memoir. Tomás's half-finished pages, with their gaps where the ink had failed, waiting for Greta's engineer's patience and Coral's memory of his voice.

She stood, and brushed the dust from her knees, and went to the window.

The northern sky had gone the color of gunmetal all day, and now it was working itself lower, the swell coming into the sound long and heavy from the northeast. Dara would have seen it. By morning he'd be at the door with his weather face on, saying *three days, maybe four, and then it'll blow for a week,* and the arithmetic would begin again—but this time she'd be counting toward the boat instead of away from it. This time the storm was not the enemy. The storm was cover, if it broke right. The storm was the gap in the wall they needed.

A light burned in the tower above her—her light, still, for eighty-one more days by the Commissioner's count, though she had just privately cancelled the account. Below, in the sound, the water slapped black against the pier. Somewhere out past the headland, in the dark, a fishing fleet rode at anchor, and among its crews a plan was already moving the way plans moved on this island: without paper, without voices, from kitchen to kitchen, until it simply existed.

Three cups on the dresser. One trunk, unpacked.

Coral Okafor turned from the window and went to get the lamp lit early, because the sky was falling and tomorrow Dara would knock, and whatever he asked, she already knew her answer: *We don't wait for the thirty-first of January. We go at the next dark tide—and I'm putting my girl on your boat myself.*
