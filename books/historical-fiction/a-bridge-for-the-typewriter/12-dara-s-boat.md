## 12. Dara's Boat

Dara came up the cliff path at low tide, which was how Coral knew it was serious. The fisherman never climbed unless he had to; his knees had been wrecked by thirty years of hauling nets in weather that would kill a lesser man, and he guarded them the way a miser guards coin. Yet here he was, breathing hard at her door before the morning light had properly decided to arrive, cap in his hands, salt crusted white along his shoulders like a second skin.

"The window," he said, without preamble. "It's closing."

Coral stood back and let him in. Greta was already awake—Coral could hear her moving about in the lamp room above, learning the mechanism by touch the way her father once had—and the girl's presence in the house had become so ordinary in three weeks that Coral had to consciously remember she was a secret, a crime, a girl who did not officially exist.

"Tea?" Coral asked.

"No time for tea." Dara lowered himself onto Tomás's chair anyway, because there was nowhere else to sit and because his knees demanded it. He looked at his hands. "Weather office on the mainland says we've got four days of clear water, maybe five, before the next system comes down from the north. After that—" He made a gesture with one flat hand, a boat sliding under.

"Four days."

"Four days." Dara met her eyes then, and Coral saw that he had not slept. "And the patrols have doubled. I counted three passes yesterday where I used to count one. They're running dark now, no lights, which means they don't want to be seen, which means they're looking for something. Somebody talked, or somebody guessed, or somebody's just being thorough because it's winter and thoroughness fills the hours."

Above them, faintly, came the click of brass. Greta, oiling the clockwork.

"Does she know?" Dara asked quietly.

"Not yet. About the patrols."

"She'll need to know."

"I'll tell her."

Dara nodded slowly. Then he said the thing he had clearly climbed the cliff to say, and said it fast, the way a man rips off a plaster. "I'll run her. Thursday, if the glass holds. Kilmore Sound, neutral flag, there's a trawler out of there that owes me from '31. She can work passage. Nobody asks questions on a boat that smells of fish." He paused. "But I need fuel, Coral. Enough for the crossing and back with margin. I haven't got it. The requisitions took everything over my own needs, you know that."

"How much?"

"Fuel's gone up since the autumn. Twelve pounds gets me what I need. Ten, if I bargain hard and the seller's feeling kind."

Ten or twelve pounds. Coral had four in the tin behind the flour, saved against emergencies she had never let herself name.

* * *

After Dara left, Coral sat at the kitchen table with her accounts book open in front of her and did sums that refused to come out right. She had kept meticulous records since Tomás died—not because anyone required it, but because arithmetic was a form of prayer, each column balanced a small act of faith that the world still obeyed rules. Lighthouse pay, rationed. Egg money from Mrs. Quill, who overpaid shamelessly and would not be corrected. Four pounds, two shillings, sixpence.

She needed eight more. Minimum.

The trunk sat in the corner of the bedroom where it had sat for eleven years, packed and unpacked according to the season of her despair. She went to it now and knelt, and lifted out the things she knew by weight: the good wool coat that had been too warm for this coast but might be needed somewhere colder; the silver photograph frame, empty since the picture had yellowed past saving; the linen, the letters, the small hoard of a woman preparing forever to leave.

And beneath the linen, wrapped in an old chart of the soundings, Tomás's chronometer.

She unwrapped it there on the floor, kneeling, and held it in both hands.

It was a beautiful thing—heavy brass case, the lid engraved with a ship that Tomás swore was the *Maiden's Reach*, though Coral had never found the vessel in any registry. It had kept perfect time through every year of their marriage; he wound it every Sunday at six, after the lamp was lit, and he would hold it to his ear first as though listening for its heartbeat before giving it the key. *A chronometer isn't a watch, Cor,* he'd told her once, when she'd laughed at the ceremony he made of it. *A watch tells you what time it is. A chronometer tells you where you are. There's a difference. A watch is a guest. A chronometer is a compass.*

He had bought it secondhand from a marine dealer in the port town the year before Greta was born—the year before Heinrich went home to Germany and the letters began. Heinrich had helped him choose it. Coral remembered that now with a strange, cold clarity: the two of them bent over the dealer's tray, Tomás uncertain with money as always, Heinrich turning the instrument over in his engineer's hands, opening the back, examining the escapement with a jeweler's loupe he carried on his watch chain. *This one,* Heinrich had pronounced. *The balance spring has been replaced but well. This one will not lie to you.* And Tomás had bought it on his friend's word, and it had never lied to him, not once, in all the years since.

Now she turned it over and opened the back herself, the way Tomás had taught her, and looked at the ticking heart of it, the polished wheels, the balance spring Heinrich had approved. It ticked against her palms like something alive. Like a pulse. Like the small, stubborn insistence of a man long dead that time should go on meaning something.

Eight pounds, Dara had said, at best, for a chronometer in wartime. Less, probably. Dealers knew desperation when they saw it.

She closed the back. She wrapped it in the chart again.

She did not put it back in the trunk.

* * *

She told Greta that evening, in the lamp room, while the light swept its slow white arm across a sea the color of hammered pewter. The girl was standing at the clockwork with an oil can, absorbed, and Coral watched her a moment before speaking—the way she moved now, sure-footed on the gallery grating, unafraid of heights her father had never allowed her to demonstrate. Three weeks had changed her. Her English was growing teeth. And she had repaired the typewriter a week ago, cleaning the rust from its type bars with vinegar and patience until it sang again, and Coral had not yet found words adequate to thank her.

"Thursday," Coral said. "Dara will take you Thursday, if the weather holds. To Kilmore Sound. There's a boat there—a trawler—that will carry you further. You'll work your passage. You won't be traveling as a passenger, so no papers will be asked for."

Greta set down the oil can very carefully, as though it might detonate.

"Thursday," she repeated.

"Thursday."

"And you?" The girl's eyes were Heinrich's eyes, gray and direct, and they went through Coral's prepared speech like water through paper. "You stay here."

"I keep the light."

"That is not what I am asking."

Coral turned to check the lens, though it needed no checking. "My orders may come any day. Transfer. When they do, I go where they send me."

"You could come. On Dara's boat. There is room for a keeper as well as a daughter."

"There is not room for a keeper. And if there were—" Coral stopped. Started again. "If I go, Greta, I go as a fugitive. Everything I am, everything Tomás was, becomes evidence against us both. But if I stay—if I'm here, keeping the light, answering the census man's questions with my chin up—then you are one girl on a fishing boat, and I am a loyal widow who knows nothing. One story protects the other."

Greta was silent for a long moment. Below them the sea worked at the rocks with patient malice.

"My father wrote that you were the bravest person he knew," she said at last. "I thought it was a thing fathers write. Politeness." She picked up the oil can again. "Thursday," she said, and turned back to the clockwork, and Coral understood that the conversation was over because Greta needed it to be over, and that grief, in this family, was handled privately, in engine rooms, with tools.

* * *

Wednesday morning Coral rowed the tender across the sound to the port town, the chronometer wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of the boat, and told herself all the way across that she was only getting it valued. That knowing its worth was prudence, not surrender. The tide was with her, which she chose not to read as an omen either way.

The dealer's shop was called Marner & Son, though there was no son anymore—there had been a war, and the son was under a white cross in Flanders, and old Marner had never taken down the sign. He sat in the back among clocks, surrounded by their uneven ticking, like a man in a ward full of uneven heartbeats.

Coral laid the chronometer on his counter and unwrapped it.

Marner put down his loupe-pen and his tea, and picked it up, and Coral watched his face do what faces did: the flicker of appetite, quickly mastered. He opened the back. He held it to his ear. He turned it under the lamplight and read the maker's mark and the serial number and something else, scratched small inside the case cover, that made him look up.

"This belonged to Thomas Okafor," he said. "Of the light."

"It did."

"And the engraving here—" His thumbnail indicated the ship. "*Maiden's Reach*."

"You know her?"

"I knew her." Marner set the chronometer down with unexpected gentleness. "I serviced her instruments, years back. Before your husband had her." He was quiet a moment. "I heard he died."

"Three years ago. The eleventh of March."

Marner nodded. He opened the back again, and ran a finger near the balance spring, and Coral saw the exact moment the dealer became the craftsman, the appetite giving way to respect. "Well replaced," he murmured. "Whoever did this work knew what he was about. Better than factory. See how he compensated—" He caught himself, closed the case. "Eight pounds," he said. "It's worth twelve to a collector, but collectors aren't buying, and I've got to make my margin. Eight."

"Ten," said Coral. "And you service nothing. You sell it exactly as it is, scratch inside the cover and all, to someone who will wind it Sundays."

Marner looked at her for a long moment over the counter covered in dead men's watches.

"Nine," he said. "And I'll sell it to whoever appreciates it, which is all any of us can promise about anything."

"Nine," said Coral, "and you keep it out of the window. Somewhere it won't get salt air on it."

"Madam, my whole shop is salt air."

"Then somewhere innermost."

They shook on nine pounds, and Marner wrote out the receipt, and Coral took the money in notes and one half-crown and put it in her coat pocket where it lay against her body, heavy and light at once, like a coal and a feather.

At the door she stopped.

"The scratch inside the cover," she said. "Did you read it?"

"I did."

"What does it say?"

Marner cleared his throat. "'For T., who keeps faith with the dark. H.'" He looked uncomfortable. "Sentimental, the Germans."

"Yes," said Coral. "Appallingly." And she went out into the gray morning before her face could do anything Marner might put into his ledgers.

* * *

The crossing back was harder than the crossing out; the wind had backed north, which Dara would later call the first lie of the coming system. Halfway across, spray came over the bow and soaked her to the waist, and Coral found herself laughing—a short, ugly bark of laughter that scared a gull off the gunwale—because she had just sold the truest compass she owned to buy gas for a boat she would not be aboard, carrying a girl she had known three weeks, and the arithmetic of it was so absurd, so entirely unlike anything in her accounts book, that laughing was the only available response to the sum.

*A chronometer tells you where you are.*

Tomás, she thought, pulling at the oars, breath burning, wind trying to take the words out of her mouth: *you are here. You are exactly, precisely, unbearably here.*

* * *

That evening she counted the money out on the kitchen table in front of Dara: nine pounds plus her four, thirteen in all, more than enough even at the inflated rate. Dara looked at the notes, and then at her face, and then at the bare place on the shelf where the chronometer had stood for eleven years between the barometer and the jar of spare wicks, and Coral watched him understand exactly what he was looking at, item by item, the way fishermen read water.

"He'd have done the same," Dara said finally. "For her."

"I know. That's rather the problem."

Dara took seven pounds and pushed the rest back across the table. "Seven covers it, bargaining hard like I said. Keep the rest. Fuel for the tender. Winter's not done with us."

He stood to go, knees cracking, and at the door he paused with his hand on the latch, and did not turn around, and spoke to the wood.

"Glass is falling faster than the weather office thinks. I've been doing this water forty years and I can feel it in the moorings." He was quiet a moment. "Thursday morning, early tide, before first light. If I don't like the sky when I wake, I'll send no signal and you keep her hidden another day and think no ill of me."

"And if the sky holds?"

"Then I take her across, and God and the patrol boats can argue about the rest." He opened the door onto darkness and wind. "One more thing, Mrs. Okafor. There was a navy launch in the sound today. Not the usual coastal one—a bigger hull, and they weren't fishing." He pulled his cap down. "They came close enough to my nets that I saw the officer on the bridge. Young fellow. Civilian coat, navy escort. Taking bearings."

The door closed behind him.

Coral stood a long time in the kitchen with the leftover notes in her hand and the wind rising outside, and tried to persuade herself that civilian coats on navy launches meant surveys, inspections, routine. But she had spent eleven years reading this coast, and eleven years reading Elias Brandt's careful face, and she knew what bearing-taking looked like when it was aimed at a lighthouse that was already exactly where charts said it was.

They were not measuring the island.

They were measuring who came to it.

Upstairs, in the lamp room, Greta was winding the clockwork for the night, and the light swept out over the blackening sea, and somewhere out in all that darkness, early tide was fourteen hours away and falling.
