## 13. Interrogation by Lamplight

The boat came at dusk without a horn, without a flag, sliding out of the grey water like something the sea had decided to return. Coral saw it first from the gallery—a long dark hull, no markings she could read at that distance—and her hands went cold on the rail before her mind had finished the arithmetic. Supply boats came on Tuesdays. Patrol boats came with sirens. This one came silent, and silence was its own kind of announcement.

She went down the spiral stairs two at a time, which Tomás had forbidden and which she did now anyway, because the years since his death had taught her that some rules belong to men who are no longer here to be protected by them.

Greta was in the kitchen, mending a net with Mrs. Quill's borrowed needle, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, a smudge of lamp oil along one forearm like a brushstroke. She looked up when Coral came in, and whatever was in Coral's face must have been legible even across the language between them, because Greta set down the net before Coral said anything at all.

"Boat," Coral said.

"Patrol?"

"No." Coral listened to herself say it. "Worse. Quiet."

There were three places Greta could go: the loft behind the oil stores, where the heat would give her away by morning; the cliff path, which ended in nothing but rocks and a rising tide; or under the floorboards of the front room, where Tomás had built a crawl space in 1931 to store spare wicks and brass fittings, and which Coral had kept swept and dry for reasons she had never once said aloud, not even to herself. She had told herself it was for storage. She had known, in the way you know a letter is going to come before it comes, that she was keeping a hollow ready.

"The boards," she said. "Now. Take the lamp. Take your shoes. If I tap twice—two taps—stay still no matter what you hear. No matter what."

Greta was already moving. That was Heinrich in her, Coral thought—the economy of motion, no wasted gesture, a girl assembled out of engineering principles. At the edge of the trapdoor she stopped, looked back once, and said something fast in German that Coral didn't understand and didn't need to. Then she was down, and Coral slid the boards over her, and dragged the rag rug across them, and put Tomás's chair on top of the rug because a room should look lived-in and a chair is the most innocent thing in any house.

Then she opened the door before anyone knocked, so that knocking would never happen, so that the house would look like it had been expecting no one and got whoever it got.

* * *

The man who climbed the path from the landing wore civilian clothes cut like a uniform and boots polished past the point of practicality. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the pleasant unhurried face of a man who had made a career out of being underestimated. He carried a leather case and an umbrella he hadn't needed; the storm was holding off tonight, gathering itself somewhere out west, saving its breath.

"Mrs. Okafor." He knew her name. Of course he knew her name. "Inspector Hale, Coastal Security. Forgive the hour—we don't always choose our tides." He smiled as though he'd said something charming.

"You'll have come a long way for nothing," Coral said. "I keep the light. There's no one else on the island but Dara O'Leary, and he sleeps like the dead."

"I'm sure Mr. O'Leary does." Hale stepped inside when she stepped aside—not invited, not refused, simply through, the way water goes through a gap. His eyes moved over the room the way Elias Brandt's never had. Brandt had catalogued; this man appraised. She felt him take inventory of the typewriter under its cloth, the three cups on their shelf—one chipped, all mismatched—the rug, the chair, the small damp rectangle where the rug met the floorboards and had not yet dried from the sea air that came up through every crack on this island.

*Every crack*, she thought. *He will notice the cracks.*

"You'll want tea," she said, because offering was what keepers' wives did, and because her hands needed employment more than they needed steadiness.

"Thank you, yes." He sat in Tomás's chair without asking, and something in Coral's chest closed like a fist. "You've had visitors lately, I understand. A census officer. Brandt."

"He counted me in October. One soul, one trunk, one light."

"Mm." He accepted the tea and held it without drinking, turning it slowly, watching the surface. "Mr. Brandt's reports are thorough. Very thorough. Sometimes thoroughness raises questions of its own." He looked up then, and his eyes were not pleasant at all. "Tell me about the Germans, Mrs. Okafor."

"There are no Germans here."

"On this island, no. Perhaps not today." He let that sit. "But your late husband trained under a German engineer. Heinrich Voss. The optical works at Bremerhaven. Nineteen twenty-one through nineteen twenty-six. A remarkable correspondence continued afterward—I'm told there were letters. Quite a number of letters. Over quite a number of years."

Coral poured her own tea and sat opposite him, spine straight, hands wrapped around the cup so the tremor would have something to hide in. Under the floorboards, beneath her own two feet, a girl was breathing. Coral made herself think of that breathing as a sound she could not hear, and therefore did not exist, and therefore must never be given a reason to stop.

"My husband learned his trade from many men," she said. "That's how trades are learned."

"And after the war? After nineteen-fourteen?"

"After the war my husband came home and took postings wherever the Board sent him. We came here in 'twenty-nine. He died in March of last year. You may verify all of it with the Board, with the parish register, and with God, if you have connections there."

Hale laughed—a real laugh, brief and genuine, and somehow worse than a smile. "They warned me you'd be difficult. They said, *the widow keeps the best light on the coast and knows it.*" He set the cup down. "Mrs. Okafor, I'm going to be frank, because frankness saves everyone time. Enemy nationals maintained contact with certain persons on this coast during the years leading up to the present emergency. Some of that correspondence has been recovered. Some of it mentions a lighthouse. An island. A woman described as—" he consulted a card he drew from his case, though Coral would have bet her husband's chronometer he knew the words by heart—"described as *braver als die Angst*. Braver than the fear."

Under the floorboards, silence. Coral kept her face still and her heart hammering against a door she would not open.

"German is a language," she said. "It contains many sentences. If someone wrote flattery to my husband about his work, that's no crime of mine. And if letters existed, the sea has taken better archives than mine. Ask the winter of 'thirty-four."

"So they're destroyed."

"So I couldn't produce them if you drowned me for them, which I'd thank you not to attempt, as I'm the only keeper this light currently has."

Hale studied her for a long moment. Then, mildly: "May I see the rest of the house?"

* * *

He went through it the way a customs man goes through luggage—politely, thoroughly, touching everything and owning nothing. He lifted the cloth from the typewriter and ran one finger along the carriage, and Coral watched the dust come away on his glove and thought, absurdly, *Tomás would have minded that.*

"Whose machine?"

"My husband's."

"He typed?"

"Badly. With two fingers and great conviction." She heard her own voice do something it hadn't done in months—lighten. It startled her, the ease of the lie, and then she understood: it wasn't ease, it was craft. It was the same craft as trimming a wick in a gale, hands steady because they must be, because forty boats depended on the flame and terror was a luxury scheduled for later. Courage was a performance. The terror played underneath, in a locked theatre, to an audience of one.

"He was writing something," Hale said. "A memoir, Brandt's notes suggest. Where is it?"

"Burned." This lie came hardest, and she watched herself make it anyway, smooth as pouring. "Paper rots here. Everything rots here. The sea takes the words and the damp takes the paper and what's left is a widow and a light."

"Hm." Hale replaced the cloth over the typewriter with exaggerated care, smoothing the corners, and Coral understood that the courtesy was itself a message: *we know the machine is recent, we know the ribbon is fresh, we know a memoir burned so cleanly leaves no smell of smoke in a house that smells of salt and lamp oil.* But he wanted more than suspicion. Suspicion filled files; convictions filled careers. He needed the girl, or the letters, or the confession, and he did not have any of the three.

He stood in the center of the front room last, and Coral felt the whole island hold its breath. His gaze traveled the floorboards, plank by plank, the way a man reads a page. Down there, six inches beneath his polished heel, Greta Voss lay in the dark with her hand pressed over her own mouth.

"The tide comes up under these old houses," Hale said conversationally. "Rot sets in from below. Boards loosen. Things fall through." He looked at her. "You'd hear it, wouldn't you? Anything loose?"

"I hear everything," Coral said. "It's the job. The fog bell, the wind, the mechanism. A keeper learns to sort the sounds that matter from the ones that don't." She lifted her chin. "Nothing down there but Tomás's spare wicks. Would you like me to show you, Inspector? I can move the furniture myself. I've done heavier."

It was the gambit she hadn't planned and only recognized after saying it: offer the thing you cannot afford to hide, and watch whether he wants it enough to pay the price of taking it. Because moving the chair, rolling back the rug, prying up boards in the home of a lighthouse keeper while a storm stacked itself on the horizon—that required a warrant, a witness, paperwork. And Hale had come quiet, unannounced, in a boat with no name. Whatever he was doing tonight, it was not yet official. She had understood that from the umbrella. A man planning a search brings men. A man fishing alone brings an umbrella he doesn't need.

Something shifted behind his pleasant face—respect, or recalculation, or the particular disappointment of an angler watching a fish refuse the hook.

"No need," he said. "Not tonight."

* * *

He stayed another twenty minutes, drinking tea he never finished, asking questions arranged like stepping stones: When had the supply boat last come? Did she receive mail regularly? Had a fishing boat sheltered here during the October fogs? Was she acquainted with the shopkeeper Quill? The fisherman O'Leary? The census officer Brandt?

To each she gave the truth, trimmed and salted, or the lie, planed smooth. Yes, the boat came Tuesdays. Mail came with it, when weather allowed. Boats sheltered here in fogs; that's what harbors were for. Mrs. Quill sold her flour. Dara sold her fish. Brandt counted her and drank her tea and left, and if the Board had complaints about its officers' tea consumption, she suggested he take those up with the Board.

At the door, Hale paused with his case in hand.

"One more thing," he said. "Off the record, as it were." He turned the collar of his coat up against the wind that had finally begun to arrive, dragging the storm in behind it like furniture. "The woman who wrote those letters—Voss's daughter, if she exists, if such a person ever set foot anywhere near this coast—she'd be charged under the emergency regulations. Alien in time of war, aiding the enemy, the whole catalogue. But." He looked out at the darkening water, and for one moment his voice lost its polish. "I read the file, Mrs. Okafor. All of it. The father spent nine years keeping British lights burning true. Nine years, and then they interned him anyway, and he died in a camp on a wrong charge that took eighteen months to unpick—after he was already in the ground." He turned back to her, and his eyes were tired in a way that no amount of boot-polish could disguise. "So if such a girl ever did wash up on this island, in this storm season, in this weather—well. The sea gives and the sea takes. Who could prove otherwise? Not I. I see very poorly at night. Always have."

Coral stood very still. The lamp above them turned its patient circles. Somewhere beneath her feet, a girl was listening to a stranger decide, sentence by sentence, whether she would live.

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Coral said.

"No," Hale agreed. "I don't imagine you would." He descended the path, and she watched until the boat's running light shrank to a spark, and then to nothing, and the sea folded shut behind him.

* * *

She waited ten minutes. Then twenty. Then her own knees informed her that standing was over.

Two taps on the boards—her signal reversed, her hand shaking so badly the second tap landed soft. She pulled up the floorboards and reached into the dark, and Greta caught her arms, and for a moment neither of them moved, keeper and cargo, both of them trembling in different languages.

Greta climbed out into the lamplight, pale as paper, hair plastered to her skull with sweat, and looked around the room—at the chair back in its place, the rug lying flat, the three cups on their shelf—as though confirming the world had survived without her.

"He asked," she said, in careful English. "About the letters."

"He asked about everything."

"What did you tell him?" Greta's eyes searched Coral's face. "You lied to him. I heard. Your voice never—" she made a small gesture, level, horizontal, steady as a spirit gauge. "Never moved. Not one degree. How do you do that? How do you lie with your heart going like that—" she touched her own chest, "—and the voice stays flat?"

Coral looked at her own hands. They had gone cold now, in the aftermath, the way they always did—the bill arriving after the meal.

"Twenty years of storms," she said. "You learn to keep the light steady while everything else shakes. It's the same muscle."

Greta crossed the room and did something she had never done: she took Coral's cold hands in her warm ones and held them, engineer's fingers, calloused at the pads, and said something low and fierce in German, and then translated herself, word by word, as though translation were a gift she could wrap:

"*Braver than the fear.* He wrote that about you. In the letters. I used to ask him—who is this woman braver than the fear? And he said, *the keeper's wife who packs her trunk every year and never leaves.*"

Coral laughed—a short, cracked sound with grief inside it, because the trunk ritual, her secret shame, her private madness, had been observed all those years ago and filed away in German affection like a specimen worth keeping.

Outside, the storm arrived in earnest, the first great gusts slamming the cottage walls, the tower groaning its familiar note. And through it, faint but unmistakable, cutting across the water from the direction of the mainland lights: a patrol siren. Then a second. Then the deep-throated horn of a naval vessel, far out, sweeping the channel.

Greta went to the window. So did Coral. Out in the black, searchlights were waking along the coast, one after another, like eyes opening down the length of the shore.

"They weren't looking for me tonight," Greta whispered. "They were getting ready to."

Coral watched the lights sweep the water and felt the next chapter arrive in her chest like a change in pressure. The window was closing. Whatever was going to be done—the crossing, Dara's boat, the neutral port—would have to be done now, in the teeth of the worst weather in a generation, or never done at all.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're going to see a fisherman."
