## 14. The Typewriter Speaks

The typewriter had been Greta's project for three weeks before Coral understood what she was doing.

It began with the small sounds. Coral had grown used to the girl's presence in the cottage the way she'd grown used to weather — by its noises. The scrape of the chair. The kettle's whistle at half past seven, always a minute early because Greta could not bear to let it scream. And now this: a new sound threading through the others, metallic and patient. A key striking. Then another. Then a pause, and the carriage bell, and the zip of the platen returning.

Coral stood in the doorway of the back room with a basket of mending she had no intention of doing, and watched.

Greta sat at Tomás's desk in Tomás's chair, her spine straight as a mast, her hands hovering over the keys of the Underwood that had sat rusting on that desk for four years. The machine gleamed. That was the first thing Coral noticed — gleamed, where it had been furred orange with salt-spray rust since the week after the funeral. Someone had stripped it down to its bones and rebuilt it. Someone had oiled every pivot, cleaned every type slug, trued the carriage rail with what looked like jeweler's patience. The ribbon was new; Coral didn't know where Greta had gotten ribbon, and had decided some months ago that there were certain questions a keeper learned not to ask.

Greta's fingers came down. *Clack.* Came down again. *Clack-clack.* She typed the way she did everything — with an engineer's economy, no wasted motion, her eyes moving ahead of her hands like a helmsman reading water.

She was typing German words. No — not quite. English words with German bones, sentences that leaned left the way Heinrich's letters did, and between them, in the margins of the page, little pencil annotations in two hands: one small and slanted (Greta's), one that made Coral's chest tighten so suddenly she had to grip the doorframe.

Tomás's hand.

He'd written his memoir in German. Of course he had. Fourteen years working beside Heinrich Brandt at the Nordlicht light outside Bremerhaven had left Tomás Okafor thinking in two languages and writing in the one that felt like home. When they'd married, when they'd come out here to this rock at the edge of everything, he'd kept the habit — drafts in German, fair copies in English, pages of each stacked in the drawer Coral had locked the day after the funeral and had not opened until the requisition officer forced her to.

Coral had read none of it. She had told herself it was prudence — a censor's eye, a widow's caution. But the truth was simpler and worse. She could not bear to hear his voice. Not the voice in her head, which never stopped, but the voice on paper, which had stopped forever on a Tuesday in March when the sea took the dinghy and gave back only an oar.

So she had locked the drawer, and Greta had picked the lock — or rather, hadn't bothered; she'd simply asked, in her careful broken English, holding up the key-ring like a surgeon asking for a scalpel: *"May I? It is stuck. Machines do not like to be forgotten."*

That had been three weeks ago. Now the typewriter spoke.

* * *

"What is it you're writing?" Coral asked that evening, though she knew.

Greta looked up. Lamplight caught the fine bones of her face — Heinrich's face, younger, unlined, the same stubborn jaw. "Translation," she said. "Your husband's book. He writes—" she searched, her English fraying at the edges the way it did when she was tired or moved, "—he writes like the sea. Long waves. You must wait for the whole wave to know what the sentence says."

"And in English?"

"Shorter." A small smile. "I am making him shorter. I am sorry."

"Don't be." Coral set down the mending basket she'd been carrying around all day like a prop in a play she couldn't remember auditioning for. "Why?"

Greta turned in the chair and pulled the top page free. She held it out across the desk, and for a moment Coral saw her hesitate — saw the question pass behind her eyes, the same question Coral herself lived inside: *is this wanted, is this mine to give.*

"He begins with your father," Greta said. "Page one. He says he would have been nothing without the man who taught him knots."

Coral took the page the way you take something hot from a fire — quickly, before you can think better of it.

*My name is Tomás Okafor, and I was taught the light by a German, and if that is a crime then it is the only crime I am proud of.*

Her breath went out of her.

She read it twice. Three times. The words were Greta's — shorter, cleaner, the long German waves compressed into English breakers — but underneath them, unmistakable, was the cadence she'd fallen asleep to for nineteen years. The dry turn at the end of a sentence, the joke folded flat into the last clause so you almost missed it. *The only crime I am proud of.* He would have said exactly that, standing at the gallery rail with the wind pulling his words apart, grinning at whoever was near enough to catch it.

"You've been at this three weeks," Coral said. Her voice came out strange. Level. Keeper's-voice, the one she used in gales. "Every evening."

"Yes."

"While I pretended not to hear."

"Yes," Greta said, and then, gently, with the directness that still startled Coral sometimes, the directness of someone raised by Heinrich: "You were not ready. I waited for the machine. The machine is ready now. So maybe you are too."

* * *

They worked through February the way lighthouse people work through anything: by watch rotation.

Coral kept the light — winding the clockwork, polishing the lens, logging wind and visibility in the ledger in her small square hand. Greta translated. And in the hours between, they read aloud.

That had been Greta's idea, offered shyly over the third cup Coral had caught herself setting out and had stopped apologizing for. "If I translate alone, it is my book. If you correct me, it is yours. If we read it aloud—" she'd shrugged, "—then it is everyone's. Your husband's most of all."

So each night after supper, while the storm-season wind worried at the shutters, Coral read Tomás's German draft pages and Greta read back her English typescript, and where they differed they argued — good arguments, fierce ones, about whether *Seelenlicht*, soul-light, should stay German because English had no word for what Tomás meant, and about whether a sentence describing the 1926 fog season should keep its full length or be cut "for momentum," a word Greta had learned from a smuggled magazine and deployed with relish.

And page by page, Coral met her husband again.

Not the ghost she'd been carrying — the ghost was heavy and silent and sat in corners. This was another man entirely, or rather the same man seen whole: young Tomás arriving at the Nordlicht in 1913 with rope-burned hands and a chip on his shoulder wide enough to land a boat on; Tomás learning lens optics from a man who drew diagrams in the margins of letters the way other men doodled hearts; Tomás in the war years, keeping a light for a nation he wasn't sure he belonged to anymore, writing, in a passage Greta rendered with visible care:

*Heinrich and I polished the same glass for thirty years. They tell me now that his country is my enemy. I have looked for the enemy in my memory and cannot find him. I find a man who once gave me his coat in a snowstorm and lied about how cold he was. If loving that man is treason, then let them hang me with the logbook for a rope.*

Coral read that passage aloud three times before she could go on. On the third reading her voice held. Greta watched her with wet eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the page hadn't already said better.

But it was the last chapter that undid her — and Greta saved it for last deliberately, Coral realized later, the way you save the deepest water for the final sounding.

The chapter had no title in the draft. Just a date: March 1933. The month of Tomás's death. The month he'd gone out in the dinghy against Coral's warning, against the barometer, against every rule he'd spent thirty years teaching other men to obey — and the reason why had been a sealed question ever since, a hole in the hull of her grief she'd learned to sail around.

The page explained it.

*Heinrich has written that things are going dark in Germany. He asks me — old friend, old brother — if there is any place under my light where his daughter might stand safely if the day comes. I have answered yes. There is room in one trunk, room in one bed, room under one lamp. I told him: send her when the storms are worst. No patrol boat sails in the worst weather, and no one watches a lighthouse in a gale except the people who love it.*

*I am going to the mainland tomorrow to arrange the papers. Coral will call it foolishness. Coral is right. But a man who keeps a light for strangers cannot refuse one to a friend.*

The page ended there. The rest was blank. The dinghy had gone out the next morning, and the sea had closed over the arrangement, and for four years Coral had believed her husband died for nothing — for a supply run, for vanity, for the ordinary stupidity of experienced men in small boats.

He died for Greta. For the promise. For the bridge.

Coral set the pages down very carefully on the desk, aligning their edges, because her hands needed a job that wasn't shaking.

"He never told me," she said.

"He was protecting you," Greta said. "My father does this also. Protects by silence." She paused. "It is a terrible habit. We must both break our fathers' habits."

"Our fathers," Coral repeated.

Greta went pink to the ears. "Forgive me. My English—"

"No." Coral reached across the desk and put her hand flat on the stack of fresh typescript — forty-one pages, double-spaced, the ink still smelling faintly of machine oil and the lemon polish Greta used on the keys. "Our fathers. That's exactly right."

* * *

They made copies the way conspirators make copies, which is to say carefully and more than once.

The original English translation stayed in the cottage, wrapped in oilcloth inside the flour tin Mrs. Quill had started leaving provisions in — a tin that had migrated, without discussion, from doorstep to pantry, and now served as the least secret secret cache on the island. One carbon copy went into the lens housing itself, flat against the brass beneath the counterweight channel, where Heinrich's last letter had lived for a year and where no requisition officer short of a demolition crew would think to look. A second carbon went to Dara, who stowed it under the floorboards of his boat without asking what it was, saying only, "Paper floats badly. Best keep it dry," which was Dara's way of swearing an oath.

"Insurance," Greta said, watching Coral seal the lens-housing copy in waxed paper. "That is the word? Insurance?"

"That's the word."

"In Germany we say it differently. We say—" she frowned, hunting the phrase, "—we say: a thing written down can be defended. A thing only spoken belongs to whoever shouts loudest." She tapped the stack of pages. "This is us, shouting quietly. My father's friendship. Your husband's. Yours. Mine." She looked up. "If they come for us, this speaks first. Before we do. Before we can be made afraid."

Coral thought of Elias Brandt's notebook, the neat columns of persons-of-interest, the questions asked too precisely. She thought of the tribunal she sometimes imagined at 3 a.m., the gray room, the gray men, her own voice failing her at the crucial moment the way voices did in nightmares.

"No," she said slowly. "It won't be made afraid. It'll do the making."

She carried the last carbon copy to the desk, slid it into the drawer that had been locked for four years, and left it unlocked. Some things were done being hidden.

Then, because the light needed trimming and the clockwork wanted winding and grief, however transmuted, does not excuse a keeper from her rounds, she climbed the ninety-one steps to the lantern.

At the top, with the great lens turning its patient circles and the February dark pressing against the glass, Coral allowed herself the thing she'd refused for four years. She spoke aloud, to no one, to Tomás, to the room.

"I read your book," she said. "You great secretive fool. You wonderful man."

The light swept out, touched the water, came round again.

Below her, faint through the stairwell, she heard the typewriter begin again — clack, clack-clack, the bell, the return — and she understood that Greta wasn't translating anymore. New words. Fresh words. A letter, perhaps. Or a beginning.

Two days later she found out which.

Greta brought the page to breakfast and laid it beside the bread like a summons. Typed, dated, addressed in full formal style to the District Maritime Tribunal, care of the mainland office. Coral read the salutation twice.

*"To the Tribunal: We, the undersigned — Coral Okafor, keeper of the light; Greta Brandt, daughter of Heinrich Brandt, engineer — submit the following testimony concerning the loyalty, character, and unfinished work of Tomás Okafor..."*

"It is finished?" Coral asked.

"It is ready," Greta said. "There is a difference. Finished means nothing more to say." She glanced toward the window, where the barometer had begun its slow slide toward the red, and the horizon wore the bruised, glassy calm that every islander on that coast had learned to dread. "Ready means we say it when they come."

Outside, far out past the point, where the water met the sky in a hard silver line, Coral saw something that made the tea go cold in her cup.

A boat. Gray hull, low profile, running without lights — and turning, slow and deliberate, toward the island.

The patrols had found them a reason to visit.
