## 15. Elias Returns

The supply boat came on a Tuesday, two weeks after the gale that had nearly taken the gallery rail, and Elias Brandt stepped off it looking like a man who had argued with himself the whole way across the water and lost.

Coral watched from the lamp room as he came up the path with his census case in one hand and a second parcel under his arm—bread, by the shape of it, though he carried it as if it were evidence. The wind pushed at his coat and he let it. That was new. The last time he'd climbed this hill, two months ago, he had walked against the weather like a man determined not to be moved by anything, wind or widow.

She went down to meet him because it would have been stranger not to. Greta was in the lamp room above, going through the turning mechanism with an oil can and a rag, and Coral had made her promise—had made herself promise—to behave as if the young woman polishing brass overhead were nobody at all. Invisibility was a discipline. She had practiced it for years. She only wished her hands would stop remembering how to shake.

"Keeper Okafor." Elias set the case down on the low wall beside the door. "I'm conducting follow-up verification. Household composition, occupancy dates. Standard procedure."

"On an island of eleven people."

"The form doesn't distinguish." He said it without smiling, but something in his mouth wanted to. He held out the parcel. "From Mrs. Quill. She said to tell you the flour order came through."

Coral took it. Through the paper she could feel the warmth where it had sat against his chest on the crossing, and she thought: *he knows*. Not everything—she couldn't say how she knew that he didn't know everything—but enough that he'd stood in Mrs. Quill's shop and let a woman who hated officials load him like a mule for the climb.

"You'd better come in," she said. "The wind's picking up and the verification will want tea."

Elias's POV:

The file weighed four ounces and felt like ballast.

He had read it nine times since London returned it stamped in red: **FLAGGED — SECONDARY REVIEW — PHYSICAL INSPECTION RECOMMENDED**. Nine times, and each reading the same words arranged themselves into the same future: a launch from the naval depot, men in grey with a warrant and a list, the door of the lighthouse opened the way you open a door you have already decided what's behind.

*Subject maintains correspondence suspected of enemy-origin. Correspondent identified as H. Brandt-adjacent engineer, German national, pre-war residency confirmed. Recommend inspection of premises and effects. Priority: moderate. Discretion: advised.*

His own report sat behind hers, the one he'd written in October, the one with the German connection carefully omitted, reduced to *household of one, no anomalies noted*. Someone upstairs had pulled the older files anyway. Someone upstairs had done the arithmetic he'd refused to do, and now his omission sat in the record like a fingerprint on glass.

They knew he'd left something out. They hadn't proved what. Yet.

So he had requested reassignment back to the island—*continuity of survey*, he'd called it, *incomplete returns, weather-disrupted enumeration*—and his superior had approved it with a look that lasted two seconds too long, and now Elias was sitting at Coral Okafor's table drinking tea from the third cup while a girl's footsteps crossed the ceiling of the lamp room above them, light and quick and utterly damning.

He could hear her. That was the thing. He could hear her moving around up there, and Coral could hear him hearing her, and neither of them said a word about it, which meant they both understood exactly what the silence cost.

"The census," Elias said, and set his cup down. "I need to ask you some questions. And I need you to decide, before you answer any of them, whether you're going to lie to me again."

Coral did not move. She had a way of becoming still that most people mistook for calm. He had learned better. Stillness, in Coral Okafor, was a fortification going up.

"I answered your questions truthfully the first time," she said.

"You told me you learned your German from a phrase book."

"Tomás had one."

"Tomás had three books in English and none in anything else. I checked the requisition inventory when I verified your husband's effects." He kept his voice level, unhurried—the voice of forms and figures, the voice that gave nothing away. "You told me there were two people on this island in the autumn count. There are eleven now, by my last tally, and I counted footprints coming up your path that suggest the number has moved again. You told me you had no correspondence. Your hands, Keeper, went white when you said it. I write things down for a living. I notice."

Above them, the footsteps stopped.

Coral looked at the ceiling. Then at him. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous than anger.

"How long have you known?"

"Since October."

"And you've done nothing."

"I've filed a report," Elias said. "That is what I do. I observe, I record, I transmit. It is—" He stopped, because the sentence he had been about to say—I *t is my duty*, or *it is all I am*, or possibly *it is the only shelter I have ever found for myself*—was true in ways he wasn't ready to say aloud at a stranger's table. At anyone's table.

Coral poured him more tea. Her hand, he noticed, had gone steady now, the way it did when the worst had been named. Fear lived in the unnamed places. She had clearly decided there was nothing left in this room unnamed.

"Then file another one," she said. "Tell them whatever keeps your conscience the shape it needs to be. But if there are men on that boat next time, you'll drink your tea somewhere else while they go through my husband's things, because I won't watch you do it twice."

"There are going to be men on the boat," Elias said.

The wind filled the pause that followed, pressing at the shutters, moaning in the chimney. Above them, very softly, the footsteps started again—and then deliberately, loudly, descended two steps of the stair and stopped. Listening. Choosing to be heard rather than hidden. Something in Elias's chest turned over at that: courage performed as an announcement. *I am here. Whatever happens at this table, I am here.*

"Within days," he went on, before Coral could speak. "A launch from the depot. Inspection warrant, physical search, removal of documents and effects for review. Your file is flagged for secondary examination. My report contradicts their intelligence summary, which means either I falsified a census record or you concealed correspondence with a foreign national, and they have already decided which explanation is more convenient. Falsified records make trouble for me. Concealed letters make a traitor of you." He laid his own file on the table between them, open to the page headed *HOUSEHOLD COMPOSITION*, his neat columns, his careful omissions. "There is a third option. It requires you to trust me, which you have no reason to do."

Coral looked at the page for a long moment. Then she reached over and turned it face down.

"Say it plainly," she said. "I've spent fourteen years learning to hear what men don't say. Say it so I can't mistake it."

Coral's POV:

He said it plainly. She would give him that—he had come up a hill in the wind carrying bread from Mrs. Quill and a lie in his briefcase, and he set the lie down on her table like a man laying down a card he'd been holding too long.

"I can amend the record," Elias said. "Household composition revised due to enumeration error—my error, noted and corrected. One additional resident: a domestic ward, minor, refugee status pending, placed under your guardianship by prior arrangement with the correspondent in question before his internment. There is paperwork for such placements. There is a whole category of paperwork for exactly this. Girls without countries, keepers without families. The system invented the form; it can hardly object when someone uses it."

"And Heinrich's letters?"

"Gone." He said it without flinching. "Burned, lost to damp, surrendered to me for destruction—any version you like, as long as it's gone before the launch comes. If they find letters signed by a German engineer in this house, no form protects you. If they find a guardian's petition and a ward's papers, then you are a bureaucratic inconvenience, and bureaucracies forgive their own inconveniences. Do you understand what I'm offering?"

She understood. What she was still working out, watching his long fingers flat on the table, was why.

"Why?" she asked. "In October you sat where you're sitting and took me apart question by question. You were good at it. You liked being good at it." She heard the cruelty in it and did not take it back. "What changed?"

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. He looked at the teapot, at the window, at the ceiling where Greta waited on the stairs. Then he reached into his coat—not the case, his coat—and brought out a photograph, creased once, and laid it where the census page had been.

Two young men on a lighthouse gallery, arms slung round each other's shoulders, laughing at whoever held the camera. Tomás, dark-haired, sunburned, twenty-six years old and already in love with the sea's worst moods. Beside him a taller man, fair, angular, one hand raised mid-gesture as though explaining the machinery of joy itself. Heinrich. She knew him from his letters, from Tomás's half-typed pages, from the flashback reel of stories she'd heard a hundred times and never tired of—but she had never seen them together. She had never seen Tomás laugh like that.

"My brother," Elias said quietly. "Heinrich taught me to read tide tables before he taught me anything else. He wrote to me until 1933. Then his letters changed, and then they stopped, and then my country and his began doing the arithmetic that ended in war." His thumb moved along the crease. "I came to this island in October to count one widow and close a file. I stayed longer than I needed to. I asked more questions than the form required. I told myself it was thoroughness." He met her eyes. "It wasn't. It was me trying to find out whether the woman my brother loved like a sister had become something I needed to be afraid of. And then I sat in this kitchen and watched you lie badly to protect something, and I realized I didn't care what it was. I cared that you were protecting it the way he would have."

The wind slammed a shutter somewhere above. Neither of them jumped.

"He never mentioned you," Coral said softly. "Fourteen years of stories and he never—" She stopped. "No. That's not right. He mentioned a nephew who visited one summer. Who was afraid of the gulls." She looked at the boy in the photograph, scowling off-camera, maybe twelve years old, furious at the birds. "That was you."

"That was me."

Coral laughed—a short, cracked sound that startled her coming out, the first laugh the house had produced in longer than she could account for. She pressed her hand over her mouth and then lowered it, because hiding it felt suddenly like a habit belonging to a woman she was finished being.

Greta came down the stairs then. She must have decided the listening was over, or that whatever was being decided at the table concerned her enough to walk into it. She stood in the doorway in Dara's borrowed jersey, oil on her hands, chin up, and looked at Elias the way you look at weather you've decided to walk through anyway.

"This is Greta," Coral said. It was the first time she had said the name aloud to anyone on the island, and saying it made the girl real in the room, made her a fact instead of a secret, and Coral found she did not want to take it back. "Heinrich's daughter. My brother's—"

"Ward," Elias said. "As of today, officially, my niece by placement, under your guardianship, per amended record." He drew a second folder from the case, thinner than the census book, its pages already filled in his small exact hand. Dates. Signatures awaiting. A guardian's petition with Coral's name printed neatly in the space reserved for it. He had written it all on the mainland, she realized. Before he ever boarded the boat. He had sat at some desk in London and built this family on paper, line by line, and then carried it across the water in a briefcase like contraband—which was, she supposed, exactly what it was.

"You'll need to sign," he said. "Both pages. And the letters, Keeper—"

"Will be gone by tomorrow night." She said it and felt the cost of it go through her like a hook through cloth. Heinrich's handwriting. Tomás's name in another man's sentences. Fourteen years of proof that she had once been loved by two people at once. "I'll keep the memoir," she added, and her voice came out harder than she intended. "The typed pages. Tomás's own words. If your tribunal wants to know what kind of traitor kept this light burning alone through every blackout order and every convoy night, they can read what his brother-in-trade wrote about him. That stays."

Elias considered her across the table. Then he nodded, slowly, and she understood that he had expected the argument and had already lost it before arriving.

"That stays," he agreed. "And one more thing." He leaned forward, and the official voice dropped away entirely, leaving something younger and rawer underneath. "The inspection launch is scheduled for Friday. But the barometric readings this week—" he tapped the case, where she glimpsed charts folded among the forms "—are wrong in a way I don't like. The depot meteorologist is calling it a moderate depression. I grew up on this coast. Moderate depressions don't drop thirty points in forty hours." He looked at her, and then up, toward the lamp room, toward the great lens waiting in the tower above them like a held breath. "If that storm arrives before Friday, it buys you time. If it arrives *with* Friday—"

"It won't matter what's in the file," Coral finished. "Because there won't be a boat that can reach us, and there won't be a light unless I keep it burning."

They looked at each other across the table, the keeper and the counter of souls, and between them the tea went cold in three cups that no one remarked upon.

Outside, far out past the shoal where the water deepened from green to slate, the sea had begun, very quietly, to organize itself against them.
