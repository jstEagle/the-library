## 16. The Great Storm

The barometer began to fall on Tuesday afternoon, and by evening it had fallen so far and so fast that Coral stood at the kitchen window watching the needle and thought, not with dread but with a strange calm: *early*.

The almanac gave the storm another four days. The plan gave them three. Dara was to run Greta to the neutral port on Friday's tide, when the forecast promised a lull between systems—a window Mrs. Quill had circled in pencil on her calendar like a date for a christening. But the glass in Coral's kitchen did not care about calendars. It dropped through Thursday night and by dawn on Wednesday it sat lower than she had ever seen it, and the horizon had gone a color she had no name for, a bruised pewter that made the sea look solid.

She went up the tower stairs two at a time and opened the lantern door to the wind. It came through the gap like a living thing, shoved her back a step, filled its lungs. Far out, the swell was already building—long, green, wrong. The gulls had gone silent hours ago. That was the oldest warning there was, older than any instrument: when the birds stop, get your boat out or nail everything down.

Greta was below, at the typewriter, her fingers moving with that engineer's certainty Coral had come to love without ever saying so. Coral put her head around the door.

"The window's gone."

Greta's hands stopped. She looked up, and Coral watched the understanding move across her face the way weather moves across water—fast, total.

"Then we go now," Greta said. Her English had sharpened all winter; she still reached for articles sometimes, still said *the sea* where Coral would have said *it*, but there was nothing broken in her now. "Or we do not go at all."

"I know."

"Then I will pack."

"Greta." Coral came into the room. The lamp through the window behind her threw her shadow long across the floorboards, across the trunk that sat half-packed against the wall, across the girl herself. "If you wait, the search comes first. If you go—" She stopped. There was no kind way to finish it. *If you go, you go onto that water.*

"If I stay," Greta said quietly, "I am found in your house, and they take us both, and everything Tomás wrote and everything my father wrote becomes evidence instead of memory." She stood. She was taller than Coral had first thought, the first day in the fog; she seemed to grow every time Coral looked away. "You told me once that the light does not choose its ships. It only burns. So. We burn."

Coral had no argument left that wasn't just fear wearing an argument's clothes.

They got Dara's signal off by nine—a single cloth hoisted on the flagstaff, the agreed sign, *come early, come now*—and by eleven Dara's boat came around the headland with the first spits of spray already breaking over her bow, and Dara himself at the tiller looking like a man who had argued with God and lost but was going to make his point anyway.

He came ashore at the slip, hauled the dinghy up himself, and stood dripping in the lamp room doorway while Greta gathered her one bag.

"Window's closing," he said. "I've seen it close faster than this. I've seen it open again after. I don't trust either of those facts."

"You don't have to take her," Coral said.

"Yes, I do." He said it flatly, the way he'd say the tide table. Then he looked at her, and something moved under the flatness. "Coral. The patrols doubled yesterday. Two boats off the southern channel, steam, not sail. If I'm stopped—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They all knew what happened to a fisherman found carrying a German girl, and what happened after that to the woman who'd sheltered her.

"Then go before the front hits," Coral said. "Outrun it. You can."

"On that water." He shook his head slowly. "Nobody outruns anything today. We leave when she's ready and we pray the storm is slower than a bad engine, which it never is."

That was when the knock came at the downstairs door—not the islanders' knock, which was a fist and then silence because everyone on the island let themselves be heard rather than seen, but three measured raps, evenly spaced, a mainland knock, a clerk's knock.

Coral's heart performed a small cold arithmetic before her feet even moved. *Three raps.* Only one man knocked like that.

Elias Brandt stood in the rain with his collar up and no census book anywhere about him, and the first thing he said was not hello or forgive me but:

"It's coming in ahead of itself. Fourteen hours early, maybe more. The mainland's pulling its boats; I heard it at the harbor office before I crossed." Rain ran off the brim of his hat. Behind him the sea had begun to whiten at the crest. "And Coral—the file. Your file. The flag went up Tuesday. A launch is coming for the search. Not next week."

"When?"

"After the storm clears. That's the thinking, anyway—they won't send anyone out into this. Which means the moment it breaks, they come." He stepped inside when she moved aside, and stood dripping on the mat, and looked at Greta standing there with her bag in her hand as if he had always known exactly what this room contained. Perhaps he had. "So it's today."

"It's today," Greta said.

Elias took off his hat. Water sheeted from it. For a long moment he studied the girl the way he must once have studied names in his ledger, and Coral braced for whatever came next—the arrest, the questions, the terrible clerical correctness of him.

Instead he said: "Which boat?"

"Dara's," Coral said.

"Dara's engine flooded in October. Half the island knows it; he fixed it with parts he traded off the relief keeper at the Skerries, and none of those parts were new." He put the hat back on. "I'm telling you the odds are bad. I want you both to understand that I know the odds are bad, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make this easier."

"We know the odds," Greta said. "We have known them since before you were born, I think."

Something flickered across Elias's face—not offense. Recognition. He nodded once, sharply, like a man closing a ledger line.

"Then I'll say the other thing," he said. "Whatever happens on that water tonight—whatever anyone sees from any patrol boat, in any light—the record will show what I write in it. That's all I have. It isn't much. But it's what I have." He turned and went back out into the rising wind before either of them could answer, and Coral stood in the doorway watching him walk down toward the slip, and understood that she had just watched a man cross a bridge and burn it behind him, and that he had done it without ceremony, the way he did everything.

* * *

They got Greta aboard at half past twelve.

The sea had come up fast—it was doing things water should not do, moving in three directions at once, the swell from the south crossing the wind-driven chop from the southeast so that the whole surface seemed to be arguing with itself. Dara's boat rose and fell in the lee of the slip like something trying to sleep through noise. Mrs. Quill was there, though nobody had sent for her, with a parcel of bread and boiled eggs wrapped in oilcloth, thrusting it into Greta's hands and saying nothing, absolutely nothing, her face doing all the talking a face could do.

Dara checked the engine. It caught on the third pull, coughed, steadied.

"Get below," he told Greta. "Stay low. If I shout, you bail. That's the whole of your job."

She climbed down the side and disappeared into the wheelhouse and then, at Dara's gesture, below it, and then it was only Coral on the slip and Dara at the tiller and forty feet of black-green water between them, slapping the stone like a thing impatient.

"Coral." Dara had to raise his voice over the wind now. "The light. Whatever else happens tonight—that light has to burn. There'll be boats out that aren't mine. The mail boat didn't make harbor; she turned back for the sound. If your light goes dark—"

"I know my work, Dara O'Flaherty."

"I know you do." His jaw worked. "It's not your work I'm worried about. It's your heart. A light needs a steady hand and a cold eye, and tonight you'll have neither, because my passenger down there is the nearest thing to a daughter you've got, and if you hear me come to grief on that rock and you're up in that tower holding a lamp—"

"Then I hold the lamp," Coral said. "That's the whole vow, Dara. That's all it ever was."

He looked at her a long moment through the rain. Then he grinned—Dara's terrible grin, the one he wore at funerals—and put the tiller over, and the boat swung out into the channel, and Coral stood on the slip and watched it shrink into the gray until there was nothing left of it, not even the smoke, and the sea closed over the place where it had been as if it had never been at all.

* * *

The storm arrived properly at four o'clock, and it did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a verdict.

There was no gradual rising of the wind such as she had known in twenty years of keeping lights. There was a wall of sound coming out of the southeast, and then the wall was upon them, and the rain did not fall but traveled horizontally, in freight-loads, striking the tower's east face like thrown gravel. The whole structure leaned into it and groaned—an old, deep, structural voice Coral had never heard from it before, and hearing it now she felt the animal fear rise in her throat and swallowed it down where all the other fears lived.

She worked.

That was the whole of her religion and she performed it now with both hands. She lit the mantle and watched it catch, blue then gold. She wound the clockwork that turned the lens, checking the mercury bath, checking the weights, her fingers finding each familiar mechanism in the failing light without needing to see it. The great lens began its slow rotation, gathering the flame and throwing it seaward in its long white blade, and Coral watched the beam swing out into the chaos and felt—as she always felt, but never so much as tonight—that small stubborn miracle: *I am here. I am lit. Come find me.*

By six, the island was drowning.

She could hear it even through the lantern's thick glass—the sea coming over the top of the low ground where the cottages stood, coming for Mrs. Quill's shop, coming for the slip, erasing the distinction between land and water that men had spent ten thousand years pretending was permanent. A piece of the jetty went by in the white, whole planks cartwheeling. Something that might have been a roof.

At seven the clockwork jammed.

She heard it happen—a hard clunk somewhere below her feet, the rotation stuttering, stopping, the lens frozen with its beam pointed uselessly at the land while out on the water the darkness swung unmarked. She went down the tower stairs in the dark, taking them too fast, cracking her shin on something, not caring, into the service room where the mechanism sat behind its glass case, and found the trouble at once: a pin, a wretched little brass pin, sheared clean through with the vibration, the whole train of gears locked on its broken tooth.

Her hands were shaking. That was the truth of it. Her hands were shaking and would not stop, because down in the black water somewhere between here and the neutral port was a fishing boat with Greta in it, and every second the beam pointed the wrong way was a second Dara was steering blind, and her body knew this even if her training pretended otherwise.

*Steady hands and a cold eye,* Dara had said. She pressed her palms flat against the cold iron until the shaking had nowhere to go but inward, and breathed, and got the pin's stub out with pliers and a patience she did not feel, and fitted a spare from the drawer that Tomás had stocked fifteen years ago with a foresight that reached, tonight, across his own death to keep strangers alive.

The mechanism shuddered, caught, resumed. Upstairs the beam began its sweep again.

Coral sat down on the service room floor, in the dark, with the storm hammering the tower like fists on a locked door, and allowed herself thirty seconds of being a woman alone on a floor. Then she got up and climbed back to the light.

* * *

She saw Dara's boat at nine-forty, or believed she saw it—a suggestion of movement in the maelstrom south of the sound, a dark shape lifting and vanishing, lifting and vanishing. Impossible to say. Impossible to know whether they'd made the outer channel or been driven back or—

The thought refused completion. She fed it to the fire instead. She kept the wicks trimmed and the glass clean and the clockwork wound, and the storm came back again and again like something with a grudge, testing the tower, finding it standing, testing again harder.

At half past eleven, the door below hammered.

Not three measured raps. A full-body hammering, desperate. She went down with the storm howling through the stairwell behind her and hauled the door open on Elias Brandt, half-drowned, hatless, holding onto the frame with both hands as if the wind had personally appointed itself his adversary.

"The sea's over the road," he shouted. "I couldn't get back to the cottage. And—" He stopped. He was looking past her, up the stairwell, at the rotating glow. "Your light's burning."

"Of course my light's burning."

"The mainland towers are dark. Both of them. The telegraph office said the storm took the power along the whole coast." He came inside, sagging against the wall, water pooling under him. He looked, for the first time since she'd known him, entirely young. "Coral, do you understand? Yours is the only light burning between here and the sound tonight. Everything out there is steering by you."

Everything out there.

Dara. The mail boat. Whatever other hulls the storm had shaken loose from their moorings, their harbors, their sense. All of it steering by the blade of light she was keeping, all of it trusting a woman it had never met to stand awake in a tower all night with shaking hands and a heart full of a girl on a fishing boat.

The wind struck the tower again, that deep structural groan running up through the stone and into her bones.

"How long?" Elias asked. "How long does it blow like this?"

She looked at the barometer on the wall. The needle lay flat against the pin at the bottom of its travel, as far down as the instrument could measure, and beyond.

"Till it stops," she said. "Come up, Elias. If we're the only light on this coast, then we're going to need more than two hands to keep it."

He followed her up the stairs, and above them the great lens turned, and turned, and turned, and out on the black water—somewhere, impossibly, still somewhere—Dara's boat ran for the channel with Greta below decks, bailing, bailing, bailing.
