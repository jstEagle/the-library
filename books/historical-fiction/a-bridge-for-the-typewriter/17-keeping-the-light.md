## 17. Keeping the Light

The clock in the lamp room said four minutes past eleven when the sea came over the gallery rail.

Coral heard it before she saw it—a sound like the whole Atlantic drawing breath—and then the glass above her went white, and water burst through the storm pane on the seaward side, not a leak but a fist, a column of it, smashing across the floorboards and taking with it the brass bucket, Dara's oilskin from its hook, and half a lifetime of her certainty that this tower would outlast anything.

She was on her knees in six inches of freezing water before she understood she'd been knocked down.

"Coral!" Elias's voice, somewhere near the service door. "Coral—"

"Here." Her own voice surprised her. Flat. Practical. The keeper's voice, Tomás's voice. "The pane's gone. Get the shutter."

He came out of the dark with the iron hurricane plate clutched to his chest like a shield, and together they wrestled it up over the broken frame while the wind did everything in its considerable power to take it from them. His hands were bleeding. Hers were too; she noticed distantly, the way you notice weather in another country. They got the bolts through. Two of three. The third hole wouldn't line up and the plate sang against the frame like a struck bell, but it held.

Behind them, the light turned.

That was the thing. That was the whole of it. However the night went, the light had to go on turning.

* * *

Greta had the lamp glass steady.

It was not a job for two hands—it took both of hers wrapped in the flannel rag Coral had torn from an old petticoat, braced against the housing where the great lens breathed its slow heat—but Greta held it the way her father must have taught her to hold things, with an engineer's economy, no wasted tension, just grip and patience. The whole tower shook around her. Spray hammered the remaining panes. Twice the vibration had nearly walked the mantle off its seat, and twice the flame had guttered down to a blue whisper before Greta steadied the glass and coaxed it back.

"She is frightened," Greta said, meaning the lamp. She said it without drama, the way you'd report a fact about a colleague. "Frightened lamps make bad light. You have to hold them still."

Coral, hauling the second oil can toward the winding gear, thought: she has been talking to machines her whole life, this child of an engineer, and every one of them has answered her better than people ever answered me.

"Talk to her then," Coral said. "Keep her calm."

And Greta did—murmuring in German, low and constant, words Coral couldn't parse but didn't need to. It was the same tone Tomás had used with the mechanism, the same tone Heinrich's letters had carried even across the censor's desk: *I know you. I know what you're for. We'll do this together.*

The clock crawled. The wind found new registers. There was a note in it now, high and sustained, that Coral had never heard in thirty years of storms, and she understood, with the cold clarity that exhaustion brings, that this was the one the old men meant when they said *the great gale*, the one that came maybe twice in a century and took boats, houses, coastlines. Dara was out in it. Dara was out in it because of her, because of the girl holding the lamp steady, because of a promise made in German in 1924 and never once reneged upon.

The light would hold or Dara would die. There was no softer arithmetic.

"Winding gear," she said aloud, and Elias was already there.

* * *

They worked by call and answer, the way crews do, and by two o'clock Coral had stopped thinking of him as the census man at all.

"The weight," he said, both hands on the crank.

"Full drop. Wind it smooth—smooth, don't fight her."

He wound. She watched the cable, called the turns. When the weight reached the top he sagged against the gear, breathing hard, and she saw that his palms had opened along the lifelines and were bleeding freely, and that he hadn't mentioned it because he knew mentioning it would change nothing.

"Mr. Brandt."

"Elias." He didn't look up. "If we're going to die in the same room, you might as well use my name."

"If we're going to die in the same room," Coral said, "we're doing it badly. The light's still burning."

Something moved across his exhausted face that was almost a smile. He straightened, put his hands back on the crank.

It had occurred to her, sometime in the last hour—somewhere between the third rewinding and the moment the gallery door began to drum in its frame like something demanding entry—that she knew almost nothing true about this man. He had come to her island with a government book and a government face and he had catalogued her, weighed her, found her wanting. And now here he stood, blistered and salt-soaked, winding a dead man's clockwork so that a fisherman could find his way home past a rock that had drowned forty men before either of them was born.

"Why are you here?" she asked. Not suspiciously. There was no room left for suspicion. Just the question, raw.

Elias kept cranking. "Because in March I wrote a man's name in a book," he said, "and in October a boat will be trying to reach a harbor, and only one of those things will matter tomorrow." A turn. Another. "Because I have spent nine years writing down who people are, and tonight is the first time I've done anything about it."

The cable paid out clean. The light turned. Below them, Greta's voice rose and fell, patient as tide.

"That girl," Elias said quietly. "When the patrol comes. When all of it comes. I want you to know what I'll say."

"You don't have to—"

"I wrote her father's name in March," he said. "Tonight I'm unwriting it. That's all. That's the whole of my confession, Mrs. Okafor, and I've chosen the strangest possible place to make it."

The wind chose that moment to find the tower's fundamental frequency and lean on it. The whole structure groaned—a long, animal sound, timber and iron speaking with one throat—and the clock swayed on its bracket and the flame ducked and Greta's hands tightened on the glass and the light swung wide, wide, wide—

—and came back.

Coral let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since Tomás died.

"There," she said, to the lamp, to the girl, to the night. "There."

* * *

At half past three the sea took the boathouse.

They heard it—a concussion under the wind, a sound like a door slamming in the earth itself—and then nothing, and the nothing was worse. Coral went to the landward pane and looked down into the chaos where the path ran, where the boat shed had stood since before her marriage, and there was only moving water, gray-black, filled with the island's small belongings.

Dara's punt was up there too. Everything was up there. Everything except the *Merrow*, which was out in the middle of it, running for the light.

"He'll see us," Greta said. She had come off the lamp only long enough to look, her hands still wrapped in the rag, holding them against her chest like injured birds. "He will see us, won't he?"

Coral looked at the flash sweeping out across the waste of water. Four seconds bright, seven dark. Their old signature. The one Tomás had chosen, the one Heinrich had calculated, the one printed in every pilot book from here to Norway.

"He's seen it a thousand times," she said. "He'll be watching for it."

"And if the light stops?"

"Then he watches for it anyway," Coral said, "and doesn't find it. So it doesn't stop."

She went back to the gear. Behind her she heard Greta return to the glass, and resume the low German murmur, and if the girl's voice had thickened, none of them remarked on it. There was work, and then more work, and the night measured itself out in windings—in drops of the weight, in checks of the oil feed, in the endless small corrections of hands against failure. Coral's blisters broke somewhere past four. She wrapped her hands in strips of the same petticoat and kept going. She had a sudden clear memory of her mother saying, decades ago, in a kitchen an ocean away: *a woman's hands heal, child; it's the rest of her you have to watch.* She laughed out loud, once, alone with the mechanism, and the sound startled her more than the storm had.

* * *

Near dawn the wind changed its mind.

Not gently. It didn't die; it turned—swung a full quarter in the space of twenty minutes, which every sailor's bone understood as worse than the blow itself, a sea crossed against itself, standing up in heaps. But the direct assault on the tower eased, as though the storm, having spent five hours trying to pull the lighthouse apart stone by stone, had decided instead simply to drown the world around it and leave them to witness.

In the first gray light Coral counted what remained. The storm pane gone, shuttered with iron. The gallery rail buckled inward like a rib. The boathouse: gone. The path to the landing: gone, presumably, along with most of the landing. The goat shed had migrated twenty feet and survived, tilted, with the goat inside it complaining—a sound that undid Greta entirely, and the girl sat down on the wet floorboards and laughed until she cried, and Coral knelt and held her, and Elias stood at the broken window with his ruined hands hanging at his sides, looking out at the water like a man reading a page.

Then he stiffened.

"Sail," he said. "Or smoke. There—north of the channel mark."

Coral was up. Out across the heaving gray, right where the channel should be, something small and stubborn was making its way toward shelter—a boat, wallowing, half-swamped, flying a scrap of canvas that might once have been a jib. Too small for a patrol boat. Wrong shape entirely.

"Dara," Greta whispered.

They couldn't signal anything the light wasn't already signaling. There was nothing to do—that was the unbearable part, the part Coral would carry for years: three people in a tower, watching a boat fight for its life a mile off, able only to keep the light burning in daylight, uselessly, faithfully, because stopping it was unthinkable and starting it again might fail.

So the light turned on into the gray morning, four seconds bright, seven dark, and the little boat crawled in behind the channel mark, and found the lee of the point, and was there, and then was not visible, which meant—Coral made herself understand—meant harbor. Meant alive.

Greta slid down the wall to the floor. Elias sat down beside her, abruptly, like a marionette with cut strings. Coral stood at the broken pane with the wind pulling her hair loose and the light sweeping round and round above her head, and she thought: *Tomás. Did you see. We kept it.*

* * *

They came up the path an hour later, four of them, bent double against the dying wind—Dara in front, gray-faced, soaked to the soul, and behind him three figures Coral didn't know, strangers, island men, and one of them carrying a sea chest.

Dara stopped in front of her at the door of the tower. Water ran off him in ropes. He looked at the light still turning overhead, then at her, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

"The boathouse is on the beach at Kilmore," he said finally. "Half of it. I passed it coming in." A pause. "The *Merrow* held. She's holed above the waterline and she'll need a new forestay and probably a new skipper's nerves, but she held."

"Good," Coral said, because there was no other word big enough.

Dara looked past her, into the tower, and his eyes found Greta—small and salt-white against the mechanism—and then came back to Coral, and he nodded, one slow dip of the chin, and in that nod was the whole conversation he and Mrs. Quill and everyone on the island had been having without words since autumn: *we know. We've known. It changes nothing that matters.*

"There's more," he said. "That's why I brought these men. Mrs. Okafor—the storm drove a patrol launch aground on the south shingle last night. Broke her back on the rocks. Two men drowned, the rest are ashore and stranded until the weather eases." He wiped his face with a hand that shook. "But the third man, the officer—he wasn't on the launch. He came over on the mail boat Tuesday, ahead of the blow. Asked the Quill woman for directions to the lighthouse."

The morning went very still around Coral, the way water goes still over deep places.

"Asked directions," she repeated.

"Left yesterday afternoon on foot," Dara said. "Before the worst of it. Nobody's seen him since." He shifted, and the sea chest in his arms caught the light, and Coral saw that it was heavy, official, stenciled with numbers. "This washed ashore at the point an hour ago, tied to a kit bag. It's government property, Mrs. Okafor. It's got your name in it."

He held it out.

Overhead, unbothered, faithful, the light turned through its last daylight revolution, four seconds bright, seven dark, while Coral reached out with her bandaged hands to take whatever the storm had brought her.
