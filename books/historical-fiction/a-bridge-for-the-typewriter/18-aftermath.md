## 18. Aftermath

Coral woke because the wind had stopped.

For eleven hours the storm had pressed its shoulder against the tower until she forgot there had ever been another sound in the world—only the roar, only the glass flexing in the frame like something alive trying to breathe. Now there was silence so total it felt like a held note finally released. Her hands ached. Her shoulders ached. The blisters from the winding crank had opened sometime around four o'clock and she had wrapped them in strips of Tomás's old shirt without stopping, because stopping would have meant thinking about what was out on that water.

She went down through the tower, past Greta asleep on the lamp room floor with her cheek against the lens housing and her hands still curled as if around the glass she'd steadied all night, past Elias slumped in the chair he'd refused to leave, his census book shut for the first time since he'd arrived on her island. She let them sleep. The light burned above them, steady now, automatic in everything but name.

The door fought her when she opened it, swollen with salt air. Outside, the world had been rearranged.

The harbor path was gone in places, the stones lifted and set down elsewhere like a child's blocks. Mrs. Quill's shop had lost its roof to the sea; the counter stood open to the sky, and Coral could see the flour tins stacked neatly beneath an overturned rowboat, saved by someone in the night. Dara's boat—the *Maren*, named for a mother or a sister or a girl he'd never told anyone about—sat high on the shingle above the tideline, hull cracked along one plank but whole. Whole. Coral put her hand against it and felt it real under her palm.

Two other boats were not.

She saw it before anyone told her. The *Kestrel's* mooring buoy floated empty, turning slow circles in the flat gray water. And out past the point, wedged in the rocks below the ruined jetty, lay the *Hollis*, broken-backed, her mast gone, her name half-scraped away by stone. Men were already walking the tideline, heads bent, moving with the terrible unhurried patience of people looking for bodies.

Coral stood in the cold and counted. Two boats lost. Three families on this island who would spend today learning names of their dead. And one boat cracked but living, which meant one girl alive in her lamp room, which meant the light had held, which meant—

Which meant it had been worth every blistered hour. She repeated it to herself like arithmetic. It did not quite close the ledger, not with the *Hollis* lying there with its back broken. But it balanced enough to walk on.

* * *

By eight o'clock the islanders had organized themselves the way small communities do after catastrophe—not by command but by gravity. Dara moved among the boats assessing damage with his sleeves rolled up, saying little, touching each hull like a doctor taking pulses. Mrs. Quill had reopened her shop in the wrecked half, serving tea off a plank laid across two barrels, and people came there first as if to a hearth. Elias emerged from the tower at some point and stood beside Coral without speaking, and when she looked at him he said only: "You kept it burning."

"We kept it burning," she said.

He didn't argue the pronoun. That, too, felt new.

Word came up from the tideline just after nine: the *Hollis* had carried two men, and both had made it into the rocks alive, battered and hypothermic but breathing, hauled out by fishermen from the mainland village who'd rowed out at first light. One of the *Kestrel's* crew was missing—a boy of nineteen, Pádraig Neeley—but his skiff had been found overturned and empty, and men held out hope he'd swum to the far islet where there was a bothy and fresh water. The search would continue. The island held its breath sideways and worked anyway, because work was the only prayer anyone trusted.

It was nearly noon when the supply boat came in—and with it, the thing Coral had been waiting for since the storm's first howl, the thing she'd known would come riding behind the weather like a shark behind a wounded fish.

The relief crew. Six men in oilskins too clean for the island. And at their head, stepping onto her shingle with a leather case and a face like a closed ledger, a security officer she had met once before, in another life, across another desk.

"Mrs. Okafor," he said. "Keeper. We've come about your light."

* * *

They walked up to the cottage in a loose formation, the officer matching her stride. Behind them, Elias hung back with the relief men, and Coral caught one glance between him and the officer—a flicker, quickly buried, of recognition. Of course they knew each other. Elias had requested reassignment back to the island; somewhere on the mainland a superior had signed off on it, and somewhere in that same building a flag sat pinned to her file. The officer knew exactly why he'd come back. He'd simply never said.

"The light performed adequately during the gale," the officer said, climbing. "Remarkable, given the reports of its condition. There are questions."

"There are always questions," Coral said.

"About staffing. About correspondence. About persons resident." He let the word sit. "We'll begin inside, if you please."

Greta was in the kitchen when they entered. There had been no time to hide her, no warning, no plan—only the girl standing at the stove in one of Mrs. Quill's second-hand coats, three cups already set out on the table because she had learned the ritual in two weeks better than Coral had learned it in two years, pouring tea for Elias, who sat in Tomás's chair with his file open and his face carefully blank.

The officer stopped in the doorway. His eyes went to Greta and stayed there, the way a man's eyes go to a wound.

"And this," he said.

Every version of this moment Coral had rehearsed in the dark hours of the storm—all the lies she'd built and rebuilt, the cousin from Cork, the shipwreck survivor, the deaf-mute niece—rose up in her throat and died there. Because Greta turned around. Because the girl was holding the teapot in both hands, knuckles white, chin up, standing the way Heinrich must have stood at twenty in a photograph Coral kept under the lens plate, and Coral looked at her and understood that whatever she said next, she would be saying it over this girl's shoulder for the rest of her life.

She set down the lie and picked up the truth.

"This is my kin," Coral said. Her voice came out low and even, and it surprised her, how steady a voice could be when the ground was going out from under it. "Her father was Heinrich Brandt-Adler, engineer of the Nordlicht works, and he taught my husband this light. He called Tomás brother, and Tomás called him the same, and I have the typed pages to prove it in his own machine's hand. This is his daughter, and she is mine now, and her name is Greta."

The room went very quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Kin," the officer repeated. "By what document?"

"By document of my own keeping," Coral said. "And if you want the longer answer, you'll find it in forty pages of testimony written by a dead man who served this light for nineteen years, translated by the girl you're calling a question. Sit down. Have tea. I don't pour it for strangers twice."

Elias made a sound that might have been a cough. When Coral glanced at him, he was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him—not the cataloguer's neutrality, not the doubt of chapter upon chapter of his own conscience, but something like awe. As if he'd spent months measuring her in figures and had only now seen the thing the figures were for.

The officer did not sit. But he did not move toward the door either, and in that suspended moment Coral heard boots on the path outside—Dara's uneven tread, Mrs. Quill's brisker one, and behind them others, the whole island arriving without being summoned, filling her doorway and her yard with the silent arithmetic of witnesses. Mrs. Quill took in the scene with one sweep: the officer, the girl, the three cups. Then she walked past all of them and began slicing bread.

"Well," she said, to no one and everyone. "If there's to be an inquest, there'll be food. Nobody testifies hungry in my experience, and I've sat through four."

* * *

The search party came back at dusk with word that the boy Neeley had been found alive on the far islet, half-frozen, swearing cheerfully at his rescuers in two languages. The island's grief loosened by one name. What remained of the evening was given to the dead boats' owners, to tarps nailed over roofs, to the slow business of counting loss.

The officer stayed. He had requisitioned Mrs. Quill's spare room, and Coral understood that he meant to stay as long as it suited him, which meant the tribunal she'd been dreading had simply grown legs and walked to her island. Fine. Let it live here among them, eat Mrs. Quill's bread, drink her tea. Things that lived on an island learned its rhythms eventually.

That night, after the visitors slept, the three of them sat in the kitchen while Greta fed pages into the typewriter and typed, slowly, the last lines of the memoir—Tomás's voice rising out of the machine letter by letter, the sentence he'd left unfinished in 1935 finished now in his brother's daughter's hand:

*A lighthouse does not ask the boat where it comes from. It asks only: are you still on the water? Then hold on. I am here.*

Coral listened to the keys strike and thought: he is not finished speaking after all. None of us are.

Elias turned the census book to a blank page and uncapped his pen.

"What will you write?" Coral asked him.

He considered it a long moment, the lamplight catching the scar on his knuckle where the crank had bitten him during the storm.

"The truth," he said. "All of it. Properly sorted, properly dated. It's the sorting that matters, Mrs. Okafor. A man can drown in facts or float on them, depending how he lays them out." He began to write. "Besides—I'm told islands need a recorder."

Outside, the light turned above them, and the sea, for once, let it.
