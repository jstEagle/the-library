## 19. The Tribunal

The hearing was held in the customs house on the mainland, in a room that smelled of wet wool and government ink. They had moved the long table away from the wall and set it facing three chairs on a raised dais, as if furniture alone could confer gravity. A Union Jack hung behind the dais at a slight angle, one corner folded wrong, and no one had thought to fix it.

Coral sat with her hands in her lap and her trunk at her feet.

They had let her bring it. That surprised her. She had expected confiscation at the ferry slip—a man with a clipboard and a canvas sack—but the escort, a young naval rating who had spent the crossing being seasick and apologetic, had simply carried it up the steps for her and set it down beside her chair like luggage at an inn.

"Trunk's yours," he'd said. "Till they say otherwise."

Now she looked at it and understood what she had done. For nineteen years she had packed and unpacked this trunk, rehearsed departure, kept her life measured to the weight a woman could carry alone. She had brought it here because she could not imagine sitting in any room without it within reach of her foot.

She would not need it again. She knew that the way you know weather coming off water.

The room filled slowly. A stenographer with cold-reddened knuckles. Two men from the Admiralty who did not introduce themselves and did not sit. A woman from the Board of Trade with a folder marked LIGHT ESTABLISHMENTS — WESTERN DISTRICT. And at the end of the row of officials, separate from all of them, a heavyset man in civilian clothes whom everyone else seemed to defer to without looking at directly. Security. The word arrived unbidden in Coral's mind and settled there like gull droppings on a rail.

Greta was not in the room. They had taken her to another chamber—"for her own comfort," the woman from the Board said, in the tone people use when they mean *so she cannot coordinate her story*—and Coral had held Greta's face between her hands for a moment before they led her off, and said only: "Tell them what happened. Nothing else. The truth needs no decorating."

Greta had nodded, eyes wide, jaw set in a way that was pure Heinrich. Then she was gone down the corridor, and the door closed, and Coral turned to face the dais.

The chairman was a Captain Renshaw, retired, with a voice like gravel poured slowly. He read the charges—not charges, he corrected himself, *matters for inquiry*—from a paper he clearly had not written.

"Coral Okafor, keeper of the Western Light since the death of your husband, Thomas Okafor, in March of 1935. You are asked to account for: first, correspondence in a foreign language concealed within government equipment; second, the harboring of an undocumented alien, one Greta Heinrich Brandt—" he paused at the name, frowned, went on "—arrived on this island without inspection; third, communication of the light's schedule and character to persons unknown; fourth, obstruction of His Majesty's census."

"I didn't obstruct anything," Coral said. It came out flat, not defiant. "I answered every question Mr. Brandt asked me. Some answers were incomplete. That isn't the same thing."

Renshaw looked at her over his spectacles with something that might have been interest. "We'll come to Mr. Brandt. Let's begin where you began. The letters."

* * *

They had found them, of course. When the search party came ashore with the relief crew, the security officer—the heavyset man, whose name was Collier—had gone straight to the lamp room as though he had drawn a map in advance. He had put his hand inside the lens housing the way Coral herself had taught Greta to, months ago, in the dark, by feel. He lifted out the oilcloth packet and weighed it in his palm and looked at her and said, "Nineteen thirty-one through 'thirty-six. You've been keeping these a long time, Mrs. Okafor."

"Some things are worth keeping a long time," she had said, and had not been able to tell whether it was courage or stupidity, and had decided it didn't matter.

Now the packet lay on the evidence table between two brass weights, opened, the pages fanned. Collier read aloud. He read Heinrich's careful English—German-accented even on paper, the articles slightly wrong, the feeling entirely right. He read the passages about the lens clockwork, about Tomás's shoulder that ached before rain, about the girl growing too fast, about the war coming like a tide nobody wanted to name.

"'You must forgive an old man his sentiment,'" Collier recited, "'but I have watched your husband and my daughter grow up in the same years, and I find I cannot love one without worrying after the other.'" He lowered the page. "Sentiment. Is that what you'd call it, Mrs. Okafor?"

"I'd call it friendship," Coral said. "I'd call it a man writing to the family of his closest friend across a border that politics drew between them. If that's treason, then half the marriages in this country are treasons, because half of us married people we met through strangers."

A rustle went around the room. The stenographer's keys paused, then resumed.

Collier pressed. He was good at it—patient, unhurried, the questions circling back on themselves so that each pass stripped a little more varnish. Why had she hidden the letters rather than declare them? Why had the census answers been incomplete? Had she or had she not signaled the light's pattern during the storms of January? And the girl—how exactly had the girl come to be sleeping in the keeper's quarters, eating the keeper's rations, wearing a coat Mrs. Quill had left at the door?

Coral answered everything. She had decided on the crossing over, watching the gray water, that she would answer everything. Lying had been necessary while Greta hid under the floorboards; lying now would be a betrayal of the whole point.

"The letters stayed hidden because declaring them meant losing them, and losing them meant losing the last of my husband's brother. Not by blood. By trade, by ten years of shared work, by the fact that when Tomás lay dying he said Heinrich's name and not mine, because he knew I'd be there anyway." Her voice held steady. Her hands, in her lap, did not. "The census answers were incomplete because complete answers would have brought soldiers to my island before I could get a frightened child out of the surf. The light burned every night it was asked to burn, and you may check the logs against the mainland station's observations, and you will find they agree to the minute. As for signaling—I kept the light burning in the worst storm in living memory, and if that storm carried someone home alive, then I thank God the light was lit, and I will not apologize for where the light falls."

Silence. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and gave up.

Captain Renshaw took off his spectacles and polished them, which gave the room permission to breathe. "The memoir," he said. "Mr. Collier tells me there is a typescript. Your late husband's?"

"My husband's words. Typed by his—" she stopped, chose, "—by his daughter. By our daughter. By Greta." The word *our* landed in her own ears like a dropped plate. She did not pick it up. She let it lie there in the open where everyone could see it.

* * *

They called Elias Brandt in the afternoon.

He looked worse than she remembered—thinner, collar loose, the census book itself carried under his arm like a shield he no longer believed in. He took the witness chair and gave his name and occupation, and when Renshaw asked how long he had known the accused—

"Not accused," said the woman from the Board, quietly, and Renshaw inclined his head as though conceding a point in chess.

"—the subject," Renshaw amended, "you say four visits to the island?"

"Five," Elias said. "Four official."

"And on the fifth?"

"On the fifth I went to warn her that her file had been flagged and that a search was imminent." The room went very still. Elias did not look at Coral. He looked at the middle distance, at some fixed point only he could see. "I am aware of how that sounds. I'd ask you to hear the rest."

Collier leaned forward. "You're telling this tribunal, Mr. Brandt, that a serving officer of the census deliberately disclosed operational intelligence to a subject under investigation."

"I'm telling you that I requested reassignment to that island because I could not reconcile my notes with my conscience, and that when I got there I found a woman keeping a lighthouse alone, feeding a fugitive child out of her own ration book, with the silent assistance of every family on that rock—and not one of them ever asked me for anything except to be left in peace. My job was to count souls. I counted them. There are more souls on that island than the forms allow for. That's not an error in arithmetic. That's what souls do—they accumulate where there's room."

"You omitted the German correspondence from your report," Collier said. "That omission is documented. You wrote 'no irregular correspondence observed.' You observed a great deal."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Elias finally turned and looked at Coral. What passed between them lasted two seconds and contained the whole winter: the tea, Tomás's chair, the night the light failed, the wordless pact of people who have worked a machine together through a storm.

"Because the question I was sent to answer was *is she loyal*, and the answer was so plainly yes that writing it down felt like an insult to both of us." He faced forward again. "And because I have spent eighteen months cataloguing persons of interest, sir, and I will tell you what I have learned. The people worth fearing fill their forms out beautifully. The people worth trusting hide letters in lamp housings because they loved a German engineer who taught a Nigerian lighthouse keeper his trade, and none of those facts fit in a box on a form, and I refuse to pretend otherwise."

"That's insubordination."

"That's testimony."

Renshaw raised a hand before Collier could answer, and something in the old captain's face had shifted—not softened exactly, but settled, like ballast finding its place. "One more question, Mr. Brandt. The girl. Greta Heinrich Brandt. In your professional judgment, based on five visits and whatever else you've seen—what is she to this woman?"

Elias was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its official edges altogether.

"When I first came to the island, Mrs. Okafor lived like a woman waiting for orders to leave. Everything she owned fit in one trunk. When I last saw her, there were three cups on the shelf, and a typewriter with new ribbon, and a girl's boots drying by the stove, and the trunk was empty." He spread his hands. "You can charge her with harboring. But you should know that what she actually harbored, all winter, was a family. And families aren't smuggled, Captain. They're built. Somebody has to build them. Somebody always pays for the materials."

Nobody spoke. The stenographer typed it all, the keys sounding like rain on glass.

* * *

They adjourned for two hours. Coral ate nothing. She sat in the corridor on a bench worn smooth by generations of waiting people, and at some point Mrs. Quill appeared—of course Mrs. Quill appeared; the woman had a genius for arriving where flour was needed—with a meat pie wrapped in newspaper and no explanation of how she had crossed the water.

"Eat," Mrs. Quill said. "Tribunals run on empty stomachs and full files, and you can only control one of those."

"How did you—"

"Dara's boat. He's moored at the quay with the girl's trunk and my second-best coat, and he'll wait there till they throw him off, which they won't, because he's bought fish for half the port authority's wives." She sat down heavily, arranged her skirts, and looked straight ahead at the far wall. "Whatever they decide in there, Coral Okafor, you come home to an island that knows what you are. We've had a vote. Nobody called a vote. It happened anyway."

Coral ate the pie. It tasted of nothing and everything.

When they recalled her, the room had changed in some way she couldn't name—perhaps only the light, the afternoon sun having moved to strike the brass weights on the evidence table so that the letters glinted beneath them like a shoal of fish.

Renshaw addressed her last. "Mrs. Okafor. Before we deliberate. Do you wish to say anything further?"

She stood. She had prepared nothing, which was unlike her; her whole life had been preparation. But she found she had one thing, and it was small, and it was true.

"For nineteen years I kept a light for ships whose names I never learned, for sailors I never met, for enemies and allies and neutral flags alike, because that is what the light is *for*. Nobody ever asked me whom the beam favored. It favors whoever is on the water." She laid her hand flat on the trunk at her feet, once, farewell. "I kept the light. I kept the letters. I kept the girl. I would keep all three again tomorrow, and I'm told honesty counts for something in this room, so there it is—all of it, complete, no boxes left blank."

Renshaw studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, almost imperceptibly, to the woman from the Board, and the tribunal withdrew.

The deliberation took forty minutes. Coral spent them in the corridor with her hands folded, and Greta beside her—released now, allowed to wait—the girl's knee pressing against hers, warm through the wool, saying nothing, needing to say nothing. Through the frosted glass of the hearing-room door they could see the silhouettes moving, pausing, one of them rising and pacing and sitting again.

At twenty minutes past four the door opened, and Captain Renshaw came out holding a single sheet of paper, and his face was unreadable in the way that faces are when the news is large enough to go either way.

"We have reached a determination," he said. "In the matter of the correspondence, the harboring, and the conduct of the keeper of the Western Light—"
