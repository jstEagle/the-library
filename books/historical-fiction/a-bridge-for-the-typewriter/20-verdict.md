## 20. Verdict

The tribunal sat in the customs house on the mainland, a room that smelled of wet wool and old decisions. Coral had imagined it differently — imagined it as a lighthouse turned inside out, some great lens of judgment that would find every corner of her and burn it clean. But it was only a room with a green baize table and three men behind it, and a fourth man taking notes whose pen scratched like a mouse in the wall.

Two weeks since the storm. Two weeks since the search party came ashore with the relief crew and found Greta standing on the gallery steps, lamp oil to her elbows, refusing to run. Two weeks since Coral had walked out of her own hiding place — out of the careful invisibility she'd built like a sea wall for twenty years — and said, *She's mine. She's my kin.* A lie and not a lie, both at once, and the words still lived in her mouth like a coal.

Now they read the charges.

The chairman was a Mr. Halloway, gray as gull feathers, with a voice like gravel poured slowly. He did not look up from his papers as he spoke. "Coral Okafor, keeper of the light at Winter's Reach. You are charged with harboring an enemy alien, Greta Heinrich-Voss, without declaration to the proper authorities; with concealing correspondence from a national of enemy origin; and with obstruction of a census officer in the execution of his duty."

"I didn't obstruct anyone," Coral said.

Halloway looked up then. "You lied to him."

"Yes," she said. "I did."

The third member of the panel, a woman named Inspector Reeve who had arrived late and said little, made a small mark on her paper. Coral had decided, somewhere on the boat over, that she would not perform for these people. She had performed enough. She had lain by lamplight while a girl listened through the floorboards and her heart tried to break her ribs, and she had done it well because Tomás had taught her that keeping a light was ninety percent performance — you stood your watch whether the weather believed you or not. She would stand this watch too. But she would not smile for them.

"Mrs. Okafor," Halloway said. "You understand the position you have placed this board in."

"I expect I do."

"The war is over. That is the only reason we are having this conversation instead of a different one." He shuffled papers. "The correspondence. Read it to me. The letter from the German engineer."

She had known they would ask. She had brought the letters herself — carried them across in Dara's boat wrapped in oilskin, which felt strange, like carrying a sleeping child into a courtroom. She opened the packet with hands that were steadier than she deserved.

"Which one?"

"The last one."

She found it. Heinrich's handwriting had always leaned hard to starboard, as if the page were heeling in wind. She read: *My friend — if I may still call you that, though I have no right to ask anything of you or yours — I send you my daughter because there is nowhere else. Your island was the one honest place I ever worked. Tell her about the winter of '24 when your husband and I kept the light together through the ice and neither of us slept and we laughed anyway. Tell her that friendship is not a nation. Tell her I am sorry for what my country has become, and that I am not my country, and that she is not me.*

The room was very quiet. The note-taking man had stopped writing.

"Your husband," Halloway said, "kept a memoir."

"He did. It's typed. My — " she almost said *my daughter*, caught it, let the word go anyway — "*Greta* finished translating it. The parts that were in Portuguese and German. You can read it. He called Heinrich his brother. In ink, in his own hand. If that makes me a traitor, then the word doesn't mean anything and you should say so plainly."

Reeve looked at her for the first time. Something passed over the inspector's face that might have been respect, or might have been recognition — the look of a person watching someone else refuse to be small.

"Mr. Brandt," Halloway said.

Elias rose from the bench where he'd been sitting apart from everyone, hat on his knee like a man at a funeral. Coral hadn't spoken to him since the mainland crossing. He looked thinner. He looked like a man who had been doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"You conducted the census," Halloway said. "And a follow-up visit. And you filed a report."

"I did."

"Your report makes no mention of any German connection on the island."

"No, sir."

"Why not?"

Elias paused. Not long — but long enough that Coral saw the whole shape of it: the file on his superior's desk, the flag, the warning rowed across black water in the teeth of the season. She owed him a debt she suspected he would never let her name.

"Because the census counts souls, sir," Elias said. "It doesn't weigh them. When I weighed them myself, I found I wasn't qualified for the job."

Someone behind Coral exhaled — Mrs. Quill, who had come over uninvited and unannounced and planted herself in the second row wearing her good coat and an expression that dared anyone to remove her. Dara sat beside her, hands folded, smelling faintly of diesel and salt, having told the tribunal nothing they asked and everything they needed.

Halloway consulted his colleagues. There was murmuring. The mouse-pen started and stopped.

Then the verdicts came down like weather, item by item.

"On the charge of concealment of correspondence: dismissed. The correspondence has been reviewed and entered into evidence. The board finds it establishes, in the words of the deceased keeper, a relationship of — " Halloway paused, and Coral watched him decide to say it straight — "of brotherhood, which the board declines to criminalize."

"On the charge of obstruction: dismissed. The board notes the officer involved does not consider himself obstructed."

Elias, sitting back down, did not look at her. He looked at his hat. But his ears had gone red, and Coral thought: *there's a man learning a new trade.*

"On the matter of Greta Heinrich-Voss." Halloway set down his papers entirely, which seemed significant. "The board has discretion here. The young woman is stateless. Her father is interned — whereabouts uncertain. Deportation is available to us. It is also, frankly, unavailable in practice; there is no port that will take her and no vessel we would trust to deliver her. The board therefore grants leave to remain, under wardship of the keeper of the light at Winter's Reach, subject to annual review." He looked at Greta, who stood beside Coral with her spine straight and her chin up and her father's stubbornness written all over her face. "Miss Voss. Do you understand the terms?"

Greta's English was better now. It had been better for months; she'd simply been rationing it, the way the island rationed everything. "I understand," she said. "Thank you, sir." Then, in the accent she was letting show more lately: "I will be worth the keeping."

A sound went through the room that was almost laughter. Almost.

"One further matter." Halloway gathered his papers. "The light at Winter's Reach is scheduled for automation. A crew will arrive in spring to install the mechanism. The board has received — " here he glanced at Reeve, who inclined her head fractionally — "a recommendation that the current keeper be retained through the transition period. To train the crew. To remain, in fact, as principal keeper until the board determines otherwise."

Coral blinked. "Retained. Not transferred."

"Not transferred."

Orders could come any day — that had been the litany of four years, the drumbeat under every trunk she packed. Orders could come any day, so keep your life small, keep it portable, keep it fitting in one trunk with room left over for grief. She had built a whole self on the certainty of leaving.

"They're asking you to stay, Mrs. Okafor," Halloway said, and there was, unbelievably, something like kindness in the gravel. "Do you accept?"

She thought of the lamp room. Of three cups. Of a typewriter with new keys, oiled by engineer's hands. Of a trunk upstairs that she had packed twice and unpacked twice and was, she now understood, going to have to deal with properly at last.

"Yes," she said. "I accept."

*

They came out of the customs house into a bright cold noon, the kind of day the coast rationed out sparingly, and Mrs. Quill seized Coral's arm before she'd taken three steps.

"Well," Mrs. Quill said. "Ward of the keeper. That's near enough to daughter for government work."

"It'll do," Coral said.

Dara clapped Elias on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "Recorder," Dara said. "Island recorder. Never heard of such a thing."

"There wasn't one," Elias admitted. "I invented the post. The board approved it mostly to get me out of the census office, I think. Somebody has to keep the island's records now that — " he faltered, gesturing at the general machinery of suspicion, "— now that nobody official wants the job."

"And you want it," Coral said. It wasn't quite a question.

Elias looked out at the harbor, where the water lay flat and silver and forgiving, as if it hadn't tried to kill all of them a fortnight ago. "I spent two years counting people," he said. "I'd like to spend some years remembering them instead. Someone should write down what happened this winter. What the light held. What Mrs. Quill's flour ledger proves." He smiled — a small, dry, real smile, the first she'd seen fully on him. "If it's all the same to you, keeper, I'd like a room in the cottage. I type badly, but Greta says she can fix anything with keys."

Greta, walking ahead with the oilskin packet of letters hugged to her chest like a child, turned and said, "I said I could fix the machine. The operator is beyond even me."

"That," said Mrs. Quill, "is the truest thing anyone's said all week."

*

The boat back was Dara's, and the crossing took two hours in the mild swell, and nobody talked much, which was its own kind of talking. Greta slept against Coral's shoulder in the wheelhouse, the letters still in her arms, and Coral watched the island rise out of the haze — the white tower, the black rock, the cottages with their storm-mended roofs, the smoke already rising from her own chimney because Mrs. Quill had sent her boy over that morning to lay a fire against their return.

*Home as a verb,* Tomás had written once, in the memoir, in the passage Greta had typed out and Coral had read eleven times. *Not the place you're given but the thing you do daily, the tending of it, the lighting of it.*

Orders could come any day. Except now they wouldn't. Now the orders had come, and they had said *stay*, and the strangest feeling of the whole terrible winter was not the storm or the interrogation or the courtroom — it was this: standing on her own gallery steps again with no departure date in her pocket, holding a future too large to fit in one trunk.

That evening, lamplit, Greta at the typewriter practicing scales of letters, Elias in Tomás's chair with the first blank pages of the island record squared before him, Coral climbed the stairs alone to the bedroom where the trunk sat.

She knelt before it. She opened the lid.

Everything she had hoarded against the leaving lay there in its careful layers: the spare wicks, the good coat she never wore, the photographs, the tin of Tomás's things, the dress for occasions that never came. Four years of readiness. Four years of measuring her life in what could be carried.

She put one hand flat on the wool coat and left it there a long moment.

Then she lifted the whole first layer out, piece by piece, and carried it downstairs to give away.

By the time she reached the bottom of the trunk — down past the wicks, past the photographs, down to the bare boards and the single envelope she had forgotten she'd hidden there, addressed in her husband's slanted hand and marked *For C., when you stop packing* — the house below had gone quiet except for the typing, steady as surf, patient as tide.

Coral sat back on her heels, the envelope trembling in her fingers, and understood that the verdict had not ended today.

It had only just begun to arrive.
