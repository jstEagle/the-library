## 21. What Cannot Be Carried

The tribunal's verdict had come on a Tuesday, written in a clerk's careful hand, and Coral had read it four times before the meaning settled into her bones the way the island damp always did—slowly, and then all at once.

*Ward of the keeper. Retained at post pending automation training.*

Retained. The word sat strangely in her mouth. For eleven years she had measured every object on this island against a single question: *can it be carried?* The trunk had been her answer, packed and unpacked through seasons of waiting for orders that finally came and then were rescinded. She had lived as a woman perpetually halfway gone.

Now she stood before the trunk on a bright morning three weeks after the great storm, and it was full again—not of essentials, but of everything she'd hoarded against departure. Two extra wool blankets still smelling faintly of camphor. Tomás's dress shirt with the collar that chafed him so he'd only worn it to their wedding and Heinrich's christening-day visit, laughing the whole time about English tailoring. A tin of buttons she'd never used because sewing for one person meant mending, not making. Six candles hoarded past reason. A photograph of her mother she could not have explained to a requisition officer and so had hidden inside the trunk's false bottom for over a decade, folded small as a coin.

She lifted each thing into the light coming through the kitchen window and asked the old question, and then she asked a new one: *what is it for, if I am staying?*

The blankets went into the cedar chest by the door—where blankets belonged if you meant to sleep here through winters. The shirt she pressed and hung in the wardrobe, absurd and right. Mrs. Quill would take the buttons; the woman mended for half the island and complained about button scarcity like other people complained about weather. The candles went back in the drawer where Dara could find them when he came to argue about boat schedules, because Darg argued best by lamplight, as if darkness gave his grievances weight.

The photograph she unfolded on the table and did not hide again.

Greta found her there an hour later, still sitting with it, tea gone cold beside her elbow. The girl had filled out since the storm—there was bread on her now, and Mrs. Quill's stews, and the ordinary miracle of weeks without terror. Her English grew daily, arriving in bursts: whole sentences appearing overnight like mushrooms after rain.

"You are sad?" Greta asked, pulling out the opposite chair. Three chairs at the table now. When had that happened? Somewhere around the third week, probably, when Coral stopped noticing she was setting three cups.

"No." Coral turned the photograph toward her. "Remembering. There's a difference."

Greta studied the stern woman with her high collar and her eyes that were the same grey-green as Coral's own. "Your mother."

"My mother. She told me when I married Tomás that I was marrying the sea, and the sea doesn't keep anything it can't carry away." Coral smiled despite herself. "She thought it was a warning. It took me eleven years to hear it as permission."

"Permission for what?"

"To put things down."

* * *

They started with the lighthouse stores, because order mattered to Coral and chaos to Greta was an invitation. The girl inventoried with an engineer's precision, listing lamp oil and spare glass, wicks and cleaning spirits, her handwriting slanting leftward in the way of someone taught German penmanship first. But when they came to the storeroom shelf where Coral had kept the emergency parcel—a change of clothes, identity papers copied twice, money sewn into a hem, all of it prepared for Greta's onward passage in case the tribunal had gone another way—the girl went very quiet.

"You kept this ready," Greta said. "Even after."

"I kept it ready until the verdict came. And then I sat up all night deciding whether hope required contingency." Coral took the parcel and unwrapped it slowly, laying the papers flat, unpicking the hem with a seam ripper, coins dropping onto the table one by one like small bells. "I've decided it doesn't. Hope requires breakfast."

Greta laughed—that startled bark of laughter she still produced as if surprised by her own lungs—and it did what it always did to the room, which was enlarge it.

The money went into the tin on the mantel. The clothes went to Dara's youngest sister, who was growing fast and poor faster. The papers Coral burned in the stove, watching the ink curl, and felt nothing but warmth on her face. That night she slept without dreaming of patrol boats, which she counted as a kind of graduation.

* * *

The cottages came next, because storms do not respect grief or paperwork.

The east row had taken the worst of it—three cottages with roofs peeled back like sardine tins, one wall of the Henderson place lying in the yard whole and upright, as if the wind had grown tired mid-theft and set it down. Insurance was a word from newspapers; on this island, repair was done by whoever could hold a hammer.

They came on a Saturday: Dara and two of his brothers, Mrs. Quill in a man's coat with her sleeves rolled, the Henderson boy carrying nails in a coffee tin, and Elias Brandt in clothes that had clearly been bought for a desk and were learning hard lessons about salt air and slate dust.

Coral watched him work for a while before she admitted to herself that she was watching. He'd changed since the tribunal—or perhaps since before it, since the night he'd stood beside her in the lamp room with his hands blistered on the winding crank, keeping the light through twelve hours of screaming dark. He filed papers differently now. Slower. As though aware that every word was a person.

"He is not what I expected," Greta said quietly, passing Coral with a hod of mortar. "A German girl hears 'census man' and thinks of certain things."

"What did you expect?"

"Worse. Or better—more obviously bad. Not a man who carries roof slate for old women and blushes when Mrs. Quill feeds him."

"He does blush," Coral agreed. "It's his most useful quality."

But she said it softly, and she caught Elias glancing over at the sound of Greta's laughter, and there passed between the three of them across the ruined yard something that needed no words: the acknowledgment that a census book and a fishing boat and a storm-lamped night had made them complicit in each other's lives, permanently, whether they'd chosen it or not.

By dusk the Henderson roof held against the sky again. Mrs. Quill produced a kettle from somewhere—she always produced kettles from somewhere—and they drank tea sitting on upturned buckets among the debris, and the Henderson boy fell asleep against his uncle's shoulder, and nobody spoke of tribunals or patrols or the war gathering itself across the water like a held breath.

Coral sat among them with her cup and understood, with the slow certainty of tide turning, that this was the thing she had been packing the trunk against finding. Not safety. Company.

* * *

The last of it happened in October, on a day of hard clean light.

She took the trunk out to the yard and emptied it completely, turned it upside down, shook it. Eleven years of readiness rattled out: sand from some previous life, a spare key to a door in a town she no longer claimed, the false-bottom panel itself, which she pried loose and split for kindling.

Dara appeared at the gate, drawn by the noise the way gulls are drawn by gutting tables. "You're emigrating, then?"

"I'm staying."

"I know. I'm asking why you're abusing a perfectly good trunk about it."

"The trunk and I are renegotiating terms."

He came in and sat on the wall while she worked, offering commentary in the manner of men who show love through critique. The trunk, stripped of its cargo, was just wood and brass corners and a smell of camphor fading into plain air. She scrubbed it out with soda and left it open to the sun, and by afternoon it smelled of nothing—which is to say, of possibility.

Into it, deliberately, she placed three things.

The typewriter first. Tomás's Remington, its rust scaled away by Greta's patient hands, its keys working again with that particular clatter Coral had once flinched at and now found she listened for, the way you listen for a familiar footstep in another room. The memoir lay finished inside its carriage—two hundred pages in Greta's translation, testimony typed in triplicate, a copy already lodged with Elias, a second sent to Heinrich through channels that were slow and dangerous and somehow functioning. The machine had become what Tomás had half-joked he wanted it to be: a bridge. It carried his voice across the water to a man in an internment camp who read those pages aloud to other internees, Greta said, in a shed at night, men who needed reminding that friendship had existed before nations lost their minds.

Three cups second. Mismatched—one of Tomás's old ones with the chipped saucer, one bought from Mrs. Quill's shop the week Greta arrived, one that had been Coral's alone for eleven years and was now simply the oldest of three rather than the only one. She wrapped them in the good tea towel and set them in the trunk's corner like ballast.

And third, folded on top: nothing. Empty space. Room for whatever came next—a letter from Heinrich, a grandchild someday, God willing decades away, a storm-damp coat belonging to a census man who blushed. The trunk had spent eleven years teaching her how to leave. It could learn, she supposed, how to stay.

She carried it upstairs to the room that had been storage and was becoming, by increments, a proper bedroom for Greta, and set it under the window where the sea light would fall across its brass.

Then she went down and put the kettle on, because hope requires breakfast, and it was nearly noon, and any moment now the door would open—it always opened now—and someone would come in needing tea, and she would be home to make it.

Above her, the new automatic mechanism ticked in the lamp room where Greta was calibrating it, faithful as a heart, and below her the sea kept the island in its two long hands, and Coral Okafor, keeper, widow, witness, put three cups on the table and waited, gladly, to be interrupted.
