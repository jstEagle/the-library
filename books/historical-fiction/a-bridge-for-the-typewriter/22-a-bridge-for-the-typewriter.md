## 22. A Bridge for the Typewriter

The spring came the way springs came to the island—late, grudging, and then all at once, as if it had been standing offshore waiting for permission.

Coral noticed it first in the grass on the cliff path. All winter it had lain flat and salt-burned, a brown mat that gave nothing back to the eye. Then one morning in April she walked down to check the fog signal housing and found it green, upright, absurdly confident, as though no storm had ever pressed it to the earth. She stood there longer than the errand required. Below her, the sea had gone from iron to slate to something almost blue, and a pair of gulls were arguing over a fish head with the particular fury of creatures who have survived another winter and intend to enjoy being alive about it.

Above her head, the light turned without anyone.

It still unsettled her, some mornings. She would wake at two o'clock out of twenty years of habit, already reaching for her boots, and lie in the dark listening to the faint mechanical whir from the lamp room—the clockwork of the rotation drive, steady as a pulse—and remember that she was not needed. That the light would sweep its beam across the water whether she slept or rose, whether she lived or died. For a woman who had spent eleven years being the only thing between the ships and the rocks, it was a strange kind of grief, being unnecessary.

But then, she had learned something over the winter about what necessary meant.

* * *

The typewriter lived in the kitchen now, on the table by the window where the morning sun came through. Tomás's typewriter, salt-rusted and half-dead when Greta had found it, resurrected by those clever engineer's hands—new ribbon, cleaned type bars, the carriage oiled until it ran smooth as a boat on calm water. It had become, without any formal decision being made, the center of the house. Letters accumulated around it like driftwood around a piling.

On Tuesday mornings, Greta typed letters to Heinrich.

They went by way of Mrs. Quill, who had a cousin with a fishing smack that called at ports where questions were not asked, and from there into Germany through channels Greta understood better than she explained. The replies took six weeks or eight. When one came, Greta read it aloud at the table, translating as she went, her English improving month by month until now she only stumbled on the words that mattered most.

This Tuesday, Coral sat across the table with her tea going cold and dictated.

*"Tell him the light is automated now,"* she said. *"Tell him they put in the new mechanism in February and I supervised the installation, which I suspect was their idea of an insult but which I treated as an honor. Tell him the crew they left behind are boys, mostly, and they don't know a clockwork escapement from a hole in the ground, and every time something sticks they come running up the path to me as if I were still the keeper."*

Greta's fingers paused. *"Still der Wärterin,"* she murmured, translating, and smiled. *"He will like that."*

*"Tell him Elias has been made the island's official recorder, which means he now counts souls instead of cataloguing them, and he is insufferable about it. He has a new ledger with columns for births and deaths and marriages and he keeps asking me when I intend to provide material for the third column, which I have told him is none of a recorder's business."*

Greta laughed—she laughed easily now, that was one of the changes Coral tracked the way she'd once tracked barometers—and typed. The keys struck with a sound like small sure footsteps. Outside the window, Dara's boat was coming around the point with the tide, and Coral watched it without meaning to, checking its trim against the swell out of habit, finding both satisfactory.

*"And tell him—"*

She stopped.

Greta looked up. Her hands rested on the keys. At nineteen, with her hair drying loose from the morning wash and ink smudged on one finger, she looked so much like her father in certain lights that Coral sometimes lost the thread of a sentence just watching her.

*"Tell him what?"* Greta asked gently.

*"Tell him his brother's memoir is finished. Both languages. Tell him I have read it four times and each time I find my husband alive in it, which is either a miracle or very good typing."*

"It is very good dictation," Greta said. "You argue with every sentence."

"Your father argued with every sentence too. It's how engineers say they love you."

Greta typed it. Coral heard her own words go down onto the page in German and felt again that small vertigo she always felt now, seeing her life pass through other hands and come out the other side unchanged—or changed, rather, into something that could travel. That could cross water. That could reach a man in a ruined country and tell him: you are remembered, you are family, the bridge holds.

That was what the typewriter had become. Not a machine. A bridge. Tomás had never finished building his, the one he'd talked about in the memoir, the metaphor he'd been too embarrassed to commit to paper except once, late at night, drunk on Mrs. Quill's elderberry wine: *a lighthouse is a bridge that doesn't touch both shores.* Heinrich had taught him the trade; the memoir carried the trade forward; Greta's fingers carried the words; and somewhere in Hamburg or Bremen or wherever the war's wreckage had scattered him, Heinrich Brandt—no relation to Elias, though the coincidence of names had caused one long confused evening last autumn and considerable laughter since—would read them and know that everything he'd built had held.

"Finished," Greta announced. She pulled the page free and read it back, correcting her own grammar twice, and Coral signed the bottom in the space left for her name—a formality, since the letter was dictated in her voice, but a formality she insisted on. Your name on a thing meant you stood behind it. She had learned that before a tribunal, and she did not intend to unlearn it.

* * *

Afternoons, when the weather allowed, Coral walked the island.

This had been her grief ritual once—the circuit of the cliff path, the visit to Tomás's grave in the churchyard above the harbor, the counting of things. She had measured her life in what fit into one trunk, and on her walks she had inventoried: this chair, worth taking; this table, not; this coat, worn through, leave it; this habit of pausing at the second stile to look back at the light, take it if you can, leave it if you must.

She still walked. But the inventory had changed.

At the second stile she stopped and looked back, because that was simply what she did now, and saw the cottage with its mended roof—Dara and three of the fishing boys had re-slated it in March after the storm damage, refusing payment, accepting only dinner. Smoke stood up straight from the chimney. In the garden plot behind it, Greta had planted things in rows so precise they could only have been laid out with a surveyor's string, potatoes and cabbage and, along the south wall, a hopeful line of beans that might or might not forgive the salt wind.

Further along, Mrs. Quill's shop, where Coral now bought flour in quantities that would have told the whole story all over again even if no story needed telling. Mrs. Quill came out to the door as she passed, handed her a parcel of bacon ends "going spare," and asked after Greta's cough with the studied casualness of a woman who kept a second stock of ration cards under her counter for exactly one customer, and would deny it under oath, and had denied it under oath, at the tribunal, magnificently.

"She's well," Coral said. "Typing."

"Typing." Mrs. Quill sniffed, which was her word for approval. "Girl'll wear that machine out."

"She'll rebuild it first. That's the difference between us and the engineers. We use things up. They use things up and then hand them back better."

Mrs. Quill looked at her for a moment with an expression Coral had learned to read over the winter: the look of a woman deciding whether to say the true thing. She usually decided yes, eventually.

"You're different," she said. "Since winter. You used to walk like you were leaving. Now you walk like you're keeping."

Coral considered denying it. There was no point. "The trunk's empty," she said. "I gave the last of it away at Easter. Tomás's good coat went to young Peadar Dara for his first crewing. The linens to the Quills. The chronometer—" she touched her pocket absently, where it wasn't anymore, where it hadn't been since December, traded for the fuel that had put Greta's escape-boat to sea ahead of the worst storm in living memory. "Well. The chronometer's in the water, one way or another. Doing its work."

"And what did you keep?"

"The typewriter. Three cups." She smiled. "Everything else was ballast."

Mrs. Quill snorted, but her eyes were wet, and she turned back into her shop with the abruptness of a woman protecting a reputation, and Coral walked on, lighter than she had any right to be, toward the churchyard.

Tomás's grave was tidy. Greta weeded it—claimed it as an engineering responsibility, the maintenance of structures—and had set a small stone at the head with an inscription in two languages, which had taken some persuading at the quarry yard and some outright forgery of paperwork afterward, Elias having decided that some records were better created than requested. *THOMAS OKAFOR, KEEPER OF THIS LIGHT. BRUDER. BROTHER.*

Coral stood there the length of a pipeful of the tobacco she'd taken up again and mostly regretted, and told him the news, as she always did. The light was automatic. His memoir was read in two countries. His daughter-in-everything-but-paper was planting beans. His enemy-turned-brother wrote every month, and his letters were funny, which Coral had not expected, and sad in places, which she had, and ended every single one with the same sentence in English, in his own imperfect handwriting: *Tell Coral she was right to keep the door open.*

"I was right," she told the stone. "Though it would have been cheaper to be wrong. You always did pick the expensive friendships."

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere below, a curlew called its one note across the flats. She stayed until the cold got into her knees, and then went home, and the word home sat in her chest the whole way without weighing anything at all.

* * *

Evening. Elias came up the path at six, as he did on Thursdays, carrying a loaf and his ledger and the particular air of a man arriving at a place he has stopped pretending is official business.

He had changed over the winter too. Thinner, somehow, and easier—the watcher's coiled watchfulness gone out of his shoulders. The tribunal had cost him his advancement; everyone knew it; nobody on the island cared, least of all Elias, who spoke of his demotion from mainland office to island recorder the way other men spoke of rescue. "They sent me where the files are smallest," he'd said, "and the people are largest. I consider it a promotion they haven't noticed yet."

Tonight he brought more than bread. Coral saw it before he was through the door—that set to his jaw, the ledger held slightly apart from his body as though it contained something with edges.

"Post," he said. "Official. And before you make that face, it's not what you think. Read it."

She read it. Greta read it over her shoulder, breath quickening. It was from the Board of Trade, or the Admiralty, or whichever gray office owned such things: a request—nearly a plea—that Keeper Emerita Okafor consent to remain in an advisory capacity during the automation transition period, extended indefinitely, remuneration nominal, lodgings provided at the keeper's cottage, *in recognition of services rendered during the storm of 14 December.*

"Services rendered," Coral read aloud. "Fourteen hours on that mechanism with my hands blistered shut, and they give me a sentence."

"They gave you a pension supplement too. Second paragraph."

"So they did." She set the letter down. Looked at Greta. Looked at Elias, whose face was doing a poor job of neutrality. "And the rest? The part you're holding back like a man with a bad catch?"

Elias exhaled. Set the ledger on the table, opened it to a marked page. "There's a girl in Hamburg," he said. "Sixteen. Heinrich's sister's daughter. Her mother died in March. Heinrich can't get her out himself—you know his situation—and he's written asking..." He stopped. Looked at Greta, whose hands had gone very still on the back of a chair. "He's written asking whether the door is still open."

The kitchen was quiet. The sea said what the sea always says. Above them, faintly, the automatic light began its turn, sweeping its patient beam out over the darkening water, touching both shores without needing to.

Coral thought about a trunk, packed and unpacked for eleven years. Thought about three cups, and how a person could wash a fourth and dry it and set it beside them and feel the whole shape of a household change around that one small act. Thought about home—which she had once believed was a place you were assigned, a light you were posted to, a list of objects you could carry away when they took it from you—and how wrong she had been, and how expensive the lesson, and how completely worth it.

She picked up the pen.

"Greta," she said, "take a letter. *Dear Heinrich. Tell your niece the crossing is hard but the landing is easy. Tell her there is work here, and weather, and a woman who argues with every sentence, and a room with a view of the light. Tell her the door was never closed. Tell her it does not close.*"

Greta's hands found the keys. The first letter struck home, then the next, then the next—small sure footsteps on a bridge that reached, tonight, all the way across the water.

Outside, the beam turned. Inside, the cups waited on the shelf, and there was room for one more.
