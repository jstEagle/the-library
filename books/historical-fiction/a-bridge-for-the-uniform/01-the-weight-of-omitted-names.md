## 1. The Weight of Omitted Names

The snow came down on Vellmar the way it always did in November — not falling so much as settling, as if the sky had grown tired of holding it and simply let go. Sebastian Almeida watched it through the infirmary's tall east window while he crushed dried chamomile between his palms, and thought, not for the first time, that snow was the most honest weather there was. It covered nothing. It only made visible what was already beneath.

"Orderly."

Sister Brandt's voice from the corridor, pitched low out of respect for the sleeping ward. He set the mortar down and went to her. She was a broad woman with forearms like a laundress and eyes that had seen too many winters to be surprised by any of them, and she jerked her chin toward the far end of the ward without ceremony.

"The new one. The veteran they brought up from the river depot this morning. He's going tonight, I think. He keeps asking for someone."

"Someone?"

"A name." She frowned, working at it. "He says it like a question. Like he's asking permission."

Sebastian had learned, over fifteen years in this trade, that the dying rarely asked for what they wanted. They asked for what they'd been denied. Water when the wells were fouled. A hand when no one would touch them. A name spoken aloud when the reports had gone quiet.

"I'll sit with him," he said.

* * *

The man was perhaps fifty, though the war had done its arithmetic on him early and subtracted generously. His face was gray as the river ice would be by January, and his hands lay on the blanket in the particular stillness of hands that have stopped belonging to anyone. A private's greatcoat hung on the peg beside the bed, its buttons mismatched, its collar worn to thread where another man's chin had rested before him. The army gave its coats longer lives than its men.

Sebastian pulled the chair close and sat, the way he always did — on the left side, within reach of the hand, low enough that a waking man saw a companion rather than an official hovering over him. He had been a nurse once, before the titles fell away from him one by one until only *orderly* remained, and even now his body remembered all of it: how to read breath, how to time a morphine dose against the tide of pain, how to wait.

The man's eyes opened partway. They found Sebastian and held on.

"You're not the priest," he said. His voice was a dry leaf.

"No."

"Good." Something almost like humor moved behind the gray. "The priest writes things down."

Sebastian kept his face still. "So do I," he said carefully. "Temperatures. Pulses. What you eat."

"Not that kind of writing." The man's fingers twitched against the blanket, restless, marching to some old drum. "I was at Kesselbach. You know Kesselbach?"

Everyone knew Kesselbach, or knew of it — the salient that ate two divisions in the autumn of '17 and was never once mentioned in the dispatches afterward except as *the sector was consolidated*. Sebastian knew it better than most. He had been there.

"I know it," he said.

"They sent us three hundred and eleven men into the wood on the fourteenth of October." The number came out whole, unbroken, the way numbers do when they've been carried so long they've worn smooth. "Three hundred and eleven. And the report said two hundred and six engaged, casualties consistent with operations." The man's breath caught, held, released. "Consistent with operations. That's what it said. My lieutenant read it to us after, like scripture. Consistent with operations."

"What happened to the other hundred and five?" Sebastian asked, though he already knew. He had written some of them down himself, in another life, in another hand.

"Some died. That's war, isn't it, orderly? Some die." The man turned his head on the pillow, and his eyes were very clear now, terribly clear, the clarity that comes just before the end when the body stops bothering with its usual economies. "But forty-one of them didn't die in the wood. Forty-one of them were wounded and couldn't walk back, and the order came down that stretcher parties were suspended because of the shelling, and then the cold came, and they froze. In the wood. For three days we could hear them calling. Then for two days we couldn't hear anything at all, which was worse."

Sebastian did not move. Fifteen years of practice held him still, but underneath the stillness something old and heavy shifted its weight, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks.

"And the rest?" he asked quietly.

"The rest ran." The man smiled, and it was the saddest thing Sebastian had seen in a season full of dying. "Can you blame them? They ran, and they were caught, and there wasn't any trial because there wasn't any time, and there wasn't any record because records take ink and ink takes paperwork and paperwork takes men who aren't busy shooting other men. So they were shot at dawn behind the transport yard and listed as—" his lips shaped the phrase with bitter precision— "*unfit for record.* Unfit. Like spoiled meat. Like a boot with a broken lace."

"Names," Sebastian said. It was barely a word at all. "Do you remember their names?"

The man's brow furrowed. Then, slowly, like a man opening a door he had kept locked even from himself, he began.

"Hollmann. Josef Hollmann, he was from the vineyards somewhere, he talked about his brother's cellar the whole march out. And Weiss — little Weiss, the telephonist, seventeen years old if he was a day. And Brandt — no, not Brandt, that's the sister here, forgive me — Brenner. Anton Brenner. And the sergeant who wouldn't give the order, Sergeant Vogel, they shot him first, standing, and he looked at the lieutenant the whole time and never once closed his eyes—"

He went on. Sebastian listened. Outside, the snow thickened against the window, erasing the road, the wall, the world, and inside the ward the other men slept or pretended to, and Sister Brandt passed twice on soft feet and did not interrupt, because she had seen this before too: the dying paying out their cargo, and someone sitting to receive it.

When the man finally slept — truly slept, the shallow bright sleep that is only a rehearsal — Sebastian stayed in the chair a while longer, writing the names on the back of a temperature chart in pencil, pressing hard, the way one presses on something that must survive being carried.

Josef Hollmann.
Weiss (given name unknown; telephonist, 2nd company).
Anton Brenner.
Sergeant Vogel — executed without trial, 15 October 1917, Kesselbach.

Four names. Four more than the record carried.

At half past two in the morning, Private — the coat bore no name tape; the army had stopped sewing them in — Private Nothing died between one breath and the next, so quietly that Sister Brandt, dozing in her chair by the stove, only woke when Sebastian stood and drew the sheet up.

"He gave me something," Sebastian told her.

"His effects?"

"Better than that."

She looked at him for a moment, this strange orderly who sat with the dying more often than any chaplain and who never seemed to lose sleep over it, though everyone lost sleep eventually in Vellmar. She didn't ask. In fifteen years she had learned that Sebastian Almeida answered exactly the questions he chose to answer, and that prying at him was like prying at frozen ground: you broke your tool and got nowhere.

* * *

His room above the infirmary was small and cold and he liked it that way. Cold kept things honest. He lit the lamp, turned the wick low — a flame too bright invited attention through curtains, and attention was the one currency he had spent fifteen years refusing to earn — and knelt to pull the trunk from under the bed.

The trunk held a dead man's life. That was how it had begun, anyway: the notebook had belonged to Corporal Emmerich Voss, a stretcher-bearer killed at Saint-Aubin in the spring of '17, whose effects were to be burned along with the rest of the field hospital's paper when the sector collapsed. Sebastian had been twenty-four and newly certain of exactly one thing, which was that burning was not the same as forgetting, whatever the officers believed. He had taken the notebook out of the fire crate and put it inside his shirt, and he had felt the cardboard against his ribs all night like a second heartbeat, and by morning he understood that he had not stolen a notebook. He had adopted a debt.

Fifteen years later the debt had children. The original notebook was full, and two more volumes had followed it, bought one at a time from the stationer in Vellmar, paid for in cash, carried home wrapped in butcher's paper among onions and bread. Inside them, in a small careful hand that never varied because variation was evidence, were the names.

Not all the names. He had learned long ago that completeness was impossible and that chasing it was how ledgers got found. But the ones he could reach — murmured by the dying, smuggled out in letters, traded across canteen tables by men who needed to tell someone and didn't know why — those went in. Date, place, unit where known, manner of death, and the sentence the official record had used instead. *Unfit for record. Died of exposure, non-combat. Returned to duty (deceased).* That last one was his favorite, if favorite was the word for a wound you kept touching: a man shot at dawn and entered in the regimental book as having returned to duty. Somewhere, presumably, clerks were still waiting for him to report.

He unlocked the trunk — the key on a cord around his neck, under his shirt, next to the skin, where it had lived so long he sometimes forgot it was there until it pressed cold against his chest on winter mornings, a small iron reminder — and lifted out the third volume.

The lamp hissed. Snow ticked against the window like fingernails, gentle, patient.

He wrote the date. He wrote *Kesselbach wood, October 1917,* and beneath it the account as the man had given it, compressed to its bones: *Stretcher parties suspended by order; 41 wounded left in wood, froze over approx. 3 days; calls heard by unit. Official return: 'casualties consistent with operations.'*

Then the names. Hollmann. Weiss. Brenner. Vogel.

His pen paused over Vogel. There was a rule he had made for himself in the beginning, one of the few rules, and it was this: write only what can be corroborated or witnessed, because a ledger that exaggerates is a ledger that can be dismissed. He had no witness for Vogel except a dying man's memory. But the dying man had described the sergeant's open eyes with such terrible specificity that Sebastian knew the image had not been invented. Memory like that is not manufactured. It is endured.

He wrote it down.

When he finished, he did what he always did: he read the page back once, closed the volume, and sat with his hands flat on the cover, feeling the weight of it. People imagined that carrying a secret was like carrying a stone — that it grew heavier with the years. It wasn't true. The ledger weighed less every year, gram by gram, as the paper aged and dried. What grew heavier was everything else: the silence itself, packed down like snow, layer upon layer, until it had the density of stone and the temperature of it too.

He thought, as he thought every night, about the bridge.

Vellmar lived and died by its bridge. In summer it rang with carts and cattle and the river boats' cargoes moving toward the mainland; in winter it closed — officially for the ice, unofficially because the garrison preferred the town sealed, a town sealed was a town predictable — and for five months the only way out was the ferry, when the ferry ran, which was when the garrison allowed it to run. Every autumn Sebastian told himself the same lie: this year I'll send it across before the closing. This year I'll find the courier, the crate, the moment. And every autumn the frost came faster than his courage, and the ledger went back under the bed, and he told himself that keeping it safe was the same as keeping it alive, and that alive was enough.

Fifteen years of that arithmetic. He was good at it now. That was the shameful part — not the fear, but the competence. He could lie to a colonel's adjutant with his pulse steady. He could watch a trainload of inspectors burn a field archive and keep his face arranged into the mild vacancy of a man thinking about lunch. He had made himself invisible so thoroughly that sometimes, late at night, he wondered whether anything was left underneath — whether Sebastian Almeida was a man at all anymore, or just the shape a silence had learned to wear.

The dying soldier's voice came back to him. *They were shot at dawn and listed as unfit for record.*

Unfit. As if a name could spoil. As if memory went bad like meat.

He blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the snow, and for a long time he did not sleep. When he did, he dreamed of the wood at Kesselbach, which he had walked through once in the spring of '18 on a supply detail, and how the thaw had raised things out of the ground that no one was ordered to collect, and how he had collected them anyway, in the only way left to him — by kneeling in the wet black earth and saying each recovered name aloud to no one, once, before covering it again.

* * *

Morning came white and windless. Sebastian was in the ward early, changing dressings, emptying pans, doing the small anonymous work that made him furniture in the eyes of the garrison — and furniture, he had learned, was never searched.

Sister Brandt met him by the stove with two cups of the barley coffee that passed for coffee in Vellmar, and handed him one, and said, in the tone of a woman remarking on the weather:

"There's a new flag on the commandant's car. Did you see? Came over the bridge yesterday evening, before the closing. Big black staff car, mud to the windows."

"I heard it," Sebastian said. Mud to the windows meant it had come a long way, and fast, and recently. Staff cars that clean meant ceremony. Staff cars that filthy meant business.

"They say it's Colonel Hessler himself. Come to oversee the winter checkpoints." She sipped, grimaced at the coffee out of habit rather than disappointment. "And they say — this is Frau Kellner's talk, from the canteen, so weigh it accordingly — that he's asking questions. About records. Old ones. Irregular ones, whatever that means."

The cup was warm in Sebastian's hands. He drank, slowly, and let none of it show, because fifteen years of practice was not a skill that ever rested.

"Irregular records," he repeated mildly. "In a town like this? Our biggest irregularity is the price of coal."

"Mm." Sister Brandt looked at him a moment longer than the joke deserved. Then she shrugged and went back to her charts, and the ward filled with morning light and coughing and the ordinary machinery of the living.

But Sebastian stood a while at the east window, looking down at the bridge.

The river was icing at the edges, thin white teeth growing inward from both banks. By week's end the chain barriers would go up at either end of the span, and the garrison sentries would take their posts, and Vellmar would become what it became every winter: a room with the door shut. And somewhere in that black mud-flecked car, or soon in the commandant's office above the checkpoint, a man whose signature Sebastian had seen on documents he was never supposed to see would begin asking his courteous questions about pages that should have burned.

Sebastian finished his coffee. Beneath his shirt, against his skin, the trunk key lay cold as a coin from a riverbed.

There were, he counted silently, one hundred and twelve names in the ledger now. One hundred and fifteen, as of last night.

And the bridge was still open — barely, technically, for another few days of frost and luck.

For the first time in fifteen years, he let himself think the whole thought through to its end: *it has to cross this winter. All of it. Or it dies in this town with me.*

Down at the checkpoint, a soldier was already dragging the first chain barrier out of its shed.
