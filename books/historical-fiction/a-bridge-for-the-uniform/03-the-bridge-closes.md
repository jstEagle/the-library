## 3. The Bridge Closes

The frost came to Vellmar in the night like a debt collector — silently, and with authority.

Sebastian Almeida felt it before he saw it. He had risen before dawn out of fifteen years' habit, the infirmary's rhythms written into his body now as surely as a heartbeat, and when he opened the shutter over the scullery sink to draw water for the kettles, the cold reached up through the pump handle and took his fingers by the wrist. He looked down at the yard and found it transformed: every stone wearing a thin white skin, the woodpile furred silver, the puddle by the drainage grate locked into a pane of clouded glass that cracked faintly under his boot heel when he crossed to the well.

Above the rooftops of the lower town, above the chimneys bleeding their first smoke of the morning, the river ran grey and slow and heavy, carrying slush in its current the way a tired man carries firewood. And across it, half-veiled in river mist, stood the bridge.

It was not a beautiful bridge. It had been built for wagons and artillery, five spans of grey stone on squat piers, its parapets scarred and patched where shrapnel had gnawed them during the retreat years. But it was the only crossing for thirty kilometres in either direction, and everything in Vellmar — the grain, the coal, the letters, the lies, the dead men's names if they were ever to travel anywhere — had to pass over those five spans or wait.

By noon the garrison would have posted the closure notice. Sebastian knew this the way he knew the date of his own blood donation ledger, the way he knew the anniversary of Saint-Aubin without ever writing it in any calendar. First hard frost, then the chain crews, then the checkpoints. Every year it came, and every year, for fifteen years, it had meant nothing more to him than colder water and longer nights in the ward.

This year it meant the road was gone.

He carried the kettles through to the infirmary hall, past the eight beds that were all the town could sustain through winter, past old Weissmann in the corner bed who slept with his hands open on the blanket as though still waiting for someone to put something in them. The stove ticked and breathed its iron warmth. Outside the tall east window, the light came down thin and colourless, the kind of light that made everything look already remembered rather than merely seen.

"Almeida." Sister Brech, the head nurse, stood at the dispensary counter crushing something in a mortar with more force than the task required. She was sixty and built like a door and she had run this infirmary longer than Sebastian had been in Vellmar, which was saying something, because he had been in Vellmar eleven years and had never once told her why he'd come. "Commandant wants the ward inventory by Thursday. New man doesn't trust old paperwork, apparently."

"New man?"

"Hessler." She said the name the way one sets down something heavy — flat, careful, done quickly. "Transferred from the eastern district. Arrived Tuesday with two staff cars and a clerk who looks twelve. There's to be an announcement at the market square at midday. Winter order."

"I'll have the inventory ready," Sebastian said, because there was nothing else to say, and because saying small true things had kept him alive through larger false times. But his hands, wrapping the kettle's wire handle in cloth, were very steady, and he noticed their steadiness the way a gambler notices a tell — with the sudden, cold understanding that steadiness itself could be a performance, and that he had been performing so long he could no longer locate the audience.

* * *

The whole town turned out at the market square, because in Vellmar the winter order was a kind of theatre everyone attended whether they loved it or feared it, and usually both. The square wore its frost like a borrowed uniform; the fountain was dry and lidded; the fishmongers had covered their stalls and gone home. Snow had begun around ten o'clock, fine and deliberate, the flakes small as punctuation.

Colonel Hessler stood on the steps of the customs house beneath the imperial eagle, whose gilt had dulled to the colour of old butter. Sebastian positioned himself at the edge of the crowd, beside a stack of empty crates, in the practiced manner of a man who has spent fifteen years learning exactly how far back from things to stand.

Hessler was not what Sebastian had expected, which unsettled him more than anything the man actually said. He had expected bulk, bluster, some cousin of the red-faced captains he remembered from the front. Instead the commandant was lean and grey and precise, perhaps fifty-five, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten — medium features, medium height, a voice pitched exactly between courtesy and command. He wore his greatcoat buttoned to the throat despite the crowd's muffled industry of scarves and mittens. He did not shout. He read.

"The bridge closes today at four o'clock," Hessler said, and the telegraph operator's boy relayed each line to the clerk beside him, who wrote it down, which struck Sebastian as absurd until he understood the man intended the announcement itself to become record. "The river will carry ice within the month and the crossing will be unsafe for wheeled traffic until thaw. A checkpoint will operate at each end of the bridge. All persons crossing, in either direction, will present papers. All goods will be inspected. This is not a punishment. It is an order, and orders are how a town survives a winter intact."

A low murmur moved through the crowd, the sound of a town doing arithmetic: the ferryman's winter wages, the post schedule, the price of coal coming by the long road or not at all.

"Additionally." Hessler folded his paper in half, once, precisely, and looked up. For a moment his gaze traversed the crowd with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory. "In the spring, the district will conduct a review of wartime records. Hospital registers, burial returns, casualty reports. The war has been over for a year. It is time our paperwork matched our peace." He paused. "Irregular records will need to be accounted for. I mention this now so that no one is surprised in April."

The snow fell into the silence. Someone coughed. Father Anselm, standing near the fountain with his collarless black coat and his ruined cassock's ghost of a white band, went very still, and Sebastian saw it — saw the stillness — because he knew stillness the way other men knew speech, having lived inside it for fifteen years.

Then Hessler thanked the town for its attention, descended the steps, accepted a salute from the sergeant of the new checkpoint detail, and was driven away in a staff car whose tyres hissed on the fresh snow. The crowd broke apart into knots of conversation, most of it about coal. Sebastian remained by the crates a moment longer, watching the place where the car had been, reading the tyre tracks like a sentence he would be parsing for months.

*Irregular records.*

Not *missing* records. Not *damaged* records. Irregular. As though someone had already counted the pages that should exist and found the count short.

* * *

That evening the chain crews closed the bridge.

Sebastian watched it happen, because he made himself watch, because watching was a discipline and hiding was only ever watching taken to its furthest degree. From the embankment above the northern abutment he stood in the falling dusk among a handful of other townspeople — silent, breath rising in columns — while six soldiers uncoiled the great winter chain from its drum and drew it across the full width of the roadway, link by groaning link, and padlocked it to the iron ring set into the parapet stone on the far side.

The chain was old and painted black and thick as a man's forearm, and when the last link settled against the last link with a sound like a heavy book closing, a few people in the little crowd sighed — not dramatically, just the exhalation of people accepting a season. Sebastian's breath did not come out at all for several seconds.

Five spans. One chain. Thirty kilometres to the next crossing, and the next crossing was through hill country where the roads drifted shut by December anyway. The mainland post office — the only post office that mattered, the one with the mail boat connection in thaw and the district sorting room and the honest clerks who stamped things and filed things and sent things where they were addressed without asking what they were — lay on the other side of that chain, and would lie on the other side of it until the ice broke.

He had known this, of course. He had known it since he first unpacked his trunk in the infirmary attic eleven autumns ago and placed the field ledger at the bottom, beneath the linens, wrapped in oilcloth. He had known it every year when the frost came. But knowledge is a thing you can hold at arm's length, and tonight the arm's length was gone; tonight the chain was drawn across the road and somewhere behind him, in the lit windows of the customs house, a precise grey man was asking questions about pages.

He walked home the long way, along the river path, because the river path was dark and dark was where he thought best. The ice was forming at the margins already, delicate lacework fringing the stones, advancing inward with the patience of something that answered to no one. In fifteen years the river had never frozen solid enough to cross on foot. He checked it anyway. He checked it every year. Some rituals are prayers, and some are rehearsals, and some you cannot distinguish even from the inside.

At the bend below the chapel he stopped. Above him, small in the hillside, the chapel's single window burned yellow, and he thought of Anselm going still at the words *irregular records*, and wondered what a defrocked priest kept in a graveyard shed, and how much of his own fear was really recognition.

* * *

The infirmary was quiet by nine. Sister Brech had gone home; the night porter dozed over his newspaper in the entry alcove, which suited Sebastian, because the newspaper provided cover for both of them. He made his rounds — Weissmann breathing in his slow tidal way, a farm boy with a broken ankle sleeping off the morphine, the rest of the beds empty and sheeted like furniture in a house awaiting sale — and then climbed the narrow stair to the attic.

His room was small and clean and deliberately unremarkable: a bed, a shelf of unremarkable books, a washstand, a chair. Eleven years of living had been arranged here with the care of a stage set, everything visible chosen, everything important invisible. He knelt at the foot of the bed, pressed the floorboard at its north corner — it gave with the small wooden sigh he knew better than his own joints — and lifted out the bundle.

Oilcloth. Then a linen sack that had once held surgical dressings. Then the ledger itself.

It was not a grand object. That had always been part of its survival — it looked like nothing, a soldier's notebook bound in scuffed grey card, water-stained, the sort of thing a hundred thousand men had carried and lost and left in ditches. Its previous owner had been a corporal named Brandt who had died at Saint-Aubin with his pockets full of unsent letters, and Sebastian had taken the notebook off the dead cart in the grey hour before dawn, and in the first page, above the printed regimental stamp, he had written a line he still could not reread without his chest tightening:

*For the ones the reports forgot. May someone find you.*

Fifteen years. Forty-one pages of entries in his own hand, growing steadier over the years, the ink changing from the cheap violet of the field hospital to the brown-black of a town clerk's pen. Names, ranks where he knew them, dates, places, and — in a column he had ruled himself, in the beginning, with a borrowed straightedge — the word the official reports had used instead. *Unfit for record.* Shot without trial at the crossroads behind the third dressing station. Left in the wire because the retrieval party was refused. Died of wounds untreated because the convoy was redirected for men whose paperwork was in order.

He did not open it tonight. He sat on the edge of the bed with it on his knees, feeling its weight — less than a kilogram of paper and card, and heavier than the stove, heavier than the chain on the bridge — and listened to the building settle around him, the pipes ticking, the wind finding the eaves.

Three days ago the ledger had been a private grief, a vigil kept alone. Tonight it was contraband. The difference was one man's voice on a custom house step, one carefully folded piece of paper, one word — *irregular* — and the entire geography of his life had reorganised itself around that word like iron filings around a magnet.

He thought: *I could burn it.*

He had thought it before, in eleven years, maybe a dozen times, always late at night, always with the specific vertigo of a man standing at a high window. Burn it and be only an orderly again. Burn it and sleep like other men. Burn it and let the dead stay erased, since erasure, whatever else could be said of it, was at least quiet.

But the frost had changed the arithmetic. Burning required only courage, and courage was available at any hour. Keeping required luck, and luck had just been locked behind a chain on the far side of a freezing river until April. And in April, inspectors. In April, men with clipboards and authority going shelf by shelf through whatever archives the district still held, and a grey colonel who had announced, publicly, gently, in advance, that irregularities would be *accounted for* — which was a mercy, if you believed in mercy, and a dragnet, if you didn't.

And beneath all of it, the question he had managed not to ask for fifteen years rose now with the patience of the ice advancing from the riverbank: *If keeping the ledger was protection, protection for whom? For them, who could not speak? Or for me, who could not bear to act?*

He wrapped the ledger back in its layers, returned it to the floor, pressed the board home. Then he sat at the washstand with the candle and wrote, on ordinary infirmary stationery, the top half of the ward inventory due Thursday — Weissmann, the farm boy, the stock counts, the mundane liturgy of a quiet life — and his handwriting was perfectly steady, and he hated it.

* * *

The knock came at half past ten, three measured strikes, and Sebastian's body understood before his mind did — he was across the room and standing naturally beside the bookshelf before the second strike finished, fifteen years of drills surfacing from wherever such things are stored.

Through the door: "Orderly Almeida? Commandant's compliments. He requests your presence at the customs house tomorrow evening, seven o'clock." A pause. "Regarding hospital service records. His Excellency is reviewing personnel files for the spring audit."

Sebastian opened the door. A young lieutenant, snow on his shoulders, breath steaming, holding a card. Sebastian took the card. Thick stock, good printing, a time and an address and nothing else — an invitation dressed as a summons, or a summons dressed as an invitation, and no way to know which except by attending.

"Shall I confirm?" the lieutenant asked.

"There's no need," Sebastian said. "I'll be there."

The lieutenant saluted — a formality so far out of proportion to an orderly's station that it could only have been instructed, could only have been a message in itself — and crunched away down the stairs. Sebastian closed the door and stood a long time in the candlelight with the card in his hand.

Across the river the chain hung black against the running water. Somewhere south, beyond the hills and the drifts and the thirty kilometres of closed road, the world went on receiving mail. And here in Vellmar, in eleven days, an orderly with clean handwriting would sit down opposite the man whose signature might already sit in some file beside the word *ledger*, and lie, and hate how easily it came.

He pinched out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the river move under its new lace of ice, carrying its slush patiently toward the sea, toward everywhere he could no longer go.
