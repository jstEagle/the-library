## 5. What the Chaplain Buried

The graveyard kept its own hours, and Father Anselm kept the graveyard's.

He was there before light most mornings, before the frost had decided whether it would be frost or rime, moving between the rows with a broom he had made himself from birch twigs and a length of broken shovel handle. No one had asked him to tend the ground. No one had asked him anything since the winter of 1918, when the garrison chaplaincy had been taken from him as cleanly as a tooth pulled — a brief letter, an official seal, and then the peculiar freedom of being nobody at all. A defrocked priest was a curious object in a garrison town. Too conspicuous to ignore entirely, too pitiable to punish further. The soldiers called him *the gravedigger* without malice, and he had come to accept the title the way one accepts a limp: it was his, it was accurate, and it spared everyone the embarrassment of more precise language.

The truth was that he liked the dead. They did not lie to him. They lay in their rows — ninety-one of them now, though only sixty-three had stones — and they asked nothing except that someone remember which mound held which man, and Anselm, who had failed them in every way that mattered while they breathed, could at least do this.

That morning the cold came up off the river like something with intention. It found the gap between his collar and his neck, worked into the knuckles gripping the broom, settled into the old break in his left foot that ached each November like a debt collector. He swept snow from the flat stone of grave forty-four, a boy named Weiss who had died of his wounds three days after Anselm had prayed over him and been assured by the surgeons that prayer was all that remained anyway. Then forty-five. Then forty-six, unnamed on the marker because the paperwork had been lost, or because no one had looked for it, or because — and here Anselm's broom always slowed — because the man had been shot by his own side and the army did not put its own bullets on its own stones.

He finished the row and stood a moment, breath steaming, looking down the long white slope toward the river. Vellmar's bridge ran across the frozen water like a sentence someone had started and abandoned mid-clause. In another week the ice would thicken around its pilings and the town's whole life would contract to the far bank of that sentence: no carts out, no mail in, no way across for anyone until the thaw released them all together.

He was thinking about this — about confinement, about how much easier it is to bury a man than to keep him — when he saw the figure coming up through the gate.

* * *

Sebastian Almeida walked the graveyard path the way he walked everywhere, thought Anselm: as if trying to take up less space than a man actually requires. The nurse had been in Vellmar four years now, working the infirmary, and in four years he had spoken perhaps two hundred words to Anselm, most of them about laudanum dosages and bed linens. But there was a quality to him that Anselm had recognized from the first month, because he had seen it in mirrors and in the eyes of other men who had stood where they had stood: a watchfulness worn so long it had gone smooth, like a river stone. A man carrying something heavy who had learned to carry it invisibly.

"Father." Sebastian stopped at the low wall, gloved hands resting on it, not entering uninvited. That too was characteristic. "You're out early."

"The dead don't mind the cold. It's the living who complain, and I've grown tired of listening to myself." Anselm leaned the broom against the wall. "Come in out of the wind, at least. There's no warmth here, but there's less wind among the stones."

Sebastian hesitated, then came through the gate. His boots broke the fresh crust of snow along the path, and Anselm noticed — filed away, the way he noticed everything now, hoarding small observations the way a starving man hoards crumbs — that the nurse glanced instinctively toward the garrison road before doing so. Checking. Always checking.

"You buried a man Tuesday," Sebastian said. "Kolarik. I dressed his hands at the infirmary last month. The frostbite."

"I did. I gave him what words I could. There weren't many who'd have known to give him more." Anselm studied him. "Did you come about Kolarik?"

"No." The word came out carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile. Sebastian turned to face the rows of markers, the plain crosses and the blanker stones, and Anselm watched his jaw work once, twice, around whatever he had actually come to say. The river wind moved through the bare lindens at the graveyard's edge with a sound like paper turning.

"I came," Sebastian said finally, "because I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer me honestly, and I need you to decide right now whether you can."

Anselm felt something shift in his chest — not fear exactly, though fear was in it. Fifteen years of careful obscurity had made him fluent in the grammar of sentences that begin that way. "Ask."

"When you were at Saint-Aubin." Sebastian's voice stayed level, but only the way a wire stays level under load. "The autumn of 1917. The executions after the retreat — the men from the Third Column who were never court-martialed. Do you know how many there were?"

The wind again. Somewhere below, on the river, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like the earth clearing its throat before speaking.

"Why do you ask me that, Nurse Almeida?"

"Because I was there," Sebastian said. "And I think you were too. And I think neither of us has said certain names aloud since, and I want to know if I'm wrong about you before I say mine."

* * *

There it was, then. Laid on the frozen ground between them like something exhumed.

Anselm turned away — not in refusal but because some responses required that a man not be watched while having them. He walked slowly down the row of graves, past forty-six, past fifty, his bad foot dragging its familiar half-beat, and let the memory rise, which it did not need much invitation. It never did. It lived in him the way shrapnel lives in a body: mostly quiet, always present, and liable to announce itself near weather changes.

Saint-Aubin in the autumn of 1917 had smelled of carbolic and mud and, underneath both, the sweetish rot of a war going wrong. He had been thirty-nine years old, ten years a priest, six months at that hospital, and he had still believed — he could barely credit it now — that the uniform he wore as chaplain meant something, that the cross on his collar placed him outside the chain of command even as the chain of command signed his rations. Then the retreat had collapsed the front, and the Third Column had broken, and Colonel Hessler — younger then, hungrier, already writing reports with the tidy hand Anselm would later see on burial orders — had needed examples.

Nine men. Anselm knew it was nine the way he knew the number of his own ribs. Nine men taken behind the supply sheds over three days, tried by nothing, sentenced by a signature, and shot at dawn against the drainage embankment where the ground was soft enough to dig. And on the third morning, Hessler had sent for him.

"He wanted a blessing," Anselm said aloud. He hadn't meant to speak; the words simply arrived, the way water finds a crack. Behind him he heard Sebastian go very still. "The firing party. He said it was for morale — that the men wouldn't shoot straight without the Church's sanction, that God's absence would unsettle them. As if God were a matter of marksmanship." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "I refused. I told him I would pray for the dead men's souls but I would not consecrate the killing of men who had received no trial, no judgment, nothing but a colonel's bad morning. And I stood there in front of the firing party and I did not bless them, and they shot the prisoner anyway without my blessing, and it changed precisely nothing except me."

"And then?"

"Then I was defrocked. Quietly. A letter rather than a tribunal — Hessler is not a man who likes tribunals; tribunals leave records." Anselm turned back. Sebastian had not moved from beside the wall, but his hands were no longer on it; they hung at his sides, curled slightly, as if holding something. "They gave me a choice, actually, which was more mercy than I expected. Leave the garrison system entirely, or stay as a civilian laborer. Bury the bodies, tend the ground, keep my mouth shut. I chose the ground." He gestured at the white rows around him. "People assume I stayed out of penance, and they're right, but probably not in the way they imagine. You see, Father Anselm of the collar could have demanded names, could have written to bishops, could have made noise. But a gravedigger is invisible. And invisible men can count."

Sebastian crossed to him slowly, over the frozen ground, and when he spoke his voice had dropped to almost nothing, though there was no living soul within a hundred meters. "Count what?"

"Come inside," Anselm said. "It's too cold for this out here. And some things shouldn't be said standing over graves. It confuses the dead."

* * *

The chapel at the edge of the garrison cemetery was a small stone building, deconsecrated in everything but habit — the army used it for storage in summer, and in winter Anselm kept a stove burning in it and sat there in the evenings with tea he brewed from dried nettle because proper tea had become a memory. The interior smelled of candle wax and stone dust and, faintly, of incense burned years ago by a church that had since disowned him. He lit the lamp, fed the stove, poured the nettles, and watched Sebastian Almeida seat himself on the bench nearest the door — nearest the exit, Anselm noted, and understood, and forgave.

Then the priest went to the third pew from the back, knelt, and worked loose the plank beneath the seat. It came free with a groan of old nails. From the cavity beneath he lifted a oilcloth package, unwrapped it, and set on the pew between them a notebook bound in grey cardboard — a common army notebook, the kind issued to clerks and quartermasters, the kind that cost nothing and recorded everything and was supposed to be returned to stores when full.

"My ledger of the unnamed," Anselm said. "Every man I buried without his name on his stone. Every man buried *as if* he had no name, when we both knew he had one and that the name had been subtracted deliberately." He opened it. The pages were dense with his careful, cramped hand — dates, plot numbers, physical descriptions, and, where he had them, names. Names gathered from dying men's lips, from letters smuggled out in coat linings, from the murmurs of comrades who came to stand at the fence. "Sixty-eight entries. Some of them are just guesses, and those I marked as guesses, because a false name on a true grave is its own kind of sin. But most of them I know. I know them better than I know my own parish register, and I was a good parish priest, God forgive the boast."

Sebastian was looking down at the notebook the way a man looks at a mirror he didn't expect to find. His hand rose, hovered, touched the page.

"May I?"

"It's why I'm showing you."

The nurse took the book with both hands, and Anselm watched him read, and watched the reading change him — watched the controlled stillness of his face develop hairline fractures around the eyes, watched him turn pages with the exaggerated care of a man handling a wound. And Anselm understood, with the slow certainty of a bell sounding, that he had guessed rightly. That Almeida had not come to ask whether Anselm knew anything. He had come because he carried something himself, and had been carrying it alone, and had finally reached the point where silence weighed more than risk.

"There were nine at Saint-Aubin," Anselm said quietly. "After the retreat. I buried seven of them myself, in the field plots, under stones marked only with numbers. Two were carted elsewhere — I never learned where. Their families were told they fell honorably, or were told nothing, depending on whether anyone bothered to write."

"Kellner," Sebastian said. The name came out of him like something drawn from a depth. "Corporal Tomas Kellner. Fourth Column, attached to the Third during the retreat. Shot the morning of the third day."

Anselm closed his eyes. "Yes. Yes — tall man, dark hair, a scar through the left eyebrow. He argued with the escort. Not violently — he argued the way an educated man argues, as if reason might still apply. It was the arguing they disliked most, I think. I buried him at plot nineteen, field section, and I put no name on any document because documents went through the commandant's office, but I wrote him in my book." He opened the ledger again, turned to the marked page, and turned it toward Sebastian. *Plot 19. Tall, dark, scar left brow. Comrades said: KELLNER, T., Cpl., 4th Col. Wife in Vellmar, canteen keeper's family. Prayed for him by name, though no one heard.*

Sebastian read it, and then, with hands that were not quite steady, drew from inside his coat — from against his chest, where Anselm now realized the man had been keeping some flat weight all these years, all these visits, all these two hundred words about linens — a second book. Older. Water-marked. A dead man's notebook, Anselm would learn, though he didn't know it yet.

Two ledgers lay open on the pew in the lamplight, one recording the burials and one recording the deaths, and where they overlapped — Anselm could see it even before either of them said it, could see it in the matching dates, the converging details, the terrible arithmetic of two witnesses arriving independently at the same truths — where they overlapped, they corroborated. Not memory anymore. Record. Two records, kept fifteen years apart by two men who had each believed himself alone.

"Do you understand what this is?" Sebastian's voice was rough. "Together, they're — a single account isn't proof, it's a claim. But two independent accounts agreeing—"

"I know what it is," Anselm said. His heart was beating oddly, fast and light, like a young man's, and he realized with something like alarm that what he felt was hope, and that hope, after so many years, hurt almost more than guilt. "It's also a firing squad's target list, if it's found. Which is why mine lives under a pew, and yours, I suspect, lives against your ribs, and neither of us has dared to make them meet until now."

"Why now?" Sebastian asked. "Why show me?"

Anselm looked at the stove, at the flames eating the wood he had split that morning, and gave the question the honesty it deserved.

"Because I am tired, Nurse Almeida. Tired of sweeping snow off stones that tell lies. Tired of praying for men whose names I cannot say at the altar because there is no altar and saying them aloud would kill us both. For fifteen years I told myself that keeping these lists was purpose — that remembering in secret was a kind of service. But secret remembrance is only grief wearing a uniform. It becomes purpose the moment it risks something." He laid his hand flat on the grey notebook, over the entry for plot nineteen. "I refused to bless an execution once, and it accomplished nothing except my own ruin, and I have wondered every day since whether ruin was enough — whether refusal without consequence to the guilty is anything more than vanity. So yes. Why now. Because you came through my gate asking about Saint-Aubin, which means the time has finally come when these pages might *do* something. And because—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—because Tomas Kellner has a widow, and I have stood in her canteen and drunk her coffee and never told her, and I would very much like, before I die, to stop being a coward in her particular direction."

Sebastian was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind had risen, and somewhere on the river the ice spoke again in its slow grinding voice, and the lamp flame bent and recovered.

"There's a complication," Sebastian said at last. "Hessler is asking questions. About irregular records. He came to the infirmary yesterday."

And Anselm, who had rehearsed arrest in his imagination so many times that the rehearsal had worn smooth as a rosary bead, felt the cold walk up his spine and settle between his shoulders like a familiar hand.

"Then," he said, "we should decide quickly what we're going to do with all of this — before the man who signs the reports decides for us."
