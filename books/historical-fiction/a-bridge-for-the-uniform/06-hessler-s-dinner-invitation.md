## 6. Hessler's Dinner Invitation

The invitation came on cream cardstock, delivered by a corporal whose boots left gray slush-prints on the infirmary's clean floor.

*Colonel Edric Hessler requests the pleasure of Mr. Sebastian Almeida's company at dinner. Friday, the ninth of December. Seven o'clock. Garrison House.*

Sebastian read it twice, standing very still among the smell of carbolic and wet wool, while the corporal waited with the flat patience of a man who has been told not to leave until it is received. Around them the ward breathed its evening rhythms — a cough, a rattle of bottles, old Ferencz muttering names in his sleep again, the ones no report ever carried.

"Am I to answer?" Sebastian asked.

"No, sir. The Colonel only asks that you come."

*Only asks.* In Vellmar in December, when the bridge was closed and the river was hardening into a road of white iron, there was no difference between an invitation and a summons except the color of the paper.

He said yes, of course. He thanked the corporal, who saluted with the mechanical grace of a boy who should have been still in school, and he stood at the ward window afterward watching the snow come down over the rooftops toward the river, and he thought: *he knows something. He would not feed me if he knew nothing.*

That night he did not open the ledger. He had learned long ago that fear makes men careless, and the surest way to be careless was to touch the thing you were afraid for. Instead he lay on his narrow bed in the room behind the dispensary and listened to the wind worrying the shutters, and he rehearsed, as Anselm rehearsed his litanies, the ordinary facts of his own life. Orderly at the civil infirmary since 1916. Before that, auxiliary nursing staff, provisional posting, Saint-Aubin and two lesser stations. No family. No politics. No handwriting worth remembering.

*You are a gray man,* he told himself. *You have spent fifteen years becoming gray.*

The trouble with lies, he thought as sleep came grudgingly, is that the good ones cost something every time you tell them, and you never notice the spending until the account is empty.

* * *

Friday came down white and windless, the kind of cold that made the trees crack in the park like distant rifle fire. Sebastian dressed carefully — not too well, not too poorly — in the dark suit he kept for funerals, which in Vellmar meant he kept it for everything. He walked to Garrison House through streets where the snow had been shoveled into walls taller than himself, corridors of white through which the townspeople moved with their chins tucked, and he felt the town's silence around him the way one feels a coat: familiar, close, suffocating if you thought about it.

Garrison House had been a merchant's villa before the war, all pale stone and tall windows, and Hessler had made of it something both more orderly and less human. The hedges were cut to a line a surveyor might have drawn. The path was swept to bare stone. A sentry took Sebastian's coat without meeting his eyes.

"You're punctual," said Colonel Hessler from the top of the stairs. "I find punctuality is the last honest virtue."

He came down unhurriedly, a broad man going soft at the edges but soft the way old rope goes soft — the strength still in it, only redistributed. He wore his uniform without decoration beyond what rank required, and his handshake was dry and brief, a man who measured things by contact. His eyes, pale as river ice, went once over Sebastian's face, once over his hands, and settled nowhere long enough to be caught looking.

"Thank you for having me, Colonel."

"Thank you for existing, Mr. Almeida. Do you know how rare it is, in a town this size, to find a man who reads? I checked, you know." He smiled as he said it, gesturing Sebastian toward the dining room. "The lending library keeps a register. You've borrowed eleven books this year. History, mostly. Some poetry. Kerner, even, and Kerner is not easy."

"I find him worth the effort."

"Do you?" Hessler held the door. "So do I. Shall we."

The table was set for two before a fire that had been built by someone else's hands, laid with the geometric neatness of a parade ground. Soup first, amber and steaming, served by a silent soldier who might have been the same corporal from the infirmary or another boy cut from the same cloth. Hessler talked. That was the first thing Sebastian noticed and the thing he would remember longest: how much the man talked, easily, amiably, about the price of coal and the state of the road east and the lamentable quality of the garrison's winter beef — talk as terrain, talk as patrol route, each anecdote laid out like a track across country he wanted to see whether Sebastian could walk.

"The bridge closes properly next week," Hessler said, breaking bread. "Ice on the cables, ice on the planking. After that, Vellmar is an island. Do you find that oppressive?"

"I find it quiet."

"Quiet." He tasted the word. "Yes. I've noticed that about this town. It is very quiet. Fifteen years quiet. One might almost say — practiced." He smiled to show the sentence had been a joke, and ate his soup.

Sebastian ate too. The soup was excellent, veal and sweet herbs, and he hated it.

* * *

The second course was river pike, and with it Hessler turned, with the smoothness of a ferry coming about, to Saint-Aubin.

"You were there," he said. "Nineteen seventeen, into the spring of eighteen. Auxiliary nurse, provisional commission — the records call it a 'compassionate posting,' which I take to mean they needed hands and asked no questions. Hard duty. I've read the station summaries. Typhus in the third ward, then the retreat casualties in the autumn. Terrible arithmetic." He forked a piece of fish with precision. "Tell me what it was like. The summaries never capture anything, do they? Ink dries so much faster than blood."

It was beautifully done. There was no menace in it, none that could be pointed to; there was only a large, courteous man asking an old soldier-nurse to reminisce, in a warm room, over good wine. And underneath it, precise as a probe finding the depth of a wound, the question moving.

"It was cold," Sebastian said. "Mostly, it was cold. Cold does most of the killing at a field station; people imagine wounds, but it's cold and it's fever. We worked until we dropped, slept in shifts, and the men died in an order that had nothing to do with fairness. I carried a great many stretchers. That's my true memory of Saint-Aubin — the weight of stretchers. Everything else has gone soft."

"Everything?"

"Memory is kind that way. Or cruel, depending."

Hessler considered him over the rim of his glass. "And the records? You must have handled them. A station that size generates paperwork like smoke. Admissions, discharges, transfers—" a fractional pause, the length of a heartbeat, "—deaths."

"We filled them out. The senior clerk signed them. What happened to them after, I couldn't say. Paper went into a case, the case went into the archive wagon, the wagon went wherever wagons go."

"Into fires, sometimes." Hessler said it lightly, tasting his wine. "You'll have heard. The regional military archive at Drennau burned in the spring — stove accident, officially. Very convenient stove. Seventeen years of station records for this whole sector, gone. Except." He set the glass down and turned it slowly by its stem, and for the first time all evening his voice acquired texture, grain, the sound of a file drawn across metal. "Except that burned archives are never entirely burned. Paper burns unevenly, Mr. Almeida. And some pages were never in the archive at all. My predecessors conducted certain audits — irregular audits, informal ones — and those audits noted discrepancies. Station returns that didn't reconcile. Burial registers with blank name-columns where names ought to be. Men recorded as *unfit for record*, which is a phrase, isn't it, that has always struck me as administrative cowardice wearing a uniform."

He looked up. The firelight moved in his pale eyes.

"A station like Saint-Aubin had perhaps four hundred men pass through it whose files now exist nowhere. Four hundred men, Mr. Almeida, unaccounted for by any document in the Republic. And yet somebody counted them. Somebody always counts. Clerks count, nurses count, priests count. Counting is the one form of disobedience that looks exactly like diligence." He refilled Sebastian's glass without being asked. "So I ask myself — not accusing, you understand, merely curious — if a man had been at such a station, in such years, with such hands, and had been the sort of man who counted… where would he have put what he counted?"

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock divided the silence into careful, equal pieces.

Sebastian drank. He let the pause stretch just long enough to be natural — a man considering a hard question in good faith — and then he laughed, softly, ruefully, shaking his head.

"Then you'd want a better class of man than me, Colonel. I counted bandages. If I'd tried to keep records of my own, they'd have been stolen for cigarette paper inside a week — you know how it was at the front, paper was worth more than tobacco. Whatever counting got done got done by officers, for officers, and went wherever their papers went. Into the stove at Drennau, I'd imagine, with everything else." He turned a piece of pike on his plate. "Though I'll tell you what I do remember, since you asked what it was like. The names. Not on paper — in the wards. Men saying their own names over and over, feverish, as if they were afraid of losing them. I used to think that was the saddest thing I'd ever heard. A man guarding his own name because he suspected no one else would."

For a moment — only a moment — something moved behind Hessler's stillness, some assessment recalibrating itself in the dark. Then the courtesy reassembled itself like troops presenting arms.

"That is a very good answer," he said quietly.

"It's the truth. Truth usually is good; it doesn't have to be invented on the spot."

"No," Hessler agreed. "That's its great economy. Though in my experience, Mr. Almeida, the truth is also like money — men rarely spend all of it at once. They keep some back. For emergencies."

"Then we're alike that way," Sebastian said, and smiled, and hated himself with a completeness that surprised him, because the smile hadn't shaken at all.

* * *

Dessert was poached pears, and coffee after, taken in a small study lined with maps of the district in frames. Hessler talked of lighter things — a production of *The Bartered Bride* he hoped to bring to the town hall in February, the merits of various stove manufacturers, the education of his nephew in the capital. He was, Sebastian realized with a chill that had nothing to do with weather, genuinely pleasant company. That was the horror of men like this; they were not wolves in drawing rooms, they were hosts, and the wolf was a department of them, filed away, brought out on schedule.

At the door, with his coat already over his arm, Sebastian was stopped by one last sentence, delivered to his back like a hand placed gently between the shoulder blades.

"I'm told you attend old Ferencz at the infirmary," Hessler said. "The veteran. They say he talks in his sleep."

Sebastian turned. The corridor lamp threw his shadow long up the stairwell.

"He's dying, Colonel. Dying men talk. It's mostly rivers and mothers."

"Mostly," Hessler agreed pleasantly. "Give him my regards. And Mr. Almeida — thank you for a civilized evening. In a garrison town, civilization must be cultivated deliberately. Like orchids. It requires warmth, attention—" he fastened the top button of his collar, unhurried, "—and pruning, occasionally, of anything that grows where it wasn't planted."

"Good night, Colonel."

The sentry walked him to the gate. The snow had stopped; the sky was enormous and black and salted with stars, and the cold closed over Sebastian like water. He walked home through the white-walled streets at a pace that was almost, but not quite, a flight, and his breath tore little holes in the air ahead of him, and he did not let himself think until the infirmary door was locked behind him and the lamp lit and his hands, examined in the lamplight, proved to be perfectly steady.

They were steady. That was the thing to hate. Fifteen years ago his hands would have shaken; tonight they moved among the familiar objects of his room with the calm of a surgeon's, and he understood that the calm was the damage, that terror had been trained into him so thoroughly it now wore a polite face and passed the wine and lied about cigarette paper.

He pulled loose the hearthstone behind the dispensary shelf — three turns, a lift — and took out the ledger, and sat holding it without opening it, feeling its weight change as the years went by: once it had weighed grief, now it weighed gunpowder.

Because he had heard what was under Hessler's questions, under the soup and the pears and the talk of orchids. *Burned archives are never entirely burned. Discrepancies. Blank name-columns.* The man was not guessing at shadows. He had audit notes in his possession, fragments of a dead predecessor's suspicions — and somewhere in those dry official phrases Sebastian had heard the shape of the thing Hessler believed in but could not yet prove:

*A ledger exists.*

He was hunting it the way one hunts a wounded animal in snow — patiently, by track and sign, narrowing the circle. And tonight he had walked that circle's edge, dined at its center, and smiled.

Sebastian opened the ledger to the newest page, dipped his pen, and stopped. Three names waited in his memory from Ferencz's murmuring, waiting to be inked. But for the first time in fifteen years, his hand hesitated above the paper — because writing them now was no longer memory. It was evidence.

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice boomed once, deep and resonant, like a door closing in the earth.

And on the morrow, he would learn how fast the circle was closing: for word ran through the town by morning that Colonel Hessler had ordered a house-to-house inventory of "war-era correspondence," beginning with the infirmary — and that the first search was set for Sunday.
