## 8. Ledger Arithmetic

The crypt beneath the chapel of Saint Adelheid smelled of cold stone and old candle fat, a smell that had not changed since Sebastian's first winter in Vellmar and would not change after any of them were gone. That permanence was why Anselm had chosen it. Down here, the dead kept their appointments.

He came down the worn steps last, after checking twice that the graveyard above was empty — once with his eyes and once with his ears, standing in the snow among the tilted markers until his breath had plumed itself out and he could hear nothing but the river working at its ice far below the town. Then he had descended, pulling the door shut behind him, and the world above — the garrison horns, the tramp of boots on frozen mud, the great sealed jaw of the bridge — all of it receded into something like rumor.

A single storm lantern sat on the flat tomb of some long-forgotten prior of the chapel, and around it, four people arranged themselves the way conspirators have always arranged themselves: unevenly, awkwardly, each carrying their fear in a different part of the body.

Anselm stood, because he never sat in consecrated ground anymore, not even this buried remnant of it. Ilse perched on an upturned crate she'd carried down herself, her coat still buttoned to the throat, her fingers working constantly at a fraying thread on her cuff. Marta Kellner leaned against the wall with her arms folded, as though she had come to the crypt to bargain over the price of flour, and only her eyes gave her away — moving from face to face, cataloguing, the way she must have catalogued every soldier who ever passed her canteen window: who was afraid, who was lying, who would break.

And Sebastian knelt by the lantern with the ledger in his hands, feeling its weight for the first time as a weight that could be measured in something other than guilt.

* * *

They had counted the pages before they counted the checkpoints. It seemed right to begin with what they had rather than what stood between them and the rest of it.

The ledger was not one book. It had never been one book, not really — it was a dead man's notebook begun at Saint-Aubin in 1917, then a requisition ledger whose columns Sebastian had taught to lie in two directions at once, then the backs of supply manifests, a hymnal's endpapers, the flyleaves of a French grammar he'd taken from a stretcher case who no longer needed languages. Fifteen years of fragments, gathered last spring into a binding of sailcloth stitched by his own hands, because loose pages burn faster than bound ones, and he had always assumed that someday someone would try.

By lantern light, the stitching showed gray against the dark cloth, and Sebastian worked his thumb along it now like a rosary.

"Read it out," Anselm said quietly. "All of it. We should hear what we're carrying."

So Sebastian did. He opened to the first page and read the heading he had written five years ago, when the thing stopped being instinct and became intention: *For the record that will not be kept.*

Then the sections. He had divided them long ago without quite knowing why — perhaps because a book divided is a book harder to destroy whole, or perhaps because fifteen years of practiced silence teaches a man to think in compartments.

The first section covered Saint-Aubin itself, autumn 1917 through the retreat: eleven names of men shot without trial under the authority of a signature Sebastian now knew belonged to Colonel Hessler. The second held the winter of the freeze — the wounded turned out of the evacuation trains at Verzy Siding, thirty-one names, most of them gathered from other soldiers' letters home, letters Sebastian had copied and burned. The third was the longest and strangest: the *unfit for record* files, men whose deaths existed nowhere in any army paper except as a euphemism, whose existence he had reconstructed from burial details, from Anselm's own half-remembered gravesites, from the mutterings of dying veterans in infirmaries just like the one where he now worked. Forty-six names there. The fourth section held the officers' signatures, copied and cross-referenced, the arithmetic of command laid bare — which colonel had ordered what, on which dates, countersigned by whom. And the fifth, the thinnest, held testimony: three accounts written by men who had survived what they should not have survived, given to Sebastian in whispers in the dark of hospital wards, plus a single page in another hand entirely that Anselm had pressed into the binding two days ago — the chaplain's own list, forty graves, nameless stones, matched now to Sebastian's numbers like two halves of an account finally balancing.

"Eighty-eight names," Sebastian said, closing it. "Fourteen signatures of officers. Three witnesses. One priest." He looked up. "That's what we have."

"That's what we have," Anselm agreed. "Now count what stands between it and the world."

* * *

Ilse did the counting, because counting was what Ilse did. She drew in the frost-dust on the tomb lid with her fingertip — a rough map that appeared and vanished as her breath moved over it, which seemed to Sebastian exactly right: a map that existed only while someone kept breathing on it.

"Mainland post office is at Ferrenholt, past the river," she said. Her voice was low and precise; she spoke the way she transcribed, each word checked before release. "In summer you cross the bridge and you're done. In winter—" She tapped the map. "Winter protocol. Hessler announced it Tuesday. Three checkpoints. Checkpoint one, at the bridgehead on our side — garrison regulars, crate inspections, everything weighed against its manifest. Checkpoint two, mid-bridge, at the old tollhouse. Canteen." A flicker of her eyes toward Marta, quickly away again. "Lighter inspection. They mostly wave people through, but they log everyone by name. Checkpoint three, the far bank, before the ferry road. That one's new. He put it there this week."

"Why?" Marta asked.

"Because he's looking for something," Sebastian said, "and he doesn't know how small it is."

They let that sit. In the lantern light the crypt's vaulted ceiling was lost in darkness, and it felt less like being underground than being inside a bell that had not yet been struck.

"So," Anselm said. "Three gates. One small book. What is the obvious move?"

"Don't make one," Ilse said. "That's the point. Anything hidden looks like treasure. You don't hide a thing in winter traffic — you give it a job." She rubbed the frost from the map and redrew it: the bridge as a line, the checkpoints as ticks, and then, branching off, the legitimate winter shipments she knew about because she transcribed their manifests twice a day and read every message twice, because that was who Ilse was, because God or habit had made her incapable of letting a document pass through her hands only once.

"Medicine crates go across Wednesdays," she said. "Infirmary resupply, signed by the orderly — signed by you, Sebastian. Nobody searches medicine twice, because nobody wants to carry typhoid cultures back out into the cold."

"Grave goods go across when there are burials," Anselm said slowly, catching the shape of it. "The burial registry travels with the body to the mainland parish archive. It has done for sixty years. No soldier alive wants to be the one who delayed a funeral."

"And flour goes whenever I say it does," Marta said. "Canteen requisitions. Sacks get stacked, sacks get dragged, sacks get stabbed open by bayonets when the guards are bored, and nobody reads anything inside a sack of flour but flour." She said it flatly, but her arms tightened across her chest.

Sebastian watched them and felt something turn over in him — slow, heavy, unfamiliar. For fifteen years he had carried this alone, and he had told himself that the carrying was protection: his silence kept him safe, and his safety kept the ledger safe, and somewhere in that closed loop he had mistaken survival for virtue. Now three strangers — not strangers anymore, not after tonight — were kneeling in the frost-dust of a tomb, dividing his contrition into thirds.

It did not feel like relief. It felt like handing your child to strangers on a station platform. But underneath the fear, quieter than the fear, something else was running, steady as water under ice. This was what the ledger had been waiting for. Not a courier. Not a hero. A family, assembled out of a disgraced priest, a telegraph girl, and a widow who sold coffee at the exact center of the machine that had killed her— 

Killed her husband. He didn't know that yet. He knew it only as a dread he couldn't name, because he had read the third section so many times that the names had become architecture to him, load-bearing, and he had forgotten that somewhere outside this crypt a living woman might walk beneath any one of them.

"We split it," he said aloud, and heard his own voice steadier than he expected. "Five sections, three routes, and we never send two pieces together. If one crossing fails, the rest must survive it. That's the whole discipline of it — no page ever knows where its brothers are."

Anselm nodded slowly. "The first section — Saint-Aubin, the eleven — goes with the Wednesday medicine. Small enough to fold into a false lining in an infirmary crate."

"The officer signatures," Ilse said, tapping the fourth section, "should travel separately and last. Signatures alone convict. If everything else burns, those fourteen names are still enough to start an inquiry."

"And the burial registry takes the longest piece," Anselm said. His voice had gone very quiet, the way it did — Sebastian had noticed — whenever faith and fear arrived in the same room and had to be seated next to each other. "There's a burial scheduled for the week after next. Old Frau Behrend, mainland parish. My registry accompanies the coffin. Forty pages of names fit inside a registry of the dead better than anywhere else in God's world. Let the dead vouch for the dead."

"You'd carry it yourself," Marta said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm the only one with a reason to be walking behind a coffin." Anselm smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "Besides. I've been rehearsing my arrest for eight years. It would be a shame to waste the rehearsal."

No one laughed. Ilse's fingers had gone still on her cuff, which from Ilse was the equivalent of a scream.

* * *

They assigned the crossings then, one by one, in the order the shipments would move, and with each assignment Sebastian felt the fear in the room change state — not lessen, never lessen, but convert, the way falling snow converts silently into the weight that breaks an awning. Ilse would handle the paperwork that made the medicine crate invisible; she would also watch the wire, because the wire was her country and she read its weather better than anyone. Marta's flour sacks would take the fifth section, the testimony — human words traveling inside bread, which struck Sebastian as the only mercy in the whole arrangement. The second section, the freeze at Verzy Siding, would wait for Anselm's coffin.

Which left the third section. Forty-six names. The largest and the most dangerous, the one Hessler's burned archive had left a hole shaped exactly like it.

"It stays in the crypt," Sebastian said. "Until the last crossing. Whatever happens, it doesn't move until we know the others got through."

"And if they don't get through?" Marta asked. She asked it without softening, because Marta never softened a question; she only softened afterward, in private, where no one could see.

"Then it stays here until spring does something worse to it than I would," Sebastian said. "But they'll get through."

"How do you know?"

He looked down at the ledger, at the sailcloth binding, at the gray stitching of his own hands. "I don't," he said. "I've simply decided that knowing things isn't required anymore. Only carrying them."

Silence in the crypt, except for the lantern's small negotiations with its own flame. Above them, muffled through six feet of earth and stone, the garrison horn sounded the nine o'clock, and all four of them flinched at it together — and then, absurdly, almost smiled at each other for flinching, and something loosened in the room that none of them named. Fear shared out among four people weighs a quarter as much. That was not in any ledger. It should have been.

* * *

They rose to go, one at a time, spaced apart like beads on a string, because four people leaving a chapel together at night is a conspiracy and four people leaving separately is weather. Ilse went first, hood up, small footsteps already fading into snow-silence. Anselm lingered to snuff the lantern, murmuring something over the tomb that might have been Latin and might have been an apology.

Marta did not move.

She was standing where the ledger lay, and when Sebastian reached to gather it up, her hand came down flat on the cover. Not seizing it. Resting. The way you rest a hand on a door you are afraid to open.

"Show me the third section," she said.

"Marta—"

"I've fed your coffee, Almeida. I've watched you carry your grief around like a full tray for two years and never once spilled it, and I never asked, because asking is a debt and I pay mine in silence." Her voice stayed level, but beneath it he could hear the canteen in it — the woman who heard everything soldiers said at two in the morning and repeated none of it. "Tomorrow you put forty-six names in a sack of flour and trust me with them. So tonight I want to know what I'm carrying. Read me the names."

He could have refused. There was a version of the evening where he folded the ledger shut and told her that ignorance was armor, and perhaps it was — it had been his own armor for fifteen years, and look how well it fit, look how much it had protected anyone.

Instead he opened the binding to the third section and held the lantern low, and began to read the names aloud in the crypt of Saint Adelheid, one by one, the way he had never dared read them before — not as entries, not as evidence, but as men. Rank, unit, date, manner of death. The frost on the tomb breathed and vanished and breathed again.

He was nine names in when Marta's hand, still resting on the page beside his, went utterly still — and then began, very slightly, to shake.

The name on the line was *Kellner, Tomas*. Private second class. Executed after the retreat, listed in all official correspondence as fallen honorably in action.

Sebastian looked up and saw, in the lantern light, fifteen years of a widow's careful silence coming apart — and understood that the arithmetic had just changed, and that grief had just joined the ledger.
