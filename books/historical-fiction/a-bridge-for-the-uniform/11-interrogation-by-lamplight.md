## 11. Interrogation by Lamplight

The summons came at half past four on a Thursday afternoon, delivered not by a messenger boy but by Corporal Voss himself, a broad-shouldered man who stood in the infirmary doorway as though the building had been assigned to him personally.

"Colonel Hessler requests your presence at dinner, Herr Almeida. Eight o'clock. The commandancy." He did not say *again*, but the word hung in the air between them anyway, unspoken and unmistakable.

Sebastian folded the bandage he had been rolling, smoothed its edge flat against his palm, and took a full three seconds before looking up. He had learned long ago — in a tent at Saint-Aubin where hesitation was measured in heartbeats and priced in lives — that three seconds was the longest a man could pause without appearing to pause at all.

"Tell the Colonel I'll be there," he said.

Voss nodded, turned, and left. Through the frost-furred window Sebastian watched him cross the yard with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once needed to hurry for anyone, and Sebastian understood that this too was part of the message: no urgency, no escort, no soldiers waiting at the corners. Just an invitation. Just dinner.

It was worse than soldiers. Soldiers meant fear, and fear was legible. Dinner meant patience, and patience meant Hessler already believed he knew how the evening would end.

* * *

He dressed carefully, which was itself a kind of argument. His good coat, the grey one Marta had brushed and mended at the canteen without being asked; his collar clean; his boots polished with candle wax because he owned nothing better. A man summoned to interrogation should look rumpled, apologetic, small. Sebastian intended to look like what he claimed to be: an orderly, aging, tired, unremarkable, invited to dine by a colonel with peculiar habits and no one brave enough to refuse him.

Snow had stopped falling by dusk, and the town lay under a white hush so complete that his own footsteps sounded like transgression. Lamplight leaked amber from the windows along Kaiserstrasse; somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. At the end of the street the commandancy rose against the darkening sky, its roofline heavy with snow, its windows bright and square and patient.

Corporal Voss met him at the door. Took his coat. Led him down a corridor that smelled of stove-heat and floor polish into a room Sebastian had never entered: the Colonel's private dining room, small, almost modest, dominated by a table set for two beneath a single hanging lamp. The lamp threw a circle of light across the linen and left the corners of the room in soft brown shadow. Someone had thought about that lamp. Someone had considered exactly how much of the room a guest should be able to see.

Hessler rose to greet him. He was smaller than Sebastian remembered from the checkpoint ceremonies — or perhaps distance had lent him scale — a compact man past sixty with a farmer's hands and a schoolmaster's eyes behind wire-rimmed spectacles. He wore no uniform tonight, only a dark civilian jacket, and this small theatrical mercy told Sebastian more than any speech could have: tonight they were not commandant and subject. Tonight they were two men remembering the same war.

"Herr Almeida. Thank you for coming." Hessler's handshake was dry and brief. "I hope you'll forgive the informality. Winter makes hermits of us all, and I find I've grown tired of my own conversation."

"It's kind of you to think of me, Colonel."

"Kindness has very little to do with it." Hessler smiled as he said it — a genuine smile, unhurried — and pulled out Sebastian's chair himself.

The meal was simple and excellent: pike from under the river ice, potatoes browned in goose fat, bread that tasted of someone's private flour. A single glass of white wine, then another. Hessler talked first about the bridge — the stresses of ice on the piers, the engineering opinions he did not share, the folly of the original surveyor — and then about the town, and then about nothing, comfortably, the way a man talks when he is watching rather than speaking. Sebastian ate slowly, answered when answer was wanted, and kept one corner of his attention fixed always on the corners of the room.

Twice, footsteps passed the door and did not enter. Both times Hessler raised his voice slightly as the steps went by, both times about something trivial — the price of coal, the state of the east road — and Sebastian recognized the technique for what it was. An audience of one implied listener. A suggestion that the house had ears, so that the guest would moderate himself not for what was heard but for what might be.

He moderated nothing. He spoke of the infirmary's coal shortage honestly, agreed that the east road was a disgrace, and complimented the pike.

Hessler refilled his glass. "You were at Saint-Aubin," he said.

Not a question. A fact laid on the table like a knife laid beside a plate.

"I was. Sixteen months. The casualty clearing station near the canal crossing."

"A difficult posting."

"They were all difficult postings, Colonel."

"Yes." Hessler removed his spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with a napkin, replaced them. The gesture consumed perhaps four seconds and seemed designed to consume exactly those four seconds while he watched Sebastian over the rims. "Saint-Aubin interests me lately. You'll have read about the fire, of course. The archive annex burned in the autumn of 'eighteen. Very convenient fire, as fires go — started in the records wing, of all places, and died before it reached the wards. Almost as though it knew its business."

Sebastian cut a piece of pike. "I'd left by then. But yes, we heard."

"Mm. The official report attributes it to a stove fault. The official report is thorough on the subject of the stove and remarkably thin on the subject of what the fire failed to destroy." Hessler set down his napkin, precisely, corners aligned. "When the mainland auditors finally went through the ashes last summer — and they were slow, Almeida, disgracefully slow, but that is bureaucracy for you — they found gaps. Whole series of documents missing that the fire, by every account of its behavior, could not have reached. Shelves untouched. Drawers locked. And yet pages gone."

He let that sit. Outside, wind moved along the eaves, shifting the snow.

"Field ledgers," Hessler said. "Daily registers. Disposition records for the men the clearing station processed and did not process. Someone removed them before the fire, or during it, or after — someone methodical, someone with access, someone patient. And now, fifteen years later, in my town, in my winter, I find myself responsible for producing a full inventory of garrison archives to an inspection party. And I find myself wondering, over my dinner, whether the person who emptied Saint-Aubin's shelves might have kept those papers. Might even have—" he lifted his wineglass, turned it slowly by the stem— "*added* to them."

The lamp hissed softly above them. Sebastian looked at the man across the table and felt, with strange clarity, the exact shape of the moment: the circle of light, the shadows in the corners, the pike going cold on his plate, and the old familiar sensation of standing on the bank of a river whose depth he could not see.

"Colonel," he said, "may I ask why you're telling me this?"

Hessler smiled again, and this time there was something almost warm in it, something that would have deceived a better man than Sebastian pretended to be.

"Because you're the only man in Vellmar who was there," he said. "Because you handle the town's sick and dying, and sick and dying men talk, and men who talk about the war talk about names. Because you dine alone, keep regular hours, receive no letters, and own fewer books than any educated man I've ever met — which means either you don't read, or you hide what you read." He spread his hands, an oddly open gesture. "And because I would rather settle this between two people than between two institutions. Institutions are cruel, Almeida. People needn't be."

* * *

Hessler rose, went to the sideboard, and returned with a leather folder, slim, government-stamped, tied with cord. He untied the cord without hurry and laid the folder open on the table, turning it so the contents faced his guest.

Pages. Five of them, torn cleanly along a binding edge, written in a cramped, disciplined hand that Sebastian knew better than his own reflection. Section headings. Columns. Dates from 1917. Names — some of them, the ones that survived the ink and the handling — and beside each name a disposition rendered in the ledger's own merciless shorthand: *shot, no trial. Left at railhead, exposure. Returned to unit, subsequent death unwitnessed.*

The medicine crate pages. Ilse's first crossing, seized at checkpoint one, vanished into this office — and here they were, laid out under lamplight like a hand of losing cards.

Sebastian looked at them and made himself do the hardest thing he had done in fifteen years, harder than writing the ledger, harder than keeping it hidden through six winters and two changes of garrison: he looked at them with no expression at all.

Not blankness — blankness was a performance and performances crack. He simply let his face do what an orderly's face does all day, every day, in wards and graveyards and corridors of the dying: register information without judgment. He had spent a professional lifetime teaching his features that other people's catastrophes were data. Now his own catastrophe sat on the table receiving the same professional attention.

"These came to me in a crate of quinine," Hessler said, watching him. "From the canteen supply cart. Random inspection — bad luck, or carelessness, I haven't decided which disappoints me more." He touched the topmost page with one finger, not quite reading it, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts. "You'll notice the hand. Careful. Educated. Medical shorthand in the margins — dosages, conditions. Whoever wrote this was nursing staff, or close enough. Whoever wrote this stood at bedsides."

Sebastian reached for his wineglass, drank, set it down. "What is it you imagine I can tell you, Colonel?"

"Whether these are genuine."

"Why would I know?"

"Because of the margins." Hessler drew one page free and held it up to the lamp, angled so the light fell raking across the paper. Faint marks showed there, pressed through from a page once beneath — ghost-writing, impressions of further entries since destroyed or removed. "And because whoever wrote this recorded Private Emil Ferenc, third row, second entry, as drowned during transfer — and the official record says pneumonia. Somebody knew the truth of Emil Ferenc, and the official record didn't." He lowered the page. "There are maybe four people alive in this town who could authenticate that discrepancy. Three of them are women I have no intention of frightening. You, Herr Almeida, are the fourth."

*The fourth.* So Hessler had already made his list, and Marta and Ilse were on it, and — Sebastian counted backward through the town's small population of witnesses — the third could only be Father Anselm. Hessler knew them all and was choosing, deliberately, to knock on only one door. Mercy, or the appearance of it, or the weapon shaped like it.

"The paper's genuine," Sebastian said quietly. "Wartime army stock. Watermark under the stamp. Anyone could see that."

"I didn't ask about the paper."

"No," Sebastian agreed. "You didn't."

The silence stretched. Hessler did not fill it. That was the discipline of the man, Sebastian realized — he had built his career, his checkpoints, his immaculate winter town on the willingness to let silence do work that shouting never could. He let the silence run ten seconds, twenty, until a lesser guest would have coughed, apologized, confessed to something just to break it.

Sebastian drank his wine.

Something shifted minutely at the corner of Hessler's mouth. Not quite respect. Recognition, perhaps — the look of a chess player discovering the opponent also knows the openings.

* * *

"Very well," Hessler said at last, and his voice changed — lowered, gentled, put on like a different coat. "Let me speak plainly, since plainly seems to be the only language you'll honor tonight. In a fortnight, inspectors arrive. They will go through every document in this garrison with white gloves and a mandate from people in the capital who need, after fifteen years, someone small to punish for what everyone did. If that ledger exists in this town — if pages surface, if names surface, if so much as a rumor of it reaches their inventory — do you know what happens?" He did not wait for an answer. "They don't investigate me, Almeida. They investigate the town. Every household that received so much as a parcel from the garrison stores. Every woman who served coffee at a checkpoint. Every priest who buried a soldier without papers. They will pull Vellmar apart thread by thread searching for accomplices, and they will find them, because there *are* accomplices, because no one moves paper alone. And whatever they find, whatever they conclude, the punishment will fall on the smallest shoulders available. That is how tribunals work. That is how they have always worked."

He leaned back. The lamp threw his shadow up the wall behind him, large and slightly distorted, a giant's silhouette over the modest man who cast it.

"Or," he said, "the ledger comes to me. Tonight, tomorrow, within the week. Voluntarily. And it goes into the archive fire that the garrison will suffer, regrettably, sometime before the inspection — stoves being unreliable in winter, as we've established. No pages surface. No names surface. No investigation. The town keeps its peace, its families stay whole, and the whole ugly business dies quietly the way most of history's ugly business dies." He spread his hands. "I am offering you Vellmar, Herr Almeida. All of it. Everyone in it. For the price of some paper about men who have been dead fifteen years and cannot be helped by any amount of remembering."

There it was, laid out with terrible cleanliness: the bargain wearing mercy's face, framed so that refusal became cruelty, so that every name in the ledger would weigh against every living soul in the town, so that Sebastian — if he refused — could be made to believe, and made to *tell himself*, that whatever came next was his doing.

Fifteen years ago, at Saint-Aubin, he had learned that this was how it always worked. No one had ever ordered him to forget a name. They had only ever shown him the price of remembering it, and waited politely for him to do his own arithmetic.

He thought of the dying veteran in the infirmary, murmuring names no report carried. He thought of Marta's hands wrapping bread at the checkpoint. Of Ilse reading everything twice. Of Anselm in the graveyard, straightening stones that bore no names. He thought of a dead man's notebook, filled in a tent by a young nurse who had decided, in the worst winter of his life, that somebody had to be the record.

"You've made a careful case, Colonel," Sebastian said. He set his napkin beside his plate, aligned the corners, an unconscious echo of Hessler's own precision. "But you've described a hypothetical. A ledger that might exist, an author you seem to want me to guess at, a trade you're offering to a man you haven't accused of anything. I'm an orderly. I change dressings and carry bedpans and write down what the dying say so their families can have it afterward — which is, I'm told, a service and not a crime." He rose from his chair, slowly, letting his knees announce his age. "If such a ledger exists, and if its author is foolish enough to surrender it to a man who has just explained, in detail, exactly which fire it would burn in — well. I'd have to question his judgment. But that's not a conversation either of us can have, since neither of us knows him."

He looked down at the recovered pages, lying in their neat fan on the linen, and permitted himself one indulgence, small and invisible: he read the third entry, the one he had written on a night of shellfire by the light of a signal flare, and confirmed that after fifteen years, five pages, and one seized crate, his handwriting was still steady.

"Thank you for the dinner, Colonel. The pike was excellent."

He walked to the door. Every step measured. Every step expecting, with a calm that surprised him by feeling almost like relief, the sound of Voss's boots in the corridor, the weight of hands on his shoulders, the door of the cell below.

Behind him, Hessler said nothing.

Voss appeared at the corridor's end, opened the front door, and handed Sebastian his coat. "Good night, Herr Almeida. Mind the ice on the steps."

* * *

The cold hit him like water. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, sifting down through the lamplight. Sebastian crossed the yard, turned onto Kaiserstrasse, walked past shuttered windows and sleeping houses with his heart conducting its riot entirely in private — hammering, hammering, the body's disbelief at its own survival — while his face remained what it had been all evening: the face of a tired man walking home.

No arrest. No escort. No soldiers at the corners.

He turned the implications over as he walked, and each one was colder than the snow. Hessler had let him leave because Hessler did not need him in a cell. A prisoner was a confession; a free man who dined and declined and walked home was a *thread* — and Hessler meant to follow the thread, patiently, through the town, through the canteen and the telegraph office and the chapel graveyard, letting Sebastian lead him to everyone else. Or else the invitation had done its true work already, and somewhere in the commandancy a note was being written recording that Almeida had dined with the Colonel twice, privately, and come and gone at leisure — a note that would surface later, in an inspector's file, poisoning every trust Sebastian had built, making Marta and Ilse and Anselm wonder, in the small hours, whether the quiet orderly had been bought years ago.

Mercy, Hessler had called it. It had taken Sebastian the length of the walk home to understand that the mercy had never been meant for the town at all. It was aimed at him, and it was not mercy but isolation — the oldest instrument in the arsenal, the one they had used at Saint-Aubin, the one that had worked for fifteen years.

His hand, in his coat pocket, closed around the small brass key to the infirmary's records cabinet, and found it warmer than the night deserved.

Then, as he climbed his stairs and lit his lamp, Sebastian understood something else — something that settled over him not as dread but as a strange, hard-edged calm, the calm of a man who has finally seen the whole board.

Hessler had shown him the pages. Had laid them on the table and turned them to face him. And in doing so, the Colonel had made a mistake no tribunal would ever make: he had revealed that he knew the ledger existed, knew its value, and feared it enough to bargain — feared it, which meant somewhere in the arithmetic of his careful mind, the ledger was not paper to him. It was power. It was leverage against the inspectors he expected, or insurance, or ransom.

Which meant Hessler did not intend to burn it after all.

And which meant, Sebastian thought, pulling off his coat in the cold room, that the man across the table had just become the ledger's second keeper — a rival curator, waiting out the winter, holding five pages hostage for a book he now believed would come to him voluntarily.

The lamp steadied. Outside, the river groaned under its ice, and far off toward the mainland the thaw was moving through the darkness faster than anyone had planned.

Three days later, the crypt beneath the chapel was sealed with military carpentry and a posted order: *STRUCTURAL REPAIR — KEEP OUT.*
