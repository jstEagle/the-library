## 12. Anselm's Bargain with God

The graveyard was the one place in Vellmar where Anselm still felt ordained.

The Church had taken his collar, his faculties, his right to say the words over bread and wine. The garrison had taken his pulpit and given him a shovel. But the ground itself — the frozen ground with its two hundred and eleven markers, most of them bearing no name at all, only a number and a date — the ground had never been taken from him. He had buried these men. Whatever else the world decided about Father Anselm of Saint-Aubin, the earth knew his hands.

He worked mornings now, before the frost gave way to mud, chipping at the hard rind of winter soil where the new graves would go. There were always new graves. The war had ended more than a year ago, but men kept dying in Vellmar — of wounds that had waited too long, of lungs ruined by gas, of the slow surrender that came when a soldier outlived his purpose. Anselm dug for them and prayed for them and wrote them into the burial registry in his careful, square hand, and if the entries were sometimes longer than they needed to be — if he noted not only the number assigned by the garrison clerk but the name murmured by a dying man, the regiment, the village he'd come from, the mother's name he'd called for at the end — well. No one inspected a graveyard registry. The dead were beneath suspicion. That was the one privilege left to them, and Anselm had learned to make use of it.

He was on his knees beside the open grave of Private Number 214 — a boy of twenty who had lasted four days past the armistice before his lung gave out — when he heard footsteps on the gravel path behind him. He knew the tread. He had been listening for it, if he was honest, every morning for a week.

"Father." Sebastian Almeida stood at the edge of the path, hands in the pockets of his grey coat, breath steaming. "Am I disturbing the service?"

"The service is ongoing," Anselm said, sitting back on his heels. "It has been ongoing since 1914. Sit down anywhere. All the pews are full."

Sebastian did not smile. He rarely did, though Anselm had caught the ghost of one once, in the crypt, over Marta Kellner's dry remark about the quality of checkpoint coffee. He came closer instead and looked down into the grave, at the shrouded shape waiting on the boards beside it.

"Two hundred and twelve," Sebastian said quietly.

"You've been counting."

"I count everything now. It's an occupational hazard of my condition." He crouched, forearms on his knees. "Anselm. We need to talk about the crossing."

Anselm set down his spade. He had known this conversation was coming since the night in the crypt when they had counted the ledger's sections against the checkpoints like a miser counting coins against debts. Four sections. Three checkpoints between the chapel and the mainland post. Ilse had already tried the first — and lost it, pages seized from a medicine crate that now sat, he presumed, in Colonel Hessler's office, being read by lamplight. Which meant Hessler knew what he was hunting. Which meant every crate, every cart, every coat pocket would be opened twice as carefully from now on.

Which meant someone had to carry the largest section through the tightest checkpoint in a way no inspector would dare to search.

"I've been thinking about it," Anselm said. "Since Tuesday."

"And?"

"And I believe I have found a loophole in the Kingdom of Heaven." He gestured at the registry, bound in cracked black leather, lying open on the grave's edge. "The burial registry goes through Checkpoint Two every month. It has gone through every month since the garrison was established — to the mainland records office, for verification against casualty lists. It is stamped, countersigned, and carried under escort. And no soldier in the history of armies has ever searched a book of the dead. They look at the cover, they see the cross, they wave it through. The dead have diplomatic immunity, Sebastian. Nobody declares war on a corpse."

Sebastian was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly off the bare branches of the chapel birch. Somewhere beyond the wall, a garrison bugle sounded the hour, thin and cold.

"It's the largest section," Sebastian said finally. "Forty-one names. Forty-one accounts. If you're taken—"

"If I'm taken, they find a priest carrying a burial registry, which is exactly what I appear to be." Anselm rose, knees cracking, and brushed the dirt from his trousers. "They will not find the ledger, because the ledger will not be there. Not visibly. The pages go into the binding — sewn into the spine, beneath the endpapers, behind the register proper. I've been studying how the thing is stitched. A man with steady hands could do it in an evening. I have very steady hands. I've held them steady over open graves for five years."

"And if they don't stop at the cover? If Hessler has told his inspectors to open everything now?"

"Then they'll find forty-one names written in a dead man's hand, interleaved among two hundred and twelve others, and they will have to read every one of them aloud to decide whether they matter." Anselm smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Let some corporal earn his stripe reading the evidence of his own army's sins. Every page he reads, he becomes a witness. That's the arithmetic of paper, my friend. You taught me that."

"I taught you nothing. You were keeping your own list before we ever met."

"Yes," Anselm said simply. "I was."

* * *

That evening he took the registry apart at the rectory table — the rectory having been reassigned to a garrison quartermaster, so that Anselm slept now in the vestry, on a cot, beneath the spare vestments that smelled of dust and incense and the particular melancholy of folded cloth.

He worked by candlelight because the garrison had rationed the lamp oil and because, truthfully, he preferred candles. His mother had said he was a medieval soul born three centuries late. She had also said he argued with God the way other boys argued with their brothers, which was truer and less flattering.

The dissection took him three hours. He lifted the stitching along the spine with a bone folder — a gift from a bookbinder in Lyon, another life — and eased the endpaper back, and found the hollow he had hoped for: the gap between the register block and the cover board, wide enough for folded leaves if they were folded small and sewn flat. Then he took the section Sebastian had entrusted to him — forty-one pages in that cramped, disciplined hand, each name followed by its terrible little biography — and he began the work of making contraband look like liturgy.

He folded each leaf in thirds. He pressed them flat beneath the brass crucifix on the table, all night, changing the weight at dawn. By the second evening they were thin as communion wafers. He sewed them in with linen thread, matching the original stitch holes, working from the center outward the way a bookbinder would, and when he finished, near midnight, he held the registry up to the candle and turned it, weighed it, flexed the spine. It opened the same. It smelled the same. It was, to any eye in the world, a burial registry.

Except that now it was also a testimony. Two books in one binding. The record of the dead and the record of the erased, married together in leather and thread.

He sat back and looked at it for a long time.

Then he did what he had been avoiding doing, and prayed.

Not the rote offices, not the memorized psalms that had carried him through the executions at Saint-Aubin — those had failed him anyway, or he had failed them; the theology remained unsettled. This was plainer. He sat in the vestry with his big scarred hands flat on the table on either side of the book, and he talked to God the way he used to argue with his brothers.

*I want to be clear,* he said silently. *I am not offering this sacrifice because I believe You require it. I stopped believing You require things around the time You let them shoot Tomas Beck on a Tuesday morning while I stood there holding a book. I am offering it because it is the correct thing to do, and because I am the correct person to do it, and if those two facts come from You, then fine. Consider this my signature.*

*But I will ask one thing. Not for me. For the pages. Let them cross. Let some clerk on the mainland open this book in fifty years and find the names and say them out loud, even once, even alone in a records room. That is the only resurrection I have any confidence in anymore. Work with me here.*

The candle guttered. The wind worried at the shutters. God, as usual, declined to be drawn into negotiation, which Anselm had long ago decided was itself a kind of answer — the silence of a father who trusts his son to do the obvious right thing without supervision.

"All right then," he said aloud, to the empty room, and blew out the candle.

* * *

In the days that followed, he rehearsed.

This was Marta's idea, delivered across the counter of the checkpoint canteen with her characteristic bluntness: "You walk like a guilty man, Father. You've got to walk like a bored one. Come by tomorrow and I'll play sentry."

So he came, and she played sentry, and she was merciless.

"Again. You're approaching the barrier. The corporal asks for papers. What do you do?"

"I present the registry and the transport chit."

"You're holding it wrong. You're holding it like it's made of glass. Nobody holds a boring book like that. Hold it like you hate carrying it. Like it's heavy and it's cold and you'd rather be anywhere else. Again."

He held it like he hated it. She circled him, arms crossed, in the empty canteen after hours, the stove ticking, the smell of coffee grounds and floor sawdust thick in the warm dark.

"Now he says the colonel's ordered double checks. He wants to flip through it. What do you say?"

"I say—" Anselm paused. "I say, 'Of course, Corporal,' and I open it to the current month."

"No. Wrong. You sigh first. You're annoyed. You're a tired old priest with chilblains who wants his coffee. You sigh, you say 'of course,' and then—" she stepped close, tapped his chest with one finger, "—you watch his face while he flips. Not the book. His face. Because if he finds something, it happens in his face first, and you'll need the half-second."

"Marta," Anselm said, "where did you learn this?"

She went still, just for a moment — that flicker he had seen before, the door opening a crack onto 1918 and shutting again. "I was married to a soldier," she said. "You learn to read faces. It's cheaper than reading letters." And she turned away to the stove and said, "Again. From the bridge."

They drilled until his feet ached. They drilled the sigh. They drilled the fumble with the chit, the complaint about the cold, the offer of a snuffbox he didn't actually possess. They drilled the moment — the worst moment, the one Anselm lay awake imagining — when the corporal's fingers stopped flipping and pressed flat against a page.

"For that," Marta said, "you pray. Or faint. Whichever comes more naturally."

"Fainting," Anselm admitted, "has historically been my stronger sacrament."

And yet underneath the drilling, underneath the comedy of a defrocked priest rehearsing smuggling like lines in a parish play, something graver was taking place, and both of them knew it. Because there was a version of the rehearsal that wasn't comedy at all. There was a version where the corporal did not wave him through, where the fingers stopped, where the book went onto the guardhouse table under a lantern and Anselm went with it. He rehearsed that too, privately, in the vestry at night, walking himself through it like a liturgy: the arrest, the questions, the cell. He composed answers the way he had once composed sermons — short, plain, unadorned. *I am carrying the burial registry to the mainland records office. I know nothing of any ledger. I am a gravedigger, Corporal. I dig holes and I write numbers in a book.* And then, if it went further — if they grew clever, if they cut the binding — he composed the harder answer, the true one, the one he would say looking the officer in the eye:

*Yes. Those are the names of the men your army erased. I carried them because someone had to. Do what you like with me. The names are already out of your reach, or they soon will be, and you cannot shoot a fact.*

He practiced saying it aloud, once, to the row of vestments hanging in the dark like a congregation of headless ghosts. His voice held. That surprised him. He had expected it to shake.

He thought often, in those nights, of Saint-Aubin. Of the morning they shot the deserters — three of them, boys, one of whom had wept and one of whom had laughed and one of whom had simply stared at the mud — and the colonel had turned to Anselm beforehand and said, *Give them comfort, Father, and give the firing line absolution, and give the report the word 'deserted.'* And Anselm had given the comfort and refused the rest, and it had cost him everything except the one thing he refused to spend: the list. He had written their names that same night, in the vestry at Saint-Aubin, in a book he had no right to keep, and he had understood even then — kneeling in the candle smoke, sick with fear and certainty in equal measure — that this was the only mass he would ever again be fit to celebrate. Bread and wine for the living. Names for the dead. The rest was architecture.

Now the architecture was falling down around all of them, the thaw coming early, the ice going rotten on the river, Hessler sealing crypts and burning archives, and the largest section of the only honest account of the war sat sewn into a burial registry on a cot in a vestry, waiting for a monthly escort that would leave Thursday morning.

* * *

On Wednesday night, Sebastian came to the vestry.

He stood in the doorway a long time before speaking, and Anselm, who was packing the registry into its oilcloth satchel with the transport chit and the countersigned docket, let him stand. Some silences were pastoral. You let people arrive at their own words.

"You could refuse," Sebastian said at last. "No one would think less of you. We could split the section differently. Smaller loads. More crossings. Slower."

"Slower means the thaw arrives before the last page does." Anselm tied the satchel cord in a bow — Marta had taught him that too; knots were evidence of nerves, bows were evidence of boredom. "No. This is the plan. It's a good plan. It's even, in its way, an elegant plan. The dead escorting the dead."

"That's not what I mean, and you know it." Sebastian came into the room. Up close, in the candlelight, he looked older than his years — the particular agelessness of people who have spent a decade being careful. "If you're taken, Anselm, they won't return you to us. Hessler burned an archive. He shot men without trial. He will not hesitate over a defrocked priest nobody will ask after. Your own Church won't claim you. I won't be able to visit you. Marta can't intervene without exposing herself. You would be entirely alone in there."

"My friend," Anselm said gently, "I have been entirely alone in rooms with God for fifteen years. One more cell will hardly change the seating arrangements." He closed the satchel. "Besides. You're doing the arithmetic wrong. You're counting what I might lose. Count instead what the pages weigh. Forty-one names, Sebastian. Forty-one men whose own mothers died believing lies printed in garrison reports. If the choice is my comfort against their truth, that is not a dilemma. That is a Tuesday."

Sebastian looked at him for a long moment. "Tomas Beck," he said quietly. "You still mark the day."

"Every Tuesday. It's become a very crowded calendar." Anselm smiled, then let it fade, because the moment deserved better than a smile. "Listen to me. Fifteen years ago I stood at a wall and watched men die for wanting to go home, and I did the only thing I could — I remembered. Remembering felt like enough, then. It felt like resistance. It wasn't. It was just grief wearing a respectable coat. This—" he touched the satchel, "—this is remembering with legs. This is memory that walks across a bridge and knocks on a door and refuses to leave. I have waited fifteen years for my faith to be good for something concrete. Tomorrow morning it carries a book."

"And if it ends badly?"

"Then it ends the way most things end — messily, in the cold, with bureaucrats taking notes." He picked up the satchel and slung it over his shoulder, and felt the weight of it settle against his hip, heavier than paper had any right to be. "Go home, Sebastian. Sleep. You've the face of a man who hasn't slept since the armistice, and one of us in this conspiracy should look rested."

At the door, Sebastian stopped. He did not turn around.

"Thursday morning," he said. "Checkpoint Two opens at eight. Marta will have coffee on."

"I know," Anselm said. "God willing, I'll complain about it."

And then he was alone with the candle and the vestments and the book, and he knelt — not to pray, precisely, but to practice. Tomorrow he would kneel at the barrier if they ordered him to, hands flat on the frozen planks, and he wanted the posture ready in his body so his mind would be free for the important work: watching the corporal's face.

He rose, blew out the candle, and lay down on the cot in the dark with the satchel beside him like a sleeping animal he must not wake.

He was still awake when the garrison clock struck two. He was composing, in his head, the letter he would write if he lived — a short one, to no one in particular, beginning *Dear whoever finds this: the dead were not silent, they were silenced, and here is the difference explained in forty-one hands* — when boots sounded on the gravel outside the vestry window. Measured. Deliberate. Stopping.

A shadow crossed the shuttered glass, and a fist struck the vestry door, three times, unhurried, in the manner of a man who already knows the door will be opened.

"Father Anselm." A voice he did not know, flat and official in the freezing dark. "Open in the name of the garrison commandant."

Anselm sat up. His heart was going like a drum at a firing squad, and he noticed, with a strange detachable calm, that his hands were perfectly steady.

He reached for the satchel — and then stopped, because a bow was boredom and a knot was nerves, and because whatever happened next, it would happen better if the book stayed exactly where it was, on the cot, in the dark, looking like nothing at all.

"One moment, please," he called, in the voice of a tired old man with chilblains. "I'm coming."

He put his hand on the latch.
