## 13. What Marta Saw in 1918

The soldiers came to the chapel rectory on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the whole town knew it, because Vellmar was a town that digested news the way its river digested leaves — slowly, visibly, everything eventually surfacing at the bend. Marta Kellner heard it from her daughter Lise, who heard it from the baker's boy, who had seen Father Anselm's belongings carried out into the snow and shaken, one drawer at a time, while the priest stood in the churchyard in his threadbare coat with his hands folded, watching, as if the inspection were a funeral over which he had been asked to preside.

They found nothing. Of course they found nothing. By evening the satchel was back on its hook and Anselm was sweeping his step as though the morning had never happened, but the meaning of it traveled anyway, from door to door, warming itself at every stove: *the Colonel is looking for something, and he is looking harder now.*

Marta read the news the way she read everything — twice, once for the words and once for what stood behind them — and then she put on her apron and opened the canteen, because fear was a luxury she had given up buying in 1918, when she learned what it cost to be afraid and show it.

She lit the big iron stove. She set out cups. She counted the bread, which was short, and the coffee, which was shorter, and she did not let herself think about the satchel that had hung on a priest's hook holding eleven years of her husband's death in its lining, because thinking about it made her hands do things, and her hands had a job.

But all day, as the soldiers came through Checkpoint Two stamping snow off their boots and complaining about the cold in the way of men who did not know yet how cold a winter could actually get, one thought kept rising in her like water in a well:

*You have been silent for sixteen years, Marta Kellner. And look what your silence has bought.*

*It has bought you this: a town where soldiers shake out an old man's drawers, and a ledger that says your husband was shot like a dog, and a Colonel who signed the order, and you pouring his officers coffee.*

She slammed a cup down harder than she meant to. The soldier at the counter flinched, apologized for nothing, paid, left.

Lise, wiping tables, gave her mother a look. Sixteen years old, sharp-eyed, her father's jaw. *Later*, the look said. *I know you, Mama. Something is happening behind your face, and I will find out what it is.*

Marta turned to the stove and fed it another log, and said nothing, which was, after all, the thing she was best at.

* * *

They met that night in the crypt beneath the chapel, because it had become their habit, and habit, Marta knew, was only another word for the ruts a wagon cuts in a road — the more you travel them, the harder it becomes to steer anywhere else. She came down the narrow stair with a covered basket, as though bringing supper to a shut-in, which in a way she was. The cold down there was the old kind, the patient kind, stone-cold that had forgotten sunlight so thoroughly it no longer missed it.

Sebastian Almeida had the ledger out on the sarcophagus lid, weighted at the corners with candlesticks. He looked up when she came in — that long, careful face of his, the face of a man who had spent fifteen years teaching himself to be furniture — and she saw immediately that he had heard about the search too, and that it had frightened him in the quiet way he was frightened, which was worse than shouting.

"The pages are still here," he said, as if she'd asked. "He didn't take Anselm. He wanted him to know he could have."

"That's not mercy," Marta said. "That's a cat playing with a locked door."

"No," said Ilse from the shadows near the stair, where she sat wrapped in two coats with her fingers blue despite them. "A cat plays when it isn't hungry. Hessler's hungry now. The thaw is coming early — I told you what the wire said. He's running out of time and he knows it, and men who run out of time stop playing." She paused. "I read the intercept three times."

Anselm sat apart, on a plain wooden chair he had brought down himself, looking older than he had a week ago, as though the shaking-out of his drawers had loosened something in him that would not easily be packed back. But there was a lightness in him too, Marta noticed — the terrible lightness of a man who has rehearsed his arrest and been cheated of it, and does not know whether to be grateful or disappointed.

"Marta," Sebastian said gently. "You didn't come down here with a basket."

No. She hadn't. The basket was a fiction, a prop, and she set it down on the sarcophagus beside the ledger and stood with her hand still on the cloth because if she let go of it she would have nothing to hold.

"I want to know what's in it," she said. "All of it. Not summaries. Not 'your husband is in section four, Marta, and we're very sorry.' I have spent sixteen years being managed by men who thought grief was a thing women needed to be protected from. I want to read every name in that book before any of you carry another page across that bridge."

The crypt went very quiet. Even the river under the ice seemed to hold its breath.

"Some of it is hard reading," Sebastian said carefully.

"I ran a canteen at a checkpoint for sixteen years," Marta said. "I have watched men cry into my coffee, bleed on my floor, lie to me about their mothers, and die in the road outside my window because the ferry was late. Do not tell me what is hard to read." She lifted the cloth off the basket, revealing not supper but a bottle of plum brandy and six small glasses, because if they were going to do this, they were going to do it properly, the way her mother had taught her — *strong drink before hard truth, child, and never confuse which one is doing the work.*

She poured. Four glasses. Her hands did not shake, which surprised her, and she noted the surprise and set it aside for later examination.

"I'll tell you why," she said, and drank, and began.

* * *

"Josef and I married in the spring of 1914," she said. "You know this part, maybe. Everyone in town knows the pretty part. The part where he worked the ferry and I worked the mill and we had a wedding in the chapel upstairs with real flowers, because my father paid for flowers even though everyone said it was wasteful. What nobody knows—" and here she stopped, and looked at each of them in turn, because she had decided on the walk down that she would not say this part to the floor, or to the candle, but to faces— "is that I was in Saint-Aubin in October of 1918."

Ilse's head came up. Sebastian went very still, the stillness of a man listening to a sound he has heard before in a dream.

"Not as a nurse. Not as anything official. Josef had been transferred west with the retreat and the letters stopped, and I was twenty-six years old and could not sit in Vellmar waiting for either a letter or a flag, so I went. I took work with a supply convoy — cooking, mending, whatever hands were needed for. Women did it. Nobody recorded us. That was the one mercy of the bureaucracy: it couldn't see us, so it couldn't stop us." A thin smile. "I got as far as Saint-Aubin because the roads closed behind me, and I cooked for the field hospital for eleven days. Eleven days. I peeled potatoes thirty meters from where you"—she nodded at Sebastian— "were changing bandages, and neither of us ever knew. Isn't that something. Two people can stand in the same mud and live in different countries."

"I remember the kitchen," Sebastian said quietly. "I remember a woman with flour on her arms who sang."

"Hymns," Marta said. "Badly. It kept the wounded calm better than morphine, some of them said." She swallowed. "Keep going?"

He nodded.

"On the ninth day the retreat collapsed. You know what that looked like — I don't have to paint it. Men coming back through in pieces, officers burning papers in a barrel behind the command post, that particular weather orders get when an army is embarrassed. And on the tenth day, they brought in a group of twelve men under guard. Not wounded. Not deserters in the way people mean it — they hadn't run from the line. They'd been separated from their units in the collapse, three days without food, trying to walk back to their own side. They walked *toward* the guns, do you understand? Toward. And the tribunal — a captain and two lieutenants, none of them older than my Lise is now — tried them in a barn in an afternoon."

Her voice held. She was proud of that, distantly, the way you are proud of a roof in a storm.

"They were executed the next morning. Behind the hospital, past the kitchen garden, against the drainage ditch bank. Twenty-three men total that week — the twelve, then eleven more the day after, including—" and here the voice finally bent, and she let it bend, because she had decided that too, on the walk down, that she was done with unbending. "Including a corporal named Josef Kellner, ferryman, of the town of Vellmar, who had found three lost privates on the fourth day and was walking them home when a patrol picked all four of them up."

The candles guttered in a draught from the stair. Nobody moved.

"I saw it," Marta said. "From the kitchen doorway. I was carrying a pot. I set it down on the step and I stood there and I watched my husband refuse a blindfold — he refused it, he looked down the line at those boys he'd collected like stray cats and he refused it, so they wouldn't be alone in the dark — and I watched them shoot him, and I stood in the doorway holding an empty pot, and not one officer, not one guard, not one chaplain saw me, because I was a woman in an apron, and I had already learned what I learned in that war: *if you are invisible, you can survive anything except yourself.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"And you never said," Anselm whispered. He had gone grey. "In sixteen years. Not to the tribunal afterward, not to the commission, not—"

"To whom?" Marta asked, and it was not rhetorical; she genuinely wanted him to answer, wanted one of these good, broken men to explain the arithmetic to her, because she had never been able to make it come out. "To whom, Father? To the commission that listed him as died of wounds? I had two children and a widow's pension that doubled if the death was honorable and vanished entirely if it wasn't. There was no tribunal to appeal to — the record said what it said, signed by—" she gestured at the ledger, at the whole frozen world above them— "by that signature. I wrote to the ministry twice. Both letters came back marked *no further correspondence.* And if I had pushed — if I had stood in the square and shouted what I saw — what then? They shoot witnesses too, or they disappear them more quietly, and my children grow up with a shamed name and no mother. So yes. I never said."

She refilled her glass. Her hand shook now; she let it.

"But here is what I have learned since Tuesday, gentlemen, and I will thank you not to interrupt." She looked around the crypt. "I told myself my silence was love. It was cheaper than telling myself it was fear, and easier than telling myself it was cowardice, and some days I almost believed it. But silence is not a garment you put on and take off. It grows into you. It puts down roots. Sixteen years of it, and now I pour coffee for the man who signed my husband's death and I *smile at him,* and my daughter thinks her father fell at the front with honor, and every year on the anniversary I lay flowers on a stone that lies about him, and I have begun to understand—" and here her voice did not break but hardened, which frightened her more than breaking would have— "that my silence has not been protecting my children. It has been protecting *him.* Every year I kept quiet was a brick in Colonel Hessler's wall. I built it. Me, with my apron and my potatoes and my hymns. I built his wall for him and called it motherhood."

"Marta." Sebastian leaned forward. "There is no version of 1918 where speaking saved Josef. You know that. You made the choice a mother makes, and it was—"

"Don't," she said. "Don't absolve me. I've had sixteen years of absolution and it sits in the stomach like river ice." She drew a breath. "Here is what I actually came down here to say, before I started preaching. If I testify — if I write it down, sign it, date it, swear it to whoever comes with the thaw — Hessler hangs. Not demoted. Not reassigned. Hanged, because witness testimony to summary execution without trial is the one thing the commission cannot file away, and they know it, which means he knows it. And the moment he suspects I saw Saint-Aubin — the moment one of you slips, or one page of that ledger mentions a canteen woman, or Anselm's arrest goes badly and someone talks — I am not a shopkeeper anymore. I am a loose end with two children in this town and no road out until spring."

Now she had them. She watched the understanding move across their faces one at a time — Sebastian first, who had done this arithmetic longest; then Ilse, whose eyes flicked to the stair as if already measuring escape routes she could not offer; then Anselm, who bowed his head as if in prayer, or apology, or both.

"So hear my terms," Marta Kellner said. "Not requests. Terms. First: my testimony goes across the bridge with the last section, in the same cart, on the same day, so there is no window where Hessler can take one without the other. Second: if it goes wrong — if he takes me — you do not trade anything for me. Nothing. You burn nothing, you confess nothing, you send no messages. My children lose a mother, not a name. Third—"

She stopped. Because the third term was the one she had rehearsed on the walk down, and the one that stuck in her throat now that the moment came, and she found she had to look at the candle rather than at any of their faces to say it.

"Third. When it's over — when the inspectors come and the names are read aloud in the chapel — I want Josef read last. Not first, for drama, not buried in the middle for mercy. Last. So that everyone in that room has to sit through every name that came before his and understand that my husband was one of *all of them,* that he is not special, that what was done to him was done to all of them, and that the shame belongs to exactly one man in this town and it is not the widow Kellner."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Anselm rose from his chair, crossed the crypt, and — with the gravity of a man performing the oldest rite he knew — took her hand in both of his, and said, in Latin, the words of the burial office, not for the dead but for the living: *Ego te absolvo* — and then stopped himself, and smiled, and corrected: "No. I have no right to absolve anyone, least of all you, and you wouldn't thank me. So instead: *requiem* is for the dead, but there is another office. It is called the *Te Deum.* It is what the Church sings when something long imprisoned is set free." His old eyes were wet. "We will sing that one, Marta. When it's done."

She laughed — a rough, cracked sound, the first in longer than she cared to count — and squeezed his hands, and turned to the ledger, and pulled the candle close.

"Now," she said. "Read me section four. Start at the beginning. And go slowly, because I intend to remember every word."

Sebastian opened the book.

And that was when Ilse, who had been sitting silent at the bottom of the stairs with her ear cocked toward the world above the way she always listened, suddenly held up one hand — flat, sharp, the gesture of a telegraph operator who has just heard a signal under the signal — and whispered:

"Stop. Someone's upstairs. In the chapel. More than one — heavy boots, and they're not leaving."
