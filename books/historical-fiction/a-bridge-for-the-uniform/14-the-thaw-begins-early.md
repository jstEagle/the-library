## 14. The Thaw Begins Early

The warmth came in the night like a thief who did not bother to hide his footsteps.

Sebastian woke in the infirmary's back room not to sound but to a wrongness in the air — a softness where there should have been iron. For fifteen years he had slept the way soldiers sleep in old trenches: with one ear tuned to weather. Winter in Vellmar had always been his ally, a white fortress laid around the town, sealing the roads, slowing the inspectors, buying him one more month, then another. He had come to think of the cold as a co-conspirator. Now, at four in the morning, the window glass was weeping.

He rose and pressed his palm to it. The frost that should have feathered the pane had melted into long grey streaks, and beyond it the river — frozen solid since November, silent as a held breath — was making noise.

Not cracking, exactly. Not yet. A kind of muttering, deep under the ice, the sound of water remembering how to move.

"Too early," he said aloud, to no one. The stove had gone cold hours ago; the room should have been sharp enough to sting. Instead it smelled of thaw, of wet stone and mud and the particular sweetness of things giving up their shape.

He dressed quickly and went out into the yard, where the dark had gone the colour of pewter and the snow underfoot gave way with every step, taking his bootprints like confessions. The sky hung low and bruised. A wind from the south moved through the town, and it carried no bite at all.

In January, Vellmar's river did this perhaps once in a decade. The old ferryman used to call such thaws *the river's lie* — warm enough to promise spring, cold enough afterward to freeze solid everything that had begun to trust it. But Sebastian understood immediately what the river's lie meant for them: the thaw was the deadline Ilse had pulled from the wire, and the thaw had just stopped waiting its turn.

The ice on the Vellmar stretch was still a foot thick. It would hold for days yet. But the message beneath the muttering of the water was legible enough: *I am coming back, and I do not care what you have finished.*

Behind him, in the infirmary, old Brandt coughed twice and settled. Three names had gone into the ledger because of men like Brandt; there would be no more time to gather more.

Sebastian stood in the melting yard until his shoulders ached with the strangeness of warm wind in deep winter, and then he went inside to fetch his coat and walked, fast, toward the chapel.

* * *

Anselm was already in the graveyard when Sebastian came through the lych-gate, standing among his leaning stones with his collarless neck bare to the unseasonable air, watching the ground as though it might speak. The snow lay over the graves in grey slabs, sagging, pocked where the warmth had opened small dark wells.

"You felt it too," Anselm said, without turning.

"The river's talking."

"It's been talking since midnight. I came out to listen." The priest's hands were folded behind his back, and Sebastian saw that they were trembling — not with cold. "Fifteen years I've buried men in that ground, and I have never once prayed for winter to hold. Now I find I'm bargaining again. Give me three more weeks of ice. Two. One." He laughed, short and bitter. "God and I have an old arrangement about unanswered prayers. We are renewing it."

"The crypt," Sebastian said. "How much is still down there?"

"Four sections. Yours and mine both. Marta's testimony, written out fair. Everything we hadn't sent because we were being *careful*." Anselm said the word as if it tasted of ash. "Careful men die of patience, Almeida. I've watched it happen."

They stood together in the softening churchyard, and below them, beneath the chapel floor, behind the door that had hidden monks' bones and, lately, the truth, the ledger waited in its oilcloth like something buried alive.

"We move it tonight," Sebastian said.

"With what funeral? What cart? What lie?" Anselm turned at last, and his eyes, deep-set and tired, held a terrible clarity. "Every shipment left this town is logged by Hessler's clerks. Every hand we trust has already carried something. You don't spend the same coin twice without someone noticing the weight."

"Then we find another coin."

"Or," Anselm said quietly, "we accept that the ground itself may close over it first."

Sebastian looked at him, not understanding, and Anselm nodded toward the chapel — and only then did Sebastian see the surveyor's stakes driven into the frozen verge, three of them, tied with regulation red cord, and the clipboard nailed to the chapel door flapping in the warm wind.

He crossed the churchyard so fast he nearly fell on the rotting snow.

The notice was written in Hessler's own office hand, precise and courteous, each letter formed like a man keeping his gloves on:

*By order of the garrison commandant, the chapel crypt is closed to all persons pending structural repair. The foundation has been compromised by seasonal frost action. Repairs commence immediately. Entry forbidden on pain of arrest.*

*Frost action.* In a January so warm the graves were bleeding through their shrouds.

"He knows," Sebastian said. His voice came out very flat. "He doesn't know what's down there. But he knows something is, and he's sealed the door before the thaw brings anyone who could open it legally."

"He's not sealing it against us," Anselm said, coming up beside him. "Look closer. There's no work crew scheduled, no materials ordered — I asked the sexton an hour ago. He's sealing it against *time*. When the inspectors come, they'll find a locked crypt with official seals and a paper trail saying it was closed for mundane reasons. If we break in, we break military seals. Whatever they find on us then, we'll have earned."

The wind moved between them, smelling absurdly of far-off rain.

"How long to mason it shut?" Sebastian asked.

"Two days. Perhaps three, if the mortar freezes again at night." Anselm looked up at the bruised sky. "Which, God forgive me for saying it, it shows no sign of doing."

* * *

Marta Kellner set a cup of coffee in front of him without being asked and then stood with her arms folded, which meant she already knew, which meant Ilse had already come and gone, which meant the whole town's nervous system was firing faster than Sebastian could follow.

The canteen at Checkpoint Two was half-empty at midmorning; the garrison men were being marched to the bridge to inspect the ice. Through the fogged window Sebastian could see them, small and black against the white expanse, testing the surface with augers, their breath rising in little flags of steam. The bridge itself — the great seasonal span that made Vellmar worth garrisoning — stood locked in its winter bones, planks pale with frost, the far shore a smudge.

"They're measuring the ice," Marta said. "Ilse copied the report before it went out. Six days, maybe eight, if the cold returns. After that, the bridge can take carts again, and the day it can take carts, the inspectors cross it." She sat down across from him. Up close her face showed the night she'd spent; she wore her exhaustion the way she wore everything, neatly, buttoned to the throat. "Hessler didn't seal your crypt because he guessed. He sealed it because the same thaw that frees the river frees *him*. Once the road opens, whatever he wants buried will be buried with witnesses. He's tidying."

"Marta—"

"I know what you're going to say. That we need a new plan. That the plan needs a funeral and a cart and a miracle." She slid something across the table: a supply manifest, carbon-copied, stamped. "The garrison requisitions lime for the cemetery paths every February. Two hundredweight, delivered by my canteen cart, because my cart is the only civilian vehicle with a standing permit past checkpoint two. Lime goes to the graveyard. Graveyard is next to the chapel. And nobody — *nobody* — searches a load of lime, because lime gets into everything and ruins their uniforms."

Sebastian stared at the manifest, and hope rose in him with a physical ache, and then he saw the date line and the ache curdled.

"This is dated for the twentieth. That's nine days out."

"I know."

"The crypt will be bricked by then."

"I *know*, Sebastian." Her voice stayed level but something moved underneath it, tectonic. "So the question isn't the cart. The cart is solved. The question is what you're willing to do in the next three nights to get four sections of paper through a sealed door that Hessler has made into a trap."

Around them the canteen murmured — a pair of corporals arguing over cards, the stove ticking, the river outside groaning like a ship changing course. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that would go on after all of them were gone, indifferent as weather.

"My husband," Marta said suddenly, and Sebastian's whole body went still, "was afraid of water. Did you know that? Twenty years a bargeman's son and afraid of deep water. He said rivers kept secrets and he never wanted to be one of them." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer, precisely, as if aligning it. "For eleven years I let him be a secret. I thought silence was the price of my children's safety. Then your ledger told me his name, and I learned that silence wasn't protecting anyone. It was just the cheaper thing." She looked up. "Don't tell me the risk is too high. I've paid more for less."

* * *

Ilse found him at dusk on the riverbank, where he had gone ostensibly to check the ice road markers and actually because he could not bear walls.

She came crunching along the shore path with her collar up and her telegraph satchel slung crosswise, and she did not speak until she was beside him, both of them looking out at the river that was no longer quite a river and no longer quite a lake — a broken mirror being slowly unmade, leads of black water opening along the current line like seams.

"I read it twice," she said. "The new traffic. You should hear it."

"Tell me."

"Hessler to mainland command, tonight: *Unseasonal thaw accelerates seasonal schedule. Request inspection party advance to earliest practicable crossing date. All local records consolidated and secured pending review. Chapel structures undergoing maintenance; access restricted.*" She recited it flat, in the dead rhythm of the wire, and somehow that made it worse — the destruction of them rendered in standard abbreviations. "He's inviting them early. He wants to be inspected *clean*. Sealed crypt, tidy records, cooperative town. And there was a second message, internal, to his own provost." She hesitated for the first time. "It says: *Detain and question the chaplain regarding unauthorized interments when convenient.*"

Sebastian closed his eyes. The wind came off the water carrying mist, and somewhere out on the ice a crack ran with a sound like a rifle shot, long and splitting, followed by that low muttering roar as the river shrugged beneath its ceiling.

"When convenient," he repeated. "Not yet. He's holding Anselm in reserve. A card to play when he needs one."

"That's how I read it."

They stood in the failing light. Downstream, the ferry landing's pilings stood in open water now, black stakes in moving shadow, and Sebastian thought: *three days ago that was solid. Nothing I planned three days ago is solid.*

"Ilse," he said. "The crypt door. Stone, iron-bound, mortared. Behind it, our last four sections. In front of it, Hessler's seals, which mean arrest the moment they're broken. And above it, within shouting distance, a garrison that will hear anything louder than a hymn."

"Yes."

"And you're standing there saying *yes* like that's a plan."

"No." She pushed her hood back; her hair was damp with river mist and her young face was lit from below by the last grey light off the ice, and she looked, for a moment, not young at all. "I'm saying yes because I've been thinking about the telegraph line. It runs on poles along the embankment — right past the chapel. And I've been thinking about the storm coming up from the south, the one that brought the warmth. Storms break wires all the time. And when the garrison line goes down, even for an hour, every soldier in Vellmar stands around waiting for it to be fixed, because a garrison that can't talk is a garrison that can't act." She pulled her collar back up. "You want a quiet hour for a locked door. I can buy you a quiet hour. I've bought quieter things."

She turned to go, then stopped.

"Sebastian. The river's going to win. You know that. Everything from here is just choosing what it wins *with*."

Then she was gone up the shore path, small boots striking hard against the refreezing crust, and Sebastian stood alone with the sound of the ice failing, and understood with perfect clarity that the season of careful plans was over. What remained was three nights, a sealed door, a stolen hour, and a cartload of lime that would arrive too late unless someone first committed a crime loud enough to wake the town.

Behind him the river muttered its single syllable, over and over, like a name being read from a list:

*soon. soon. soon.*
