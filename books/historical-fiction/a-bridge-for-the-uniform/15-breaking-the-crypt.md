## 15. Breaking the Crypt

The thaw did not come gently. It came like a thief working a lock in the dark — one warm day, then another, then the sound of the river shifting beneath its ice at three in the morning, a groaning that woke half of Vellmar and made the other half lie still and listen. By Thursday the snow on the chapel roof had gone grey and rotten. By Friday the inspectors' arrival was no longer a season but a date.

And by Saturday there was a funeral.

Old Tobias Brandt had died in his sleep at eighty-one, which made him, as Anselm observed over our last council in the canteen's back room, the only man in Vellmar to die of natural causes since the war began. "The town owes him this," he said, folding his hands on the table with the deliberation of a man setting an altar. "A proper burial. A procession. Bells." He looked at each of us in turn — Marta with her arms crossed, Ilse with her notebook, me with my hands around a cup I had let go cold. "And we owe it to ourselves to be seen attending. Grief is the one thing no checkpoint questions."

It was elegant, in the way that terrible plans are elegant. A funeral gave us four mourners moving lawfully through the streets after dark. It gave us a coffin — Tobias was being buried Saturday evening, per the old custom, before the ground could refuse the spade — and a coffin gives you a reason for men to carry something heavy into the graveyard at dusk. And it gave us Anselm himself, defrocked or not, standing at the grave in his borrowed stole, because no one in Vellmar would dream of questioning why a priest was in a graveyard performing the office he had performed a hundred times.

No one would question it. That was the whole of our safety, and I felt its thinness the way you feel ice thin underfoot — not by seeing the crack but by knowing how much weight you have already asked of it.

* * *

I dressed in my infirmary blacks, which had the advantage of looking like mourning and the disadvantage of being true. In the ledger's oilcloth wrapping against my chest I carried the final section — forty-one names from the winter of 1918, the retreat, the worst of it — while the other two bundles waited below in the crypt where they had slept for a week among the coffins of strangers. We were to take all three out through the graveyard in the confusion of the wake, Ilse and Marta ahead with the first two sections hidden in the empty wine crates Tobias's nephews had brought for the occasion, myself behind with the third, Anselm at the rear locking the crypt and walking out the gate last, as a priest should.

Marta stopped me at the canteen door as we left. She straightened my collar with both hands, an unnecessary gesture, a mother's gesture, and held my lapel a moment longer than she needed to.

"You'll walk fast," she said. It was not a request.

"I'll walk fast."

"And when he says run—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. We all knew which of us Anselm would position closest to the soldiers, and we all knew what he intended to do there. He had rehearsed it aloud, twice, in that mild liturgical voice, the way other men rehearse their own deaths: *If they come, I will be the one speaking. You will not look back. The dead have diplomatic immunity, but the living must earn theirs.*

"He'll say run," Marta said now, "and you'll run. Swear it on the ledger."

I swore it. I am not sure my voice believed me.

* * *

The funeral was small and honest and better than funerals get. Forty townspeople in the chapel, candles guttering in the draft from doors that wouldn't shut properly anymore because the frames had swollen and shrunk through six winters of war and none of the carpenters had come back. Anselm read the office beautifully. He always did. There was a steadiness in his voice that I had mistaken, in the early days, for faith, and understood now to be something rarer: a man who had decided exactly what he believed and what it would cost him, and had paid, and was at peace with the receipt.

*The Lord giveth,* he said, and old Tobias Brandt, who had mended boots for forty years and never once charged a soldier, was given back to the earth.

Then the bells rang — Marta had paid the bellringer herself, quietly, though she'd have denied the sentiment — and the procession formed up in the blue-black slush of the churchyard path, and the plan began its slow unwinding beneath the ordinary noise of grief.

I have carried many secrets across many years, but I have never carried anything the way I carried that ledger section through the graveyard that night: not hidden but *concealed within* the visible, wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, my coat buttoned like any mourner's, my hands folded like any mourner's, my face arranged into the particular blankness of a man who barely knew the deceased. Fifteen years of practiced silence, and every hour of it spent training me for exactly this — to walk slowly past the thing that terrifies me wearing the face of a man thinking about nothing at all.

The crypt entrance was a stone slab set into the hillside behind the newer graves, half-hidden by a yew that had gone scraggly with neglect. Anselm had kept the key since his disgrace; tending the graveyard was his punishment and his post, and no one had ever thought to take either from him. While the mourners lingered at the grave — the living lingering over the freshly dead the way children linger at windows — the four of us drifted, one by one, away from the crowd and toward the yew.

Ilse went first, wine crate on her hip, a girl helping tidy up after the service. Then Marta, carrying bread and the remains of the wake's coffee as if to store them, her jaw set in a line I recognized from her own stories of 1918. Then me. Anselm stood at the grave in plain sight of everyone, blessing the shovel, buying us our minutes with the sheer legality of a priest doing priest's work.

The slab came up easier than I expected. Below, the crypt breathed its cellar cold at us — stone and candle-tallow and the sweet dry smell of very old wood. Two bundles lay where we had left them, wedged behind the coffin of some garrison officer's grandmother, dusted with the fine plaster grit that seeps down through old vaults. Ilse took one onto the crate, covering it with sacking. Marta took the second inside her shawl, pressed against her body the way women in Vellmar carried firewood in February, close as a child.

We were climbing out when the bells stopped.

They had rung perhaps half their measure. In a town like Vellmar the funeral bells are counted, traditional, metronomic — and their sudden silence dropped over the graveyard like a lid. I heard, beneath it, voices at the front of the churchyard. Not mourners' voices. The flat, carrying cadence of soldiers given orders they don't fully understand, repeating them loudly enough to be excused for obeying.

Anselm appeared at the mouth of the crypt so quietly I hadn't heard him cross the graveyard. He slid the slab nearly closed behind us, leaving a finger's width of dark, and put his back against the yew trunk, and looked at each of us with an expression I can only call gentle.

"Patrol," he said softly. "Six men. Sergeant Voss's squad, by the voices. They're telling the mourners to stay at the grave." He paused. "They're asking where the priest is."

* * *

What happened in the next four minutes happened without spectacle, which is the only reason any of us survived it, and I have replayed those minutes so many times in the years since that I could draw the graveyard from memory blindfolded — every headstone, every drift of half-thawed snow, the exact angle at which the moon came over the chapel roof and laid a pale road down the central path.

There was no road out except that path. The wall at the back was shoulder-high but crowned with broken glass, mortared there by some long-dead abbot, and Marta couldn't take it with the bundle and Ilse couldn't take it with the crate, and I couldn't take it at speed without risking the section against my heart. So we went along the shadow of the chapel's north wall, single file, quiet as thieves in a story, and Anselm walked out the front alone to meet the patrol.

I heard it all. That is the thing I cannot put down, even now. I heard everything, and I kept walking.

He greeted them the way he greeted everyone, warmly, unhurried, a shepherd mildly inconvenienced. "Gentlemen. You've missed the service; Brother Anselm conducted it, such as I'm permitted. Was there a death in your barracks? I'm at your disposal regardless."

Voss's voice, harder: orders to search the grounds, sealed on Hessler's authority, structural inspection of the crypt, tonight, everyone out.

"The crypt?" Anselm laughed — actually laughed, low and rueful. "Sergeant, you're welcome to it. It's been falling down for a century. But the Brandt family are still saying their goodbyes at the grave, and there are ladies present. Give them ten minutes and the yard is yours."

"They said now."

"They also said," Anselm replied, and here his voice changed — took on the pulpit, took on weight — "that the commandant wanted the town calm and cooperative this month. Six soldiers dragging mourners away from an old man's grave does not read as calm, Sergeant. I'll walk out with you. I'll stand at the gate and I'll explain to every mourner personally, with my apologies, why the army needs to inspect a tomb at eight o'clock at night. Unless you'd rather give them ten minutes and keep your name out of it."

Silence. The particular silence of a sergeant recalculating.

And in that silence we moved. Ilse first with the crate, sliding through the gap in the wall where the yew's roots had heaved a stone loose — she'd found it weeks ago and told no one but me, and I blessed her thoroughness then the way I bless it now. Marta next, pressing the bundle through, then hauling herself up with a strength that surprised no one who knew her. I went third, the oilcloth bundle passed up between Marta's hands and mine, careful, careful, the glass above us catching moonlight like teeth.

Below us, at the gate, Anselm was still talking. Loudly now. Deliberately loudly, projecting the way he must have projected over artillery at Saint-Aubin, filling the whole graveyard with his voice so that every soldier's ear, every mourner's ear, was aimed at the gate and nowhere else.

"—and I will tell you what I told the colonel's men in 1917, Sergeant, which is that a man's duty does not end where his orders begin—"

"Father," Voss said, and there was something almost apologetic in it, "step away from the gate."

"Gladly. Lead on. I'd hoped to visit the cellars anyway; I'm told the commandant keeps excellent lamps."

Laughter from a couple of the younger soldiers. Anselm had them. God help me, he *had* them — six men and a sergeant, charmed and disarmed, walking a disgraced priest out of his own graveyard at the pace of a Sunday stroll while three smugglers poured themselves over the back wall with the whole of the evidence in their arms.

I was the last over. I dropped into the deep snow on the far side, rolled upright, and looked back once — once, no more, though I swore at the grave that I wouldn't even do that.

Anselm stood at the gate in the moonlight, hatless, hands folded, smiling at Sergeant Voss with the serene expression of a man arriving precisely on time for an appointment he had made months ago. As they turned him toward the lanterns of the guardhouse he glanced back across the graveyard — not at us, not at the wall, but at the graves themselves, his parish of unnamed stones, and his smile widened by a fraction, the way a man smiles at a joke shared with someone too far away to hear it.

Then the lanterns swallowed him, and the wall was empty, and the night was just a night again.

* * *

We did not speak until we reached the canteen's cellar, and even then only in whispers, and even then only logistics. The bundles were repacked. Ilse counted the seals twice, then a third time because counting twice was no longer enough for her tonight. Marta heated milk nobody drank.

It was Marta who finally said it, staring into the stove's red eye.

"He let them take him."

"He bought us the crossing," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, as if it belonged to someone reading the account aloud. "It's what he—"

"I know what it is." She turned. Her eyes were dry and furious, which is worse than weeping. "Sebastian. They searched the crypt anyway, after. You know they did. If they find the hiding place behind the old coffin — if they find anything that shows the ledger was *there* — they'll know the priest was guarding it. They won't hold him as a nuisance. They'll hold him as an accomplice." She stood, and her apron fell straight, and she looked suddenly every year of her age and every inch of her husband's widow. "And tomorrow morning, when they want to know whose accomplice—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to, because Ilse had already gone white at the table, one hand pressed flat over the intercept she carried everywhere now, folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"The inspection order," Ilse said quietly. "From the mainland. It requests records of irregular archive movements." She swallowed. "Sebastian. It requests *personnel associated with field hospital records.*"

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, the canteen's last customers scraped their chairs and left, and the door banged, and the town settled into the frozen dark that was no longer frozen, the thaw working under everything, under the river, under the walls, under us.

In the morning, if Hessler was clever — and he was clever, he had dined with me across a table and hunted a book he'd never seen and nearly won — he would not need Anselm to confess anything. He would need only to announce that a priest was in custody, name unspecified, charges pending, and wait. Because he knew, as surely as he had known at our dinner that something existed to be found, that somewhere in Vellmar there were people who would not let Father Anselm hang for a book of names.

He would be waiting for one of us to come forward.

And the last section still had to cross the bridge.
