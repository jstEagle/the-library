## 16. The Cell Below

They let me carry the oil and the bread myself.

That was the first strangeness — that no one searched the basket, that the guard at the garrison's back stairs only glanced at my face, nodded once, and stepped aside as though I were furniture moving through its accustomed rooms. I had been bringing last rites to condemned men since before the war taught me what condemned meant, and grief had made me invisible in a way that even Hessler's new order had not learned to see through. Fifteen years of practiced silence buys a man certain freedoms: he becomes a shape in a doorway, a shadow with a basket, nothing worth the trouble of a second look.

The stairs down to the cell went below the frost line. I felt it in my knees first, that older cold, the cold of earth that has never once been warm, and then in my lungs, air that tasted of stone and standing water and the particular sweetness of lamp-oil burning in an unventilated room. My breath came out white. The guard's boots rang on the steps above me and then faded, and I was alone with a door of riveted iron and a slot at eye height through which a blade of lamplight fell.

"Father Anselm," I said. "It's Sebastian."

A long pause. Then his voice, muffled but unmistakably his — that voice that had pronounced funeral rites over so many men whose names he was forbidden to say aloud, worn smooth now like a river stone. "I wondered how long they'd make you wait. They told me you'd come yesterday. I think they were lying to see if I'd pray for it."

"I came as soon as they'd allow it."

"You came when they decided you should come," Anselm corrected gently. "There's a difference, Sebastian. There is always a difference." A soft sound, cloth against stone; he was settling himself near the door, I imagined, arranging himself the way he arranged the altar, everything deliberate, nothing wasted. "Open the slot, would you? I'd like to see your face. It's been four days since I've seen anything but this wall, and the wall has very little to say."

I lifted the iron flap. Light from my hand-lamp fell through into the dark, and there he was: Father Anselm, defrocked, disgraced, keeper of the graveyard and the unnamed dead, sitting on a plank bed with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting politely for a train. They had taken his collar and his coat and left him a gray prison shirt gone soft with wear, and somehow he looked more himself without them, the way a stripped tree in winter shows you the true architecture of its branches.

His face, though. His face had aged four days' worth of years.

"They haven't hurt you," I said, and it was half relief and half question.

"No. They've been remarkably civil. That's the worst of it, Sebastian." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Hessler wants something, and a man who wants something keeps the merchandise unbruised. Sit down. You have perhaps twenty minutes by their reckoning, less if the sergeant above us is honest about the time, which I doubt. Tell me about Marta. Tell me about Ilse. Tell me the things I can't hear through three feet of stone."

I knelt on the frozen flags, set the basket between us, and pushed the bread and the small phial of oil through the slot. He received them with the gravity of a man accepting communion, which in a sense he was, though not the kind the Church would recognize anymore.

"Marta is holding," I said. "Ilse hasn't slept. The line repair crews are still working the north span — the storm damage gave her cover she didn't expect to need twice."

"Good. Good." He broke the bread, looked at it, and did not eat. "And the ledger?"

"The sections are where they are."

"Where they are," he repeated, tasting the phrase. "You've become very good at saying nothing, Sebastian. I remember when you couldn't lie at all. Saint-Aubin cured you of that, I suppose, the way it cured all of us of something." He set the bread down on his knee. "Then let me tell you what *I* know, because you came here under a pretext and I intend to ruin it. You came to give me last rites. Very well. Here are mine, given backward: I am going to tell you what happened in the commandant's office two nights ago, and then you are going to walk up those stairs and make a choice, and God help us both, I know which way you'll want to choose, and I have spent four days praying you'll choose the other."

The cold came up through my knees and into my chest. "Anselm—"

"Let me finish. I've rehearsed it like a liturgy. Indulge me." He folded his hands again. "He searched me, of course. Thoroughly, in front of witnesses, which was theater — he wanted the guards to see him find nothing. Because there *was* nothing to find. The registry section crossed the bridge with the burial cart before I ever set foot outside the chapel gates. You know that. I know that. But here is what you don't know." He leaned forward, into the lamplight, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "He doesn't care whether I carried it or not. He told me so. He sat across from me with his brandy he didn't offer me any of, and he said — I can quote him, I've had nothing to do for four days but quote him to myself — he said: *'Father, I don't need to search you. I need the town to believe I could have.'*"

* * *

The lamp flame bent in some movement of air from the stairwell, and for a moment the whole cell seemed to sway.

"He means to trade me publicly," Anselm went on, relentless now, a man emptying himself at last. "Sunday, if the thaw holds off. After mass, when the square is full. He'll bring me out in irons — real ones, I hope, I've earned at least the dignity of real iron — and he'll announce that a priest has been caught carrying seditious documents against the garrison, documents that name officers of the late war in fabricated crimes, and that the priest will be tried and transported unless those documents are surrendered voluntarily within the week. Voluntarily, Sebastian. He'll use that word. He'll make it sound like mercy. And every soul in Vellmar will understand exactly who holds the ledger, because there is only one man in this town quiet enough, careful enough, *guilty enough* to have written it, and Hessler will never have to say your name at all. He'll just look at you across the square. Once. The way he looked at men before transfers, at Saint-Aubin. You remember the look."

I remembered the look. I had built fifteen years on remembering the look.

"And then," Anselm said softly, "he waits. He lets the town do his searching for him. He lets Marta's children feel the weight of it. He lets Ilse wonder if her intercepts are known. He lets you lie awake doing arithmetic — one old priest against however many names remain in your book — until either the pages appear on his desk or I go east on a prisoner transport in spring, and either way, Sebastian, either way, the record dies. Because if you give him the ledger, he burns it, and if you don't give it to him, he makes sure the town learns to hate the keeping of it, and no one ever keeps such a book again. That is the whole of his strategy. He doesn't need to find us. He needs to teach us that finding us was never necessary."

I knelt there on the freezing stone and understood that I had walked down these stairs believing I was visiting a prisoner, when in truth I had been summoned — by circumstance, by Hessler's design, by whatever machinery turns beneath human cruelty — to be shown the price list. One life, itemized against the rest. The chaplain who buried the unnamed dead against the names themselves. Anselm against the ledger. A man I loved against the only thing I had ever done that mattered.

"Is it true?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded far away, someone else's voice, a boy's voice from a field hospital seven winters deep. "That you'd rather I let you go east?"

"Sebastian—"

"Answer me. You started this liturgy. Finish it."

He was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above us a door slammed; snow slid off the eaves with a soft exhausted sigh. When he spoke again his voice had lost its homiletic polish and become simply an old tired man's voice, speaking from the floor of a hole in the ground.

"No," he said. "It isn't true. I'm afraid, Sebastian. I sit in the dark and I'm afraid like a child, and I pray and the prayers don't take the fear away, they just keep it company. If you could buy me out of here with those pages tomorrow morning I would bless you with both hands and never lose a night's sleep over it. I am not a saint. God knows I am not a saint; that's why He took the collar away." He looked at me straight on, and it cost him; I watched it cost him. "But I am telling you the truth anyway, because the truth is the only thing I have left to give, and the truth is this: those names are not yours and they are not mine. We were only their clerks. And a clerk does not get to ransom the archive to save himself. Not even to save the other clerk."

* * *

Twenty minutes, I had been given. I had perhaps five left when I finally spoke the thing we had both been circling.

"There may be a third way."

"Name it."

"Hessler wants the ledger to surface publicly so he can seize it and discredit it in the same motion. He wants the surrender to happen *here*, in Vellmar, under his authority, before the inspectors arrive — because once inspectors are on this ice, the ledger stops being sedition and becomes evidence." I was thinking aloud now, fast, the words coming the way they used to come during triage, when the wounded kept arriving and thought became a physical act. "So we deny him the location. We deny him the timing. If the remaining sections cross before Sunday — if they're already stamped and witnessed at the mainland post when he stands me in front of the town — then his trade collapses. He'd be offering to release a hostage for goods already delivered. He'd be a man selling a bridge after everyone has crossed it."

Anselm stared at me through the slot. "Cross before Sunday. In forty-eight hours. With checkpoints sealed, with his patrols doubled since my arrest, with the river rotting under everyone's feet." His voice dropped. "And with him watching you, Sebastian. Don't you see, that's the trap inside the trap. He didn't arrest me to stop the ledger. He arrested me to start your clock. Every hour you spend trying to save me is an hour the pages sit still, and every plan you make to move the pages forces you to weigh my life in the balance, openly, consciously, and he knows — he *knows* — that a man like you will break under that weighing, not today, maybe, but by Saturday night. Mercy is the knife he's using. He sharpened it himself."

Above us, boots on the stair. The sergeant's tread, unhurried, announcing that the world outside our conversation still existed and wanted its room back.

I put my hand flat against the cold iron door. Through an inch of metal I felt nothing, of course — no warmth, no pulse, nothing human travels through iron — but I pressed anyway, the way you press a hand to a grave.

"Anselm. If it comes to choosing—"

"It won't come to choosing," he said. "That's the lie he needs you to believe. Listen to me. Marta walked ammunition past armed men for two years and never trembled. Ilse reads a wire twice because the first reading tells her what it says and the second tells her what it *means*. And you—" the ghost of his old smile, brief as a struck match— "you wrote down the names no one else would write while the men who forbade it stood close enough to touch you. Four people, Sebastian. Hessler thinks he is trading with one guilty man. He has no idea he is besieged by four."

The boots stopped at the top of the stair. The sergeant's voice came down, bored and official: time.

I stood. My knees had gone numb without my noticing; I nearly fell, caught myself on the wall, and Anselm rose too, unhurried, gathering his dignity around him like the coat they'd taken.

"Go to Marta," he said quietly. "Tell her what I told you — all of it, including that I'm afraid. She deserves the truth; she paid more than anyone for our silences. And then get the sections moving, Sebastian. Whatever it costs. Because I'll tell you what I told God on my first night in this cell, when I'd finished being afraid: better that the stone remembers the names than that I live to see another spring. Better the record than the recorder. He can have my body. He has always been welcome to it. But the ledger—" and his voice cracked, just once, on the word, and healed itself immediately— "the ledger belongs to the dead, and the dead do not negotiate."

I turned to go. At the top of the stairs, with the gray light of the yard falling down toward me and the guard waiting with his ring of keys, I stopped and asked the question I had carried down and failed to ask:

"If it goes wrong — if we fail — will you forgive us?"

From below, faintly, through iron and stone and four days of darkness, came his answer, calm as a bell:

"I forgave you at Saint-Aubin, my son. All of you. That was never the hard part."

* * *

Outside, the thaw had begun in earnest. Water ran in the gutters of the garrison yard with a sound like quiet applause, and off the river came a smell I had dreaded for weeks — mud and open water, winter letting go early. On the drill ground, soldiers were sweeping the ice clear of snow, exactly as ordered, exactly as reported, making the crossing clean and visible for men who would arrive within days to burn what remained.

I stood in the yard with my empty basket and did the arithmetic one final time, and found it had changed. It was no longer one life against many names. It was hours. Forty-eight of them, maybe fewer, each one a page that could cross or could burn, and somewhere in the middle of the counting I understood what Hessler had actually built with his courteous trap: he had made me choose, yes — but he had assumed I would choose alone.

Four people, Anselm had said. Besieged by four.

I turned toward Checkpoint Two and the canteen, walking fast, and behind me the cell door closed on its hinges with a sound like a book shutting — and like a book opening.
