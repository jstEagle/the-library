## 21. Names Read Aloud

The chapel had never held so many people, and it had never been so quiet.

Sebastian stood at the front with the ledger open on the lectern where Father Anselm's missal usually lay, and he was aware of the book the way a man is aware of a wound — not as an object but as a fact of the body. Four sections, recovered from the mainland post office two days ago under the seal of the inspection commission, returned to the town that had paid for them in silence. The paper had crossed the river twice, hidden in medicine crates and burial registries and a widow's supply cart, and now it lay in the light of the chapel's east windows like something that had drowned and been brought back up.

Outside, the river was still breaking up. The thaw had come early and violent, and the ice went out in groans and detonations all through the night, so that the town had slept badly and woken to a sky the color of wet slate. It seemed right to Sebastian that the water should be loud on this morning. The water had carried everything away for fifteen years — men, reports, seasons. Today it could carry the sound of names instead.

Anselm stood beside the lectern in a cassock that had been brushed and mended for the occasion, though he remained, by the commission's own ruling, a priest without a parish. Marta Kellner sat in the third pew with her hands folded, wearing the black she had worn for six years for a death she had been told was honorable. Ilse was at the back, near the door, because she said she liked to see who came in — and because, Sebastian suspected, she still did not entirely believe she belonged among the rest of them. He would have to cure her of that.

Hessler was not present. The commission had taken him across the river at dawn, composed and correct to the last, his gloves on, his cap at the proper angle, a man being escorted to his own arithmetic. Sebastian had watched the boat go from the bridge and had felt nothing he could name — not satisfaction, not even the relief he had promised himself for fifteen years. Only a strange lightness, as if a coat he had forgotten he was wearing had finally been lifted from his shoulders, leaving him cold and unaccustomed to his own weight.

* * *

They had debated, the four of them, in the crypt that was no longer sealed, whether the reading should happen at all.

"The commission has the ledger," Ilse had said, practical as ever. "It's evidence. It's timestamped. It exists now in the way that matters."

"The commission has copies," Sebastian had answered. "The town has nothing. The town is the one that lost the men."

Marta had said nothing for a long time. Then: "My children were told their father died a hero. If the names are read, they will learn otherwise in front of the whole town." A pause. "Read them. I'll bring the children myself. A boy should hear the truth from a room full of people who won't let him be ashamed of it."

And Anselm had said, in the voice he used for the difficult blessings: "A church is where a community keeps what it cannot carry alone. Let it carry this."

So here they were.

* * *

The pews filled slowly, the way people come to a thing they are not sure they have permission to attend. The garrison soldiers came in a body, uncertain, their new commanding officer — a gray, tired major sent over with the commission — seated stiffly near the front as if the chairs might be a trap. The townspeople came in twos and threes: the baker whose son had been listed as *unfit for record* in the winter of '17; the ferryman who had carried Marta's cart across the ice; old women in shawls who had spent the war years sewing bandages and being told, politely, nothing at all.

Some came, Sebastian understood, to hear their own grief confirmed. Some came to hear it contradicted. Some came only to see what a ledger looked like — whether the dead had weight on paper, whether the ink was any different from the ink of the reports that had erased them.

It was not different. That was the whole horror of it, and the whole point. The same ink, the same paper, the same careful hand. Only the intention underneath had changed.

Sebastian waited until the doors at the back were closed and the shuffling stopped. The east light had strengthened; it lay across the lectern in a long gold bar, and the dust moved through it like something living. He touched the first page and found his hand was steady. Fifteen years of hiding this book had taught him, at least, how to hold it.

"I was a nurse at the field hospital of Saint-Aubin," he began, and his voice, in the stone quiet, sounded like a stranger's. "Some of you knew that. Most of you knew me only as the orderly at the infirmary who doesn't talk much. Both are true. The second was a discipline I practiced for a long time."

He looked out at them. The major's face was carefully blank. The baker's wife had already taken out a handkerchief.

"In the reports that came out of that hospital — and out of this garrison, in the years after — there is a category of death that has no name. *Unfit for record.* I have thought about those three words for fifteen years. Unfit. As if a man could be so poorly made that even his dying could not be written down." He turned a page. "This book is the record the reports refused. It is not complete. It was never complete. It is what one man could see from one cot to the next. But every name in it is a name someone in this town or some town like it was never allowed to say."

He looked down.

"Johann Brecht. Private. Died of his wounds on the fourth of March, 1917, while the duty officer refused the surgeon the last of the morphine. Recorded in the official report as: unfit for record."

The name went out into the stone and came back changed. Somewhere in the middle pews a woman made a small sound and was held by her neighbor's arm. Sebastian waited, then went on.

"Emil Sartorius. Private. Shot at the retreat from Saint-Aubin without trial, on the word of a sergeant who had quarreled with him over a boot. Recorded as: unfit for record."

* * *

He read for an hour, and then another hour. He had rehearsed nothing; he had thought, in the nights since the commission's ruling, that he would read the names the way he had written them — quickly, in the dark, like contraband. But aloud, in daylight, in front of the living, the names would not be hurried. Each one demanded its pause. Each one opened a small door into a room he had shut for years: the smell of carbolic and frozen mud, the sound of the wind finding the gaps in the tent walls, the particular silence of a cot where the breathing had stopped and no one had yet come to turn the sheet.

He read the deserters and the men who had died of cold because their blankets went to the officers' ward. He read the boys who had been listed as wounded and had died of wounds the reports described as slight. He read the ones Anselm had buried without names, and when he came to those, Anselm rose from his place and stood beside him, and the two records lay open together on the lectern — the ledger and the burial registry, the man who had watched them die and the man who had put them in the ground — corroborating each other the way they had in the crypt, the way they always would.

Some people wept openly. Some sat rigid, staring at the floor, and Sebastian understood that for them the names were not comfort but accusation — that they had known, or half-known, or chosen not to know, and the book was turning over the stone they had built their peace on.

And some walked out.

The first was a garrison sergeant, a thick-necked man who had served under Hessler since before the war's end. He stood in the middle of the reading of the sixth name, his jaw working, and left without a word, the door closing behind him with a sound like a shot. Then a shopkeeper Sebastian knew by sight. Then two more soldiers. Then, hardest of all, a gray-haired woman in the third row — the mother, Sebastian realized with a lurch, of a lieutenant named in the ledger as the officer who had ordered the withholding of morphine. She rose with terrible dignity, gathered her coat around her, and walked the length of the aisle with her chin up, and Sebastian read the next name at the exact volume as the last, because that was the discipline, that was the whole discipline: the record does not flinch and neither does the reader.

The door opened and shut. The name went out into the stone.

Ilse, at the back, caught the door each time before it could slam, holding it until the walker was through, and letting it close soft. Sebastian saw this and understood it as a kind of mercy he could never have thought of — the smallest person in the room, landing the gentlest blow.

* * *

Near the end, he came to the entry he had both dreaded and saved.

He had shown it to Marta weeks ago, in the canteen after closing, and had watched her read her husband's name in the ledger's careful hand — *Alois Kellner, corporal, executed after the retreat on the orders of Colonel Hessler, without trial, without witness permitted, for an act of refusal the men who served with him called courage* — and had watched her go very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. She had not spoken for a long time. Then she had said: *He refused to fire on the wounded of the retreat. He told me once he would rather be shot than shoot a man who couldn't shoot back. I thought he was being poetic.* And she had laughed, a terrible small laugh, and then she had cried in a way Sebastian had never seen a woman cry, silently, entirely, as if she had practiced that too.

Now she sat in the third pew with her children on either side of her — the boy fourteen, the girl eleven, both in their best clothes, both solemn with the borrowed solemnity of children at funerals. Sebastian met her eyes. She nodded once.

"Alois Kellner," he read. "Corporal. Executed on the fourteenth of October, 1918, on the order of Colonel Hessler, without trial. He refused to fire on wounded men of the retreating column. The men of his company, who were made to witness, have testified —" and here he paused, because Marta's own testimony, given to the commission three days ago in a voice that never shook, was now part of the record, timestamped and witnessed, the silence she had kept for her children's sake finally converted into the one thing that could protect them — "have testified that he died without begging and without hatred. Recorded in the official report as: killed in action, honorably."

He looked up. "The report was wrong in every particular except the last word."

The boy in the third pew sat very straight. He did not cry. He looked at the ledger the way a boy looks at a thing he intends to remember the shape of for the rest of his life, and Sebastian thought: *there. That is what the reading was for. Not the commission, not the history books, not even the stone. That face.*

Marta took her son's hand. She did not weep. She had, Sebastian thought, already done all her weeping in private, in six years of it, and what was left was harder and better: a woman restored to her own knowledge, sitting upright in the fact of it.

* * *

When the last name was read — the last of two hundred and eleven, and Sebastian's voice had gone to gravel by the end, and Anselm had taken over for the final page without being asked, his own voice steady as a bell — the chapel did not know what to do with itself.

There was no hymn planned. There was no benediction, because Anselm was still, by law, no one's priest. People looked at one another. And then the baker's wife, the one with the handkerchief, began quite simply to sing — an old harvest hymn, tuneless and cracked and utterly certain — and the chapel caught it the way dry ground catches rain, and for three minutes the room held two hundred and eleven names inside a song that had been sung at every funeral in the valley for two hundred years.

Afterward they came to him in a slow stream. The baker, who shook his hand and could not speak. The ferryman, who said only, "My brother's name was in there. Fourth page. Thank you." The gray, tired major, who came last and stood awkwardly and said, "The commission will want to know if you intend to publish this," and Sebastian said, "The commission has its copy," and the major, after a moment, almost smiled, and said, "That's not what I asked," and went away.

Some shook his hand. Some would not look at him. A woman whose husband's name was not in the ledger — a man who had died properly, honorably, in a listed battle — said, bitterly, "And what about the ones you didn't write down?" and Sebastian said, "Yes. What about them. Bring me their names and I will write them down too," and she stared at him and left, and he thought she might come back.

The record stood. That was all. It did not heal anyone; he could see that already, in the red eyes and the set jaws and the sergeant who had stormed out and was probably, at this moment, drinking in the canteen and telling anyone who would listen that the nurse was a liar and a traitor and worse. The record did not make the town whole. It only made the town honest, and honesty, Sebastian had learned, is not a comfort. It is a foundation. You build on it or you don't.

* * *

Evening came, and the chapel emptied, and the four of them remained — the keeper of the ledger, the keeper of the graves, the keeper of the canteen, the keeper of the wires — sitting in the pews like survivors of a flood, too tired to move.

Ilse spoke first. "The commission leaves Thursday. They've asked for statements from anyone in town with knowledge of garrison operations during the occupation." She looked at Marta. "They've asked for yours specifically. Again. There's a lawyer coming over from the mainland — a prosecutor. He wants to talk about what happened at the retreat. About who else gave the orders. About who else carried them out."

The chapel went quiet in a new way.

"Hessler didn't pull a single trigger himself," Marta said at last. Her voice was flat and even, the voice of a woman who had finished weeping and arrived at the far shore. "There were sergeants. There were lieutenants. There was the officer of the guard at the retreat —" she named him, the name Sebastian had read aloud that morning, the name whose mother had walked out — "and he lives forty miles from here, and he has children, and I have spent six years knowing exactly what he did."

Anselm stirred. "Marta. You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." She stood, and straightened her coat, and for a moment she looked, in the last of the east light, like a woman walking a supply cart across a frozen bridge. "But the ledger gave my husband back his name. It seems only fair that I give the ledger the rest of the truth. All of it. Every name I've been keeping too."

She went out through the chapel doors into the cold blue evening, and the sound of the river came in after her — the ice going out at last, all that long winter breaking and moving and finally, noisily, passing.
