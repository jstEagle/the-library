## 2. A Question Too Many

The morning the census taker came to Corvane's map shop, Ada Sandoval was grinding ink.

She had been at it since before dawn, as she was every morning now that there was no one else to do it. The mortar and pestle made a low circular sound, stone on stone, like the sea deciding something. Outside, Vellamare was waking — cart wheels on cobbles, the first cries of the fishwives, a church bell counting six — but inside the shop the shutters were still drawn and the air smelled of lamp oil, gum arabic, and the particular cold sweetness of old paper. Ada liked the shop best in this hour, before it had to pretend to be anything but what it was: a room full of lines that told the truth about the world.

She did not hear the knock at first. Then she heard it again — three measured raps, official in their evenness — and her hands went still in the mortar.

No one knocked on the door of Corvane's. Customers had stopped coming months after Master Corvane died, when the trade licenses lapsed and the sign above the door began to flake. The neighbors had learned not to look at the shop too long; looking invited questions, and questions, in this city, were rarely innocent. Ada herself came and went by the alley door, kept no ledger under her own name, signed nothing. For two years she had lived the way she drew coastlines: precisely, quietly, along edges no one bothered to inspect.

She set down the pestle. Wiped her ink-stained fingers on her apron, then thought better of it and took the apron off entirely, folding it beneath the counter where the ledgers hid. In its place she draped Master Corvane's old shop coat, which hung past her knees and smelled of him — tobacco and cedar oil and something mineral, like wet slate. A costume. She had gotten good at costumes.

The knock came a third time.

"Open, please. Bureau of Census and Enumeration."

The voice was tired rather than commanding. Ada crossed the shop floor, passing the great table where the atlas lay under its dust cloth, and unlatched the door.

The man on the step was younger than she expected — thirty perhaps, with the pale, indoor complexion of someone whose work was done in other people's parlors. He wore the grey coat of the Bureau, unadorned, buttoned wrong at the second button, as though he had dressed in haste or in a hurry to be done with himself. His satchel was leather, scuffed at the corners. His eyes, when they met hers, were brown and quick and immediately, apologetically lowered.

"Good morning," he said. "Forgive the hour. I'm Wren. Elias Wren." He produced a folded card, printed with the Bureau's crest — a tower over an open book — and held it out as though it might bite him. "I'm conducting the household enumeration for this district. It shouldn't take more than a quarter hour."

Ada looked at the card without taking it. "There's been a mistake," she said. "This is a business premises. No household."

"It's listed as both." He consulted a slim ledger bound in grey cloth. "Corvane's Map Shop, Lantern Row, with a resident occupant. The previous survey recorded one occupant. Aurelio Corvane, master cartographer." He looked up. "Is he in?"

"He's dead," Ada said. "Eleven months."

Something moved behind the census taker's face — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "I'm sorry for your loss. And you are?"

Here it was. The question she had rehearsed against for two years, the way a sailor rehearses against drowning. *And you are.* She had answers prepared — half a dozen of them, worn smooth from turning over in her mind at night. She chose the oldest, simplest one.

"The apprentice. I keep the shop."

"The apprentice," Wren repeated, and wrote something. The scratch of his pencil sounded very loud in the quiet shop. "Your name?"

"Ada."

He waited. The bell of Saint Ferren's finished striking six somewhere behind them, and the silence afterward had weight.

"Just Ada?" he asked, not unkindly.

*Just Ada.* The phrase sat between them like a coin too small to buy anything. She could feel her pulse in her throat. Behind her, under the counter, the ledgers waited with their careful false entries — *A. Sand, journeyman*, a name that was almost hers, a name that was a locked door painted to look like a wall.

"Sandoval," she said, and hated how it sounded — how it came out too quickly, too cleanly, like a line inked in one stroke to hide the tremor of the hand.

"Sandoval." He wrote it. "Relation to the deceased?"

"None. Apprentice. As I said."

"As you said." He glanced past her shoulder into the shop, and she watched his gaze travel the way a stranger's gaze always traveled it: up the walls of hanging charts, across the drawers of copperplate, to the great table under its cloth. People never knew where to look in a map shop. There was too much world in it. "May I come in? There are standard questions. Occupancy, means of support, that sort of thing. It's easier standing at a counter than shouting through a doorway."

Every instinct she owned said *no*. But refusal was itself a kind of answer, and Ada had survived this long by understanding that in Vellamare, the dangerous thing was never the question — it was the hesitation before the answer. She stepped back and opened the door wider.

"Mind the ink," she said. "It stains."

***

Elias Wren noted, as he stepped into the map shop on Lantern Row, that his boots left faint prints of harbor mud on a floor clean enough to eat from, and felt, absurdly, like a trespasser in a church.

He had walked forty-one doors since dawn. Forty-one doors, forty-one names taken down in his neat hand: occupations, ages, births, deaths, the small arithmetic of the district. Most people answered him the way people answered weather — resignedly, wanting only to be done with it. A few had wept. One woman on Rope Walk had slammed the door. This was normal. This was the work. You counted the city because the city wanted counting, and if the reasons the Ministry gave for the counting were vague — *for provisioning, for representation, for the public record* — well, vagueness was the native language of all government, and a man could eat his bread and mind his own affairs and sleep at night.

Except that Elias had begun, in the last month, to notice things he wished he hadn't.

He noticed, for instance, how many households had thinned since the last enumeration. How many names he crossed out with the notation *departed* and no destination given. He noticed that certain questions on his form — new questions, added since spring, printed in a different typeface than the rest — asked not merely who lived where but who had *learned their trade where*, and *under whose name they practiced it*, and whether any members of the household possessed "documents, papers, or records of historical character." That last one had troubled him from the start. It had nothing to do with census-taking. Censuses counted heads. They did not inventory memories.

And so when the girl behind the counter of Corvane's said *the apprentice* in a voice like a door closing, and *Sandoval* in a voice like a door being painted shut, Elias noticed that too.

The shop was extraordinary. He had passed it a hundred times and registered it only as a dark storefront with a faded sign. Inside, it was a cathedral of paper. Charts rose to the ceiling on every wall — coastlines he half recognized, harbors he'd sailed out of as a boy, the whole crooked spine of the Vellamare shore rendered in inks gone amber with age. There was a smell he couldn't name: stone and oil and something older, the way libraries smell of time more than paper.

"Means of support," he said, finding the line on his form. "Since Mr. Corvane's death. The shop's license lapsed in—" he checked "—in March of last year. Who holds it now?"

"No one. The shop is closed. I'm only... finishing work. Commissions already paid for."

"Paid by whom?"

"A gentleman in the north. A collector." She said it smoothly. Too smoothly — each word set down in its place like a stone in a wall.

Elias wrote *commissioned work, private client* and then sat with the silence a moment. She was watching him with an expression he recognized from years of knocking on doors: the expression of someone calculating distances. Not to the exit — to him. Measuring how much of her he had seen, and what it would cost if he saw more.

She was young — five-and-twenty at most — with ink on her fingers and, when her sleeve shifted, ink at the edge of her jaw where she must have pushed back her hair with a stained hand. Her eyes were grey-green, the color of shallow water over rock, and they did not move nervously the way frightened people's eyes moved. They stayed on him. Steady. Which was worse, somehow. Frightened people he could reassure and pass by. This one was *managing* him.

"You trained under Corvane," he said. It wasn't on the form. Not yet.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Nine years."

"And before that? Where does an apprentice come from? Family in the trade?"

The pause lasted two seconds. To anyone else it would have read as memory gathering. To Elias, who had spent four years listening for the difference between recollection and invention, it rang like a cracked bell.

"No family in the trade," she said.

"But family."

"I was an orphan, sir. The parish placed me here when I was thirteen. You can ask Saint Ferren's if you need it written down." A flicker of something — anger? fear wearing anger's coat? — crossed her face. "I didn't know apprenticeship required a pedigree."

"It doesn't." He kept his voice level, mild. "But the new schedule asks it, and I'm obliged to put something down. Under whose authority do you practice your craft? That's the question. It's new this year." He turned the form toward her briefly, letting her see the printed line, the different typeface. *Name of master or guild under which the respondent practices. If none, state so.*

Her gaze dropped to the page and he saw her read it — really read it, the way literate people read, fast and complete — and he saw the moment she understood that the question wasn't about guilds at all. Practicing a craft under no recorded name meant existing outside the record. Outside the record meant undocumented. Undocumented meant, depending on who received this form, any number of things, few of them gentle.

"Under Corvane's," she said. "Until his death. Since then, under no one. I finish his contracts. When they're done, I'll go wherever the trade takes me."

"Where will that be?"

"I haven't mapped it yet." The ghost of something crossed her mouth — not quite a smile. "That's rather the point of maps, isn't it. Places you haven't been."

***

Ada poured the census taker a cup of chicory coffee mostly because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

She despised herself for the shaking. Nine years of training under a man who believed that a steady hand was a moral quality — *the line doesn't forgive trembling, girl, and neither does history* — and one grey-coated clerk with a pencil had undone her. She carried the cups to the counter where he stood and set them down with exaggerated care, and he murmured thanks and wrapped both hands around his as though he genuinely wanted it, and for a moment they were just two people drinking bad coffee in a beautiful room, and the thought was so strange and so painful that Ada had to look away.

When had anyone last stood in this shop and *looked*? Looked at the charts, at the work, at her? Two years of being furniture. Furniture didn't get asked its name.

"You know this coast well," Wren said. He had drifted along the wall of charts, and his finger hovered near — not touching, he seemed to understand you didn't touch — a large survey of the southern shore. Saltmarsh Bay. The seawall drawn in Master Corvane's surest hand, the drowned streets marked with fine blue hatching, as if rain had fallen on the map itself. "My brother used to fish off Pier Nine. Before."

Before. The word hung in the shop like smoke.

"Before the flood," Ada said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Vellamare divided their lives that way now, whether they admitted it or not.

"Yes." He was still looking at the map. "These streets — they're labeled. Names on every one. Anchor Lane. The Cordwainers' Row." He frowned slightly. "The official charts don't carry those anymore. I checked, when the survey route assigned me this district. Everything south of the seawall is blank water. Just depth soundings."

"Master Corvane kept the old names," Ada said. Carefully. Watching him.

"Why?"

Why. Such a small question. Such an enormous one. She thought of Master Corvane standing at this same wall late at night, lamplight on his white beard, tapping the drowned streets with one blunt finger. *Names are evidence, Ada. A street with a name existed. Erase the name and you tell ten thousand people their houses were never houses, their lives were never lives. Ink is cheaper than levees, and lasts longer.*

"Because he was a cartographer," she said. "Maps are supposed to be true."

Wren turned from the chart and looked at her fully then, and Ada felt it again — that terrible sensation of being seen, of light falling on a thing kept deliberately in shadow. She had spent two years perfecting invisibility, and she understood now, watching his tired brown eyes take her measure, that invisibility was not safety. It was only danger deferred, accruing interest.

"That's an unfashionable opinion," he said quietly.

"In this city most true things are."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were also a document requiring authorization. He set down his cup. "I should finish the schedule. A few last items."

"Of course."

"Occupants' papers." He did not look at her as he said it. He looked at his form. "Standard. Proof of residency, proof of identity. Trade license, baptismal record, anything of that nature."

"I have my apprenticeship indenture," Ada said. This was true. It was signed *Aurelio Corvane*, and it named her only as *the girl, Ada*. It proved everything except that she legally existed. "It's filed. With the parish registry."

"I could note that it's available for inspection."

"You could."

He wrote it. She watched the pencil move and understood that he was choosing — right now, in front of her, with the patience of a man ruling a line — how much truth to write down. He had asked where she learned her trade. He had asked under whose name. He knew. Whatever the exact shape of it, he knew that *Ada Sandoval, apprentice, available for inspection* was a sentence with a hollow center, and he was deciding whether to fill the hollow or leave it be.

"Why are you hesitating?" she asked. She hadn't planned to say it. The question escaped her the way ink escapes a nib.

Wren's pencil stopped.

"You've written and crossed out twice," she said. "You think I don't watch? I draw for a living. I notice marks."

For a long moment he said nothing. Somewhere outside a gull screamed over the roofs of Lantern Row, and the light through the shutter cracks lay across the counter in thin gold bars, and Ada thought: *this is the moment. Whatever happens next begins here.*

"My brother," Wren said at last, still not looking at her, "was a typesetter. On Tanner's Row. After the war he was — enumerated. That's the word they used. Enumerated. Taken in for documentation review." He capped his pencil with unnecessary precision. "There was never a review. There was never anything. My mother wrote letters for nine years. The answers came back citing forms that don't exist." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were not tired anymore; they were awake and unhappy and full of something that looked, uncomfortably, like recognition. "So when I hesitate, mistress Sandoval, it's because I am very good at my job, and my job has lately become asking questions that end people. And I find I dislike knowing which of my answers do the ending."

The shop was silent. Ada's heart was going hard against her ribs.

"What will you write?" she asked.

He considered his form. He ruled a small, straight line with his pencil — a surveyor's mark, a boundary — beside her entry, some private notation of his own, and then closed the ledger.

"I'll write what's verifiable," he said. "An apprentice, resident, employed in finishing the deceased master's lawful commissions. Papers on file with Saint Ferren's." He shrugged, and the gesture was so weary and so human that something in Ada's chest, something armored for two years, cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. "The rest isn't census business. Censuses count heads. What's inside them is nobody's schedule."

He gathered his satchel, drained the chicory, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the new schedules aren't going to the district office this season. They're going to the Bureau of Records. Directly. There's a man there — Agent Calloway — collecting materials relating to the flood year. Private correspondence. Survey documents. Anything 'of historical character.'" He glanced back at her, once, briefly. "You'd be surprised what counts as historical. Old charts. Working notes. Things masters leave unfinished."

The bell above the door gave a small dying click as it shut behind him.

Ada stood a long while among her maps, in the gold-barred light, listening to his footsteps fade down Lantern Row. Then she went to the window and parted the shutter slats and watched the grey coat diminish toward the harbor, and she understood two things at once, the way one understands both banks of a river from mid-bridge.

That she had been seen — truly seen — for the first time in two years, and had not vanished.

And that somewhere across the city, a man named Calloway was making a list of papers, and her master's life's work was on it, and she had perhaps until spring.

She let the shutter close. Above her head, on the wall, the drowned streets of Saltmarsh ran blue and patient under their old true names — Anchor Lane, Cordwainers' Row — waiting, as they had waited ten years, for someone to read them aloud.
