## 3. What the Water Took

The seawall at Grayharbor Point was built to be walked on by men who never looked down.

Ada had learned that in her first year with Master Corvane, when he brought her here at dawn and made her stand at the parapet while he measured the drop with a knotted cord. *The wall is for ships, girl, not for grief*, he'd said, tapping the stone with his brass-tipped cane. *But every wall in this city is also a lid on something.* She had been twelve, and hungry, and she had not understood him then. She understood now, ten years almost to the day, standing where he used to stand, looking down at what the stone held shut.

Below her, the sea lay flat as poured pewter over the drowned quarter of Saltmarsh.

On a clear morning—and this was one, cruelly clear—you could still see it. The tips of chimneys. The weathervane of the Pier Nine lockhouse, its cockerel rusted mid-turn. At low tide, and if the light came from the west, the topmost courses of the old market arcade showed beneath the water like a jaw of teeth. Fishermen worked these waters now; they called the drowned streets by their old names without thinking, because the charts they bought at Corvane's shop still carried them. *Anchor off Marsh Row. Drop your pots past Tanner's Cut.* The names outlived the streets. Names were patient that way.

Ada set down her satchel on the parapet and took out her sketchbook, though she did not open it yet. This was ritual. She allowed herself one hour a month for it—no more, because grief kept to a schedule or it would keep you instead. Master Corvane's rule, too. He had been a man of rules the way other men were men of drink: totally, and to his own destruction.

*One hour, Ada. Then you close the book and draw something living.*

She opened the sketchbook to the page she had been working for three years: the chart of Saltmarsh as it had been. Not as the official maps showed it—a blank rectangle of pale blue water stamped DROWNED LANDS, RECLAIMED BY THE TIDE, 1864—but as it had stood, street by street, stair by stair, before the water came. She had rebuilt it from memory and from her master's papers, and it was nearly done. Only Pier Nine remained unfinished, because she could not remember it whole, and no document she had found yet remembered it for her.

She began to ink. The wind came off the sea smelling of kelp and tar, and the nib whispered against the paper, and for a while there was nothing in the world but the line—the coastlines and quay-edges, the little hatching for warehouses, the careful italic of her hand: *Lockhouse, Pier Nine. Est. 1841.*

He had loved this quarter. That was the thing people forgot about Master Corvane, if they ever thought of him at all after the fever took him—people mostly remembered the sourness, the secrecy, the shop shuttered half the year, the old man who snapped at customers and sold surveying instruments to naval officers without once meeting their eyes. They did not know he had walked Saltmarsh every week for thirty years. They did not know he had mapped every drain in it.

Ada had asked him why, once. She was fourteen, and it was raining, and he had come back from the quarter soaked through with mud drying on his boots in ridges like dried tears, and she had been bold enough—or foolish enough—to ask what was so interesting about drains.

He had looked at her for a long moment over the rim of his spectacles, rain running off the hem of his coat onto the shop floorboards, and then he had said: *Because a city tells you what it loves by what it repairs, and what it intends to lose by what it lets rot. Come here. Look at this.*

And he had unrolled a sheet of his own drafting—not a finished map, but a working one, covered in dates and levels and small furious annotations in red ink—and shown her the numbers. The tide gauges. The rainfall records he had copied from the harbor office by befriending, bribing, and finally outliving three different harbormasters. The maintenance ledgers of the Saltmarsh levees, which he had reconstructed from fragments because the originals had been removed from the Bureau of Records in the first winter after the war.

*What do you see?*

She had seen a map covered in red. She had said so.

*You see a man shouting,* he said. *Look closer. The levee on the landward side—the east levee, the one against the hills—fifteen years of repair requests, all refused. Fifteen years. And then look at the sea levee, the western one. Repaired every season. Fresh stone. New timber. Do you understand what that means, girl?*

She had not. She had been cold and wet and wanting her supper.

*It means they were not going to save it,* he said, and his voice had gone very quiet, which with Corvane was always worse than shouting. *A city that means to keep a place fortifies it against everything. A city that means to drown a place fortifies it against only the sea. You build up the western wall so that when the eastern one fails—and it will fail, because you have let it fail—you can say, honestly, we tried. Look how strong our walls are. The water must have come from the sky. From God. From rebellion. From anything but us.*

He had rolled up the sheet then, hard, the paper cracking, and put it away, and said nothing more about it for months.

But he had said it again, later. Many times, in many ways, over the years that followed. And always with the same caution, the same terrible care—he spoke of it only in the shop with the shutters drawn, only to her, only obliquely in his written notes, which he wrote in the cramped cipher-shorthand of his old army surveying days and burned by the handful each month. *The eastern wall*, his notes said, again and again, in a hundred variations. *Cut clean. Cut from inside. No scour pattern consistent with failure. The break is too straight. Water does not cut straight lines. Men do.*

Ada had not believed him at first. That was the wound under all her other wounds, the one she never let herself touch: that she had argued with him. That she had been sixteen and sensible and frightened of him a little, and had told him that governments did not do such things, that the flood had been an act of God or of war—everyone knew the rebels had broken the levees rather than yield the quarter, everyone knew it, it was in every printed history—and that his numbers could be wrong.

He had not grown angry. He had taken off his spectacles, polished them slowly on his sleeve, and put them back on, and looked at her the way he looked at a faulty instrument.

*Everyone knows,* he said, *is the sound a lie makes when it has finished digesting. I do not ask you to believe me, Ada. Belief is cheap. I ask you to copy the numbers into your hand and carry them until they are heavy enough that someone must listen. That is all a mapmaker is. Someone who carries weight until others can bear it.*

That had been two years before the fever. He had grown stranger in those two years—more shut-in, more secretive, sending her on errands to the harbor with sealed packets she was not to open, meeting at odd hours with a widow named Quill who smelled of bilgewater and gunpowder tea. She had thought him mad, or merely old. She had resented the cipher work he began assigning her, hours of transcribing his notes into symbols, though he never let her read the plain text of them. *Copy, do not comprehend*, he said. *Comprehension can be extracted under questioning. Copying cannot, if you do not know what you copied.*

She had copied. She had not comprehended. And then in the autumn of '71 he had gone out in the first cold rains to walk the seawall one last time—it was his habit, on the anniversary, to stand where she stood now—and come back shivering, and take to his bed, and die in nine days of the lung fever, lucid to the end and saying almost nothing, except on the last night.

She had been sitting with him, holding a cup of broth he could no longer swallow. His eyes had cleared suddenly, that animal clarity the dying have, and he had gripped her wrist with a strength that astonished her, and said:

*The atlas. Finish the atlas. Not the atlas they think it is—the true one. Everything in the drawer, Ada. Everything in the drawer is heavier than gold, and I have left it to you because there is no one else, and I am sorry, I am sorry, I am—*

The grip had failed between one word and the next. She had sat there until the candle guttered, holding a dead man's wrist and a cooling cup of broth, and she had not cried. She had not cried at the burial either, in the paupers' row where the city buried masterless old men, with four mourners and a priest who kept checking his watch. She had cried once, since, alone, at the seawall, within her allotted hour, and then closed the book.

Now she closed the sketchbook and stood at the parapet and looked down at the water, and let the hour be over.

The tide was turning. She could see it in the way the current combed across the submerged rooftops, dragging long veils of sand off the old market arcade, exposing—for just a moment, in the slanted morning light—a stretch of cobbled street, black and perfect, preserved under its shroud of sea. Somewhere under there was Tanner's Cut. Somewhere was the pie shop whose smell she could still summon if she let herself. Somewhere were three hundred people who had not gotten out the night the eastern levee let go, because the order to evacuate had come four hours late, and the water had come fast.

The official histories called it regrettable. *Tragic necessity of wartime engineering failures*, the commemorative plaque on this very seawall read, in letters gone green with salt. *Let the waters remind us of the cost of division.* Every anniversary, the mayor laid a wreath on this parapet, and the newspapers printed engravings of the wreath, and everyone knew, everyone knew—

Her master's voice, in memory: *the sound a lie makes when it has finished digesting.*

Ada looked at the plaque. Then she took out her pencil and, in her sketchbook, on the margin of the Saltmarsh chart, she wrote a single line in her small precise hand, right where a cartographer would normally note the sources of her survey: *This map is a witness. It does not forgive.*

She looked at the words a long time. It was the most dangerous sentence she had ever written. It was, she understood dimly, the first truly honest sentence she had ever written, because all her life she had hidden behind the line—behind precision, behind craft, behind the idea that a drawing could be neutral if only it were careful enough. Nothing was neutral. Her master's maps of Saltmarsh were not neutral; they were accusations drawn at a scale of one inch to forty feet. Her silence was not neutral either. Ten years of silence, and the lie had digested itself into print and stone and schoolroom lessons, and next spring—she had seen the announcement posted at the customs house, and walked past it twice before she let herself read it—the Bureau of Records would publish the official *Historical Atlas of Vellamare*, with the drowned quarter rendered in blank blue and the matter closed forever.

Closed forever, unless something else reached print first. Unless the numbers grew heavy enough that someone had to listen.

Ada pressed her palm flat against the cold stone of the parapet, the way she had seen her master do, as if taking an oath from it or giving one.

"I'll finish it," she said aloud, to the water, to the chimneys, to nobody. Her voice sounded strange to her, unused to carrying anything but measurements. "I'll finish it, and I'll put Pier Nine back on the map if I have to dive for it."

The tide slid over the cobbles and covered them again, indifferent, patient. Patient was her weapon too, she thought. She picked up her satchel.

There was one thing she had never dared search for—the bottom drawer of her master's drafting desk, the locked one, the one he had told her on pain of dismissal never to open, and which she had honored for ten years the way you honor a grave. But graves held things, and she had just promised the sea to finish the atlas, and the atlas could not testify to anything but beauty unless it had proof stitched into its margins.

Proof. Her master had been a man who never destroyed anything he might someday need, and who burned only what he had already copied.

She turned from the seawall and began the long climb up from Grayharbor Point, toward the shop, toward the desk, toward the drawer, walking faster with every step, as if the promise she had just made were already pulling her forward by the collar.
