## 4. The Master's Locked Drawer

The rain came in off the harbor that evening the way it always did in late autumn—sideways, patient, as if it had all night and intended to use it. Ada Sandoval lit the shop lamps one by one, moving through the dim rooms of Corvane & Sons with the economy of someone who had long ago stopped expecting anyone else to do it. There were no sons. There never had been. The sign above the door was a fiction Master Corvane had maintained for thirty years because, he used to say, *a shop that sounds like a family sells better than a shop that sounds like a lonely old man*. Ada had laughed at that once. She did not laugh now.

She had been inking the southern coastline for six hours. Her right hand ached from wrist to knuckle, the good ache, the honest ache of work done properly, and she flexed her fingers over the blotting paper and watched the lamplight catch on the wet black of the ink where the River Vell met the sea. Somewhere beneath those lines, drowned under forty feet of green water, lay the streets of Saltmarsh.

She did not look at them. That was the discipline. You drew what was there, not what you grieved over, or your hand would wander and the line would lie, and a lying map was worse than no map at all. Master Corvane had drilled it into her in her first year: *The ink remembers what you felt when you drew it, girl. Draw angry and the coast will look like a scar.*

Tonight she drew carefully. Tonight she drew calm.

The shop settled around her as the evening deepened—the tick of the barometer, the whisper of rain against shutters, the particular creak of the floorboard by the drying rack that she had never gotten around to asking him to fix and now would never ask anyone to fix, because there was no one to ask. Grief, she had learned, lived in small unfinished things. A loose board. A coat still hanging by the door. A half-inked atlas.

She capped the ink bottle and cleaned the nibs, and it was only because she cleaned the nibs—only because her hands needed something to do while her mind refused to settle—that she noticed the drawer again.

*Again*, because she had noticed it a hundred times before and a hundred times looked away. It sat low in his worktable, below the shallow drawers of pens and burnishers, a deep drawer with a brass lock gone dull with age, and Master Corvane had kept it locked for as long as Ada had been in the shop. Nine years. She had dusted around it, oiled its hinges once at his instruction, and never—not once—tried it. A locked drawer belonged to its owner the way a closed letter belonged to its addressee. That was simply how things were done. How things were *done*—as if he had not been dead these four months, cold in the ground behind Saint Ferrel's, his grave unmarked by request, his name already beginning to slide out of the city's mouth the way names slid when nobody spoke them.

But tonight her mind would not hold still. Tonight it kept circling back to the seawall, to the gray water lying flat and innocent over Pier Nine, to the way he had gripped her wrist in this very room, two weeks before the fever took him, and said—

She had tried all day not to hear it again. It came anyway, in his voice, hoarse and urgent: *"It wasn't the sea, Ada. The sea doesn't choose which side of a levee to break. Find my notes. Whatever happens, find—" and then the coughing had taken the rest, and the rest had never come back.*

Find his notes. She had searched everywhere else. Every cabinet, every portfolio, every tube and flat file and rolled map in the shop, twice over, looking for survey notes that might not even exist, that might have been the fever talking, the last delirium of an old man who had spent ten years muttering about Saltmarsh until muttering was all that was left of him. She had told herself that story often. It was a comfortable story. It let her keep inking coastlines instead of doing whatever terrible thing he had asked of her.

Her hand rested on the drawer's brass pull without her deciding to touch it.

The lock was small, old-fashioned, a simple lever tumbler of the kind made by the ironmongers on Cooper's Lane. She knew locks a little—every cartographer did; you could not spend your life among cases and flat files and not learn how they fastened—and she knew that this kind yielded to patience more often than to force. But she stood there a long moment anyway, rain ticking against the shutters, telling herself one more time that she could walk away, go home to her room above the chandlery, sleep, come back tomorrow and draw the northern coast and pretend the drawer was just furniture.

Except pretending had a cost now. Except a census taker named Elias Wren had stood in this shop three days ago with his neat little book and asked, in a voice too mild to be accidental, *where she had learned her trade, and under whose name.* Except the Bureau of Records had been buying private papers all winter, Marta Quill said, paying good coin for anything old enough to remember the war. Except somewhere in this city, people were being paid to forget, and she was spending every night making sure one document remembered.

Ada went to the kitchen and fetched the thin blade she used for scraping vellum.

* * *

The lock took forty minutes and cost her two broken blades and a strip of skin from her left thumb. She worked kneeling on the cold flags with the lamp pulled low, feeling for the lever through the keyhole the way Master Corvane had taught her to feel for the grain beneath a paper's surface—slowly, respectfully, listening with her fingertips. When the mechanism finally gave with a soft click that sounded enormous in the empty shop, she stayed kneeling a moment longer, thumb bleeding onto her apron, heart going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

Then she opened the drawer.

Inside, on top, lay a folded oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord, and beneath it a confusion of ordinary things: spare nibs, a stub of sealing wax, a tin of pins, a folded shirt collar gone yellow. Her first reaction, absurdly, was disappointment. All those years of locked silence, and it was nibs and collars. She untied the oilcloth with hands that would not quite steady.

Papers. Perhaps forty sheets, ragged at the edges, some of them charred brown along one side as if they had been pulled from a fire—or pushed into one, and rescued. The top sheet was in her master's hand, but not his careful drafting hand; this was his working script, cramped and slanted, the writing of a man recording faster than he wished to think:

*Night of the 14th. Water level at the Saltmarsh culverts fell three inches between the second and third bells, then rose past flood mark within the hour. A falling level before a surge is not tide behavior. Tide does not retreat before it attacks. Something upstream was opened.*

Ada read it twice. Then she turned the page, and the world tilted very slightly on its axis and did not right itself again.

*Interviewed the culvert keeper's boy (Osei's lad? name uncertain—he would not give it). Says soldiers came to the landward face of the north levee after curfew, "with tools that clink." Says his father ordered him inside and would not speak of it after. Father has since been conscripted to the coastal works and does not answer letters.*

Landward face. She set the page down on her knee because her hands had begun to shake, and she looked at the words as if looking hard enough might change them. It could not change them. She had drawn that levee a hundred times in practice, walked its spine with her master when she was fifteen, learned its cutwaters and its sluice gates the way other girls learned psalms. The north levee of Saltmarsh faced the river on its eastern side and the town on its western side. The sea broke against the east. The landward face—the western face—faced nothing but cobbles and warehouses and the backs of houses full of sleeping people.

Water comes over a levee in a storm. Everyone in Vellamare knew that; everyone had repeated it for ten years, until repetition had worn the sentence smooth as a pebble: *the storm surge breached the levees, God rest the souls lost to the sea.* But water does not come *through* a levee from the dry side. If the breach began on the landward face—if men with clinking tools had stood on the western slope on the night of the 14th—then the water had not attacked Saltmarsh.

Someone had opened a door and let it in.

She turned another page. Charred along the bottom edge, the ink surviving in a scorched lacework:

*Comparison of official report (Bureau of Records, printed) with my own soundings taken the following spring. Official account: breach length 40 feet, caused by scour at high water. My soundings: breach length 90 feet, walls cut clean above the scour line for a distance of 60 feet. Stone does not scour square. Stone cuts square when men cut it. I have measured it. God forgive me, I have measured it and I cannot unmeasure it.*

There were more. Soundings tables in his tiny exact numerals. Sketch plans of the levee cross-section, the cut faces hatched in his unmistakable hand. A list of names—culvert keepers, levee masons, a harbormaster's clerk—each with a date and a note: *will not speak,* *gone to the mines,* *dead, winter of '65,* *afraid.* And near the bottom of the packet, wrapped separately in a scrap of linen, a fragment of paper only half a page long, burned nearly in half, bearing a single legible line in a different hand altogether—a clerkly hand, official, stamped with the ghost of a seal whose crest she could not quite make out through the scorch:

*...by order of the Provisional Authority, the works at Saltmarsh are to proceed on the night appointed, and no record shall...*

The rest was fire.

Ada sat back against the leg of the worktable, the papers spread across her knees and the floor around her like the pieces of a shattered instrument, and for a long time she did not move. The rain had stopped. In the silence the barometer ticked, and her own blood ticked louder.

So it was true. Not fever-talk. Not an old man's obsession. He had known, he had *measured*, he had written it down and hidden it in a locked drawer four feet from where she slept her midday naps, and he had died with it locked because he had run out of time to decide what a dying man should do with a thing worth dying for.

Terror came first, and it came whole. It moved through her like cold water through a bilge—she could feel it finding its level, filling the spaces between her ribs. These pages were not grief. These pages were treason. The victors of the civil war had flooded a district of their own city to drown a rebellion, and then they had written a history in which the sea was the murderer, and for ten years the entire apparatus of Vellamare—the Bureau, the magistrates, the printing houses, the census takers with their neat books—had existed to keep that sentence polished. And here she knelt on the floor with the unpolishing of it spread across her knees. People had been conscripted into silence. People had *died* of it, according to his own hand. What would they do to the woman holding the proof?

And underneath the terror, rising through it like heat through floorboards, something else. Vindication, though the word felt too clean. It was closer to fury that had finally found its object. For four months she had mourned him privately, quietly, the way she did everything—grief folded up small and put in a drawer. Now she understood that his death and the drowned quarter and the locked drawer and her own careful invisibility were not separate sorrows. They were one thing. One machine. And she had its gears in her hands.

*I have measured it and I cannot unmeasure it.*

"Neither can I," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the empty shop, younger than she expected, and certain in a way she did not feel.

She worked through what remained of the night, and she worked the way he had taught her: precisely, without sentiment, because precision was the only prayer she knew. She copied nothing—that would take nights she did not have and leave evidence in two places—but she catalogued. On a fresh sheet of her own paper she listed each document in order, described it, noted its condition and its char-marks, assigned it a number. Thirty-one sheets. Four sketch plans. Two tables of soundings. One witness interview, partial. One burned fragment bearing a partial official order. At the bottom of her list, after a long pause, she wrote: *Provenance unknown beyond this room. Chain of custody: Master Ambrose Corvane, deceased; Ada —* and there she stopped, pen hovering, and for the first time in nine years in that shop she could not write her own name. Not because she did not know it. Because writing it on a page connected to these pages felt like signing her own warrant.

She wrote: *Custodian unnamed.* Then she blotted it, and hated herself a little, and understood that someday soon she would have to stop doing that. Just not yet.

She rewrapped the packet in its oilcloth. Then she considered the drawer, open and empty, its lock broken beyond any pretense of accident, and decided it must never be found empty. She filled it with the yellowed collar, the pins, the sealing wax, a bundle of worthless sketches from her first year—enough to satisfy a casual search, not enough to reward one. She wiped her prints of lamp-oil and blood from the brass. The scrape-blades she wrapped in rag and dropped through the grate of the stove, where they lay glowing briefly among the ashes like small dead fish.

Last of all she carried the packet to its hiding place, and here she made herself think like a mapmaker, because hiding a thing was only mapping it in reverse: you chose terrain, you chose routes, you anticipated the searcher. Under a floorboard—too obvious; floors were the first place men pried. Behind a panel—second place. In the roof—inaccessible and therefore memorable, and memory was the enemy of secrets. In the end she took it down into the cellar, past the racks of drying maps, to the old stone cistern in the corner that had fed the shop's well before the city mains were laid, dry these twenty years and choked with rubble. Deep in the cistern, behind a stone she loosened with the heel of her hand, there was a cavity that had probably held a pipe fitting once and now held the truth about Saltmarsh, wrapped in oilcloth and then in tarred sacking besides, because water found everything eventually and she meant to outlast it.

She replaced the stone, swept the cellar, climbed the stairs, and banked the stove.

Dawn was coming up gray over the harbor roofs when she finally stood in the middle of the shop and looked at the room as a stranger might—as a Bureau agent might. The worktables. The flat files. The atlas itself, half-finished, lying open on the great table where anyone who entered would see it: forty plates of the Vellamare coast in her master's style, and threaded secretly through them, in coastlines and soundings and marginal notes disguised as navigational trivia, the geography of a crime. Anyone who knew how to read it—and now, thanks to the drawer, *she* knew how to read it—could see that the atlas was not a memorial. It was an indictment, drawn line by line over four months by an orphan with no surname worth speaking.

And three days ago a man with a census book had asked her name.

Ada stood in the graying light with her scraped thumb throbbing and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that the shape of her life had changed overnight and irreversibly. Yesterday she had been a woman keeping a dead man's shop. Today she was the sole custodian of evidence that the government had murdered a piece of its own city—and the only living person who could prove it. Worth killing for. Worth killing *her* for. The thought should have sent her running to Marta Quill's door at first light. Part of her wanted to. Part of her wanted to bury the papers deeper and ink coastlines until she died old and safe and silent, and she examined that part honestly, turning it over like a flawed plate, and found she could not honor it. He had measured it. She had read it. There was no unmeasuring now, not for either of them.

She washed her face, bound her scraped thumb, and put on her shawl. Outside, the rain had cleared and the gulls were working the harbor wind, and somewhere beyond the seawall, flat and quiet under the morning, Saltmarsh lay drowned beneath its forty feet of forgiving-looking green.

Marta Quill, then. Marta, who had once moved contraband for a cartographer and asked no questions, and who would certainly ask them now.

Ada locked the shop behind her and stepped into the street, carrying nothing visible, everything that mattered.
