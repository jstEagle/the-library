## 5. The Widow Who Smuggles

The harbor at low tide smelled of tar, rotting kelp, and the particular sourness of fish guts left too long in the sun. Ada Sandoval had been to the Vellamare docks exactly four times in her life, and three of those had been with Master Corvane, who walked the quays the way other men walked through churches—head bowed, eyes everywhere, hands clasped behind his back as though in constant prayer against being noticed. The fourth time was now, alone, with a locked drawer's contents burning against her ribs like a coal sewn into her coat.

She had wrapped the half-burned survey notes in oilcloth, and the oilcloth in a plain canvas satchel that a fishwife might carry, and still she felt them. Every person she passed, she imagined could hear them—the dry whisper of scorched paper, the rustle of proof. A customs officer glanced her way near the fish market and Ada turned immediately toward a stall, bought a herring she did not want, and held it, cold and slippery and absurd, until the man moved on.

*You are an errand girl,* she told herself. *Errand girls buy fish. Errand girls have no surnames worth speaking.*

Master Corvane had given her Marta Quill's name only once, and obliquely, in the last autumn of his life. He had been drinking the plum brandy he kept for occasions no one ever celebrated, and he had said, staring at the ceiling beams of the shop: "If anything happens to me, girl—and things happen to men who make maps of Saltmarsh—you go to the widow on Rope Walk. The one with the green door and the boat named after a flower. Tell her the tide brought you." He had refused to elaborate, and Ada, whose entire education had taught her that masters explained nothing they did not wish to explain, had not asked.

Rope Walk was a lane of leaning houses behind the chandlers' row, where the smell of hemp rope soaked everything and the laundry lines crossed overhead like rigging. The green door was the third from the corner, its paint blistered by salt air, and beneath it, drawn in chalk small enough to be dismissed as a child's scrawl, was a five-petaled flower.

Ada stood before it for a long moment, herring juice dripping from her fingers onto the cobbles.

Then she knocked.

The woman who opened the door was not what Ada had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected. Marta Quill was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with a face like driftwood—weathered, grain-visible, handsome in the way of things shaped by force rather than intention. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned up with what appeared to be a sail needle. She looked at Ada the way a harbormaster looks at an unregistered vessel: assessing tonnage, cargo, and likelihood of trouble.

"You're Corvane's girl," she said. Not a question.

"I—" Ada's rehearsed speech collapsed. She had planned to say *the tide brought me*, planned to watch the woman's face change. Instead she heard herself say, "He's dead."

"I know he's dead. Whole harbor knows. Heart failure, they said." Marta Quill's voice made it clear precisely how much she believed the phrase *heart failure*. She stepped back from the doorway—not a welcome, exactly, but a withdrawal of the barrier. "Well. Come in before the whole street sees you standing there looking like a drowned cat carrying a fish."

Inside, the house smelled of pipe smoke and dried lavender, an odd domestic combination that suggested two lives lived in the same rooms. There was a kitchen with a scrubbed table, a stove banked low, and—dominating the back wall—a shelf of ship models built with obsessive precision. Ada's eye went to them involuntarily; she had spent her life among precise things, and these were precise. Every ratline, every tiny block and deadeye.

"My husband built those," Marta said, following her gaze. "Before the war took him. Sit down. And put that fish on a plate before it ruins my floor."

Ada set the herring down with trembling awkwardness, and Marta Quill poured two cups of chicory coffee without asking whether Ada wanted any, sliding one across the table.

"Now," she said, sitting opposite, folding her forearms on the wood. "Corvane sent you?"

"He—he told me, if anything happened—"

"I know what he told you. *The tide brought you.*" Marta's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly not. "He was a dramatic old ink spider. Go on then. Say it."

"The tide brought me."

Something shifted in the driftwood face. It was subtle—the way a coastline shifts, an inch of erosion invisible unless you have surveyed it before. Marta looked at her cup, then out the small window toward the water she could not see but clearly felt, and when she spoke again her voice had lost some of its harbor-gravel edge.

"Eleven years I ran things for him. Charts out, letters in, once a crate of French paper during the embargo that I told the customs men was church candles. He never trusted easy, that man. Trusted you enough to send you here?"

"He didn't send me. He just—" Ada's throat tightened. "He died before I needed to come. He didn't know I'd need to."

Marta studied her across the table, and Ada had the disorienting sensation of being surveyed herself, measured and sounded like unfamiliar water. Most people looked through her—that had been the design of her whole adult life, her own design most of all. This woman looked *at* her, thoroughly, and Ada found she did not entirely hate it.

"Let's have it, then," Marta said. "Whatever you're carrying in that satchel that you've been holding away from your body since you walked in, like it's a live eel. You think I don't read posture? Twenty years married to a smuggler and ten more working beside him. Put it on the table."

Every instinct Ada possessed screamed against it. The notes had left the locked drawer only twice since she'd found them—once to be examined by lamplight, once to be hidden again behind the loose brick in the shop's chimney. To take them out here, in a stranger's kitchen, in daylight—

But that was the thing. Marta Quill was not a stranger. Marta Quill was the only person alive besides Ada who had been named by Master Corvane as trustworthy, and trust, in Ada's experience, was a currency so rare it might as well have been minted in moonlight.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She unwrapped the oilcloth with the same care she used for vellum and laid the survey notes on the scrubbed table between the chicory cups and the herring plate.

Marta Quill did not touch them at first. She leaned over them, reading, and Ada watched the color move in her face—not draining, exactly, but rearranging, like a tide pulling back from something it had covered for years.

"These are levee surveys," Marta said quietly. "These are the *Saltmarsh* levee surveys. The landward faces."

"Yes."

"With cut-marks measured. Chisel work. From the inside." Marta sat back slowly. Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to a small brass locket at her throat. "Ten years I've wondered. Ten years I've told myself maybe the storm did it, maybe the rebels blew them trying to break the siege, maybe—gods forgive me—maybe it didn't matter anymore because nothing would bring them back." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "My sister lived on Anchor Lane. In Saltmarsh. Her and her man and the three boys. They never even found all of them."

Ada's voice came out very small. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be exact." Marta pulled the notes toward her and read them properly now, page by page, and Ada watched a transformation she would remember for the rest of her life: grief converting itself, in real time, into function. The widow's shoulders squared. The wet fury in her eyes cooled into something harder and more useful. "Where did Corvane get these?"

"He made them himself. He was contracted for the postwar harbor survey—before the Bureau revoked his commission. He kept measuring anyway. For ten years. That's what killed him, I think. Or what got him killed." Saying it aloud, in front of another person, made it real in a way it had never been. "There are more. Enough to prove the flooding wasn't rebel sabotage. Enough to prove it was ordered from the landward side, by our own side, after the district surrendered."

"I knew it," Marta said, but softly, without triumph—as if the confirmation were a stone she had been carrying anyway and now simply knew the weight of. She gathered the pages, squared their edges with a practiced hand—she handled documents like someone who had handled many documents that could hang her—and pushed them back toward Ada. Then she stopped. Pulled them back. "No. Give them here. They don't go home with you."

"They have to go home. I'm not finished copying—"

"Listen to me, girl, because I'll say this once." Marta leaned forward, and the full force of her attention was like a squall coming off open water. "The Bureau of Records has been buying private papers all winter. All winter, do you understand? Agents going door to door in the respectable quarters first—offering gold, offering amnesty for unpaid taxes, offering whatever it takes—for letters, ledgers, account books, anything written by anyone who lived through the war years. They paid old Bessor the rigger forty crowns for his father's shipping logs. Forty crowns for scribblings about herring catches. Nobody pays that for herring."

Ada felt the floor tilt slightly, or wished she could blame it on the floor. "They're collecting testimony."

"They're *drowning* it. Same as they drowned the quarter, only this time with coin instead of seawater. Everything that remembers gets bought, boxed, and carried off to the Bureau vaults where no one sees it again except men whose job is forgetting." Marta tapped one blunt finger on the survey notes. "And you've been sitting in a shuttered map shop with the crown jewels of the whole collection, telling no one, hiding nothing from nobody badly. How long before they knock on Corvane's door? They will, you know. His name is on every survey contract from that decade. Sooner or later some clerk cross-references and sends an agent with a purse."

The chimney brick. The loose chimney brick that had seemed so clever. Ada thought of the drawer standing empty, of the plates stacked in the back room, of herself, alone, with a lock pick she'd fashioned from a broken compass needle and no plan beyond *keep working, keep invisible.*

"I can hide them," Marta said. "I hid contraband through two blockades and a war. I have a boat with a false keel that's carried brandy, gunpowder, and once a fugitive bishop, and none of it was ever found. But—" and here she raised the finger again, "—I hide them for you, not from you. You don't get to hand them over and wash your hands and go back to inking coastlines like nothing's true. These papers need doing something *with*, eventually. Do you understand the difference?"

"I—" Ada did understand. It was the difference between a map kept in a drawer and a map published. Between knowledge and testimony. She had known it since the night she'd found the notes, and she had been pretending not to know it, the way she pretended about so many things. "I understand."

"Do you?" Marta's eyes narrowed, but there was something else in them now, buried deep, something almost gentle trying to surface through thirty years of harbor weather. "You look like him, you know. Corvane. Not in the face. In the way you sit like asking for help costs money."

"It does," Ada said. "In my experience."

Marta snorted—an honest, inelegant sound—and stood, taking the oilcloth and wrapping the notes herself with quick, seamanlike folds. "Well. Unlearn it under my roof. Anybody who carries proof that the government murdered a thousand people in their beds doesn't get to be proud about accepting a cup of coffee." She disappeared into the back of the house, and Ada heard floorboards lift, heard the scrape of something heavy, heard the soft sound of concealment done well. When Marta returned, her hands were empty and her face was business again. "They're safe. Safer than your chimney brick, which any sweep could find, and don't tell me you didn't use a chimney brick, I can see soot on your cuffs."

Ada looked down. There was indeed soot on her cuffs. She laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh in longer than she could measure—and pressed her hand over her mouth as if to catch it back.

"Come Sunday," Marta said. "For supper. Not for smuggling. For supper." She said it gruffly, defensively, as though offering soup were a greater vulnerability than offering to hide treason. "You're skin and bone and you've been living alone in a dead man's shop, and whatever you're going to do with those papers, you'll do it better fed. There's someone you should meet too, when you're ready. Old archivist, used to be with the state records before they threw her out. Odile Ferrant. Drinks too much and knows everything. If your notes are what they look like, you'll need someone who can prove it official."

"A forger?"

"An *authenticator*," Marta corrected sharply. "There's a difference and she'll box your ears if you confuse them. Sunday. Green door. Don't bring a fish."

Ada rose. At the door, with her hand on the blistered latch, she paused. Something wanted saying, and she had spent twenty years not saying things, and the words came out crooked and unpracticed.

"Your sister," she said. "On Anchor Lane. When I finish the atlas—I'm finishing his atlas, the whole thing, with Saltmarsh mapped the way it was, every street, every house—I could put names along the lanes. If you told me them. So they'd be somewhere. On the record. Permanently."

Marta Quill stood in her kitchen with the lavender and the pipe smoke and the little ships frozen mid-voyage on their shelf, and for a moment the driftwood face cracked clean through, and Ada saw the young bride beneath, and the sister, and the woman who had waited at this window for a boat that never came home.

"Anchor Lane number eleven," Marta said, voice rough as a mooring line. "Serena Quill. Her husband Dov. The boys were Tam, Joss, and little Fen, who was six." She swallowed. "Spell it right. She always said nobody spelled it right."

"I spell everything right," Ada said. "It's the trade."

She walked back along Rope Walk with the afternoon sun slanting gold off the harbor water, and she noticed—strangely, alarmingly—that the coal was gone from against her ribs. The notes lay behind a floorboard in a widow's house, and for the first time in weeks Ada's chest held something other than fear. It took her a dozen steps to name it, because it was a thing so rarely issued to orphans and apprentices that she hardly recognized the currency.

Not being alone.

She had reached the corner of Rope Walk when she saw him: the census taker, Elias Wren, standing at the mouth of the fish market with a leather folio under his arm, speaking to a man in the grey coat of the Bureau of Records. And as Ada watched, the Bureau man unrolled a document on a barrel top, and pointed—not at the market, not at the harbor—but back up the hill, toward the map shop.
