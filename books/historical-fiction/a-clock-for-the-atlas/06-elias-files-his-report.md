## 6. Elias Files His Report

The Bureau of Records kept its ink in locked cabinets, and this had always struck Elias Wren as the most honest thing about the place.

Other institutions pretended their power was in men—in magistrates, ministers, the marble-faced officials whose portraits hung in the customs house with their hands tucked into their coats as though warming themselves on their own importance. But Elias knew better. Power lived in ink. In ledgers. In the small, patient act of writing a name into a column, or declining to write it at all. He had spent eleven years learning exactly how much a single line of ink could weigh, and he had learned it the way everyone learned anything in Vellamare: too late, and at someone else's expense.

Now he sat at his desk on the third floor of the Bureau, beneath a window that looked out on the tiled roofs of the Tanner's Row district, and he could not make his hand move.

The form before him was Census Schedule 12-B: Irregular Registrations, Persons of Undocumented Status or Questionable Residency. It was a grayish rectangle of paper, ruled and boxed, demanding answers in its neat printed capitals—*NAME OF SUBJECT. STREET OF RESIDENCE. APPARENT TRADE. BASIS OF SUSPICION.* It asked for nothing unusual. He filed six such forms in an ordinary week. The harbor districts of Vellamare were full of people who had slipped through the cracks of one war or another: widows with dead husbands' papers, sailors landed from ships that no longer existed, children who had been nine years old when Saltmarsh drowned and had come up out of that water without names worth keeping.

He had watched the apprentice work. That was all. That was what he kept telling himself.

Three days ago, on a Tuesday thick with harbor fog, he had climbed the crooked stairs to Corvane's map shop on Lantern Street with his ledger under his arm and his questions arranged in the order the manual prescribed: name, age, trade, household. Routine verification of the district's commercial premises. The master cartographer had been dead eleven months; the shop's registration still listed him as its responsible party; somebody had to account for the discrepancy.

And then a girl had opened the door.

She was perhaps nineteen, narrow-shouldered, with ink stains worked so deep into the creases of her fingers that they looked less like dirt than like veins, like something her body had decided to grow. She had said almost nothing. She had stood in the doorway of that dim shop with shelves of rolled charts rising behind her like a forest of pale trees, and she had answered his questions with a careful, flat courtesy that was itself an answer—the courtesy of a person who had rehearsed being unremarkable until she could perform it in her sleep.

She would not give her surname. *Apprentice,* she said. *I am the apprentice. The records will show Master Corvane took apprentices.*

The records showed nothing of the kind. Elias had checked them that same afternoon, sitting where he sat now, pulling the registry volumes down off the shelf with the mechanical efficiency of eleven years' habit. Corvane had registered one apprentice, fourteen years ago—a boy, discharged after two years for unsuitability. No girl. No Sandoval. Nothing.

An undocumented person maintaining a commercial premises under a dead man's name. It was, by the letter of the Registration Act, a matter for Schedule 12-B. By the practice of the Bureau in the winter of 1874—when the Bureau had grown teeth, when its forms had begun migrating from dusty shelves to the desks of men who wore uniforms under their coats—it was considerably more than that.

Elias dipped his pen. Held it over the paper. Watched a bead of ink gather at the nib, black and perfect and heavy, and did not let it fall.

* * *

His lodgings were two rooms above a chandlery on Fishgut Lane, which the landlords of better streets called Saltern Row when they wrote to their friends in the capital. He had lived there for nine years, alone, in a state of order so complete it amounted to a form of prayer. His boots were polished. His shirts were folded. On the shelf beside his bed stood a row of books arranged not by subject but by height, and among them, spined in cracked green leather, was a volume of coastal surveys that had belonged to his brother.

Tomas had been the loud one. That was how Elias thought of the difference between them, in the shorthand of grief: Tomas loud, Elias quiet; Tomas certain, Elias careful; Tomas convinced that the world was a machine that could be fixed if you found the right lever, Elias convinced that the world was a machine that would grind you fine if you stood too close to the gears. When the civil war ended and the Ministry of Restoration began its great project of national reconciliation—which meant, in practice, its great project of national forgetting—Tomas had been twenty-four years old and working as a junior clerk in the Hall of Deeds, and he had believed, with the whole burning certainty of his nature, that records were sacred. That a thing written down was a thing made true. That if you kept the paper safe, the paper would keep the people safe.

They had come for the archives in the spring of 1866. Not soldiers—clerks. Men with lists, working methodically through the Hall of Deeds, removing documents that the new government classified as *seditious provenance*: land grants to rebel families, shipping manifests from blockade-running, parish registers from the Saltmarsh district. Tomas had protested. Elias had never learned exactly what Tomas said, or to whom, because three weeks later his brother's lodging was emptied, his name was struck from the census rolls of Vellamare, and when Elias went to the Bureau to ask—forbidden, but he went—he was told, by a supervisor who would not meet his eyes, that there was no record of any Tomas Wren.

No record. Which meant, in the arithmetic the Bureau used, that there was no Tomas.

Elias understood now, in the way a man understands the mechanics of a trap once he is standing outside it, that his brother had been erased twice: once by the purge, and once by the paperwork of the purge. The second erasure was the crueler, because it was the one that held. A man could survive being arrested; families survived arrests every year; there were procedures, petitions, the slow machinery of appeal. What no one survived was becoming unwritten. The regime had discovered, in those first years after the war, that it did not need gallows for everyone. It needed only ink.

And Elias Wren had walked into the Bureau of Records the following autumn and asked for a position, because grief does strange things to the grieving and the Bureau was the only building in Vellamare where his brother had demonstrably existed. There were requisition logs. There were duty rosters. For eleven months of 1866, Tomas Wren signed his name in a clerk's hand on lines that no purge had reached, and Elias had gone to those pages the way other men went to graves.

He had told himself, then, that he took the job to guard the paper. To be, inside those walls, a small stubborn memory of what ink could do. Eleven years later he could not honestly say whether that had ever been true, or whether he had simply been a frightened man making terms with the thing that frightened him.

* * *

The candle had burned down two finger-widths before he returned to the form.

He had brought it home deliberately. This was against regulations—all completed schedules were to remain on Bureau premises—but the regulations were enforced in inverse proportion to their importance, and a junior verifier carrying work home marked him in his supervisor's eyes as diligent rather than criminal. Diligence was useful camouflage. Elias had spent eleven years cultivating a reputation for diligence the way other men cultivated gardens: patiently, against poor odds, for reasons he could no longer entirely remember.

He read what he had written so far.

*Subject observed at premises known as Corvane & Sons, Cartographers, 14 Lantern Street, in capacity of resident apprentice. Subject declined to provide family name. Age approx. 18–20 years. Apparent trade: drafting, inking, color preparation. Basis of suspicion:*—

And there his pen had stopped, three days ago, and there it stopped again.

*Basis of suspicion.* The phrase was doing a particular kind of work, and Elias knew its grammar intimately. It did not ask whether the subject had done wrong. It asked only what the *verifier* suspected—and suspicion, once entered into a schedule, became a fact. It propagated. A flagged file begat a watch report; a watch report begat an inquiry; an inquiry begat, these days, men in gray coats arriving at a shop on Lantern Street with a warrant and a strongbox. He had seen the chain work end to end often enough. He had forged links of it himself, small ones, links he could tell himself were harmless: a note here, a cross-reference there, always accurate, always true, always—this was the lie he had built his eleven years around—merely recording what already existed.

But the girl on Lantern Street did not already exist. That was the whole point of her. She had built herself out of absence, out of the negative space the city left for people who could not afford to be solid, and she was surviving in it the way lichen survives on stone, quietly, in the cracks. If Elias wrote her into a schedule, the Bureau would do what the Bureau did. It would render her. In gray coats and strongboxes and, eventually, in the past tense.

He thought of her hands. Ink-stained to the wrist, the right hand's middle finger callused in the specific ridge-pattern of a ruling pen held long hours. He had noticed it because it was the same callus his brother had worn, in the year Tomas had taught himself surveying before the war closed the engineering school. Small kinships everywhere, if you looked. The city was knitted together out of them, out of dead men's habits living on in strangers' hands.

She had lied to him, of course. Badly, in one sense—her claim about registered apprentices collapsed under ten minutes' research—and yet in another sense so well that it had taken him three days to admit what he was actually looking at. Because she had not merely lied; she had performed the entire choreography of a person with something to lose, and the performance had been so practiced, so worn smooth with use, that behind it Elias had glimpsed not a criminal but a history. People rehearsed invisibility for years. People perfected it in childhood. Somewhere in that careful flat voice was a whole biography of doors closing.

Under whose name are you registered? he had asked, and she had looked at him—not with fear, exactly, but with a kind of weary assessment, a weighing, as if calculating precisely how much of her the question would cost—and had said, *The shop is registered. That should be sufficient.*

It should not have been sufficient. It was not sufficient. And he had written in his field notes, in the shorthand of the trade: *subject evasive; recommend follow-up visit.*

Which was already a betrayal, he understood. However small. However procedural. He had already begun the file. Already set the first stone of the wall.

* * *

On Thursday morning his supervisor, Deputy Registrar Hallow, stopped at his desk with a cup of the Bureau's terrible coffee and the genial manner he reserved for subordinates he was measuring.

"Wren. You covered Lantern Street this week?"

"I did. Commercial verification, upper section." Elias kept his eyes on his blotter. "Mostly current."

"Corvane's old shop?" Hallow sipped his coffee. "We've had interest in that inventory. Upstairs interest." He said *upstairs* the way men in the Bureau said it—as a direction rather than a floor, a compass bearing pointing somewhere none of them admitted to working. "Materials of the late master. Correspondence, drafts, survey materials. There's a purchase program underway. Private papers of historical significance." A thin smile. "The Ministry is very interested in the historical significance of things lately."

"I noted an irregular residency at the address," Elias said. Each word placed like a stone in shallow water. "A person remaining at the premises without registration. I'm completing the schedule."

"Good man." Hallow moved on down the aisle of desks, and Elias sat with his heart going hard and slow, like a fist knocking on a door from inside a coffin.

*Upstairs interest.* He knew what that meant in the winter of 1874. The purchase program was not scholarship; it was salvage—or rather, it was drowning dressed as collecting. Every private paper that might contradict the official account of anything was being gathered into ministry storerooms where it would be catalogued, preserved, and never seen again. He had processed the requisition orders himself. He knew the categories. And one of the categories—one he had told himself, filing it, meant nothing—was *materials pertaining to hydraulic works and civil engineering undertakings of the reconstruction period.*

Hydraulic works. Levees. Water.

Saltmarsh had been underwater for ten years this coming spring, and the official account had not changed since the week it happened: rebel sabotage, rebel munitions, the district's own defenders breaking the levees to flood the approaches and cover their retreat. A tragedy of their own making. The story had the elegant self-sealing quality of all good lies—it blamed the dead, and the dead could not object—and it had stood for a decade because no document had ever surfaced to trouble it.

Why, then, did the Ministry want Corvane's papers?

Elias turned the question over through the afternoon while his pen moved through routine verifications, and by evening he had assembled the shape of it without quite daring to look at it directly: a master cartographer, obsessive, meticulous, working the harbor districts for thirty years; a man who had surveyed the Saltmarsh levees more than once in his career, whose professional habit would have been to measure everything, including—especially—the things the government preferred unmeasured. If there were survey notes from the winter of 1864 still existing anywhere in Vellamare, they existed in a shop on Lantern Street, in a drawer or a tube or beneath the floorboards of a dead man's workroom, guarded by a girl with ink in her skin who would not give her name.

And upstairs wanted them. Before anyone else could have them. Before they could become what they would inevitably become in the wrong—or right—hands: evidence.

His own field notes, he realized, were sitting in that shop's future like a lit fuse. *Irregular residency; subject evasive; recommend follow-up.* Follow-up visits brought watchers; watchers brought warrants; warrants brought the strongbox men, who did not distinguish carefully between contraband persons and contraband paper. His schedule, if he finished it—if he submitted it—would put the Bureau's weight down on 14 Lantern Street within a fortnight, and whatever the girl was guarding would go into a storeroom, and she would go somewhere worse.

Or she would go nowhere at all, if the papers weren't there. If she was only what she appeared: a squatter, an orphan, an apprentice of nothing. Then the schedule cost her a registration hearing, a fine perhaps, the ordinary grinding of the machine. And Elias's conscience would have nothing to answer for except the daily freight it already carried.

He could not know which it was. That was the exquisite cruelty of it. The form demanded certainty his conscience could not supply, and so the form sat unfinished, and the ink dried in its well, and outside his window the fog came up Fishgut Lane smelling of tar and cold salt, wrapping the chimneys, muffling the bells of Saint Ferren's that counted the hours for a city that had stopped listening.

* * *

He dreamed of Tomas that night, which was common enough, but the dream was new. In it his brother stood in a room of shelved paper that stretched away into dark, holding out a document, saying—without sound, the way dream speech works—*read it, read it, it's all here, I wrote it all down.* And Elias in the dream reached for the page and found it blank, and woke at four in the morning with his lamp still burning and the unfinished schedule square on his desk where he had left it, waiting, patient as all bureaucratic things are patient.

He sat down. He picked up the pen.

What he wrote next took him nearly an hour, because he was a careful man, and care, in the Bureau's language, meant lying slowly. He recorded the irregular residency. He recorded the subject's refusal of a family name. He recorded the apparent trade and the approximate age. And under *basis of suspicion*, in the small exact hand that eleven years had trained, he entered a sentence technically true and wholly hollow: *Basis of suspicion: premises operating under registration of deceased principal; subject unable to produce personal documentation at time of visit. No indication of seditious materials observed. Recommend administrative resolution only.*

*Administrative resolution only.* Four words that would, if Hallow read them as they were meant to be read, sink the file into the low dull wetland of routine processing, where schedules went to age for months without action. Four words that were also, he knew, a confession to anyone who chose to look—a flag planted in the open hand of his own disloyalty. Hallow was not stupid. Upstairs was not stupid. If the girl mattered, if the papers mattered, then a junior verifier who visited a flagged address and came back recommending *administrative resolution only* was either incompetent or compromised, and neither diagnosis ended well.

He read the form through twice. Then he folded it, sealed it, and set it on the left side of his desk—away from the out-tray, toward the pile of schedules awaiting supplementary verification, the great sedimentary drift of postponed work in which a document could rest for weeks, even months, if nobody asked after it.

Not submitted. Not destroyed. Suspended, like everything else in his life, between the man he was paid to be and the brother he had failed.

He blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the chandlery's rigging knock against the seawall below, and he understood with perfect clarity that suspension was not a decision. It was a debt accruing interest. Sooner or later the file would surface, or the girl would surface, or upstairs would send someone less hesitant to Lantern Street, and he would have chosen after all—retreat simply deferred the choosing, it did not erase it.

In the drawer of his desk, beneath the blank schedules, lay a copy of the Bureau's current requisition list for the purchase program—the categories, the target estates, the names of dead men whose papers the Ministry wished to make historically insignificant. He had carried it home without quite admitting why, the way one carries home a stone from a beach, proof of a place one has been and does not mean to return to.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would decide what to do with it.

But tomorrow, when he came down the stairs into the fog with the list in his coat pocket and his conscience in the condition of a tide halfway turned, the first thing he saw on the corner of Fishgut Lane was a bill pasted to the notice post by the ferry stairs: a printed announcement, ministry-blue, of the tenth-anniversary commemorations for the Saltmarsh flood—and beneath it, in smaller type, the standing order he had somehow never before let himself truly read:

*All public gatherings in commemoration of the events of March 1864 are prohibited by order of the Ministry of Restoration.*

Ten years, he thought. Ten years, and they were still afraid of the mourners.
