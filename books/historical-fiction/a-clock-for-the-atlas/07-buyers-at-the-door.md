## 7. Buyers at the Door

The bell above the shop door had not rung in eleven days, and Ada had come to think of its silence as a kind of weather—favorable, predictable, hers. She was at the back bench with a graver in her hand, trimming the edge of a copper plate she had no business trimming, when the bell rang.

She did not look up at once. That was the first discipline Master Corvane had taught her, before she understood there was anything to be disciplined *for*: *when a stranger enters a room, finish your stroke.* A hand that jerks betrays a mind that startles, and a mind that startles is a mind with something to hide. So she finished the stroke—a long, clean line along what would become the northern lip of the harbor—and only then set the graver down in its groove on the bench, exactly parallel to the burnisher, and turned.

Two men stood in the doorway of Corvane & Sons, Mapmakers. One was young and broad and bored-looking, dressed in the gray wool of government service without insignia, positioned slightly behind and to the left of the other in the manner of a man paid to be furniture. The other was perhaps fifty, slender as a folded chart, in a coat of deep oxblood that had been cut by someone who understood that money should whisper. He wore spectacles with round lenses and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face, as though dispatched ahead of him to scout the ground.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it to be obeyed. "I wonder if I might trouble the establishment."

Ada wiped her hands on her apron—an apprentice's gesture, unremarkable—and crossed the shop floor at an apprentice's pace, shoulders slightly rounded, eyes down but not servile. She had practiced this walk for years without knowing what she was practicing for.

"The shop's closed to trade," she said. "Since Master Corvane passed. I only keep the premises."

"Ah." The man's smile deepened by a degree, and his gaze went past her shoulder, moving over the shelves with the slow, inventorying pleasure of a customs officer. "Corvane. Yes. We were so sorry to hear of it. A great loss to the city. A great loss to *learning*." He said the last word the way another man might say a woman's name. "My name is Hollis. I represent parties interested in the preservation of cartographic materials. Private parties," he added, and let that sit in the air between them like a coin placed on a table.

Behind him, the young one yawned behind his fist and then, catching something from his employer, didn't.

* * *

She had imagined this moment—not this precisely, not this coat or these spectacles, but the shape of it—ever since she had knelt beside Master Corvane's bed in the fog of that last February and heard him say *finish it* with breath he could not spare. She had imagined soldiers, mostly. Boots on the stair, the door broken, the drawer pried open. She had imagined being dragged out into the lane while neighbors watched from their windows and did nothing, because watching and doing nothing was the civic liturgy of Vellamare. What she had never imagined was courtesy. Coin offered politely. A man who would say *we were so sorry to hear of it* about a death he might well have arranged to hasten.

"We've no stock to sell," she said. "Master Corvane settled the accounts before—" She stopped herself from saying *before the fever took him*, because she had learned that volunteered details are the first stones that build a liar's cairn. "Before he died. Anything of value went to his creditors. What's left is fixtures and dust."

"Dust," Mr. Hollis repeated, delighted. He stepped further into the shop—he did not ask; he simply occupied space until the space agreed to be his—and drew one gloved finger along the shelf nearest the door. He examined the fingertip. "And yet such well-kept dust. Tell me—" he turned, and the round lenses caught the gray light from the window so that she could not see his eyes at all, only two small silver coins where eyes should be, "—do you find many callers? For the premises of a closed shop?"

"Some. Debt collectors, at first. A cousin from Sarrow, once."

"And no scholars? No gentlemen of the historical persuasion?"

"No, sir."

"Hm." He removed the glove, finger by finger, and folded it into his pocket with the care of a man putting away evidence. "Then let me be plain, since plainly is how I prefer to do business. My employers are compiling a private collection. Materials relating to the coastal surveys of the late civil period are of particular interest. Master Corvane was, in his day, among the finest surveyors on this coast. His field notes, his working papers, his plates, his correspondence—anything of that nature would command a very generous price. Generous," he said again, letting the word do a slow turn. "More generous than such materials could ever earn sitting in a drawer."

"There are no field notes," Ada said. It came out steady. She was almost proud of it. "He worked directly to plate, near the end. Said paper was for men who couldn't commit."

Hollis smiled at that, genuinely, and something in the genuineness was worse than the performance had been. "A sentiment after my own heart. May I?" He was already moving toward the back of the shop, toward the bench, toward the flat wooden cabinet where three finished plates of the atlas lay under linen—not the dangerous ones, she had made sure of that weeks ago, only coastlines, only innocent water—but still her heartbeat climbed into her throat and lodged there like a swallowed stone.

"Sir, I don't think—"

"You wouldn't be expected to." He paused at the bench and looked down at her work: the half-cut plate, the ink, the burnisher laid parallel in its groove. He looked at it rather longer than a man looks at dust. Then, softly, almost tenderly: "Fine line work. Very fine. Whose hand is this?"

"Mine," Ada said. And then, because silence where a servant should apologize is itself a signature: "I do the upkeep. Cleaning the plates, keeping the rust off. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Hollis echoed. He did not say it as a question. He said it the way a man repeats a password to confirm it has been received correctly, and Ada felt the floor tilt a degree beneath her feet, the sensation of standing on a map that someone else is redrawing.

He bent over the cabinet, opened it without asking, and turned the top plate toward the light. The northern lip of the harbor gleamed wet-black where her line ran. Hollis studied it for ten full seconds, which is nine seconds longer than any buyer studies a plate he believes worthless.

"This," he said, "is very good work. This is, in fact, better than good. This is Corvane's late manner—the way he'd begun weighting his soundings, all that anxiety in the hatching—but the hand is younger. Steadier." He straightened and looked at her over the rims of his spectacles, and now, at last, she saw his eyes, and they were pale and mild and absolutely without mercy. "You say you only clean the plates."

"I clean the plates."

"Mm." He set the plate down with exaggerated gentleness, aligning it to the linen exactly as it had lain, and that exactness frightened her more than anything else he had done. A careless man could be misread. This man left nothing out of place because nothing he did was without intention. "Well. It happens sometimes. An apprentice absorbs the master's hand. Like a household sharing a cough." He laughed—a small, pleasant sound—and turned to face her fully, and the boredom drained out of the young one behind him, who stood up off the doorframe as if a spring had been released. "Let me speak frankly, then, since we're both professionals. There are persons in this city who believe Master Corvane kept papers he ought not to have kept. Those persons are prepared to pay handsomely for them. But they are also prepared to pay somewhat less handsomely"—the smile again, the scout sent ahead—"to learn who else may have been interested in them. Do you follow me?"

"I'm sure I don't, sir."

"No," he said. "I expect you're sure of a great deal." From inside his coat he produced a card—not printed, she noticed, but engraved, the letters cut into the stock so that they could be read by touch in the dark—and laid it on the bench beside the burnisher, square with its edge, precise as everything else he did. "If anything should surface. A drawer, a strongbox, papers found sewn into a coat lining. Anything at all. Day or night." He put his glove back on, finger by finger. "The offer will not improve with age. Neither, I'm afraid, will the alternative to it, though I hesitate to name such a thing on a morning so fine."

Outside, gulls wheeled over the rooftops crying their thin complaint, and the harbor light came through the window the way it always did, and Ada stood very still on her own shop floor and made her heartbeat walk back down out of her throat by main force.

"Good day to you, Miss—"

"Sandoval," she said.

It was out before she could catch it. Her own name, given freely, to a man hunting her master's ghost—she heard it leave her mouth the way one hears a plate crack under the burnisher, too late, the flaw already running through it. She had gone years without speaking it aloud. Years of being *the girl at Corvane's*, *the apprentice*, *miss* with no surname attached, and now she had handed it over like a calling card because politeness had ambushed her.

Hollis stopped in the doorway. He turned back, and the smile returned, and this time it reached his eyes, which was somehow the worst thing yet.

"Sandoval," he repeated, tasting it. "How interesting. Not a name I associate with this establishment." A pause, delicately measured. "But then, so much in this city isn't what it's called, is it? Good morning, Miss Sandoval. Do give the matter some thought. Thought," he said warmly, "is free. Everything else, as you've seen, has a price."

The bell rang them out. The door swung shut. Through the glass she watched the two figures cross the lane, unhurried, the young one falling back into his place behind and to the left, and turn the corner toward the Customs House spur, and were gone.

* * *

Ada did not move for a long time. Then she crossed to the door, shot the bolt, dropped the shade, and stood in the sudden brown dusk of the shut shop with her palm flat against the wood, listening to her own blood.

*Sandoval.* He had it now. Written somewhere, remembered somewhere, cross-referenced against whatever registry held the names of apprentices and servants and the uncounted poor. Eleven days of silence, eleven months of careful erasure, undone in a single reflex of manners. Her master had taught her to finish her stroke before looking up, and she had mastered that lesson and every lesson like it, and none of it—none of it—had taught her how to refuse her own name when a polite man asked for it in the tone one uses to ask the time.

She thought of the drawer. It sat three steps behind her, under the plan chest, locked, its key sewn into the hem of her second-best skirt where she could feel it shift against her ankle when she walked, a small hard secret keeping time with her pulse. The half-burned notes lay folded inside in oilcloth: the levee measurements that ran the wrong direction, the cut-face sketches marked *landward*, the figures in her master's failing hand that proved the water had been invited in. Evidence. She had thought the word meant safety—that to hold proof was to hold power. Now she understood it meant the opposite. Proof was not power. Proof was weight, and weight must be carried, and everyone who saw you carrying it knew exactly where to cut you open to take it.

She pulled the card from the bench and read it by the light leaking around the shade's edge. No Bureau crest. No address beyond a number on the Customs House spur. Only a name—*A. Hollis*—engraved deep enough to read by fingertips, and beneath it, in letters so small she had to hold it to her eye:

*We buy quietly. We pay promptly. We do not buy twice.*

Ada carried it to the stove and held it to the coals and watched the engraving blister and blacken, the fine cut letters glowing briefly orange like channels of lava before collapsing into common ash. Then she swept the ash into the bin, mixed it with the day's shavings so no whole fragment remained, and went and stood over the plan chest with her arms wrapped around herself.

They had not come because of anything she had done. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. They had come because Master Corvane had died, and death leaves drawers, and drawers invite buyers. The hunt had begun on the day he closed his eyes, and she had spent a year pretending she was hiding when in truth she had merely not yet been found. Invisibility was not a wall. It was a tide, going out.

She thought of Marta Quill, up at the harbor, with her boat and her silences and her gruff way of saying *sit down and eat* as if feeding a person were a form of argument. She had promised herself she would not go to Marta unless she had no other choice, because going to anyone was a debt and debts were chains, and Ada Sandoval had sworn at sixteen she would owe nothing to nobody.

She looked at the plan chest. Under it, the drawer. Inside it, the truth of four hundred drowned people, waiting for a mouth.

The choice arranged itself with maplike clarity, the way choices do once the fear has finished drawing its coastlines: carry it alone and be found alone, or find hands to help bear the weight and risk that some of those hands belonged to Hollis's employers.

She took her cloak from the peg. She took the key from the hem of her skirt. And as she knelt to unlock the drawer, she heard again the mild, merciless voice at her shoulder, offering generously, buying quietly, promising not to buy twice—and she understood, with a cold settling certainty, that the next visitor to this shop would not bother knocking first.

The lock gave its small click. Outside, far off toward the water, a single bell buoy tolled in the swell, counting a tide that was already on its way in.
