## 8. The Archivist Who Fell

The stairs smelled of tallow and old paper, which was, Ada thought as she climbed, the only honest smell in Vellamare.

Marta Quill went first, her broad shoulders taking up most of the stairwell, one hand trailing the wall where the plaster had gone soft with damp. She did not knock at the top door. She never knocked anywhere, Ada was learning; knocking, Marta said, was for people who expected an answer. She simply pushed, and the door swung inward on a room that seemed to have been assembled by a storm and then abandoned by it.

Paper everywhere. Not the ordered paper of Master Corvane's shop—no bundles squared to the edge of a desk, no drawers with brass fittings and labels in a careful hand—but paper as sediment, as driftwood. Folios heaped on chairs. A broken-backed ledger propping up the short leg of a table. Sheets furred along the walls like moss, pinned or slipped behind whatever would hold them. In the middle of it all, in a chair that had once been good walnut and was now mostly scars, sat a woman with a bottle at her elbow and a magnifying glass hanging from a ribbon around her neck like a saint's medal worn backward.

"Odile," Marta said, without greeting. "I've brought you work."

"You've brought me nothing for six years." Odile Ferrant's voice came out low and gravelled, the voice of someone who had shouted at ministers once and had been paying for the volume ever since. She did not rise. She looked past Marta entirely, at Ada, and her eyes were quick and bloodshot and missed nothing. "And who is this? No. Don't tell me. Let me guess. Someone wants something authenticated."

Ada had prepared a speech on the walk over. She had rehearsed it against the rhythm of her own footsteps: *Madame Ferrant, my name is—* except she had no name she could safely give, and so the speech had collapsed somewhere around its second word, and what remained was a girl standing in a doorway holding a wrapped bundle against her chest the way other people held infants.

"She doesn't want anything authenticated," Marta said. "She wants to know if it's real."

"There is a difference?" Odile lifted the bottle, considered it, set it down again unopened—a small performance of restraint that fooled neither of them. "Sit down, girl. Both of you sit. There are two chairs and one of them has a cat problem. Choose wisely."

There was no cat. Ada took the chair anyway, because her legs had decided the matter before her caution could object, and unwrapped the oilcloth from the survey notes with hands that were not quite steady. She had not shown them to anyone but Marta. She had barely let herself look at them alone; there were hours when looking felt like theft and hours when it felt like prayer, and she had stopped trying to tell the two apart.

Odile leaned forward. For a long moment she did not touch anything. She looked the way Master Corvane used to look at a coastline he didn't trust—not reading, exactly, but interrogating.

Then she reached out with two fingers, turned the top sheet toward the grey light from the window, and said, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its gravel:

"Oh, you poor fool. You brave, dead fool."

* * *

It took Odile Ferrant eleven minutes to establish that the notes were genuine, and she spent the next hour explaining why, and Ada understood, somewhere in the middle of that hour, that she had found either a savior or a disaster, and that these might be the same person.

"Look here." Odile's finger hovered above the page without touching it—the discipline of it startled Ada; everyone else she'd ever seen handle old papers had pawed them like market cloth. "The ink. Corvane wrote his field notes in iron-gall, always, and he mixed it himself, too strong, always—you can see how it's bitten into the fiber here, browned the paper around every stroke like a scorch. A forger working after the flood would use shop ink, gentler, and it would sit *on* the paper like grease on water. This is in the paper. Ten years in the paper." Her finger moved. "Now the hand. Corvane's capitals lean left when he's writing fast and upright when he's copying fair. These lean left. He was writing fast. He was frightened or furious, and knowing Corvane—" she paused, and something moved across her face that was not quite grief and not quite contempt, "—knowing Corvane, both."

"You knew him," Ada said.

"I catalogued him." Odile laughed, a dry, terrible sound. "For nine years I was the state's keeper of surveys. Every map, every chart, every levelling book that passed through any government office in this province came across my desk to be numbered and shelved and forgotten properly. Corvane fought with me twice about filing conventions and won once, which is a better record than anyone. He was the only surveyor in Vellamare whose work I never had to correct." She turned another sheet. "And then the flood happened, and the official account arrived on my desk, and I read it, and I knew it was wrong."

"How?"

"Because I had read the water levels." Odile said it flatly, the way a person states a thing they have stated a hundred times to people who did not want to hear it. "The commission's report says the levees failed under storm surge. Storm surge comes off the sea. It pushes water *in*. But the breach pattern—the silting, the debris lines, the way the drowned streets flooded from their landward ends first—I had the surveyors' soundings, girl. I had them shelved three feet from my own desk. Saltmarsh filled from the canals side. From *inside*. Somebody opened the city's own sluices and cut the levee where it faces the fields, and the storm was cover, and everyone who could do arithmetic about water knew it, and everyone who could do arithmetic about water kept their mouths shut."

"And you didn't."

"I asked a question." Odile smiled, and it was the ugliest smile Ada had ever seen, and she thought she understood it completely. "One question. In writing. On departmental letterhead, because I was young and believed in procedure. I asked why the flood chronology in the commission report contradicted the tide tables for that night. Six weeks later I was dismissed—for irregularities in my accounts, which were invented, and for intemperance, which was true, and I have been improving at it ever since." She gestured at the bottle with something close to affection. "They didn't burn my files, though. That's the joke. They couldn't be bothered. They just shelved me out of the building and assumed a woman without a post would drink herself quiet. They were nearly right."

Marta snorted from the doorway, where she had installed herself with her arms crossed, watching the exchange like a woman watching weather.

"But this—" Odile's hand flattened above the notes, not touching, hovering, reverent and hungry at once. "This is the half-burned field book. This is the levelling observations from the week before the flood. If these say what I think they say—if Corvane ran his levels along the landward face and recorded the cut—" She stopped. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice had changed, dropped its performance entirely, and what was underneath was frighteningly precise. "Girl. Where did you get these?"

"They were my master's."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have."

Something passed between them then that Ada could not have named if she'd tried. Odile looked at her for a long time—at the ink stains worked so deep into her fingers they looked like shadow under the skin, at the way she sat with her spine straight even in a stolen chair, at the bundle she guarded with her forearm even while talking—and whatever calculation she performed, she performed it quickly.

"Corvane had an apprentice," she said. "He mentioned her once. He said she ruled a line truer than he did and trusted nobody, and that both facts would keep her alive longer than they deserved to." A pause. "What was your name?"

Ada said nothing.

"No," Odile agreed softly. "Quite right. Keep it. Names are how they find you, and names are also how they *prove* you, and one day soon you're going to need the second more than the first, but today is not that day." She finally touched the notes—one fingertip, at the corner, tilting the page toward the light. "Do you know what you are holding?"

"Evidence."

"Evidence is what a magistrate admits." Odile shook her head. "This is *provenance*. This is the thing the Bureau of Records has been buying and burning all winter, whether they know each piece individually or not—they are burning the winter, girl, they are buying up private papers by the crate because a tenth anniversary makes people sentimental and sentimental people open their trunks. Somewhere in some ministry office there is a list, and your master's name is on it, and everything with his hand on it is being gathered up to be weighed and destroyed. And you walked it across town in an oilcloth wrap."

Ada's stomach dropped. She thought of the agent in the shop, the generous coin, the smiling questions about *any materials of the late Master Corvane*, and her mouth went dry.

"Then it should go somewhere safe. Marta said—"

"Marta's boat is safe for contraband. Brandy keeps. Paper in a bilge keeps badly." Odile waved a hand. "No. Listen to me, because I am going to say this once and then I am going to drink, and you are going to let me. Hiding these notes is worthless. Do you understand? Worthless. A hidden truth changes nothing. Corvane hid his knowledge for ten years and it drowned with Saltmarsh all over again. The regime does not fear documents in cellars. It fears documents in *hands*. Many hands. Public hands. It fears a document it cannot buy back because five hundred people have already read it." She leaned forward, and the magnifying glass swung on its ribbon and caught the light. "These notes need authentication—witnessed, sealed, dated, chained from Corvane's hand to yours beyond any magistrate's argument—and then they need to be *published*, before the official history prints in spring and seals the lie in gilt letters forever. After that, they can burn whatever they like. The truth will already be loose, and loose truths are like spilled ink. You cannot recall them. You can only watch them spread."

The word *published* landed in Ada's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and heard no bottom.

"I'm not published," she said. "I'm not anything. I'm not even—"

She stopped herself, but too late; the shape of the sentence had escaped, and Odile, who had spent thirty years reading incomplete records and inferring what was missing, completed it without cruelty and without mercy.

"You're not even a person, as far as the census goes. No entry, no surname spoken aloud, no papers." She nodded slowly. "Yes. I know the type. I've catalogued thousands of you—or rather, I've catalogued the absence of you, the gaps where you should be, whole districts of people the state decided not to see. Saltmarsh was full of them. That was rather the point of Saltmarsh." She sat back. "So. An uncounted girl carrying the last unburned testimony of a murdered district, asking a dismissed archivist what to do with it." She picked up the bottle at last and poured two fingers into a cup that had probably held tea that morning. "What do you want from me, gap-in-the-record?"

"I want to know if it can be done."

"It can be done." Odile drank, and set the cup down, and her eyes when they came back up were clear in a way that made Ada suspect the drinking was armor worn over a very sharp mind rather than rust eating through it. "It will take provenance built link by link—receipts, witnesses, dates, a chain of custody no magistrate can break. It will take survivors willing to sign their names, which means putting them in danger, which means you will have to ask them anyway. It will take a printer with more courage than sense or more debt than courage. And it will take until spring, which is less time than it sounds, because the Bureau works faster than grief does." She ticked the items off on her fingers like a woman counting change. "That is what it takes. Now the real question."

"Which is?"

"Whether you are willing to be counted." Odile said it plainly, and the plainness was the knife. "Because publication is not hiding, girl. Publication is standing in the square with the thing in your hands. They will come for whoever holds it. They came for Corvane—don't look at me like that, you know it in your bones or you wouldn't have kept his name out of your mouth since you walked in. So before I teach you a single thing about authentication, before I spend one hour of what's left of my reputation on this, you answer me honestly: when the moment comes, will you stand behind these pages with your body, or will you run, and leave them to be found by someone braver?"

The garret was very quiet. Down in the street a cart rattled past, and gulls argued over the harbor, and Marta shifted her weight by the door and said nothing, watching Ada the way she'd watched her wrap the notes—like a woman deciding whether cargo was worth the crossing.

Ada thought about the seawall. About the drowned streets she had drawn from memory and soundings, street by street, name by name, because no living map admitted they had existed. About her master's last instruction, given in a whisper with the shutters closed: *finish it, and finish it truly.* She thought about how she had spent seven years learning to be invisible, and how invisibility had begun to feel less like safety and more like disappearing by inches, a self-administered drowning, and how tired she was of holding her breath.

"I don't know," she said.

Odile raised her eyebrows.

"But I know I'm done pretending the answer doesn't matter," Ada went on, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "Ask me again when I've learned enough to be worth killing."

A silence. Then Odile Ferrant began to laugh—really laugh, harsh and rusty, a laugh that had clearly not been used in years and complained about the disuse—and she poured a second finger into the cup and pushed it across the cluttered table toward the girl.

"Oh, you'll do," she said. "Come tomorrow morning. Bring the notes, bring every scrap of Corvane's paper you possess, bring receipts, letters, anything with dates on it. We begin with chain of custody, and we do not stop until you can defend every document in that bundle against the best forger-hunter the ministry employs—which, fortunately for us, is me, before they dismissed me, and I taught him everything he knows." She raised her own cup an inch. "To the archive, apprentice. May it outlive us all."

Ada lifted the cup. The brandy burned all the way down, and she did not cough, and Odile watched her not cough with something that was almost approval.

Outside, the light was failing over the rooftops, and Marta walked her down the stairwell in silence until the bottom step, where she stopped and said, gruffly, not looking at her: "She hasn't taken a client in six years. Whatever you said up there, don't undo it."

At the corner, turning toward the harbor and the shop and the locked drawer waiting in the dark, Ada realized she had forgotten to be afraid of being followed, and that this, more than anything Odile had said, told her how much had already changed.

Behind her, in the lit window of the garret, the archivist was already pulling folios from the drifts of paper, sorting, remembering—preparing, though Ada could not yet know it, to teach her the lesson that would cost them both the most:

that a record is only as strong as the person willing to stand inside it.
