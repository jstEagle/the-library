## 9. Learning to Be Counted

The garret smelled of cold tallow and older paper—paper gone soft at the edges, paper that had been wept over or rained on or hidden under floorboards during years Ada could only guess at. Odile Ferrant had lit every candle she owned, which was not many, and the flames made a low golden country out of the clutter: folios stacked like sediment, registers with cracked spines, a magnifying glass hanging from a nail like a saint's medal.

"Sit," Odile said, without looking up. She was sorting papers into three piles that looked identical to Ada and were clearly worlds apart. "Not there. There. Where I can see your hands."

Ada sat on the one stool that wasn't buried. "My hands?"

"A cartographer's hands. I want to see what they do when you're thinking." Odile finally raised her head. Her eyes were clearer this morning than they had been the night before; either the drink had worn off or she was holding it at bay by an act of will as deliberate as any survey. "Good. You fold them. Corvane did the same. You people think you're so quiet, but the hands confess everything."

"You knew my master."

"I audited his deposits twice and accused him of forgery once." A thin smile. "He never forgave me for being right about the second thing and wrong about the first. Sit still. We have perhaps four hours before the tavern below opens and its customers begin needing the privy, which shares my stair. I intend to use them."

She pulled a folio across the table and opened it with the tenderness of a woman handling a sleeping child. Inside lay documents—not the half-burned notes Ada carried, but their cousins: Bureau requisitions, ledger pages, a printed circular bearing the Ministry of Interior's seal.

"Lesson one," Odile said. "A document proves nothing. Say it back to me."

"A document proves nothing."

"It proves nothing. It *argues*. It stands in a room full of men paid to disbelieve it and it argues. And whether it wins depends entirely on what stands behind it. So." She held up one blunt finger. "Provenance. The chain of custody, unbroken, from the moment of creation to the moment of presentation. Who wrote it, who kept it, who inherited it, whose hands touched it between. Every link accounted for. One gap—" she snapped her fingers, and the sound was shockingly loud in the little room, "—and opposing counsel drives a coach through it. Tell me about Corvane's notes. From the beginning. When did he first put pen to paper on Saltmarsh?"

Ada's hands tightened in her lap. She made herself loosen them. "Before the flood. He—he began surveying the district in '61, I think. He had commissions from the Harbor Trust, soundings, that sort of thing. He kept his own copies of everything. He said—" She heard her master's voice in her memory, gravel and pipe smoke: *An official map tells you where things are. A private map tells you why.* "He said official maps answer *where*, and he wanted to know *why*."

"And after the flood? After the water came in November of '64?"

"He worked on it obsessively. Comparing old surveys to new soundings. Measuring how fast the quarter filled, where the silt settled. He said—" Ada stopped. This part she had never told anyone, because she had never fully believed it until two nights ago, crouched over the contents of a locked drawer.

"Say it," Odile said quietly.

"He said the silt told the truth even when the surveyors didn't. That the levee hadn't failed from the seaward side, the way the official record claimed. Storm surge scours. It gouges. But the breach channels he sounded were clean-cut, landward-facing, like—they'd been cut. Dug. Deliberately."

Odile closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, something in them had hardened past bitterness into purpose. "I reached the same conclusion from the other direction. From the paper. In '66 I processed the requisition orders for that winter. Powder stores drawn from the garrison magazine in quantities that no flood-repair operation would need—and then the requisition book itself vanished from the archive, replaced by a copy with different figures. Sloppy copy. The clerk who forged it used ink from a batch that wasn't milled until '65." Her mouth curved without warmth. "I asked one question in one memo. Within the month I was dismissed for 'chronic irregularity of temperament.'"

"That's not a real offense."

"No," Odile agreed. "But it is a real charge. Lesson two, apprentice: the regime does not destroy inconvenient records anymore. It replaces them. Understand what that means. Somewhere in the Bureau of Records there is a version of the Saltmarsh flood file so clean, so complete, so boringly administrative, that no one reading it would ever suspect. Against that, your burned scraps and my memory are worth very little indeed—unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless we make our version impossible to ignore. Which brings me to lesson three." She rose, joints cracking, and went to the window, peering down at the street out of habit rather than necessity. "Witnesses. Paper argues; people testify. A living witness who swears an oath before a magistrate creates a fact that must be contradicted to be dismissed, and contradicting a sworn witness publicly is expensive. The Bureau lies cheaply in ledgers. It lies expensively in courtrooms. We need witnesses, Ada. Survivors. People who saw soldiers near the levees, heard the powder charges, lost kin in ways the official account cannot explain. And we need them before spring."

"Spring?"

"The official history." Odile turned from the window. "You didn't know. Of course you didn't. They announced it last autumn—*A Reconciled Account of the Civil War and Its Consequences*, commissioned by the ministry, compiled by their own historians. It will contain the flood chapter. The definitive one. Once it prints, the lie stops being a lie and becomes simply *history*—taught in schools, cited in courts, repeated by priests. Every year that passes after publication, our evidence gets weaker and theirs gets stronger. We have until the presses run. Perhaps five months. Perhaps less."

The candle nearest Ada guttered and steadied. She watched the flame do it and thought about nothing else for a few seconds, because the alternative was to think about what Odile had just said.

"And if we publish first?" she asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Then we force them into our terrain. An authenticated atlas, publicly distributed, with provenance documented and witnesses named—their history arrives already contested. Scholars will compare. Clerks will gossip. Magistrates will have both versions in front of them. The lie survives only as long as it goes unchallenged in print." Odile came back to the table and sat, leaning forward, and for a moment the drunk in the garret vanished entirely and Ada saw the woman who had run half the state's archives before her thirtieth year. "Publication is the whole game. Not hiding the notes. Not smuggling them abroad, though Marta has doubtless suggested it. Publication. Here. Where the drowned lived and the living remember."

Ada stood up. She hadn't decided to; her body simply refused to keep sitting.

"You're asking me to sign my name," she said.

"I'm asking you to put your name where the regime can read it."

"I don't have a name. That's the point. That's been the point since Master Corvane died and I burned his letters and started signing myself *assistant* on invoices. Do you know what happens to an undocumented apprentice in Vellamare? They don't arrest you. Arrest requires paperwork, and paperwork requires that you exist. They just—" she made a small gesture toward the harbor, toward the gray water beyond it, "—disappear you. Like Saltmarsh. Like everyone the city has ever found inconvenient. If I publish, if I stand behind these documents, I stop being invisible. I become a target with a face and an address and a name that no registry protects."

"Yes," Odile said.

"*Yes?* That's your answer? Yes, you'll be hunted?"

"Yes. You'll be hunted, and arrested, and quite possibly worse—and here is the part you do not yet understand, girl, so attend to me." Odile's voice did not rise. It dropped instead, into a register that Ada felt in her sternum. "You are *already* hunted. The Bureau has spent all winter buying up private papers; Marta told you that herself. They know Corvane kept records worth fearing—that's why they're collecting. The moment they trace his effects to you, and they will, your invisibility ends anyway. The only question left is whether you spend your exposure buying anything. Hiding buys nothing. Silence buys nothing. But a name attached to a published truth—" she tapped the folio, "—that purchases something no cell can hold."

Ada turned away. The garret's one window showed a slice of Vellamare: rooftops descending in terraces toward the harbor, gulls stitching the air, the seawall visible at the far edge where the city ended and the drowned quarter began—a long gray line, ordinary as anything, holding back eleven feet of water and ten years of silence.

"I trust lines," she said, mostly to herself. "Lines don't betray you. A coastline drawn true stays true. People—" 

"People are the only thing lines are *for*." Odile's voice softened by a degree, no more. "Every line on your atlas marks ground where someone walked, prayed, argued, buried their dead. You've spent six years pretending the map is the territory because the territory frightens you. Well. The territory is about to run you down regardless. You may as well choose your ground."

Silence stretched. Below them, faintly, came the clatter of the tavern preparing to open, the shared stair about to lose its privacy.

"Five months," Ada said at last. "Provenance. Witnesses. Publication. You make it sound like a recipe."

"It is a recipe. The ingredient nobody can supply but you is courage, and I regret to inform you it cannot be faked in a workshop." Odile gathered her three piles back into one drift, the audience concluded. "Go home. Look at your notes. Then walk to the seawall and look at the water and decide whether you are an archivist of the dead or a cartographer of the living. Come back when you know."

Ada was halfway down the dark stair when Odile's voice followed her:

"And, girl—the memorial is next week. Ten years. There will be mourners at the seawall despite the ban. Mourners make excellent witnesses, if anyone thinks to bring paper."

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the harbor road, because her feet chose it before her mind did. The morning was bright and cold, the kind of light that flattered the city mercilessly—whitewash glowing, fishing boats drawn up like sleeping animals, the fish market roaring with commerce and argument. Vellamare at its best. Vellamare as it appeared on the official maps: complete, coherent, whole.

She tried to imagine her name on the title page. *Surveyed and drawn by—* By whom? Six years of erasure, self-administered daily. She had signed herself assistant, journeyman, *a hand of the house of Corvane*, anything but the surname her mother had given her before the war took her mother too. The name existed nowhere now except in her own throat, and it sat there like a stone she had swallowed so long ago she'd mistaken it for part of her body.

And yet.

At the shop she unlocked the door with the three turns she always gave the lock, checked the drawer's false front, and stood for a while in the dim familiar smell of ink and size and old wood, letting the fear speak. Let it say everything it had to say. *They will come for you. They will come quietly, the way they came for the requisition book, and there will be no hearing, no crowd, no record of you at all. You will be a gap in the chain of custody. Unprovenanced. Disappeared.*

The fear was articulate. It had had years of practice.

But underneath it, fainter, another voice—one that sounded less like her own and more like the silt in the breach channels, telling its patient physical truth: *The water came from the landward side. Someone cut the levee. Eleven hundred people drowned in their beds, and the city drew a blue wash over their streets and wrote "inlet" on the map, and every year the tide comes back over them as if nothing happened.*

She thought of Odile, dismissed for one memo, keeping her candles lit anyway. Of Marta Quill, who had said *bring me the notes, then*, gruff as handing over a loaf. Of Elias Wren's questions, and the strange hesitation in them—hers was not the only conscience in this city at war with itself.

Fear confronted the cost of justice and found, to her surprise, that justice had brought witnesses of its own.

Ada sat down at the workbench, pulled the half-burned notes from the drawer, and began, in her smallest steadiest hand, to draft the title page of the secret edition. *Surveyed by Aurel Corvane, Cartographer, 1861–1873. Completed and attested by—*

Her pen hovered over the blank.

She left it blank. Not forever. But tonight, blank—and in the margin beside it she sketched, quickly, without letting herself think, a small tide clock, its face marked with a single hour.

Tomorrow she would go back to Odile. Tomorrow she would start building the chain, link by link. But first, before the week was out, there would be mourners at the seawall—mourners who remembered faces, names, the sound the night wind made through the breach—and Ada Sandoval would be standing among them with paper in her pocket and a pencil sharpened to a needle's point.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour. Ten years ago tonight, they had not rung at all.
