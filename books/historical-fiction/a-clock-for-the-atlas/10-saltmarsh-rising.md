## 10. Saltmarsh Rising

The ban had been posted on every corner of the Lower City for a week: *No assemblies within the Saltmarsh district or upon the seawall promenade, by order of the Ministry of Public Order, in perpetuity.* In perpetuity. Ada had read the notice four times, copying the phrasing into her memory the way she copied coastlines—by the weight of each curve, the exact angle where one stroke met another. *In perpetuity* was a cartographer's word. It was what you wrote on a map when you intended a thing never to change. The ministry had not banned a gathering; they had banned a place from meaning anything ever again.

Which was, of course, precisely why the mourners would come.

Ada went up to the seawall an hour before dusk, taking the long way through the tanner's lanes so that no one could mark her direction. She wore her hood up and Marta Quill's oilskin cape, which smelled of pitch and herring and made her shapeless, sexless, forgettable—the silhouette of a hundred dock workers trudging home. Beneath the cape, strapped against her ribs, she carried her sketchbook wrapped in waxed cloth, and beneath that, close as a second heartbeat, three pages of Master Corvane's half-burned survey notes. She had promised Marta she would leave them behind. She had lied. If the Bureau took her tonight, they would take everything, and the notes would at least drown with her rather than feed their fires.

The seawall itself rose before her like a jaw. Ten years of salt crust whitened its granite teeth, and above it the harbor wind came raw off the water, carrying the smell of the drowned quarter—not decay after all this time, but something older and colder, the mineral breath of streets that still existed below the tide. Ada knew those streets. She had never walked them alive, but she had drawn them a thousand times from her master's plates: Gullery Row, the Lantern Stair, Pier Nine with its customs house, the little octagonal market square where the Saltmarsh clock had stood before someone pried it loose and sold it. Corvane's maps showed them all, inked with a tenderness he had shown nowhere else in his work. He had labeled every street twice—once in the official nomenclature and once, in his small cramped hand along the margins, with the names people actually used. *Thieves' Walk. The Wailing Steps. Old Nesta's Court.*

She stopped at the low parapet and looked down at the water, and the water looked back, flat and gray-green and patient.

"Ten years," said a voice beside her, and Ada flinched before she understood it was only an old woman, muffled in black shawls, who had come up to the wall as silently as grief itself. "Ten years, and they think a notice will keep us home." The woman spat over the parapet, deliberately, into the tide. Then she turned her face to the water and began, very quietly, to recite names.

Ada stood still and listened. The names came slowly, spaced like stones across a ford: a fishmonger, a midwife, two brothers who kept the rope-walk, a girl of six whose name the woman pronounced with particular care, as if setting it down on a shelf. Other figures had gathered along the wall by then—a dozen, two dozen, emerging from the fog in ones and twos, each pausing some yards distant from the last, maintaining the careful spacing of people who knew they were committing a crime but could not stay away. No one spoke above a murmur. No one sang, though somewhere behind Ada's shoulder a man was humming, very low, the first three notes of a lament that the rest of the city had been forbidden to finish.

It was not a crowd. It was a coastline—scattered, continuous, all along the wall, impossible to arrest without arresting the whole shore.

Ada found a gap between a fishwife with a basket of unsold sprats and a young dockhand missing two fingers, and she leaned against the parapet as if she were merely resting, and she opened her sketchbook under the cover of the cape.

* * *

She had not planned it. That was the truth she would examine later, turning it over like a coin to see whose face was stamped on it: she had come to mourn, or to remember, or simply because the tenth anniversary pulled at her the way the moon pulls water. But the sketchbook was part of her hands now; Corvane had trained her never to be anywhere without it. *An unrecorded observation is a wasted one, girl,* he used to say, sharpening his pencils with a knife he kept dull on purpose, *and worse than wasted—it is a lie you tell your successors.* So she drew, because drawing was how she thought.

She began with the old woman and her litany. The shawls made it easy—dark folds, a face like a walnut carved by weather, the lips moving around each name. Ada worked fast, in the shorthand of line that Corvane had called "the honest lie": not the whole truth of a face, but the few strokes that made it undeniable. A nose broken and healed crooked. Eyebrows like storm gulls. The way the mouth pressed shut after each name, holding the next one down deep until it was ready.

Then the fishwife, broad and salt-pink, standing guard over her basket as though even here the sprats might be confiscated. Then the humming man, revealed when he turned—a stevedore by the shoulders, eyes closed, the three-note phrase rising and falling like something breathing. Then more. They kept coming. By full dark there must have been eighty souls strung along the promenade, and lanterns began to appear—small ones, shuttered, set on the parapet at intervals, each flame a small act of treason.

And faces. Faces everywhere, and every single one of them marked by the same ten years.

Ada saw it gradually, the way she would see a pattern emerge in survey data, point by point until the contour declared itself. These were not merely mourners. Look at the hands: too many of them scarred in the specific way of people pulled from water, or dug through wreckage, or burned hauling neighbors out of flooded houses. Look at the ages: almost no one young except children born after—the flood had taken the old and the rooted, and the regime had taken the young men afterward, quietly, into records purges and consignments east. Look at how they stood: not clustered like strangers at a spectacle but spaced like survivors of the same shipwreck, each one knowing exactly which others had been beside them in the water, keeping those distances sacred.

These were the people who had been inside Saltmarsh when the levees broke.

Her pencil slowed. Her heart did not.

She had spent weeks thinking of Corvane's notes as the center of gravity—the half-burned pages proving the levee cuts faced inland, proving tools and powder and soldiers, proving intent—and the atlas as the vessel that would carry them. Documents, documents, documents. Odile Ferrant had begun to teach her why documents alone were fragile: paper can be forged, provenance can be disputed, a state archive can dismiss any scrap it does not wish to see. But living testimony, sworn and public and repeated—*that*, Odile had said, was harder to burn, because you would have to burn the witness, and burning witnesses has a way of lighting other fires.

And here they all were, gathered in defiance of a perpetual ban, wearing their testimony on their bodies like medals no government had ever pinned.

Ada turned to a fresh page and wrote, in the tiny ciphered hand she used for things that must never be read aloud: *Not just faces. NAMES. Addresses if I can get them. Who was where. What they saw. The water reached the Lantern Stair at half past nine—the wall of it—ask anyone standing near me NOW and they will tell you the hour, they have carried the hour for ten years.*

A woman nearby was weeping without sound, her knuckles white on the parapet. Beside her a man held up a shuttered lantern and, tilting it for a moment toward the sea, let its light spill briefly onto the black water—as if showing something to someone below. Others followed, one after another, a slow ripple of light passing down the wall, each lantern opening for a heartbeat toward the drowned quarter and closing again. Eighty heartbeats of illumination aimed at nothing but tide.

Ada sketched that too. Some things deserved more than words.

* * *

"You're drawing."

The voice came from very close, and Ada's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. She snapped the sketchbook shut inside the cape and turned, already composing the excuse—*I'm left-handed and idle, sir, I doodle*—but the words died when she saw who had spoken.

A boy. Twelve, perhaps thirteen, in a coat cut down from a man's, sleeves rolled in careful wads. He was thin in the particular way of children who had been thinner. His hair was cropped brutally short, prison-fashion or workhouse-fashion, and he looked at Ada not with the wariness of a child approaching a stranger but with the flat assessment of one adult recognizing another's trade.

"I saw your hand moving," he said. "Under the cape. You hold the pencil like my mother did. She was a sign-painter." He paused. "Before."

"Before," Ada repeated softly.

"The flood." He said it the way people say the name of a dead relative—without ceremony, because the ceremony is worn smooth with use. "She painted the shop signs on Gullery Row. Everyone knew her letters. They were—" His jaw worked. "You couldn't get paint that color anymore. Vermilion. The whole row was full of it."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, she opened the sketchbook again, letting the lantern-light find the page: the walnut-faced woman, the fishwife, the stevedore mid-hum. The boy studied them with fierce attention, and something moved behind his eyes—hunger, recognition, the ache of seeing a skill applied to sacred material.

"That's Widow Bren," he said, pointing. "She pulled four people out through a roof hatch on Pier Nine. Four. Nobody ever wrote it down." His finger moved. "That's the rope-walk man. His brothers both went. And her—" the fishwife "—she lost her boat and her husband in the same night and never once cried where anyone could see, so don't put crying on her."

"I hadn't," Ada said. "Look."

The boy looked. Whatever he found in the lines seemed to satisfy some tribunal sitting in his chest, because he nodded once, sharply, and crouched beside her in the shadow of the parapet.

"They'll scatter at the bell," he said. "We always do. When the harbor bell rings nine—half past, really, they changed the bells after, so everyone comes at eight and counts in their heads till it feels like half past nine—we go, quick, all directions. You should too." He glanced up at her, and for a moment the adult flatness cracked and he was only a child on a cold wall. "But if you're making a book of us—"

Ada's pulse knocked hard against her ribs. "If I were," she said carefully, "what would you want in it?"

He didn't answer at once. Down the wall, the lanterns continued their slow breathing, open, closed, open. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone very quiet and very precise, the voice of a rehearsed sentence finally delivered.

"My mother's name. Sera Ovid. And that she painted the best vermilion letters in Vellamare, and that she stayed in the water helping people up until the water took her, and that she was not a rebel, whatever the histories say, she was a *sign-painter*—" His voice broke and he mastered it the way children master things far too large for them, brutally, immediately. "That's all. Just put it where it won't wash off."

Ada took his hand—thin, chapped, cold—and pressed the pencil into it, guiding it to the bottom margin of the page. "Then you write it," she said. "Your hand, your spelling, however it comes. A record in the witness's own hand is worth ten in anyone else's. My master taught me that."

The boy printed, tongue between his teeth, laboring: *SERA OVID. SIGN PAINTER. SHE HELPED PEOPLE UP.* Below it, after a hesitation, *HER SON WAS THERE.*

"There," Ada said, when he'd finished. "Now it exists in two places at once. That's how you make a thing hard to kill."

He stared at the page as if it had caught fire in a good way. And Ada sat back on her heels in the cold wind with her heart hammering out a rhythm entirely unlike fear, and felt the shape of the next months rise in her mind the way a coastline rises out of fog when the light finally turns: not one atlas, not one set of notes, but a census. A counter-census. Every survivor along this wall, every name, every hour, every rooftop hatch and rescue and loss, collected the way she collected soundings—one careful fix at a time, until the drowned quarter stood fully mapped in testimony, and no official historian could print a single elegant lie without printing it beside a hundred sworn truths.

Elias Wren had asked her, in the shop, with his terrible gentle questions: *who counts you?*

She understood now. No one counted these people. So she would.

* * *

The bell rang at nine o'clock—the wrong hour, the adjusted hour—and the wall dissolved like sugar in rain. Lanterns shuttered, figures peeled away into the fog-bound lanes in every direction, the old woman's litany cut off mid-name, and Ada folded her sketchbook against her heart and moved with the current, hood down, head down, one more dockworker going home.

But at the foot of the Lantern Stair—its steps descending now into open water, the top third alone remaining above the tide like a broken tooth—a figure detached itself from the shadows and fell into step beside her, and she knew the walk before she saw the face: the careful, bureaucratic walk of a man who measured cities door to door for a living.

Elias Wren, out of uniform, in a plain fisherman's jersey two sizes wrong for him, glancing back over his shoulder the way amateurs do and professionals pretend not to.

Ada stopped walking. "If the Bureau sends men to memorials now—"

"The Bureau doesn't know I'm here." He kept his voice under the wind. "And neither does anyone else, and it needs to stay that way, so walk, please, and don't look at me like that." He waited until they were moving again, side by side, two silhouettes among many. Up close she could see he hadn't slept; there were gray crescents under his eyes, and his hands were shoved deep in his pockets as if to prevent them from acting independently.

"You shouldn't have come," Ada said. "This is exactly the kind of assembly your reports exist for."

"I know what kind of assembly it is." Something in his voice cracked along a fault line. "I stood at the top of the stair for twenty minutes watching them light the lamps, and I thought: I have counted eleven thousand people in this city since spring, and not one of them has ever once looked at the water. Not one. And these people can't stop looking." He exhaled, a long plume of breath torn away by the wind. "Sandoval. Your file moved."

The cold went through the oilskin as if it weren't there.

"Moved where?"

"Up. Out of the routine trays. There's a new designation—they call it *priority provenance review*—it means the Bureau of Records has taken an interest, and when Records takes an interest, they don't send census men anymore." He stopped at the mouth of the tanner's lane, where their paths must divide, and turned to face her fully, and she saw that beneath the exhaustion he was afraid, genuinely afraid, and fighting it the way the boy on the wall had fought his tears. "They've assigned an agent named Corbier. I've seen him work. He doesn't knock and offer coin the way the last one did. He starts with the servants and neighbors, and he works inward, and he is very, very good."

The lane was dark, the tide loud below them, the drowned quarter breathing its mineral breath over everything. Ada thought of the sketchbook against her ribs, the boy's printed line—*HER SON WAS THERE*—the eighty lanterns opening and closing along the wall like a promise.

"How long?" she asked.

Elias Wren looked back once, toward the seawall, toward all that scattered testimony dissolving into the fog, and when he answered, his voice was almost a whisper.

"He requests his warrant Thursday. If I were you, I would not sleep in your own bed Wednesday night."
