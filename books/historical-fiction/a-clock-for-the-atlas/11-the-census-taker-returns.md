## 11. The Census Taker Returns

The report had been on Elias Wren's desk for eleven days.

It sat beneath a stack of other documents—fuel allotment appeals, a dispute over a bakery's employee count, the usual sediment of the Winter Enumeration—and every morning he moved something on top of it, and every evening he uncovered it again and read the first line as if it might have changed.

*District Seven, Harbour Ward. Premises: map shop, late proprietor Corvane, M. Occupant: female apprentice, name unrecorded, papers not produced.*

He had written it himself in his careful census hand, the hand that had filled four thousand pages in nine years of service, and he could not make himself carry it down the corridor to Supervisor Brast's office. It was not a long walk. Forty steps. He had counted them once, at two in the morning, standing in his lodgings with his coat already on, telling himself he would deliver it before the office opened and be done with it. He had gotten as far as counting.

Nine years ago, when Elias was twenty-two and hungry for exactly this kind of steady work, a clerk from the Bureau of Records had come to his mother's tenement with a ledger and a list of questions, and Elias had answered them all at the door like an honest fool: yes, his brother Teodor lived here; no, Teo was not presently at home; yes, he worked at the print house on Cordwain Lane; yes, he had been there last week. The clerk had written everything down in a hand very much like Elias's own, thanked him, and gone. Six weeks later men came for Teo—not Bureau men, but they had come with a paper that bore a Bureau stamp, a paper compiled from answers given at doorways by people who did not understand what their honesty would buy. Teodor Wren was entered into no further record anywhere. His mother died asking about him. His brother became a man who counted other people for the government that had erased his own.

So Elias knew precisely what a flagged file meant. He had watched the mechanism work from the inside for nearly a decade, turning its cranks, feeding it its daily ration of names, telling himself each individual entry was too small to matter—that he was a raindrop, not a flood—and now here was one entry, one girl in a shuttered map shop who would not say her own surname, and his hands would not move it down the corridor.

* * *

"You're slow this winter, Wren." Brast stood in the doorway of Elias's cubicle without knocking, as always, a heavy man who wore his authority the way other men wore wet wool, uncomfortably and with visible effort. "Harbour Ward should have closed a fortnight ago."

"Harbour Ward is harbour ward," Elias said. "Half the doors don't open and the other half lie."

"Then you'll be glad of the amendment." Brast dropped a folder on the desk. It landed on top of the eleven-day-old report, which somehow made the situation worse, as though the two documents had found each other. "Cross-reference came down from Records. Any premises connected to the late Master Corvane are to be enumerated twice. Full detail. Papers verified, not just attested." He tapped the folder with one thick finger. "There's interest at a high level. You understand what high level means."

"It means I do the enumeration twice," Elias said.

"It means you do it *properly*," Brast said, and looked at him a moment longer than the sentence required. "Your draft on the apprentice. Where is it?"

Elias's face did what it had been trained by nine years of doorways to do: nothing. "In the pile. I submit completed wards in batches, sir. You know my method."

"Your method is why your ward is late." Brast grunted, turned, paused. "The Corvane file goes up separately. This week, Wren. There are people above me who've asked after it twice, and I don't enjoy being asked twice."

When he had gone, Elias sat looking at the folder. Inside was the cross-reference order, stamped with the seal of the Third Section—the section that did not enumerate anyone, that only consumed what enumerators gathered. He had never in nine years received an instruction directly bearing that seal. Raindrops did not get letters from the sea.

He understood then, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own name on a list, that the girl in the map shop was already being hunted, and that his report—the report he hadn't submitted—was the hound's collar. Somewhere above Brast, someone had pulled a thread, and the thread ran through Elias's careful hand, through his doorway questions, through the moment he had lingered too long over her ink-stained fingers and asked where she had learned her trade. He had thought he was being discreet. He had simply been early.

They would send agents to that shop within the fortnight whether or not he filed anything further. The flag existed independent of him now. Which meant—his mind turned this over slowly, the way you turn over a coin to check which side is showing—that his silence protected nothing, and his compliance protected less, and the only thing left in his power was the thing he had never done.

Elias Wren lied to his supervisor for the first time in nine years that afternoon, and it was a small lie, well made. He told Brast that the Corvane premises had been re-enumerated and the occupant was merely a day servant of no fixed surname, illiterate, transient, likely gone by spring—a person of no archival value whatsoever, hardly worth the Third Section's postage. He wrote it up in his best census hand. He dated it three days earlier than the true date. And when he carried it down the forty steps to Brast's office, his heart did not hammer; it was quiet, and terribly light, like a room after furniture has been removed.

* * *

The pretext he chose was fuel.

Every household in Harbour Ward owed a declaration of coal allotment under the Winter Provision Act, and no one in Harbour Ward believed this or obeyed it, which made it the perfect instrument: a question so tedious that no one asked why it was being asked twice. Elias took the duplicate forms, put them in his leather satchel alongside his lunch, and walked down through the city as the afternoon light went bronze on the wet stone, past the fish market where they were hauling the stalls in, past the customs house with its blind official windows, down to Chandler's Lane where the map shop stood with its shutters half-drawn and its sign—*Corvane, Cartographer*—weathered almost to illegibility.

She opened the door only as far as the chain allowed. When she saw who it was, the chain came off, but slowly, and she stood back from the doorway in a way that suggested the room behind her contained things she would rather he did not see. Perhaps it did. He was not there to look.

"I know it's irregular," Elias said, holding up the form. "Fuel declaration. The Bureau has decided Harbour Ward wasn't properly counted under the Provision Act, so we're—recounting."

"We were just counted," Ada Sandoval said. She did not invite him in. Her eyes went past his shoulder to the street, both directions, an instinct he recognized because he had seen it in people who had something to hide and also in people who had something to fear, and the two categories overlapped more than the Bureau's manuals admitted. "You were just here."

"The government is capable of visiting a citizen more than once," Elias said. "It's one of our defining features."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if she could afford one. She stepped back and let him in.

The shop smelled as it had before, of ink and linseed and dust burning quietly off the lamp, but different too—there was a new tension in it, a tidiness that was itself suspicious. Shelves straightened. Drawers closed. A room prepared for inspection, or recently inspected, or braced for it. He did not look around. He had learned that the kindest thing you could do in another person's home, when you came to it wearing the Bureau's coat, was to keep your eyes on your paperwork.

"You'll want the form back signed," she said.

"In theory." He set it on the counter and did not pick it up again. Outside, a gull screamed somewhere over the water, and the lamp between them fluttered in a draught from the badly fitted door. He heard himself speaking before he had fully decided to, the words arriving the way testimony arrives, reluctantly and all at once. "Miss Sandoval. I'm going to say something I shouldn't, and you're going to want to interrupt me, and I'd be grateful if you didn't, because I'm only going to be able to say it once."

Her stillness was total. Even the gull seemed to hold off.

"Your file has been flagged. Not the census file—the other kind. The kind that gets a red string around it and moves to a different building. There's a cross-reference order out of the Third Section on anything connected to Master Corvane: papers, plates, correspondence, instruments. They'll come here. Probably soon, probably not gently, and the man who comes won't ask about fuel." He kept his voice level, the voice he used for delivering bad numbers to bad offices. "I told my supervisor you were a transient servant with no surname and no value. That will buy you days, not weeks. Days. I don't know how many."

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it, not with fear—he thought—but with the outrage of someone who had done everything right, all the invisible things, for years. "Why now? What did I do?"

"Nothing." And then, because lying to her felt suddenly obscene after the clean relief of lying to Brast: "Not nothing. Him. Corvane. There's interest at a high level in whatever he left behind. I don't know what they think he had. I know what happens to premises when the Third Section takes an interest, and it isn't a search, it's a stripping." He hesitated, and then finished it, because half a warning was a cruelty dressed as mercy. "And there's a sketch being circulated. Of 'the Corvane apprentice.' It's approximate. But approximate is enough in this city, miss. Approximate hangs people."

For a long moment she said nothing. He watched her take it the way he had watched hundreds of people take bad news across a doorway—as though the information were a weight being loaded onto a cart, and you could see the axle take it, see the whole structure settle and redistribute. She was frightened. She was also, he realized with a small cold shock, calculating. Something behind her eyes was already moving crates.

"The shop," she said finally. "Is there anything—I don't know—procedure. Notice. Some way of knowing the day."

"No notice. No procedure worth the word." He picked up the fuel form, took out his pencil, and filled it in himself in a fair imitation of a hurried clerk: occupant unnamed, premises marginal, no anomalies. Falsehoods in his own hand, second lie of the day, easier than the first. "This says you're nobody. Keep it. If a man comes who isn't me, show it and be stupid. Stupidity is the one citizenship everyone respects."

"Why are you doing this?" she asked.

It was the question he had walked forty steps unable to answer at two in the morning, and he found he still could not, not honestly, not in any shape that would fit through the space between them. *Because my brother's name is written nowhere.* Because nine years of raindrops. Because her hands had ink on them the way Teo's hands had always had ink on them, and because he had asked her where she learned her trade in the voice of a man writing a list, and lists, in this country, were how people vanished.

"My job," he said, "is to count people accurately. I've begun to suspect I've never once done it." He buttoned his coat. "There's a widow at the harbour, Marta Quill, keeps the boatyard at the far end of Pier Six. If things move faster than I can warn you—if you need a door that opens at night—she's a door." He had not planned to say that either. It had come up from someplace below the planning.

"How do you know about Marta Quill?" she asked sharply.

"Madam, I count houses for a living," Elias said. "I know whose doors don't open." He moved to the door, then stopped with his hand on the latch, and made himself turn around and look at her properly—one look, direct, the look he gave no one, because giving it cost something. "You shouldn't trust me. I want to be clear about that. I've spent nine years being the thing that knocks on doors, and what I've told you tonight doesn't undo a page of it. Trust the warning, not the man."

"And if I need the man?" she said quietly.

He stood with the latch cold under his fingers and the lamp throwing his shadow long across her counter, across her maps, across the careful invisible life he had just helped condemn and could not now stop trying to save.

"Then knock," he said, "and hope I'm the one who answers."

Outside, the fog was coming off the harbour in earnest, swallowing the pier lights one by one, and Elias Wren walked up Chandler's Lane with an empty satchel and a heart that felt scoured out and strangely unburdened at once. At the top of the lane he paused, took out his notebook—the official one, the census book, nine years of faithful entries—and on the margin of tomorrow's blank page he wrote, small, in his precise clerk's hand, a single line he would never submit to anyone:

*District Seven, Harbour Ward: one resident unrecorded. Correction pending.*

Then he closed the book and went home through the fog, a liar now, officially and forever, and found that the title sat on him lighter than the truth ever had.
