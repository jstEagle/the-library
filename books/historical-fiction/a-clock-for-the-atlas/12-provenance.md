## 12. Provenance

The garret smelled of tallow smoke, cold tea, and old paper—the last of these so thickly that Ada thought she could taste it, a flavor like dust and iron and time. Odile Ferrant's room at the top of a house on Candlewick Lane was barely larger than the map shop's storeroom, and far less tidy. Every surface had vanished beneath strata of documents: ledgers fanned open like sleeping birds, bundles of correspondence tied with string gone soft as cloth, loose folios stacked in towers that leaned toward one another as if conferring in secret.

In the center of it all, on a table cleared by force the night before, lay the half-burned survey notes of Master Aurel Corvane, spread out on a clean linen cloth like relics on an altar.

"Sit," Odile said. She herself remained standing, and Ada noticed for the first time how deliberately she stood—spine straight despite the drink, hands clasped at her back like a woman inspecting troops or delivering a verdict. Whatever ruin the Bureau had made of her life, it had not touched her posture. "First lesson. Before you touch anything, you wash your hands. Not because of dirt." Her mouth twitched. "Because of respect. A document can outlive everyone who ever loved its maker. You are the newest in a long line of hands. Behave like it."

Ada went to the basin in the corner and washed, drying her fingers on the cleanest corner of towel available, which was not very clean. When she returned, Odile was looking down at the notes with an expression Ada could not read—something between hunger and grief.

"Now. You believe these prove the levee was cut from the landward side."

"Yes."

"Wrong. These papers prove nothing. They are eight fragments of charred paper bearing numbers, dates, and initials, found in a locked drawer belonging to a dead man who cannot be asked to explain them. To any magistrate in Vellamare, they are kindling. Interesting kindling." She tapped the linen cloth with one finger. "The truth is not in the paper, girl. The truth is in the *trail*. The paper is only the last footprint of many. Your work—our work—is to find all the other footprints and lay them side by side until no honest person can walk anywhere else. That is provenance. The word means where a thing comes from. It also means, if we do this properly, *where the truth comes from*. And truth with a documented origin is a weapon no ministry can melt down."

Ada sat down slowly on the one other stool. Outside the narrow window, gulls argued over something on a rooftop, and the harbor bells rang the half hour—a sound she had heard ten thousand times and never listened to until now.

"Begin," Odile said. "Tell me what you see. Not what you feel. What you *see*."

Ada bent over the largest fragment. The charred edge crumbled slightly even under her careful gaze, and Odile snapped, "Eyes, not fingers," before she could touch it. She looked, then. Really looked, the way she looked at coastlines: patiently, letting the lines speak first.

"Watermark," she said at last. "Upper left corner, where it didn't burn. A bridge over three waves. That's the Vellamare mill stock—the paper from the Granary Street mills. Expensive stock. Corvane used it for final drafts only; his field notebooks were cheap rag paper."

Odile's eyebrows rose a fraction, which Ada was learning to read as applause. "Good. Continue."

"The ink has oxidized differently across the sheet. The survey figures themselves are iron-gall—one brown, consistent. But the marginal notation—" Ada leaned closer without touching— "*L-side cut, ref. pier nine* —that's a different hand. Smaller. And the ink is blacker, fresher, though still old."

"Whose hand?"

"Corvane's. But later. Years later, I'd say. He annotated his own older work." Something turned over in Ada's chest as she said it. "He came back to these notes. After the flood, he went back to what he'd measured before it, and he wrote on them in secret, and he hid them, and someone tried to burn them, and he saved them anyway." Her voice thinned. "I inked coastlines beside that man for four years and I never knew."

"You knew him well enough to identify two separate inks in his hand across a decade of work," Odile said dryly. "Most wives couldn't do that. Now." She pulled a stool over and lowered herself onto it with the care of a woman managing chronic pain, then drew from beneath a stack of invoices a single sheet covered in her own cramped, precise script—a list, Ada saw, numbered down the margin. "Here is what an archive requires to authenticate a document. Memorize it; I will quiz you at unpleasant hours. One: material witness—that is, the object itself, its paper, ink, watermarks, repairs, everything physical. Two: chain of custody—who held it, when, and how we know. Three: corroboration—independent records that agree with it. Four: witnesses—living persons who can speak to its creation or contents. Five—and this is the one your Bureau friends rely on and we must destroy—absence of reasonable doubt. Note that phrase. *Reasonable*. Not absence of doubt. Doubt is infinite and free; any magistrate paid enough can manufacture it forever. We do not fight doubt. We make lying about our documents more expensive than confirming them."

"That sounds cynical for a truth-teller."

"Truth-telling is a trade like any other," Odile said. "Learn the economics or be eaten by them. Now—the chain of custody. Walk me through it. Where did Corvane get these papers?"

Ada frowned. "You know already. Why ask me?"

"Because the magistrates will not ask you questions you know the answers to. They will ask the ones you don't. Begin."

Ada closed her eyes and summoned the shop—not as she lived in it now, shuttered and silent, but as it had been, lamplit, smelling of sizing and cedar, the master hunched over his desk with his spectacles pushed up into wild gray hair.

"He commissioned the survey himself. Before the flood—I remember because he told me once that he'd surveyed Saltmarsh twice, once for the harbor commission in 'sixty-one and again on his own account in 'sixty-three. He kept the commission receipts in the green box with the tax papers. I've seen them. I filed them myself after he died—they're still there, in the green box, third drawer of the flat cabinet."

"Receipts for a survey commission dated before the flood," Odile said, and there was something fierce and quiet in her voice, "establishing that Corvane measured those levees, those streets, those tide heights, *while they still existed*. Which makes these fragments not opinions but comparisons. Do you see? A man cannot falsify a past he accurately recorded before the fact. If the pre-flood survey matches the post-flood analysis, the mathematics alone convict somebody. Water finds its own level, girl. So does arithmetic." She pulled another sheet toward her. "Now. Between the survey and the burning, who touched these notes?"

"Corvane."

"Dead men make poor witnesses. Who else?"

Ada thought. The shop had never been busy—mapmaking did not invite foot traffic—but there had been visitors. Deliveries. "There were apprentices before me. Two that I know of; both left before I came. There's a boy who runs errands—Pilo, from Tanner's Row. And the man who supplied his drafting papers. And—" She stopped.

"And?"

"Marta Quill." The name felt strange in her mouth, half-owned already. "She moved things for him. Packages, once or twice. He never said what. I assumed—"

"Assume nothing. Record everything. Did she handle these?" Odile tapped the fragments.

"I don't know."

"Then find out. If she carried the wrapped bundle from the shop to wherever he kept it, she is a custody link. If she saw the wrapping, the seal, the date—even better. A living witness with no stake in the maps is worth more than a dozen documents, because the Bureau can seize documents but they cannot unsee through a widow's eyes." Odile leaned back, and the lamplight caught the deep lines around her mouth. "Which reminds me. Your master's will. Have you got it?"

"There wasn't one. Or none filed. He always said he'd write one when the atlas was done." Ada's throat tightened. "It never got done. Until I finish it."

"So the shop's contents pass by intestacy—which means the state can claim them whenever it likes, which explains why they haven't yet. They're waiting. They want to know what's in the drawers before they inventory them publicly. Clever of them." Odile made a note on her sheet. "We need the green box receipts copied by a notary before the Bureau inventories the shop. Once an official inventory exists, every document in that shop becomes state property and we become thieves for holding copies. Do it within the week. Pay the notary whatever he asks and tell him nothing except that it's estate paperwork. Boredom is the best disguise; nobody searches a boring thing."

* * *

They worked until the candle had burned down to a stub and Odile's hands began their fine tremor—the shaking came in the evenings, Ada had noticed, along with the craving she managed by chewing dried ginger root and swearing at intervals.

The second lesson was corroboration, and it sent Ada down into the garret's own buried layers while Odile supervised from her stool like a general too ill to stand but too stubborn to leave the field. Tide tables from the harbor office, 'sixty-one through 'seventy. Rainfall records from the customs almanac. An engineer's pamphlet on levee construction, published two years before the flood, which stated plainly—Odile read it aloud in a voice like gravel poured slow—that the Saltmarsh levees had been built landward-thick against storm surge from the sea, and that no plausible ocean event could breach them from the waterside.

"The official history will say storm surge," Odile predicted. "Or rebel sabotage with seaward charges. Either way, this pamphlet kills both stories, because either story requires water to flow uphill. Find me three more documents like this and I'll authenticate your atlas in my sleep."

They laid the trail out on the linen cloth like a map taking shape: the commission receipts (to fetch), the pre-flood survey (the fragments), the engineer's pamphlet (to buy three copies), the tide tables (to copy), the marginal annotations in Corvane's later hand (to date—Odile believed she could, by comparing the ink's oxidation against dated samples from her own hoard). Piece by piece, fragment by fragment, the trail assembled itself, and Ada watched it happen with a feeling she had not expected and did not immediately recognize.

It took her a moment to name it. Hope. Not the wild kind—she had known that kind briefly, the night she found the drawer, hope like a struck match in a powder store. This was slower. Structural. Hope the way a map was accurate: not because any single line was true beyond argument, but because a thousand small checked details agreed with one another, and agreement, multiplied, became something no single lie could outweigh.

"It's like ruling a chart," she said aloud, surprised.

"What is?"

"This. All of it. You start with one verified point—a sounding, a bearing—and you work outward. Each new line has to agree with everything before it or you've made an error somewhere. You never trust any one mark absolutely. You trust the pattern of marks agreeing." She looked up. "That's what you're doing with the documents."

Odile studied her for a long moment. "Eleven years I ran the state archive," she said finally. "I trained forty clerks. Three of them ever understood that sentence without six months of beating it into them." She reached beneath a folio and produced, of all things, a plate of cold sausage and bread, pushing it across the table. "Eat. You think badly hungry, and tomorrow you'll need to think like a lawyer."

Ada ate. The bread was stale but real, and eating in the lamplight across from another human being—another *ally*, the word still foreign in her mind—did something quiet to the knot she had carried since the drawer opened.

"One more thing tonight," Odile said. She drew out the last item: a folded paper much handled, soft at the creases. "Your missing link. The reason these fragments can't simply be dismissed as forgeries of our own making. Look at the signature block."

Ada unfolded it. It was a receipt—no, a declaration, written in a round clerk's hand, dated the eleventh of March, 1864. *Received of Master Aurel Corvane, cartographer, one sealed packet for storage at Pier Nine lockhouse, contents unknown to bearer.* Below it, a signature: **Tammas Osei, lockkeeper**.

Pier Nine. The lockhouse at the head of the Saltmarsh sluices. The locks that had failed—or been failed—the night the quarter drowned.

"He stored them *there*," Ada breathed. "At the locks. Right where—"

"Where the crime happened, yes. Whether that was Corvane's irony or his cunning, I can't say. Possibly both; clever men enjoy their jokes even in mortal danger. What matters is this: the lockkeeper signed that he received a sealed packet from Corvane eleven months before the flood, and if the packet we have matches the seal described—" Odile pointed to a line of the declaration, and Ada read it: *wax, black, impressed with a compass-rose device*—and if the lockkeeper will swear to it in public, then your burned fragments cease to be ambiguous scraps and become a documented deposit, hidden by a man who feared exactly what eventually killed him."

Ada stared at the signature. The ink had faded to a pale sepia, but the strokes were firm, unhurried, the T crossed with a plain decisive bar. A workingman's hand. Steady.

"Does he still live?"

"He lives," Odile said. "I made inquiries before you came. Tammas Osei kept the Pier Nine lockhouse until the reconstruction authority dissolved the post—no quarter, no locks, no position. Since then he's worked the grain wharves. Lives alone. Speaks to no one about the flood; the neighbors call him the Quiet Man." She paused. "He has been silent for ten years, girl. Men who stay silent that long have usually seen precisely the thing everyone else is trying not to know. Sometimes silence isn't cowardice. Sometimes it's evidence waiting for a safe place to be deposited."

"Will he sign again?" Ada asked. "For us? In public?"

Odile snuffed the guttering candle stub and lit a fresh one, and in the new light her face was unreadable and old and, somehow, not unkind.

"That," she said, "is not a question archives can answer. That one you'll have to go and ask him yourself."

* * *

Ada walked home through streets gone blue with the hour before midnight, and the city felt different around her. It had always been lines to her—streets as strokes of ink, districts as shapes to be rendered fairly or unfairly depending on scale. Tonight it was a document. Every wall a page. Every witness walking home from the taverns a signature waiting, patient and pale, in fading ink.

She passed the mouth of Saltmarsh Road without stopping, but she felt the drowned quarter below the seawall the way one feels a bruise—present, pressure-sensitive, hers now in some way she hadn't chosen and couldn't refuse.

Tomorrow: the green box, the receipts, the notary. Then the grain wharves, and the Quiet Man, and the question that could not be researched or ruled or drawn—only asked.

She climbed the stairs to the shuttered shop, let herself in with the key she wore against her heart, and stood a moment in the dark among her master's instruments. Somewhere in that silence, she thought, a sealed packet had once left these rooms in a courier's arms, bound for the locks, carrying the truth ahead of the water like a man running before a tide.

*Hold on,* she told the dark, to the drawer, to the dead, to the living stranger whose name she now spoke aloud for the first time, practicing it softly, like a bearing she meant to follow.

"Tammas Osei."
