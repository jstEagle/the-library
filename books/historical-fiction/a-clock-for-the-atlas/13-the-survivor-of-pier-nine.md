## 13. The Survivor of Pier Nine

The lockkeeper's cottage sat at the end of the Eelwater cut, where the canal gates met the sea and the whole mechanism of Vellamare's harbor breathed in and out with the tide. Ada had walked past it twice already that morning, telling herself she was only studying the locks—the great black timbers of the gates, the chains furred with barnacle, the spillway running silver over its sluice—and not working up the courage to knock.

She had learned this much about Tam Osei in three days of asking: that he kept the Eelwater gates alone, that he drank little and spoke less, that he had held his post through every regime of the last twenty years because lockkeepers, like lighthouse men and gravediggers, were beneath politics. And one thing more, whispered to her by a fishwife who would say nothing else on a public street: *He was at Saltmarsh the night it went under. He was the one who opened the relief channel, or didn't—depends who tells it. Either way he came out dry and half the parish didn't.*

That was why she was here. Not for the rumor—rumors were salt water, they dissolved into everything and proved nothing—but because Master Corvane's notes named him. In the margin of a survey sheet dated four days before the flood, in her master's small exact hand: *T.O., lockkeeper, Pier Nine — saw the levee party land. Will not sign.*

Will not sign. Ten years old, that sentence, and yet it had waited for her like a door left ajar.

She crossed the shingle to the cottage and knocked.

The man who opened it was fifty or older, though the sea had bleached him strangely—young hands, old eyes, skin the color of weathered rope. He looked at her the way lockkeepers look at weather: assessing what she might do to him, and whether he could survive it.

"Girl," he said. "You've walked my seawall twice."

"I need to talk to you about the night of the ninth of Floodmonth."

For a long moment he said nothing. Gulls screamed somewhere behind her, wheeling over the cut. Then he stepped back from the doorway—not an invitation exactly, more a surrender to inevitability—and let her in.

* * *

The inside of the cottage was immaculate the way only lonely men's houses are immaculate: everything in its place because there was no one to move it. A stove, black and polished. A table scrubbed white. On the wall, a row of brass tide gauges, each labeled in a careful hand, and beneath them—a detail that caught at Ada's chest—a single framed chart of the harbor, drawn in the style of Corvane's shop. Drawn, she realized leaning close, by Corvane himself. Her master's compass rose, unmistakable, its needle tipped with a fleur so fine you needed good light to see it.

"You knew him," she said.

"He drew that for me the year I took the post." Osei set a kettle on the stove without asking if she wanted tea; it seemed to be simply what happened when someone entered this house. "Paid me in charts when I couldn't pay him. Sat at that table there and talked about Saltmarsh like it was a living thing. Like the streets were veins and the water owed them something." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I thought he was mad. Then the ninth of Floodmonth came, and I stopped thinking anything at all."

Ada sat across from him and laid nothing on the table. No papers, no proof. She had learned that much already: evidence frightened people; questions could be answered or refused, but evidence made you complicit the moment your eyes touched it.

"My name is Ada," she said. "I was his apprentice. I'm finishing his atlas."

The kettle began its low gathering note toward a boil. Osei watched it as if it might tell him something.

"There is no finishing a dead man's atlas," he said. "There's only carrying it until it's too heavy, and then putting it down somewhere, and then some other fool picks it up."

"That may be. But I need you to tell me what you saw at the levee."

"And then what?" He turned, and his eyes were terrible in their plainness—no drama in them, just arithmetic. "You write it down. You show it to someone. Someone shows it to someone else. And one morning soldiers come to a lockkeeper's cottage at the end of the Eelwater cut, and they ask me why I'm telling stories, and they take me out on the water at slack tide, and that's the last anyone hears of Tam Osei. That's not cowardice, girl. That's memory. I've watched how this city treats people who remember."

"I know how it treats them." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "I've spent ten years being nobody on purpose. No name spoken aloud. No papers. I hid inside my own life like a coin dropped down a well. So don't tell me what fear costs—I've paid the rent on it since I was eleven years old."

The kettle boiled. He poured. Steam rose between them, and for a while neither touched their cups.

* * *

"The water came from the landward side."

He said it suddenly, the way a man pulls a splinter—fast, before his nerve can fail. He did not look at her. He looked at his own hands, wrapped around the chipped blue cup, as though the hands were the ones confessing.

"Everyone says storm surge. Everyone says the sea broke in and drowned the quarter, poor Saltmarsh, cruel weather. But I keep the gates. I have kept gates since I was fifteen years old, and I know what water does, and water does not come uphill. The flood channels ran backward that night. The sluices at the Marsh Road culvert were open when the tide was still two hours from full. Somebody opened them from the culvert house, and somebody cut the levee at the old brickworks bend—cut it, spaded and shored, the way you'd dig a field drain. The breach faces were clean. I saw them at first light, before the water even settled. Clean cuts, both sides."

Ada's pulse filled her ears. This matched the notes. This matched the burn-shadowed survey sheets hidden now beneath Marta Quill's floorboards, the measurements proving the breach profile was worked, engineered, deliberate. But hearing it in a human voice, in a kitchen that smelled of tea and lamp oil, was another order of truth entirely. Maps argued. Men testified.

"You saw them do it?"

"I saw the boat." His voice had gone flat, reciting, a man reading from a ledger he'd memorized against his will. "Nine o'clock, maybe half past. Too dark to count heads—six, seven men, no lanterns, which is itself an answer, isn't it? Working parties carry lanterns. Saboteurs don't. They landed at Pier Nine, right below my gatehouse, and one of them called up to me—friendly as Sunday—said the Bureau had ordered emergency sluice maintenance ahead of the storm and I was to stay inside my cottage until morning on pain of arrest." He laughed once, a sound with no humor in it anywhere, like a plank breaking. "And I did. God help me, I went inside, and I barred the door, and I sat on my bed with my hands over my ears while a district drowned."

"The water would have taken you too, if you'd interfered."

"Yes." He set the cup down with exaggerated care, as though it were loaded. "That is precisely what I tell myself. Every night for ten years, that is what I tell myself. Some nights it even helps."

Outside, a barge groaned through the outer gates, and the floor of the cottage trembled faintly with the turning of the screws. Ada waited until it passed.

"Master Corvane came to you. Afterward."

"Four days after. He stood where you're standing and asked me to sign a statement. Said he was building a record that no ministry could bury, because it would be buried in ten thousand copies." Osei's gaze drifted to the chart on the wall, to the fine compass rose. "I told him no. I told him I had a post, a pension, a roof. He didn't argue. That was the worst of it—he just nodded, like a man confirming a measurement he'd expected, and he wrote something in his book, and he said, 'Then live long, Tam, and keep the gates. Perhaps one day someone will come to whom silence costs more than speech.'" He looked at her then, really looked, and Ada felt seen down to the watermark of herself. "I always wondered who that would be. I didn't think it would be a girl who walks like she's trying not to disturb the air."

Her face warmed. "I'm not asking you to march anywhere. Or sign anything today. I'm asking—" and here she found the true shape of it, the thing she had carried up the seawall path and along the cut without knowing she was carrying it, "—I'm asking you to stop pretending you saw nothing. To me. Just to me, for now. Because I have his notes, and the notes say what you say, and notes can be called forged, and forged things can be burned. But a living man who stood at Pier Nine cannot be unmade by any ministry in this country. Not entirely. Not if he chooses to exist."

Osei was quiet for a long time. Through the window behind him, Ada could see the outer gates standing open to the gray morning sea, the tide just beginning its long patient pull back in, filling the channels, undoing the ebb the way it did twice a day, every day, without permission from anyone.

"There's a woman," he said at last. "Brann's widow, used to sell mussels off the Saltmarsh quay. She comes by my gate some evenings and we don't talk about it, either of us, and it's the closest thing I have to company. If I speak—if this ever becomes noise—she's the kind of person who pays for it. Not me. Me they'll drown quietly. Her they'll make an example of." His jaw worked. "I'm not refusing you, girl. Understand that. I'm telling you what I'm carrying, so you know the weight of what you're asking me to lift."

"I understand," Ada said softly. And she did—it was the same arithmetic she ran in the dark hours, Marta's face, Odile's shaking hands, Elias Wren's careful lies. Every truth she wanted printed had a price column, and the prices were people.

"Come back," Osei said. He rose, and the interview was plainly over; he was already reaching for his oilcan and his coat, resuming the shape of a man whose job was to stand between the sea and the city and say nothing about either. "When you're ready—when it's real, when there's paper and witnesses and whatever it is you're building—come back. I'll give you my answer then. It may be yes." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "It may be that a man can only hold a thing like this for ten years before his arms give out anyway."

Ada stood, and hesitated, and then did the thing she had decided on the walk out: she took Corvane's chart down from the wall—his chart, given freely years ago—and laid it on the table, and beside it a smaller sheet from her satchel: a fresh-drawn chart of the harbor, her own hand, her own compass rose copied faithfully from her master's, but signed, small and clear, in the corner. *A. Sandoval.*

"A gift for the gatehouse," she said. "Mine has a name on it. When you decide, decide knowing who's asking."

She let herself out before he could answer, because she had learned that from him, or from the sea, or from ten years of hiding: some doors must be left open behind you, or a person cannot breathe.

* * *

The walk back along the cut was long and the wind was rising, pushing at her back like a hand impatient with her pace. Ada pulled her collar up and let her mind do what it always did under pressure—draw. She mapped the conversation as she walked, fixing landmarks: *saw the boat, six or seven men, no lanterns; sluices opened from the culvert house two hours before high water; clean-cut breach faces at the brickworks bend.* Three facts, each independently checkable against the survey notes. Corroboration. Not a signature yet, but a skeleton of testimony, and skeletons could be clothed.

Odile would want it written down tonight, in the proper form, dated and witnessed. Marta would grumble that Ada had gone out alone again and done something reckless with her face. And Elias—

She stopped walking.

Because she had noticed, halfway down the seawall stairs that morning, a man reading a shipping notice near the fish market, and had thought nothing of it. And again now, a figure at the far end of the cut, hat low, making no move at all except to be exactly where a walking man would pass him in twenty minutes' time.

Coincidence dressed itself in many coats. But Vellamare's wind was at her back, and hers had been the only footsteps on the seawall path, and the figure at the end of the cut had begun, very quietly, to walk.
