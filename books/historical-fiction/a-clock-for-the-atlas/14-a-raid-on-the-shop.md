## 14. A Raid on the Shop

The knock came at half past six in the morning, when the light through the shutters was still the color of pewter and Ada had been awake all night inking the estuary channels of a coast that no longer existed.

She knew before she opened the door. Not because anyone had warned her—no one had—but because for eleven days she had lived with the survey notes folded into oilcloth beneath her mattress, and a body learns to hear danger the way sailors learn to hear weather. The knock was not a customer's knock. Customers knocked twice, apologetically, the way people do when they suspect a shop might be dead. This was four blows, evenly spaced, struck by someone who had never in his life waited to be admitted anywhere.

Ada stood very still in the middle of the workroom, one hand still wet with ink.

They would come in whether she opened the door or not. She had understood that within a second of the first blow—the sound of hinges being tested, the murmur of more than one voice on the step. So she did what she had trained herself to do for ten years, the discipline Master Corvane had drilled into her without ever naming it: she made the room lie.

The false bottom of the locked drawer had held nothing but blank paper since she'd moved the notes—she had learned that much caution already—but there were other things to think of. Her own sketchbook lay open on the drafting table, filled with practice copies of Saltmarsh's streets, each one labeled in her hand. She closed it and slid it behind the folios of ordinary harbor charts, where its spine would look like every other spine. She banked the stove with the day's failed proofs, watched the edges curl and blacken, and set the kettle on top so the room would smell of nothing but morning tea.

Then she wiped her hands, tucked a loose strand of hair under her cap, and opened the door as if she had been asleep until this moment and mildly resented being woken.

There were five of them. Four wore the grey coats of the Bureau of Records, and the fifth—a soft-bodied man with rings on his fingers and the careful smile of someone who enjoyed other people's misfortunes at a comfortable distance—wore good black wool and carried himself like an inspector of vineyards. He produced a document without being asked, unfolded it, and held it up as though Ada could read it from arm's length, which she could, because she had drawn documents like it herself, once, in a fairer life.

"Official requisition," he said. "Bureau of Records, Third Section. We're instructed to inventory and secure the professional effects of the late Master Aldous Corvane, cartographer, deceased." He pronounced *deceased* with relish, as if the man had died specifically to inconvenience him. "You are?"

"The apprentice."

"Name?"

"Corvane took me in off the parish rolls," Ada said. "He never used my name. In the shop ledger I'm entered as 'the girl, wages quarterly.'" That much was true, and truth was the best mortar for a wall built quickly. She let her accent flatten itself, let her shoulders round into the stoop of someone who scrubbed floors and fetched glue and was not worth remembering. "Am I in trouble, sir? He owed rent, if that's it. I've been trying to sell the stock, honest I have, but nobody wants charts of a drowned quarter—"

"We'll want the ledgers too," the man said, already moving past her into the shop, his boots leaving prints on her clean floor. "All of them. And you'll stand there and answer questions while we work, and you'll touch nothing. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

* * *

They were thorough. She had expected vandalism—stories about Bureau raids always involved broken glass and drawers pulled out and hurled across rooms—and there was some of that, a young agent with a pocked face who seemed to take personal offense at the neatness of the ink drawers and emptied three of them onto the table to sort through like a farmer winnowing grain. But the ringed man, whose name she gathered was Vasseur, worked differently. He worked like a cataloguer. He lifted each finished plate from the press cabinet with cotton gloves and held it to the window light, and when he found the ones he wanted—and he found them almost immediately, because she had made no secret of the atlas plates themselves, had told herself there was safety in their being known—he laid them out in a row along the long table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

Saltmarsh, in copper. Eleven plates, each one the product of months of Master Corvane's patient engraving: the streets rendered street by street, house by house, the pier lines, the old lock gates, the depth soundings taken by lamplight in the years before the water came. Each plate bore, cut deep into the lower corner where no casual buyer would look, the tiny cipher Corvane had used instead of a signature—a compass rose no bigger than a thumbnail, its north needle reversed.

Vasseur did not notice the ciphers. Or he noticed and did not understand them. Ada watched him hold plate seven to the light—the one showing Pier Nine and the lockkeeper's stair—and saw his mouth move as he read the title cartouche aloud to one of the others, and heard him laugh.

"'Surveyed 1862–1864,'" he repeated. "Sentimental old fool. He mapped it like it was going to come back."

"It's all he worked on at the end," Ada said, keeping her voice dull. "Went a bit strange after the flood. Used to stand at the seawall for hours. Everyone knew."

"Mm." Vasseur set the plate down. "And these are all the plates? All of them? No duplicates, no copies, nothing sent out to a printer?"

"All here. Who'd he send anything to? He hadn't sold a chart in two years."

That was a gamble. She watched it land—watched Vasseur glance at the dusty front window, the unlit lamp, the ledger with its thin columns of sales—and saw him decide that the shop matched the story. People believed what the room told them. It was the first law of mapmaking and, apparently, of interrogation both.

But then he turned to the desk.

Ada's heart performed a slow, cold revolution against her ribs.

The locked drawer was still locked. She had relocked it after moving the notes, had even—on Odile Ferrant's advice, delivered over Marta Quill's kitchen table with a bottle of rough red wine open between them—"improved" it: inside, now, sat a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth exactly as the real notes had been wrapped, tied with the same twine, and sealed around the edges with candle wax dripped and smudged to look aged. Inside the wrapping were not forgeries, precisely. Odile had been very clear on the distinction. They were genuine papers—old working drafts of Corvane's coastal charts, harmless, valueless—aged further with tea and iron-gall washes until they looked burned at the corners, browned, fragile. A decoy built to be found and to satisfy.

"He kept a locked drawer," Ada said before Vasseur asked. "I've never had the key. He was particular. If you break it, mind you don't cut yourself, the wood splinters."

Vasseur smiled at her, the smile of a man who enjoys being lied to competently, and put a small pry-bar under the drawer's lip.

The wood cracked. The drawer came out entire, spilled its single occupant onto the table, and the oilcloth wrapping split along one waxed seam as it landed.

Ada made herself breathe.

Vasseur unwrapped it slowly, savoring it, gloved fingers peeling back cloth the way a child peels an orange. Underneath, the tea-darkened drafts fanned out—coastlines, soundings, a half-finished inset of the harbor bar. Burned-looking at the corners. Browned. Convincing, she prayed, convincing.

For a long moment Vasseur said nothing. One of the younger agents leaned in over his shoulder. The pocked-faced one drifted closer too, and the whole room seemed to lean with him, and Ada stood with her hands folded at her apron like a servant waiting to be dismissed while her entire future was weighed in another man's hands.

Then Vasseur grunted.

"Working drafts," he said, disappointed. He flipped through them with less care than before, and Ada watched iron-gall stain transfer faintly to his white glove and thought, absurdly, *that will never wash out.* "Water-damaged, fire-licked, worthless. This is what he hid?" He laughed shortly. "Cartographers. You'd think he was guarding state secrets." He tossed the bundle back into the broken drawer. "Pack it anyway. Everything paper goes. Plates, ledgers, drawers, that folio rack—inventory it all, seal it, transport to the Third Section annex. Mark it *effects of deceased person, low priority.*"

Low priority. She turned the phrase over like a coin found on the ground, examining it for traps, and found none—or none she could see. They believed they had already found what they came for. The atlas plates, the master engravings, the visible body of the work: seized, catalogued, gone by eight o'clock. What remained hidden was, in their arithmetic, nothing at all.

They did not know about the notes at all, she realized then, with a vertigo that made the morning light swim. Vasseur had come for *effects*. The requisition said *effects of the late Master Aldous Corvane*, not *survey notes of the levee commission*. Whoever had sent him knew Corvane had mapped Saltmarsh; they did not yet know—or had not confirmed—that he had documented *why* it drowned. The hunt Odile had predicted, the winter-long buying-up of private papers, had reached her shop on rumor and suspicion rather than certainty.

Which meant somewhere in the Bureau there was a file with her master's name on it, growing thicker, and somewhere in that file, perhaps, there was room for a line item not yet written: *apprentice, unnamed.*

They took the plates. She made herself watch each one leave—made herself memorize instead of mourn, counting them out the door the way you count waves in a storm so the storm cannot make you mad. Eleven copper plates, wrapped in felt, carried out under the arms of men who did not know what they were carrying. Eleven arguments about who belonged in Vellamare, confiscated before they could be spoken aloud. Her master's last decade of hands-work, gone into a government crate stenciled *LOW PRIORITY*, and the crate nailed shut in the street outside while a neighbor's dog barked at it and the milk cart went by jingling, and the city conducted its ordinary morning over the muffled theft of its own history.

At the door, Vasseur paused. He looked back at her standing among the wreckage of the workroom—drawers gutted, folio rack empty, the smell of burnt proofs still in the cold stove—and something moved behind his eyes that was not quite suspicion. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic.

"You'll remain available," he said. "If further materials surface. If correspondence arrives. If anyone comes asking after the master's papers." He let that sit a moment longer than it needed to. "You understand that unusual activity at these premises would be reported. To us. Promptly."

"I don't talk to anyone," Ada said. "Nobody talks to me."

"No," Vasseur agreed, glancing once around the bare room. "I don't imagine they do."

* * *

The door closed. The lock did not turn behind them—they had not bothered, which was its own insult—but she heard their wagon roll away up Rope Walk, the plates shifting and clinking inside like church bells packed for war.

Ada stood in the middle of her ruined shop for exactly as long as it took her hands to stop shaking. She counted. Sixty heartbeats, seventy. Then she crossed to the drafting table, retrieved her sketchbook from behind the harbor folios, and found, when she opened it, that her night's work had saved itself: the estuary channels, the drowned streets redrawn from memory, every line intact. They had taken the copper. They could not take the geography out of her head, and they did not know yet that the geography had already escaped the shop twice over—in oilcloth under Marta Quill's floorboards since yesterday evening, and in graphite and ink in a book no larger than a psalter.

She had moved the notes the night before, on instinct, and had hated herself for the hour it cost, hauling the chest down the dark lanes to the harbor with her hood up and every footstep a drumroll. Now the instinct stood between her and ruin, and she understood something Odile had tried to teach her and she had resisted: preparation was not fear. Preparation was the only form of courage that worked twice.

She packed in twenty minutes. Sketchbook, pens, the good burnisher, Corvane's reversed-compass seal matrix, three changes of clothes, the shop ledger with its thin honest columns. At the door she stopped and looked back once—at the empty press, the gouged desk, the pale rectangles in the dust where eleven plates had lain undisturbed for years—and she let the grief come, briefly, fully, the way you let a wave break over a seawall: contained, measured, survivable.

"They took your copper," she said aloud to the empty room, to the man who wasn't in it. "They didn't take your argument."

Then she pulled the door shut and walked, not fast, not slow, a woman going to market, down Rope Walk, down Cutter's Stair, down to the harbor where the tide was turning and Marta Quill's boat rode at its mooring with the bored patience of everything in that household that had ever needed to disappear.

* * *

Marta was mending net in the bow, and she read the whole raid off Ada's face before Ada got one boot onto the deck.

"They took the plates."

"They took the plates." Ada sat down on the locker across from her. The harbor smelled of tar and herring and the cold mineral breath of the sea, and somewhere behind her, up the hill, the Bureau's wagon was being unloaded into a warehouse, and the true record of a massacre was being shelved beside tax receipts. "Eleven of them. Everything finished. He catalogued them like fruit at market. And then—" She had meant to deliver it dry, but it came out cracked down the middle. "Then he opened the drawer, and he held the decoys, and he called them worthless. Marta, he *laughed*. My master spent his last years on those plates and this man laughed at them, and I stood there and curtsied."

Marta's needle did not pause. "Better a laugh than a lantern," she said. "A man who laughs at evidence has already decided it isn't evidence. That's the safest thing a fool can be, for us." She bit off her thread and finally looked up, and her eyes were shrewd and not unkind, which for Marta was the same thing as gentle. "The notes are still under my floor. The girl is sitting in my boat. So tell me what actually happened, start to finish, and leave out nothing, because I want to know one thing in particular."

"What?"

"How they knew to come *now*." Marta coiled her line with quick, hard turns. "Odile said the Bureau's been buying all winter, quiet-like, through agents. Buying is patient. This wasn't buying. This was a requisition, signed, five men, at dawn, eleven days after you found that drawer." The coil tightened. "Somebody pointed them at this shop, Ada. Somebody who knew Corvane left materials worth a dawn visit. And there's not many somebodies who could know that."

"The printer? The paper merchant—"

"Neither of them knows what's in the notes. Neither does Vasseur, by your telling." Marta stood, joints cracking, and looked down the quay toward the customs house with an expression Ada had seen on her only once before, the day they'd buried her husband's empty coffin. "There's one office in this city whose whole trade is knowing where people live and what names they go by. One office that knocks on doors and writes down answers." She turned back, and her voice dropped. "Your census man. Wren. When did you last see him?"

"A month ago. He filed his visit and went. He was—" Ada stopped, remembering the second visit, the pretext, the warning delivered in a lowered voice at this very railing: *your file was flagged, be careful.* Remembering lying awake afterward, unsure whether the warning had been mercy or bait. "He warned us," she said. "Didn't he? That's what you're thinking. That his flag went up the chain and came back down wearing grey coats."

Marta shrugged, the gesture of a woman who had outlived the habit of certainty. "Or he warned you because they were already coming, and wanted his conscience clean either way. Or he's the only honest man in the Bureau. I've met one of those in fifty years and he drowned." She picked up her oars. "Either way, girl, you'd best decide soon what Elias Wren is to you. Because from where I sit, it looks like the Bureau just followed his paperwork straight to your master's door—and the next piece of his paperwork might follow it to mine."

Below the waterline, invisible, the survey notes lay in the bilge where Ada had hidden them an hour ago, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against the hull like a heart. Above them the gulls screamed over the fish stalls, and up on Rope Walk, in a narrow office smelling of sealing wax, a man with neat handwriting was opening a file that bore his own initials—and reading, for the first time, what had been done with it.
