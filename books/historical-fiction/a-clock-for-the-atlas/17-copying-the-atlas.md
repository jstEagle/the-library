## 17. Copying the Atlas

The boathouse smelled of tar and old salt, of rope gone soft with damp, of the lantern oil Marta rationed out each night as though it were coin. It stood at the end of a rotting pier behind the widow's house, half-hidden by nets hung to dry, and it had one virtue above all others: from the water side, its single window faced nothing but darkness and the far gray line of the seawall, so that anyone working inside could be seen by no one but the sea.

Ada had come to think of that window as a companion. Each night when she lifted the shutter and saw only the black water holding the last light of the sky, she felt something loosen in her chest—the particular relief of a place that kept her secrets.

They worked after midnight, when Vellamare's dogs had finished their arguments and even the Bureau's patrol boats had gone in. Marta set the watch hours like doses of medicine: two hours of copying, a quarter hour of silence while they listened, then two hours more. The first night, Ada had chafed at the interruptions. By the fourth, she understood them. Fear, she was learning, was not the opposite of work. It was the rhythm section of it.

The master atlas lay open on the trestle table under three lanterns, and it was more beautiful than Ada could bear some nights. Corvane had drawn Saltmarsh before the flood—every quay, every stair that ran down to the water, every warehouse and washhouse and chapel of the drowned quarter—with a precision that was itself a kind of love. Along the margin of the Pier Nine sheet, in his small upright hand, he had written the names of street sellers: *herring, hot; mussels, cold; ribbons, Sunday only.* A city rendered so completely that killing it became legible as murder.

Six copies. That was Odile's number. *One for the Bureau to burn,* she had said, *one for a foreign consul, one for a university library across the border, one buried, one traveling, and one that stays here to shame them.* "No single seizure," she had repeated, tapping the table with a forefinger that trembled more than she liked, "can destroy the truth. Not one seizure, girl, not even a very thorough one."

So they copied. And on this fifth night, the boathouse held all five of them, which still startled Ada when she looked up from her work and saw it: light and industry spilling across the tar-black floorboards where once there had only been nets and her own solitary dread.

* * *

Odile Ferrant sat nearest the master atlas because her eyes were worst, and her hands were the problem tonight.

She had been an archivist for thirty years; her body knew the motions of faithful reproduction the way a sailor's body knows rope. But the shaking had come on worse since the raid on the shop, since the news that Tam had been followed home twice by men who walked like they were paid to walk. She would lower her pen to the paper and watch it hover there, swaying like a compass needle near iron, and her mouth would go tight with a fury that had nowhere to spend itself.

"Damn these hands," she said quietly, for the third time that hour. "They ruled ledger lines for the Ministry of Records for eleven years without a waver. Now they carry on like a drunkard's."

"Rest them," Marta said from the doorway, where she stood wrapped in a fisherman's coat three sizes too large, a spyglass in one hand though the fog made it useless. "Copy tomorrow."

"Tomorrow the official history is one day closer to the shelves." Odile flexed her fingers, stretched them flat against her knees. "Everything is tomorrow with you, Quill. Tomorrow is how lies get printed."

But she did rest them, laying her hands palms-down on the table as if pinning something there, and her eyes went instead to Elias Wren, who sat at the far end of the trestle with a stack of blank sheets and a straightedge, ruling guide lines with the intense concentration of a man defusing something.

He was not good at it yet. His lines came out either too faint to ink over or so heavy they bled through to the next sheet, and twice he had snapped a lead because he pressed like a man writing a threat. But he came every night, and he did not complain, and he had stopped—Ada noticed—sitting with his back to the window.

"Lighter," Odile called to him now, in the tone of a woman who had corrected apprentices for decades and found it the one pleasure age could not take. "You are suggesting a line to the penman, not engraving it into the history of the world."

"I'm used to forms," Elias said. "Forms have boxes. You fill the box, the box is satisfied." He drew another line, lighter this time, and studied it with visible suspicion. "There are no boxes in cartography."

"There are no boxes anywhere worth working," Odile said. "Again."

Ada watched him try again, tongue between his teeth, and felt the strange doubled seeing that had come to her lately whenever she looked at Elias Wren: the census man, the instrument of the state's great counting, kneeling over blank paper learning to serve the uncounted. She remembered the terror of his first visit to the shop, the way his questions had fallen like taps on a wall, searching for the hollow place. She remembered thinking: *if he writes my name in his book, I am finished.* He had never written it. Or rather—he had written it somewhere, flagged it, and then spent everything he had to unwrite it, and she was only beginning to understand what that had cost him.

"Your lines are improving," she said, because she had noticed that praise sat strangely on him, like a coat cut for a different man, and because strange coats needed wearing to be broken in.

Elias looked up. In the lantern light his face was younger than it seemed by day, less careful. "My supervisor rules lines too," he said. "On the district tables. To divide neighborhoods into counts. I used to think it was neutral work. Lines." He turned the straightedge over in his hands. "A line is never neutral. It always asks: who is on which side."

"That," said Odile, "is the first intelligent thing a census man has said in my hearing," and Marta laughed low from the doorway, and something in the boathouse warmed a degree that the stove could not account for.

Ada bent back over her sheet. She was copying the harbor chart, the hardest one, the one with soundings in Corvane's own hand—depths taken, the notes proved, in the weeks before the flood, depths that showed the channel silting exactly where the official story claimed the rebels had blasted it open. Her pen moved the way it moved when she worked well: not fast, not slow, in that current where the hand stops being yours and becomes the riverbed the ink flows through. She had loved this feeling all her life without having a name for it. Lately she had begun to understand it differently. Every stroke was a vote. Every sounding, a testimony. She was not copying a map. She was casting ballots.

* * *

Near midnight the fog thickened until the lantern glow stopped a foot beyond the windows, and Marta came inside and shut the door and said, "No boats moving. Even smugglers are home. We can stop listening for a while and listen to each other instead."

They ate. Marta's stew, from the pot she carried down nightly, bread gone hard but softened in the broth. They ate the way people eat who are tired in the bones and honest about it—without ceremony, elbows on the table, passing things wordlessly. Ada looked around at them and had the sensation, new and vertiginous, of standing inside the thing she had wanted all her life without daring to want it. A table. People at it. Herself among them, counted.

Tam Osei sat with his bowl nearly untouched, turning his spoon in slow circles. He had come every night since the compact in Marta's kitchen, though his statement remained unwritten and unsigned, and everyone let it lie. He did the least skilled work—trimming sheets, mixing ink from the cake sticks, feeding the stove—but he did it with a steadiness that shamed them all, and sometimes, when the copying went badly or the fog pressed close, it was Tam's calm that held the room together like a keel.

"Tell us about lockkeeping," Elias said to him, abruptly and gently at once. "I've lived in Vellamare nine years and I couldn't tell you what a lockkeeper does."

Tam looked up. For a moment Ada thought the wall would hold. Then he said, "I kept the water polite." A small shrug. "Raised it for the grain barges, dropped it for repairs, kept the flood tides off the low streets. Ten thousand small decisions a year, and nobody notices any of them unless the water goes where it shouldn't." His spoon stopped. "That's the whole trade. You're only noticed when something terrible happens. So you learn to make sure nothing terrible happens." He looked at the dark window. "And then one night it does anyway, and it isn't your doing, and ten years later people still look at you sideways in the market, because a lockkeeper near Pier Nine must know something."

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked.

"Do you?" Odile asked, softly, without archivist's steel. "Know something?"

Tam met her eyes. Ada saw the war in his face, ten years long, neither side winning. "I know what I saw," he said. "I've known it every day since. That's not the same as saying it." He gathered his bowl and the ink cakes and went to the far end of the boathouse, ostensibly to tidy the tool chest, and no one followed him, because Marta caught Ada's eye and shook her head once: *not yet.*

* * *

The second shift began at two. This was when the work turned holy, in Ada's private taxonomy—not the fresh hours when they started brisk and full of plan, but the deep ones, when the body tired and only purpose kept the hand awake.

Odile had recovered enough steadiness to attempt the index sheet, the simplest of the plates, and she worked now with lips moving silently, reading each entry as she reproduced it, as if the names themselves needed speaking to survive the transit from one page to another. Street names. Wharf names. Congregation names. At the bottom, in the space Corvane had left for a dedication, the original read only: *For the drowned, that they may be found.* Odile copied it twice, once per copy, and both times her voice moved though no sound came out, and both times her hand did not shake.

Marta took the turn at the window. She had rigged a system of signals—a red rag over the pier post if anyone approached along the shore path, two knocks on the shutter if a boat showed lights—and she watched the fog with the patience of a woman who had spent twenty years watching harbors for revenue cutters. Once, a light did move out there, distant and aimless, a fisherman lost or drunk, and Marta tracked it until it swung away south, and then she poured herself a thimble of gin and offered the bottle around. Only Odile accepted.

Elias was on his fourth sheet of ruled paper, and his lines had come true at last—even, confident, spaced to the hair. He held one up to the lantern and examined it, and there was something in his face Ada recognized from her own mirror: the shock of making a thing with your hands that is exactly what you meant to make.

"It's good," she said.

"It's just lines."

"You said yourself. A line is never neutral." She took the sheet and laid it beneath her pen. "These will hold the streets of Saltmarsh. People will run their fingers along them and find their grandmother's house. Your lines, Mr. Wren. Think about that before you rule the next hundred."

He thought about it. She could see him doing it, actually thinking, the way he had thought his way from duty to defection—one honest calculation at a time, arriving against his own expectations at courage. "Elias," he said. "If we're going to spend our nights committing treason together, you should call me Elias."

"Elias," Ada agreed, and wrote his lines into testimony.

* * *

It was near dawn, the hour when the fog went gray and the gulls started their quarrels, that the mistake happened.

Ada found it as she collated the night's sheets against the master—a routine she performed with Odile's rigor drilled into her, checking each plate against the original, hunting discrepancies the way Odile had taught her to hunt them: *the eye forgives, the archive does not.*

Sheet eleven, the Saltmarsh quarter itself, the heart of the atlas, copied in Tam's careful hand. He had been practicing drawing for a week, and his linework was clean, almost Corvane-like in its restraint. But along Rope Walk—the street where he had grown up, where his mother's washing line had spanned the alley—he had done something no surveyor would do and no archivist could forgive.

He had added doors. Small, patient, lovingly rendered doors, opening off houses that the official record insisted had never existed, and beside two of them, so small you would miss them unless you hunted, initials. *T.O. M.O.*

His mother and him. Marked on a map of a street that was under ten feet of water, alive, home.

Ada stood holding the sheet a long time. Beside her Odile had gone still, and Ada braced for the correction—the *strike it, girl, an archive cannot carry sentiment*—but Odile only leaned close, spectacles down her nose, and read the initials, and was silent for the length of four breaths.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was rougher than usual. "In thirty years of state archives I never once saw a document that loved anything. Perhaps that was the flaw in the profession." She straightened. "Leave it. Copy it exactly, in every edition hence. If we restore Saltmarsh to the maps, we restore it with its doors. Let the magistrates choke on their own precision."

Ada copied the doors. All of them, street by street, as Tam had drawn them, and found that her eyes stung by the end of Rope Walk, and did not wipe them until her pen was lifted.

* * *

They finished at first light, as always. Sheets interleaved with blotting paper, bundled in oilcloth, distributed according to Odile's doctrine—two bundles to Marta's hidden locker beneath the bilge boards of the boat, one to the false bottom of the coal scuttle, one sewn into the lining of Odile's coat—and the master atlas itself returned to its case, and the case to its grave in the salt hay, and the boathouse swept and the lanterns cooled and the five of them stood a moment in the gray air, smelling of ink and tar, wrecked with tiredness, and glad.

"One week," Odile said, counting on her trembling fingers. "At this pace, copies complete in eight days. Nine with disasters. The official proof ships within the month. We are—" she calculated, wincing, "—adequately ahead. Adequacy has won stranger wars."

"Home, all of you," Marta said. "Separate ways, staggered, like I taught you. Sleep. The fog's lifting and lifting fog lifts tempers too, and daylight makes brave fools of the lot of us."

They went. Tam first, up the shore path with his collar up; then Odile, leaning on Elias's arm whether she needed it or not; then Elias alone toward the Registry quarter, carrying his ruled sheets like contraband, which they were.

Ada stayed last, as she always did, to stand a moment at the window and look at the sea. The tide was going out, peeling back from the mudflats, and far along the seawall the first carts of the morning crossed above the drowned quarter, drivers hunched, horses steaming, all of them passing over streets and doors and lives without a thought—as she herself had done, once, before Corvane put the truth of it in her hands.

Soon, she thought. Soon the city would look down and see what it was walking on.

She was turning to go when Tam's voice came from the top of the shore path, and she realized he had stopped there, had been standing there a while, looking back at the boathouse—or past it, at the seawall, at the water.

"Miss Sandoval." He had never once used her name aloud before; hearing it landed in her chest like a struck bell. He came halfway down the path, and his face in the early light was the face of a man stepping off a ledge. "The soldiers were at the market yesterday. Harassing the Saltmarsh children. The ones who beg by the fish stalls." His jaw worked. "One of them—a girl, maybe ten, she wasn't born when the water came—she had a piece of the atlas. One of the sheets from the church doors. She'd folded it up and she was keeping it under her shirt like it was warm."

The gulls cried. The tide ran out over the flats, over the roofs below.

"I'll write it," Tam said. "What I saw at the levee. All of it. Tonight, if your pen is willing."
