## 18. Tam Decides

The market on Fishgut Lane opened before dawn, as it always did, in the smell of brine and charcoal and the particular grey light that came off the harbor the way fog came off a wound. Tam Osei had worked the stalls for ten years—first hauling crates for anyone who paid in bread, later keeping the lockbooks at Pier Nine three days a week and minding a fishmonger's scales the other four—and in ten years he had learned the first rule of survival in Vellamare: be furniture. Be the crate, the barrel, the scale. Eyes that saw nothing, hands that did their work, a mouth that answered only what was asked and asked nothing back.

He had been furniture the night the levee broke. That was the sentence he carried under his ribs like a swallowed hook. He had been at the sluice-house, sixteen years old with his father's keys heavy on his belt, and he had seen the lanterns coming across the landward fields where no honest man had business at that hour, and he had put out his light and crouched among the reeds and watched soldiers drive iron bars down into the earthen spine of Saltmarsh while the district he loved slept behind them. He had watched. He had done nothing. And when the water came through with a sound like the sky tearing, he had run along the crown of the seawall screaming warnings to people who had no high ground to run to, which was not nothing but had never once felt like enough.

Ten years of being furniture. Ten years of answering *no* to questions no one asked, of letting his eyes go dull when strangers spoke of the flood, of crossing the street to avoid the widows' memorial walk because grief in company was dangerous grief.

Then, on a Thursday thick with the smell of mackerel, the soldiers came to the market.

* * *

There were four of them, revenue blues with the new ministry sash, and they moved through the stalls the way gulls move through a herring catch—not hunting anything in particular yet, just establishing that everything belonged to them. Tam marked them from across the lane without lifting his head, hands steady on the scale weights, cataloguing them the way you catalogue weather. Two young ones, nervous, boots too clean. A sergeant with the flat patience of a man who had done this many times. An officer, thin, gloves, carrying a leather folio—the kind who never touched anyone himself.

They stopped at the far end of the lane, where the oyster barrows clustered, and it took Tam a long moment to understand what he was seeing, because what he was seeing made so little sense it kept refusing to resolve into fact.

The children were being counted.

Saltmarsh orphans worked the market the way sparrows worked a threshing floor—quick, silent, invisible unless you looked for them, which nobody did. They shelled oysters for a copper, ran messages, swept scales off the cobbles. There were perhaps a dozen of them on any given morning, and every adult in the lane had an unspoken arrangement with them: you pretend I don't feed you scraps, I pretend you don't sleep under my awning. The orphans were Vellamare's smallest furniture.

The officer with the folio was making them stand in a row.

Tam's hands went still on the scale. One by one, the sergeant was pulling children out of the crowd of legs and barrels—a girl of maybe nine with her brother's coat swallowing her shoulders; a boy with the pale eyelashes of the Saltmarsh families; twins who sold cockles and always held hands when they thought no one watched—and setting them against the shuttered front of the wool factor's shop. The officer consulted his folio, then each face, and made a mark. When one of the little ones started to cry—not loudly, just the leaking kind of crying a child does when it has learned crying changes nothing—an older girl broke from the crowd and took her hand, and the sergeant barked at her to get back in the line too.

"What's the charge?" someone called, and Tam knew the voice: Widow Bren, who sold eels and feared nothing except God, and possibly not even Him.

"No charge," the officer said pleasantly, not looking up from his folio. "Enumeration. New census protocols for undocumented minors. Names and origins will be recorded." He turned a page. "Origins especially."

The lane went very quiet. Everyone understood *origins*. Everyone understood that there was no such census protocol, or if there was, its purpose was not counting but listing—that a list of children whose parents had drowned in Saltmarsh was not paper, it was ammunition. It could be used to move them, to indenture them, to lose them the way Tam's own brother had been lost in the records purges after the war, a name entered into a ledger and never exited from anywhere.

"Origins," the officer said again, to the line of children, in the tone of a schoolmaster. "Who can tell me where they were born?"

Nobody answered. The girl with the boy's coat stared at the cobbles. The crying child had gone silent in the older girl's grip, which was worse.

And Tam Osei, standing behind his fishmonger's stall with a brass weight in his hand, felt something in his chest shift position for the first time in ten years.

It was not courage. He would think about this afterward and be honest with himself about that: it was not courage, not at first. It was recognition. The officer's pen moving down the column of names, the marks in the folio, the pleasant empty voice asking children to name the place that had killed them—it was the same motion, the same pen, the same bureaucratic tide that had rolled over his family once already and left him standing on the seawall with nothing in his hands. He had spent ten years telling himself that silence was safety, that furniture survived where people drowned. But furniture did not survive. Furniture got inventoried. Furniture got hauled away when it was in the way. Silence had never once protected anybody in Vellamare; silence had only ever decided who would be taken first.

The sergeant grabbed the older girl by the arm—not hard, hard enough—and pulled her back into the line because she had tried to step between the officer and the little one.

"Names," the officer said.

Tam set the brass weight down very carefully, the way his father had taught him to set down anything that might become a weapon.

* * *

Afterward, he could not say whether he had decided or simply moved, the two things having happened simultaneously somewhere in his body before his mind caught up. He crossed the lane. Fourteen steps; he counted them the way he counted lock-turns, because counting was how he kept from shaking.

"There's no census for minors," he said. His voice came out rusty, like a winch unused for a season. "I keep records for the harbor. I'd know."

Four faces turned toward him. The young soldiers' hands drifted toward their belts. The officer smiled with professional mildness and said, "This is ministry business, friend," and Tam heard the word *friend* land exactly the way it was meant to, a coin dropped on a counter: here is your chance to be nobody again.

Behind the officer, the girl with the boy's coat was looking at Tam. She had Saltmarsh eyes—he'd have known them anywhere, that particular watchful grey—and she was looking at him the way people look at a wall they are deciding whether to trust their weight against.

His father had kept those locks for thirty years. His father had died in the water, gone down with the sluice-house rather than open the gates early and drown the low streets of Vellamare proper instead of his own district, a choice the official record had never mentioned because the official record said the levee failed. *Failed.* As though water had opinions. As though the sea had reached up over the earthworks on the landward side, through fields, past the sluice-houses, and cut its own gate.

"I'm no one's friend," Tam said. "But those children were born in Saltmarsh, and everyone in this lane knows it, and if you're writing origins in that book, write mine next. Tam Osei. Lockkeeper, Pier Nine. Born Saltmarsh, lived Saltmarsh, and I was on the levee the night it broke."

The lane had gone so quiet he could hear the harbor slopping against the pilings.

The officer regarded him for a moment with the unhurried interest of a man adding an item to a list. Then he closed the folio. "Noted," he said. "We'll want to talk to you properly, Mr. Osei. Someone will call." And he gestured the soldiers back, and the line of children dispersed like startled birds, and the market exhaled and resumed its noise, and Tam stood in the middle of Fishgut Lane understanding with total clarity that he had just written his own name into somebody's book.

It should have felt like drowning. It felt, instead, like breathing after ten years underwater.

* * *

The older girl found him that evening at the lockhouse. She came the way the orphans came everywhere, out of shadow and around corners, and stood at the edge of the lamplight holding a folded scrap of oilcloth.

"You shouldn't have done that," she said.

"Probably not."

"They'll come back for you."

"Likely."

She considered him, chewing the inside of her cheek, and then held out the oilcloth. "A woman gave me this to bring you. Widow Marta, from the harbor. She said you'd know her husband's boat." Tam did know it—the *Kindly Ann*, half legend among pier-men for the cargoes that weren't quite what the manifest claimed. Inside the oilcloth was a slip of paper with an address on Tanner's Row and a single line in a firm hand: *You said you were on the levee. There are people who can make that matter. Come when the bell rings six.*

"She said to tell you," the girl added, with the scrupulousness of a messenger who takes pride in accuracy, "that nobody will make you do anything. But that being a witness isn't the same as being brave, and you don't have to be the second to do the first."

Tam read the note twice. His hands, he noticed, were almost steady.

"Tell the widow," he said, "that I'll come."

* * *

He went at six, because a man who had spent ten years breaking promises to the dead could at least keep one made to the living. The garret above Tanner's Row smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of sickness. There were five people there, and Tam catalogued them the old way, the lockkeeper's way: a broad woman with forearms like dock rope—Marta Quill, unmistakable—standing at a stove as if guarding it; a gaunt, grey-faced woman he didn't know, propped in a chair like a monument to herself, with an archivist's stillness in her ruined hands; the census taker Wren, whom Tam recognized at once and flinched from until he saw the man's face, which wore the expression of a drowning man who has decided to stop swimming for shore and start swimming for the boat; a young woman sitting at the table with maps spread before her, inking a coastline with the absorbed precision of someone who trusts lines more than words—and who looked up when Tam entered with such directness that he understood she was the center of all this, whatever all this was.

"This is Tam Osei," Marta said. "Lockkeeper. Pier Nine." And then, with a gentleness that sat strangely on her gravel voice: "He was there the night it happened."

"He was there," the young woman repeated softly, and Tam realized she wasn't asking. She had known somehow, or hoped, and his arrival had turned hope into fact.

The archivist leaned forward. Her voice was dry as old paper, but her eyes were alive in a way that made Tam think of water finding a channel. "Mr. Osei. Before anything else, you should understand precisely what we have and what we lack. We have survey notes proving the levee was cut from the landward side. We have provenance, receipts, a chain of custody reconstructed line by line. What we lack is a living witness who can stand in a room and say *I saw men do it*, because documents accuse, but witnesses convict. Ten years, and every testimony we might have gathered has been scattered by death, exile, or fear." She folded her hands. "We are not asking you tonight. Odile Ferrant asks nobody. But I will tell you what your silence is worth now, at this hour of the world: it is worth exactly what they decide it is worth, and they have already begun writing children's names in books. Your silence purchases nothing anymore except your solitude. Your testimony—" the ruined hands opened, palms up, an offering or a surrender, "—could purchase the truth itself. Signed. Sworn. Undeniable."

Nobody spoke. The inking pen had stopped; the young woman was watching him, and Marta was watching him, and Wren was studying the floor as though the floor contained instructions.

Tam thought of his father going down with the sluice-house. He thought of the lanterns crossing the landward fields, and the sound the sky had made, and the girl with the boy's coat staring at the cobbles while a stranger wrote down where she came from. He thought: *I have been furniture for ten years, and today they came to inventory the children, and tomorrow they will come for whatever else is left unlisted.*

He thought: *the levee didn't fail. Men cut it. I saw them. I have always seen them.*

"Yes," he said, and the word came out steadier than anything he had said in a decade. "I'll sign. I'll swear it before magistrates, before God, before whatever clerk they send—I saw soldiers cut the levee from the landward side on the night of the ninth of Floodmonth, and I'll put my name to it."

The young woman closed her eyes for a moment, the way a navigator does when a bearing finally comes clear. The archivist smiled, small and fierce. Marta put a bowl of stew in front of him without comment, which he understood was the highest ceremony she offered.

Only the census taker spoke, quietly, from the corner. "Then we should work fast. Because if they've started counting the orphans, they're building a case of their own—and I think we may have very little time."

Tam picked up the spoon, and ate, and did not argue.
