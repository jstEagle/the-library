## 19. The Historian's Advance Copy

The Ministry of Histories and Records kept its advance proofs in a locked cabinet on the third floor, in a room with no windows onto the street, as though the thing it guarded were a prisoner rather than a book.

Elias Wren knew this because he had helped carry the cabinet upstairs.

That was the cruelty of it—the banality. Three weeks ago, before the flagged file and the raid on the map shop and the long sleepless arithmetic of his own guilt, he had been asked, as a man of his seniority routinely was, to help the clerks shift furniture. The proofs had arrived in crates stamped with the ministry's double anchor, and he had carried two of them up three flights of stairs without reading so much as a spine. He had joked with the under-clerk about the weight of history. Someone had laughed.

Now he stood in the corridor outside that same room at nine o'clock at night, holding a ring of keys he had no right to hold, listening to the sound of his own breathing.

He had told himself all day that he would not come. He had told himself it at breakfast, when the porridge went cold in front of him; he had told himself again on the tram, watching Vellamare slide past its lit shopfronts like a city wearing its best face for company; he had told himself most firmly of all at the door of the ministry, when the night porter had nodded to him—*evening, Mr. Wren*—because Elias Wren was a man who could be trusted, a man whose file said *reliable*, a man whose whole adult life had been built out of being exactly forgettable enough never to be questioned.

The lie he had told himself longest was this: that he only wanted to *look*. That a man could read a thing and remain unchanged by it. He had been a census taker for eleven years; he believed, professionally, in the neutrality of counting. You recorded what was there. You did not make the world by writing it down.

But Ada Sandoval had looked at him across Marta Quill's kitchen table with eyes that had measured coastlines her whole life, and she had made him understand that every line is drawn by a hand, and every hand has a reason, and there is no such thing as a neutral map. There were only maps that admitted their arguments and maps that lied about having any.

The corridor smelled of lamp oil and dust. Somewhere below, the porter turned a page of his newspaper.

Elias put the key in the lock. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. It should have shaken. A hand that shook would at least have been honest.

* * *

The cabinet held eleven copies, wrapped in brown paper and tied with government twine: *Advance Proof — Not for Circulation — Property of the Historical Commission.* He took one, and then—he could not have said why—took a second as well, and slid both inside his coat, where they sat against his ribs like slabs of ice.

There was a reading table by the wall. He told himself he would wait until he got home. He lasted four minutes.

He cut the twine with his thumbnail.

The title page first, and it was the title page that undid him—not the contents, which he had braced for, but the sheer confidence of the cover. Gilt letters on navy cloth, embossed with the harbor's official seal:

*A TRUE ACCOUNT OF THE RECLAMATION OF SALTMARSH AND THE SUPPRESSION OF THE INSURRECTION OF 1864. Compiled from Official Sources. By Order of the Historical Commission.*

Compiled from official sources. As if truth were a matter of filing. As if you could compile a drowned district the way you compiled a tax roll, and whatever survived the compiling was what had happened.

He turned the page. The paper was fine stock, heavy in the fingers, the kind that takes ink beautifully and lasts a century. Someone had chosen this paper deliberately. Someone had stood in a warehouse and touched samples and said, yes, this one—it should last.

The prose, when he reached it, was worse than he had feared, because it was *good*.

He had expected crude propaganda, the sort of thing pasted on customs-house walls, all capital letters and bleeding flags. This was nothing of the kind. This was patient, dignified, elegantly cadenced prose, the voice of a reasonable man explaining sorrowful necessities to other reasonable men. It opened not with the flood but with the war—with the years of shelling, the barricades, the atrocities committed by both sides and then quietly attributed to one—and by the time it came to Saltmarsh it had already taught the reader how to feel. The district became *the rebel enclave*. Its people became *irregulars, hoarders of grain, saboteurs of the harbor chain*. Its defenders—

Elias stopped. Read it again. Read it a third time, his lips moving, though there was no one to hear.

The defenders of Saltmarsh had held the levee road for six weeks against the government's second army. Every account agreed on that. What the accounts disagreed on—what Corvane had died rather than let vanish—was what happened on the night of the ninth of March, when the water came through from the landward side at dead low tide, an hour chosen by someone who understood tides.

Here it was, in print, in gilt-adjacent type, in prose smooth as poured plaster:

*"In the final days of the siege, the irregulars' leadership, recognizing the position was untenable, breached the levee themselves to deny the advancing army its supply route and to cover their own flight seaward. That this measure cost the lives of the very civilians the irregulars claimed to protect is the bitterest irony of the campaign, and one laid wholly at the feet of those who chose rebellion over reconciliation."*

They had named names.

Aldous Bream, dock warden. The Serel brothers, lockkeepers. Captain Ivo Marsh of the pier militia. Nine names in all, set in a neat list like a bill of lading, each one charged in a footnote with "the demolition of the western levee, evidenced by powder residue recovered at the breach."

Powder residue. Elias closed his eyes. He thought of Tam Osei's face in Marta's kitchen, the way the old lockkeeper's hands had folded around his cup as though it were something that might be taken from him. *I saw them come up the towpath,* Tam had finally said, three nights ago, his voice flat as standing water. *Soldiers. Twelve of them, maybe more, in engineer's coats. They worked at the levee from midnight till near two. I heard the timbers go. Nobody on that towpath was from Saltmarsh, Mr. Wren. Everybody from Saltmarsh was behind the walls, waiting out the shelling, same as always.* And then, after a silence: *I counted them. God help me, I counted them, and I've been counting ever since.*

Tam Osei's count, sworn and witnessed, sat hidden in pickle barrels in a boathouse on the north quay, alongside forty pages of survey notes in Master Corvane's crabbed hand showing the breach cut at an angle no wave and no powder charge could produce—a cut made by tools, from the landward side, at slack tide.

And here was the counter-count, bound in navy cloth, printed on paper meant to outlive everyone who could contradict it.

It was elegant. That was what made his stomach turn over, slowly, like a boat taking water. The lie was *beautiful*. It had a bibliography. It thanked the Bureau of Records—his own bureau—in the preface. It dedicated itself, with perfect taste, *"to the peaceful memory of all Vellamare's children."*

Somewhere in this building, or in a print shop across the river, typesetters were setting the final signatures. Booksellers had probably already received their orders. In spring—six weeks, perhaps less—this book would sit in every window on Cordwainer Street, respectable and ribboned and true-by-acclamation, and anyone who remembered otherwise would look at it and begin, very quietly, to doubt themselves. That was the real power of a thing like this. Not to convince the liars. To exhaust the rememberers.

Elias Wren had spent his career believing that a record kept was a service rendered. He had believed the ledger was innocent because he had never once asked what it was *for*.

He copied out the title page and the offending passage in his pocket notebook, his handwriting small and even, a census-taker's hand, made for facts. Then he rewrapped the proof, retied the twine with a clerk's neat bow, locked the cabinet, and walked out past the porter, who wished him goodnight and meant nothing by it and suspected nothing at all.

Outside, the rain had just begun—fine sea-rain, barely a mist, the kind that gets into everything. Elias turned his collar up and felt the stolen book press against his chest with every step, heavier than paper had any right to be.

* * *

Marta Quill answered the boathouse door with a boat hook in one hand and a kitchen knife in the other, and lowered both when she saw him.

"You're late," she said. "Ada's been walking a groove in my floor since dusk. In." She glanced up and down the quay, a habit worn into her the way grooves wear into a tiller. "Did anyone follow you?"

"I checked twice."

"Check three times next time. There won't be a fourth mistake worth surviving." She barred the door behind him and led him back through the smell of tar and brine to the lamplit room where the copying was done.

They were all there tonight, which was luck or providence or simply the gravity of bad news drawing them together. Odile Ferrant sat propped in the chair nearest the stove, a blanket over her knees despite the season, her ruined eyes moving over the pages Ada ruled and inked beneath the lamp. Ada herself bent over the master copy, hair pinned back, a smudge of ink along her jaw like a bruise, and at the sight of Elias's face she set down her pen at once. She had learned, in these weeks, to read him the way she read charts—for the small deviations that mean weather coming.

Tam Osei sat apart as always, near the door, mending a net he did not need mended.

"The proofs are done," Elias said. "Printed. Bound. Sitting in a cabinet on the third floor of the ministry, eleven copies of them, waiting to become the truth." He took the stolen volume from his coat and set it on the worktable, and the sound of it—solid, expensive, final—made everyone go still.

Ada reached for it before anyone else. Of course she did. She untied nothing; the wrapping was already cut. She read the title page standing, then sat down slowly on the copying stool as though her legs had filed some formal objection.

"Read the passage," Elias said quietly. "Page forty. The breach."

She read it. He watched her read it—the stillness of her, the terrible economy of it, no gasp, no tears, only her thumb pressing whiter against the page's edge, a mapmaker's finger marking a point on a chart where the ship went down.

"They blame the defenders," she said at last. Her voice was level. That was somehow worse than weeping. "Bream. The Serels. Captain Marsh." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and frightening. "Captain Marsh pulled forty people onto his roof that night with a boat hook. My master surveyed the breach twice and drew it once, and the drawing shows tool marks angled inward from the landward berm, and he wrote in the margin—" her breath caught, just once, and resumed— "he wrote *cut, not broken; cut with care, by men who knew the tide tables*. And they've given it a *footnote*."

"Footnotes lend credibility," Odile said grimly. "Nothing lies like a well-dressed lie. Let me see it."

Ada passed the book to her. The old archivist held it close, tilted toward the lamp, and read with her lips moving, and Elias watched thirty years of professional discipline fight it out with thirty years of grievance on her face. When she finished, she laughed—one short, awful bark.

"Combet wrote this," she said. "The cadence. The little ironies. He was a junior compiler when I was at the Bureau, before either of us fell out of favor. He used to say a nation's history was its best fiction, because it had to survive cross-examination." She pushed the book away from her as if it were warm. "This will pass cross-examination, children. It will pass beautifully. Unless—"

"Unless we get there first," Ada said.

"Unless we get there first." Odile folded her hands on the blanket. "Understand what you're holding. This proof is dated. The commission will distribute within the fortnight, booksellers within six weeks, and once it's on shelves, our atlas stops being evidence and becomes *contradiction*—and contradiction of a printed, bound, commissioned history is sedition, plain as bread. Before publication, we are archivists correcting the record. After, we are criminals defaming it. The difference between martyrdom and madness is about forty days."

Silence, except the rain on the boathouse roof and the lap of black water against the pilings.

Tam Osei spoke without looking up from his net. "My name's in their book?"

Elias nodded. "Not yours. But your trade is. Lockkeepers. It says the lockkeepers cut the sluices."

"Mm." Tam's needle paused. "Ten years I kept my mouth shut, thinking silence was safety. Turns out silence was just letting them write my confession for me." He tied off a knot, precise as surgery. "Then we'd best hurry, hadn't we."

Ada stood. Something had changed in her posture—Elias had watched it happen over these weeks, the gradual straightening, the apprentice's apologetic stoop giving way to the spine of someone who owns her own lines—but tonight it was different again. Tonight she looked less like a woman deciding than like a woman who had finished deciding some time ago and was merely catching the rest of herself up.

"Then we move everything forward," she said. "Odile, how many pages left on the testimony section?"

"Nine. With Tam's statement appended and witnessed, seven."

"Seven days of copying, if the light holds and nobody's seized. Marta—"

"I know," Marta said. "Tanner's Row. I'll get word to the printer tomorrow. He'll want the rest of his money, mind. Printers have no politics until they're paid, and none afterward either."

"And the silver," Ada said, in the tone of someone ripping off a binding. "Tomorrow I sell it. All of it. Master Corvane's, and mine." She said *mine* without flinching, and Elias understood what it cost her, and understood also that she would not thank him for noticing.

Elias cleared his throat. There was one more thing, and it had been sitting in his chest the whole walk down the quay, and now it had to come out.

"There's something else you should know. About how the proofs were guarded—or weren't." He looked at the floor, then made himself look up, because they deserved his face while he said it. "The cabinet was easy to open because I knew where the key was, because I helped install it, because I'm trusted. And I'm trusted because my report on an unnamed apprentice in a certain map shop sits unfiled in my desk, marked *pending*, while my supervisor believes I'm cultivating the lead. They raided the shop on the strength of my flag. If they hadn't found what they wanted, they'd have come back for more, using me as the hound." His voice stayed steady; he was almost proud of that. "Sooner or later they'll ask why the hound has stopped barking. When they do, everything I am becomes a liability to everyone in this room."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Marta Quill snorted, picked up the knife she'd greeted him with, and began slicing bread for the stew pot.

"Welcome to being wanted," she said. "We'll make a family of fugitives of you yet."

Ada came around the table. She picked up the stolen proof, weighed it in her hands—eleven copies, forty days, one city—and looked at Elias with those chart-reader's eyes.

"Then we don't waste the forty days," she said. "And we don't waste you. You know their filing systems, their routes, their timetables. If they've written the lie, you can tell us exactly how they'll *ship* it." A pause, and then, quieter, almost gentle: "Thank you for stealing this. I know what it cost."

"It cost nothing," Elias said. "That's the shameful part. I signed myself out past the porter, and he wished me goodnight."

Ada almost smiled. Then she turned to the window, to the rain and the black harbor where a drowned district slept beneath the shipping lanes, and her smile went wherever smiles go when they're not needed.

"Six weeks," she said softly, to the water, to the dead, to the atlas sleeping in its barrels. "They've had ten years to write their version. We have six weeks to finish ours."

Outside, far down the quay, a whistle sounded—once, short, the harbor watch changing shifts—and every person in the boathouse went silent and still, listening, until the footsteps faded into the rain.

Then Ada picked up her pen, uncapped the ink, and began to rule the next line.
