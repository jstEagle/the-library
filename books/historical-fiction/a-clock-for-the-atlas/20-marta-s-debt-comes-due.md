## 20. Marta's Debt Comes Due

The knock came at the wrong hour, in the wrong rhythm, from the wrong side of the door.

Marta Quill knew every honest knock in Vellamare the way a harlot knows her regulars: the harbor master's double rap, brisk and official; the fishwives' flat palms, impatient with cold; the soft scratch of children sent on errands they didn't understand. And she knew the dishonest ones too, though she had spent nine years hoping never to hear them again. This one was three knocks, a pause, then two more—lazy, deliberate, almost amused. A man's knock. A man who wanted her to know he was not afraid of her door.

She set down the gutting knife, wiped her hands on her apron out of habit rather than need, and looked through the gap where the shutter didn't quite meet the frame.

Silas Grebe.

He stood on her step like a man visiting his own property. Broad still, though gone soft at the middle since the war years; a coat of good wool that had not been good when she'd last seen him; and that face—that wide, easy, forgettable face that had once charmed customs officers into looking the other way while crates of untaxed brandy crossed the pier at Pier Nine. He had been her husband's partner, back when Denny Quill was alive and the *Kestrel* ran dark cargo up the coast twice a month. Back before the flood took the piers, the quarter, and Denny all in one long night.

Marta had buried Silas Grebe in her mind a decade ago. Here he stood, breathing sea air on her doorstep, very much unburied.

"Quill," he said, when she opened the door only as far as the chain allowed. "You look well. Grief agrees with some women."

"Grief's been busy elsewhere," she said. "What do you want?"

"To talk business." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had always been a good smile; that was the whole trouble with him. "May I come in? The wind off the harbor does nothing for my chest."

"The wind can have your chest. Talk here."

Something flickered behind the smile—not anger, exactly. Recalculation. He had expected to be inside by now, seated at her table, drinking her tea, establishing ownership of her room the way men like him always did. She had denied him the room. He adjusted, as he always did, without visible effort.

"As you like." He leaned one shoulder against her doorframe, close enough that she could smell pomade under the salt. "I hear you've taken an interest in old papers lately. Corvane papers. Survey notes. That sort of thing."

The words landed in Marta's chest like fishhooks. She kept her face the way she kept her accounts: balanced, unreadable, showing nothing on either side.

"I hear a lot of things," she said. "Harbor talk. Half of it's lies, and the other half is lies people tell themselves."

"Mm." He scratched his jaw. "And yet. A mapmaker's apprentice has been seen coming and going from this house. An apprentice who ought, by rights, to be a ghost. And an archivist—one Odile Ferrant, formerly of the Bureau, currently of the bottle—has been heard holding forth in the Anchor & Chain about levees cut from the landward side. Loudly. After her third glass." He tsked softly. "Odile always did lack discretion. It's why they let her go, you know. Not the questions. The volume."

"You've been asking after my guests."

"I've been listening. There's a difference, and it matters." He straightened, and for a moment the charm dropped away, and what was underneath was worse: patience. "Here is the shape of it, Quill, because I respect you enough to skip the pleasantries. The Bureau is paying for information. Well. Certain parties are paying, using the Bureau's name the way smugglers use a flag of convenience. They want the Corvane materials. They want the names attached to them. And someone—I won't insult you by pretending I don't know who—told me you could deliver both."

"Someone told you wrong."

"Did they?" He reached into his coat, slowly, theatrically, and produced a folded paper. He did not offer it through the door. He simply held it up so she could see the sketch on it: a young woman's face, inked in the hard confident strokes of a police artist. Dark hair pulled back. A stubborn chin. Eyes drawn slightly too large, the way artists do when they've been told the subject watches everything.

It was Ada Sandoval. Or near enough that any constable with the paper in his pocket would stop her on sight.

"Circulated this week," Silas said. "'The Corvane apprentice.' There's a price on the name. Not a hanging price—a finding price. They want her found before they want her caught. There's a difference there too, and the difference is worth money to whoever finds her first." He folded the paper away. "Now. You and I are old friends, or old enough. I'd hate for us to be enemies over arithmetic. So let me make you an offer, woman to woman, which is more courtesy than I'd get from the men paying me."

"Go on."

"You give me the notes—or tell me where they are, and I'll do the rest—and I lose your name. All of it. The widow, the boat, the boathouse, the girl who sleeps above your kitchen. Gone. I walk away rich and you walk away untouched. That's the friendly version."

"And the unfriendly version?"

"The unfriendly version," he said pleasantly, "is that I go to my paymasters with everything I already have—which is more than you'd credit—and collect the finder's fee anyway, and they come here with boots instead of coin. And you'll notice, Quill, that the unfriendly version costs me nothing extra. The friendly one at least puts bread in your hand." He spread his palms, a merchant concluding a fair trade. "Three days. I'll call again Thursday, after the tide turns. Think about what Denny would have wanted."

At the name, something moved in Marta's chest—old grief, older rage—but she kept it below the waterline where she kept everything.

"Denny," she said, "would have put a knife in you at Pier Nine and slept soundly after."

Silas laughed, genuinely, and that was somehow the most obscene thing about him. "Denny always liked me better than you did. Thursday, Quill."

He touched his hat brim and went down her steps whistling, a tuneless little run of notes that followed him along the seawall path until the wind ate it.

Marta closed the door, slid the bolt, and stood in her dim kitchen with her hand pressed flat against the wood, feeling her own heartbeat come back down from her throat to its proper place.

Then she went to the corner cupboard, took down the bottle she saved for funerals, and poured two fingers. She drank it standing, looking at the window, at the gray light, at the harbor beyond where the *Kestrel* rode at her mooring—Denny's boat, her boat now, eleven years his ghost and hers.

"Well, love," she said to no one. "He's finally come to collect."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did with a problem: working it the way she worked a net, turning it over, testing each knot.

Silas had not lied about much. That was the danger of him—he lied the way good counterfeiters printed, mostly true with just enough false in it to spend. He did know about Ada. He did know about Odile's loose tongue at the Anchor & Chain. He had the sketch, which meant the Bureau's net was already cast wider than Elias Wren's warnings had suggested, or else Silas had other eyes in it. Either way, the group's safety now rested on the goodwill of the worst man Marta had ever drunk beside.

Could she pay him? In what? Coin she did not have—the printer on Tanner's Row would take the last of it within the week. Information she would not sell if Vellamare itself sank a second time. Which left, when she turned the net over and over and shook it out, exactly one thing of value that was hers alone to give.

She walked down to the mooring in the late light and stood on the pier looking at the *Kestrel*.

Thirty feet of black-hulled luggerrig, built in Saltmarsh the year before the flood by a boatwright whose name was on no surviving list. Denny had bought her cheap and loved her expensively, recaulking her seams himself every winter, varnishing her rails until you could see your face in them. On the night the levee broke, the *Kestrel* had been the only reason half of Pier Nine's living were living—Denny had run her into the flooded streets again and again until the water took the pier and nearly took her, and him. He'd drowned three weeks later, fevered from the immersion, and Marta had inherited a boat, a debt to the boatwright's widow, and a silence about what the *Kestrel* had seen from the water that night that she had kept ever since.

Eleven years she had kept her. Through lean winters when selling her would have meant coal and bread and a mattress that didn't smell of mildew. Through the years when the Bureau bought up private papers and she'd hidden Corvane's letters in her bilge without telling anyone, including herself, how frightened she was.

A boat is not a person. Marta knew that. She had buried a person; she knew the difference down to the grain of it. But a boat can hold a person the way a grave holds them, and the *Kestrel* held Denny the way the sea held Saltmarsh—wholly, permanently, without giving anything back.

She ran her hand along the rail. The varnish needed doing again. It always needed doing again.

"Thursday," she said aloud. "All right. But not the way you think, Silas Grebe."

* * *

She told no one. That was the discipline of it, the part that cost most. She could have gone to Ada, shown her the sketch, watched the girl's face go white and stubborn at once. She could have gone to Odile, who would have delivered a lecture on the legal inadequacy of blackmail contracts between criminals. She could have gone to Elias, who would have agonized beautifully and uselessly.

Instead she did the arithmetic alone, because that was what she was for. Every family, in her long experience of watching families from the outside, needed one member whose job was to absorb the blows quietly and never show the bruise. Her mother had been that for her father's crew. Marta had been it for Denny. She would be it for these unlikely children she seemed to have collected—because that was what they were, whatever else they were: a girl of twenty with a dead man's maps, a ruined archivist with shaking hands, a census clerk playing traitor badly, a lockkeeper carrying ten years of silence like a stone in his belly. Someone had to be the hull. Someone had to take the water so the cargo stayed dry.

On Wednesday she sailed the *Kestrel* out past the mole alone, ostensibly to check the crab pots she no longer bothered with. Really to say goodbye, though she'd have cut out her own tongue before calling it that. The wind was light and northerly and the harbor lay smooth as poured pewter, and she let the boat drift while she sat at the tiller Denny's hands had worn pale, and she talked to him the way she did maybe twice a year.

"He's got a sketch of the girl," she said. "Good likeness, too. They're hunting her by face now, not just name. And Grebe's holding the door open for them unless I buy him off." She watched a gull hang motionless over the water. "I could give him the notes' hiding place and keep you. I thought about it. I'll admit I thought about it hard, and I'm not proud of how long." The gull screamed somewhere. "But here's the thing, love. If I feed Grebe, he feeds the Bureau, and the Bureau takes the atlas, and then those drowned streets stay drowned twice over—once in water and once in ink. And the girl goes in a cart. And the old woman dies in a cell of a question she asked too loudly. And the clerk hangs, probably, because clerks always hang first." She paused. "Whereas if I give him the boat, he gets a hull he can sell for more than his finder's fee, and he walks, because Grebe was never loyal to anything except getting paid, and a bird in the hand has always been scripture to him."

The tide turned beneath them. She felt it in the hull, that faint shudder and swing, the sea changing its mind.

"It's not sentiment," she told Denny, and found, to her surprise, that it was mostly true. "Sentiment would be keeping you and losing them. This is just ship's math. Lighten the load or sink with it." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, once, and put the tiller over. "Besides. You always said she was the finest hull in the harbor. Let her do one more job. Getting us clear counts as a job."

* * *

Thursday came in with fog, thick as wool, the kind of morning when the harbor bells sounded like they were ringing underwater. Marta had the kettle on and the chain off the door when Silas knocked—three, pause, two—and she opened it fully this time, which surprised him. She saw it surprise him, the small widening of his eyes before the charm came back down like a shopkeeper's shutter.

"Quill. I hoped you'd be sensible."

"I'm always sensible. It's my worst quality." She stepped back. "Come in. Have your tea. We'll do our arithmetic at the table like civilized thieves."

He entered her kitchen the way he'd wanted to all week, claiming it with his coat and his bulk, and she poured tea into her second-best cup and sat across from him with her own hands wrapped around the warmth, and laid it out.

"I can't give you the notes. Not because I'm brave—you'd laugh at how little bravery enters into it—but because I don't have them. They move. I move them. What I have is a place things pass through, and if I gave you a place, you'd get an empty room and my neck in a halter for my trouble. So that trade's off the table, and we both know you can't afford to wait on it, because your paymasters want results before the spring books close, and Grebe—" she looked at him steadily, "—Grebe has never once in his life waited for a payment he could avoid waiting for."

His eyes narrowed a fraction. Go on, the narrowing said.

"What I can give you is the *Kestrel*."

For the first time since he'd appeared on her step, Silas Grebe went completely still.

"She's worth forty crowns on a bad day and sixty on a good one, and any broker on Tanner's Row will confirm it. Clean papers, clean hull, new canvas two seasons back. No liens—Denny paid the boatwright's widow off before he died, God rest them both. You sell her in Vellamare, you risk questions. You sail her to Brancaster or up-coast and sell her there, she's yours entire, no history, no name attached. That's more than your finder's fee and none of the risk of dealing with the Bureau, who will, I promise you, cheat you. They cheat everyone. It's the one thing they're efficient at."

Silas set down his cup. When he spoke, his voice had lost its music. "That boat's your husband's."

"That boat's my husband's," Marta agreed. "Which is why the offer should tell you exactly how seriously I take you."

"Why?" And there it was—the real question, naked under the fog-light. "Why would you give up a thing like that for a girl you've known a season? For Ferrant, who'd sell her own teeth? For a census man?" He shook his head, genuinely baffled, and Marta understood suddenly that he had never once, in his whole life, understood why anyone did anything that wasn't purchase. It was the emptiest thing she had ever seen in a human face.

"Because they're mine," she said. "Not my property. My people. You wouldn't know the difference, so I won't waste breath explaining it. Take the boat or go to your paymasters, Silas. Those are the only two doors in this room."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him do the sums behind his eyes—the boat against the fee, the certain sale against the uncertain reward, the risk of a widow's knife against the comfort of a full purse. He had always been quick at sums. It was the only kind of quickness he had.

"The boat," he said finally. "Papers signed today. I sail her on tomorrow's tide. And Quill—if your name comes up again, from anyone, in any way, I never heard of you. That's part of the price. I'm buying my own silence along with your hull."

"I know how you work, Grebe. You've never heard of anybody."

He smiled at that—almost, for a heartbeat, the old smile from the good years at Pier Nine, when they'd all been younger and the water hadn't come—and then he finished his tea, signed the bill of sale she'd written the night before in her careful ledger hand, and left her kitchen without looking back.

Marta sat for a while with the cooling pot. Then she took the bill of sale, read it once, and burned it in the stove, because the copy in Silas's coat was the one that mattered and she saw no reason to keep evidence of her own surrender lying about.

* * *

She sailed the *Kestrel* out to the point at dusk to hand her over, because she would not watch Silas board her at the mooring like a thief taking possession. The fog had lifted and the evening was painfully clear, the sky going the color of a bruise behind the western hills, the first lamps of Vellamare pricking alight along the crescent of the shore. From the point you could see the whole harbor, and beyond the harbor wall, the flat gray reach of water that had been Saltmarsh, marked now only by the tips of the old church tower breaking the surface at low tide like a finger pointing at the sky.

Silas came out in a hired dinghy, hauled himself aboard with a seaman's economy he had never lost, and looked around the deck with open, greedy appreciation.

"She's sweet," he admitted. "Always was. Denny kept her like a mistress."

"He kept you like one too," Marta said. "Look how that ended."

Silas laughed, cast off the spring line she handed him, and stood a moment at the tiller—Denny's tiller—with the boat swinging gently toward the open water. Something crossed his face then, quickly, some brief shadow that might in a better man have been shame.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'd have hated to see you hang."

"No, you wouldn't. But it's worth something that you lied about it competently." She stepped back onto the point, onto the shingle, done. "Sail her kindly, Grebe. She remembers rough handling."

He put the tiller over, and the *Kestrel* leaned her shoulder to the wind and went, black hull going darker as the light failed, running clean and lovely out past the mole and into the open water, and Marta stood on the shingle in the cold and watched until there was nothing left to see but the last lamp at her stern, and then a darkness, and then not even that.

She did not cry. She had done her crying eleven years ago, in quantity, and had found it poor currency. Instead she stood a while longer, letting the wind have her coat, doing what sailors' wives had always done at this shore: converting a loss into a story she could carry.

Then she turned and walked back along the seawall toward the town, toward the lamplit lanes, toward the boathouse where a girl was copying drowned streets by candlelight and an old archivist was arguing with a fever and a census clerk was learning to rule lines straight—and she climbed the stairs to the boathouse loft and put her key in the door with a steady hand.

Ada looked up from the copying table, pen poised, and frowned at once, because the girl missed nothing. "You're late. And you're wet to the shoulders. And—" she stopped. "Marta. What happened?"

"A deal went through," Marta said, hanging her coat on the peg, voice level as a spirit gauge. "Nothing that touches us. Put the kettle on."

It was, she reflected, almost entirely true. Nothing that touched them. Only something that had held them, once, and carried them, and kept them, and now carried a stranger's greed up the coast into the dark.

She was reaching for the kettle when the knock came—soft, hurried, wrong-rhythmed—and Ada opened the door on Elias Wren, hatless, breathless, fog-wet, with a look on his face that emptied the room of air before he said a word.

"They've got Tam," he said.
