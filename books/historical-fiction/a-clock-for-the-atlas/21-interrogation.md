## 21. Interrogation

The summons came by hand, which was how Elias knew it was serious. Routine matters traveled through the Bureau's pneumatic tubes in wax-sealed cylinders that rattled down into the brass baskets of each department like coins into a till. A summons carried by a runner—a boy of perhaps fourteen with rain in his collar and an official's boredom on his face—meant someone wanted no record in the tubes. Someone wanted this quiet.

*Mr. E. Wren. Enumerator, Second District. You will present yourself at the Bureau of Records, Sub-Level Three, tomorrow morning at the ninth hour. Bring your field ledger.*

Sub-Level Three. In six years of service Elias had been to Sub-Level Three once, for his oath of office, when he had stood in a corridor the color of old teeth and repeated words about fidelity to the record while a clerk with a monotone watched him not as a man but as a form awaiting completion. Nobody was summoned back to Sub-Level Three for anything good.

He read the note twice at his kitchen table, then folded it into quarters and burned it in the stove, because Marta Quill had taught him—without meaning to teach him anything, simply by being what she was—that paper was evidence and evidence was a thing you controlled or a thing that controlled you.

Then he took out his field ledger and sat with it until the lamp burned low.

* * *

He did not sleep so much as lie awake conducting his own examination, question after question fired at himself in the Bureau's flat cadence, and he catalogued his answers the way he catalogued doors: honestly, thoroughly, and with an eye toward which ones would hold if someone leaned on them.

Enumerator Wren. Second District. Six years' service. Commendations for thoroughness, 1871 and 1873. No disciplinary actions. Unmarried. One brother—no. Strike that. The brother was struck from every file years ago, erased with the particular efficiency the Bureau reserved for its own embarrassments. Tomas Wren, junior archivist, Fourth Stacks, who had asked one too many questions about the provenance of the Saltmarsh flood records and who had subsequently been transferred, reassigned, relocated—and then one day simply was not anywhere at all, in any record Elias could find or buy or steal. His mother had died not knowing. His father had died not asking. And Elias had put on his Bureau coat every morning since and counted the citizens of Vellamare as though counting were a neutral act, as though numbers did not become lists and lists did not become doors knocked upon in the night.

He had told himself for six years that the counting kept people safe. Enumerated persons were persons who existed. Persons who existed could not vanish without a discrepancy appearing somewhere, in some column, in some clerk's tidy hand. He had built a whole theology out of arithmetic.

And then he had walked up four flights to a shuttered map shop with a pretext about verifying occupancy of commercial premises, and he had seen Ada Sandoval—or whatever her true name was—go white to the lips at a single question, and he had understood with terrible clarity that he was not a keeper of records. He was a hound. And somewhere upstairs, men who had never met her were deciding whether she should exist.

His report lay unfinished in the drawer. *Subject appears undocumented. Subject declined to state surname. Subject's apprenticeship under the late Master Corvane cannot be verified against Bureau rolls.* Every word true. Every word a knife he had refused, so far, to sharpen and use. He had flagged nothing, submitted nothing—but he had drafted it, and drafts lived in drawers, and drawers could be opened.

That was the thought that finally drove him out of bed at four in the morning to dress in the dark: not that he had betrayed her, but that his hesitation might do it for him.

* * *

The Bureau of Records occupied a block of pale granite near the customs house, and its grand floors—the reading rooms, the registry halls where citizens came to be entered into the great ledgers of the state—were all marble and light and the hush of civic dignity. Sub-Level Three was none of these things. Elias descended in a cage lift operated by a man who did not greet him, past floors he had never known existed, and stepped out into a corridor lit by frosted globes that gave light without warmth, the way a ledger gave knowledge without mercy.

A door stood open at the corridor's end. Beyond it: a table, two chairs, a carafe of water, two glasses, and a man rising to shake his hand.

"Elias Wren." The man's grip was dry and brief. "Deputy Controller Harrow. Sit, please. Thank you for coming promptly."

Harrow was fifty-something, silver at the temples, with the soft well-kept hands of a man whose violence was entirely administrative. That was the thing about the Bureau, Elias thought, sitting, unfolding his ledger on the table like an offering. It never raised its voice. It simply wrote, and what it wrote came true, or people stopped existing to contradict it.

"You've been with us six years," Harrow said, settling across from him and opening a thin folder—not Elias's file, or not much of one; a working folder, the kind assembled quickly. "Second District. Your supervisor speaks well of you. Thorough. Discreet." A small smile. "Discreet is a quality we prize."

"I try to be accurate," Elias said. His voice surprised him by holding steady. Beneath the table, his hands did not.

"Accuracy." Harrow nodded as though tasting the word and finding it adequate. "Then let's be accurate together." He turned the folder around and slid a sheet from it. "This is your draft report on premises at Corvane and Sons, Cartographers, Lantern Street. Drafted—" he consulted something, though Elias suspected he knew it by heart, "—the eleventh of last month. Never submitted."

Elias looked at the page. His own handwriting, copied without his knowledge—which meant his desk drawer, his locked drawer, or at least a drawer someone else's lock had opened as easily as breath. He felt the floor of his stomach give way, quietly, the way a pier gives way when the worms have done their work. But his face, thank whatever gods watch over liars and clerks, stayed composed. He had learned composure the way everyone in Vellamare learned it: from necessity.

"That's mine," he said.

"It's yours. Why wasn't it filed?"

Here it was, then. The razor. He had walked the streets of the city all night rehearsing for this moment, and now that it arrived he found the rehearsal both useless and indispensable—useless because no imagined scene matched the soft horror of Harrow's patient face, indispensable because the shape of his answer had been forged in those dark hours and all he had to do was pour himself into it.

He let the silence sit exactly long enough to suggest reluctance, then spoke.

"The report was defective, sir. I withdrew it for correction."

"Defective how?"

"My verification was incomplete." He kept his eyes level, kept them on Harrow's collar rather than his face—he had learned in depositions that a fixed gaze read either as honesty or challenge and there was no telling which, whereas a gaze slightly lowered read as deference, and deference was a language this building spoke fluently. "I identified the subject as likely undocumented and recommended further inquiry. On review I found I couldn't support the finding. The woman claims to have been apprenticed under Corvane before his death. Apprenticeship records for that period are—"

"—incomplete. Yes." Harrow laced his fingers. "We know. We've known for some time that certain records from that district were lost. Or misfiled. Or mislaid." The words were gentle and each one landed like a small calibrated weight. "You went back to the shop, Mr. Wren. After drafting the report. More than once."

Not a question. Elias drank water because his throat demanded it and because drinking water bought three seconds, and three seconds was often the distance between a good answer and a ruined life.

"To close out the entry. An enumerator can't leave a premises unresolved; you taught us that in training. I needed either to confirm the subject's status or mark the household unverifiable."

"And which did you do?"

"Unverifiable." He said it steadily. "I marked it unverifiable and closed the file."

Harrow regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere above them, muffled through granite, the city went about its business—carts, gulls, the ten o'clock bell rehearsal at Saint Brannoc's, ordinary life continuing in a world where this room did not exist. Then Harrow smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was somehow worse.

"Let me tell you what I think, Mr. Wren, and you can tell me if I'm wrong. I think you're a careful man who made an error of judgment. I think you developed a sympathy for the subject—she's young, she's alone, she has a sad story, dead master, orphan apprentice, very moving—and your sentimentality interfered with your professional duty. It happens. More often than you'd believe. The census touches nerves. People look at a face and forget they're looking at a file." He closed the folder. "Sentimentality is forgivable. It's even, in small doses, human. What isn't forgivable is concealment. If you'd come to us when you first had doubts, this conversation would be happening in my office upstairs, with coffee."

"And instead?"

"Instead we're down here." Harrow spread his hands, a gesture of regret so practiced it might have been typeset. "So here is what happens next. You will complete and submit a corrected report by Friday. You will include everything you know about the subject: description, habits, associates, the current location of the premises and its contents. And you will sign a statement attesting that your delay was due to negligence alone. Negligence, Mr. Wren. Not sympathy. Negligence carries a reprimand. Sympathy in an officer of this Bureau carries other consequences."

Elias understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that Harrow was offering him a script. Confess to being a fool rather than a friend, and live. The Bureau preferred fools; fools were predictable, fools could be promoted, fools made excellent instruments precisely because they believed themselves merely clumsy.

He also understood that the offer contained a hook dressed as mercy. Everything you know. Associates. Contents. They wanted the shop's inventory—they wanted the notes—and they wanted the web of people around Ada, and they intended to harvest it from his own hand, with his own signature, so that when the net closed it would be his handwriting on it.

"I'll need until Friday," he said. "To be accurate."

Harrow's eyes glinted. "Friday. Not a day later." He rose, extended the dry hand once more. "One more thing, and this is off the record, between clerks of the same house." He paused at the door, and for a moment something moved behind his official face—an expression Elias might have mistaken for humanity if he hadn't already seen what it was selling. "Your brother's file crossed my desk once. Years ago. Tomas, was it? A pity. Bright man. Asked questions that weren't his to ask." A beat. "Questions are like debts in this city, Wren. Somebody always collects."

He left. The lift cage rattled upward. Elias stood alone in the room with the two glasses of water, one untouched, and found that his hands, released from their work, had begun at last to shake.

* * *

He walked for an hour afterward without deciding to, his feet carrying him through streets he knew by door-knockers and lintel-marks, up toward the harbor, along the seawall above Saltmarsh. The drowned quarter lay beneath the grey water like furniture under a dusty sheet—the tips of old chimneys visible at low tide, the crooked crown of the Pier Nine crane standing out of the sea at an angle of permanent accusation. Ten years the water had covered it. Ten years the official histories had said *catastrophic failure of the levees during rebel occupation,* and ten years the survivors had said nothing, because grief with no proof is just noise to power, and power does not even bother to be bored by it.

He had counted Saltmarsh's displaced, in his second year. Four hundred and eleven households scattered across the city's edges, entered in his neat columns, occupation recorded, dependents recorded, place of birth recorded—*Saltmarsh, Saltmarsh, Saltmarsh*—a litany in a ledger nobody read. He had thought, then, that recording them was preserving them.

Now he stood at the rail with the wind pulling at his coat and understood that he had been filing the evidence of a crime as though filing were justice, and that somewhere below the surface, among the drowned doorways, the dead were waiting—with what patience, with what contempt—to see whether anyone would ever testify.

Negligence, Harrow had said. Sign here.

Tomas had asked questions. Questions are debts. Somebody always collects.

Elias took out his notebook, and standing at the seawall rail with the spray drifting cold against his face, he did the most dangerous thing he had ever done in six years of careful, invisible, complicit service: he began writing the truth. Not the corrected report Harrow wanted—not descriptions, not associates, not the location of the notes—but an account, dated and signed, of everything he knew about the Bureau's interest in the Corvane materials, the copying of his drawer, the offer made in Sub-Level Three, the threat dressed in his brother's name. A deposition, Odile Ferrant would have called it. Primary source. Chain of custody begins with the witness.

If they collected his debt, let them collect it with this in the world. Let there be a record. He was a census taker; counting was the only prayer he knew, and this was what he chose to count: the facts, all of them, in his own hand, witnessed by the drowned quarter and the grey indifferent sea.

When he finished, his fingers were numb and his heart was strangely, terribly light—the freedom he had heard described by men stepping off cliffs, presumably. He had chosen his side not in some blazing moment of heroism but in increments: an unfiled report, a lie of omission, a night of walking, and now ink on paper at a seawall rail. Choice turned out not to be a door but a staircase, and he had been descending it for months without looking down.

He tucked the pages inside his shirt, next to his skin, where paper could be felt beating against the pulse.

There was just enough daylight left to cross the city. He went first to a stationer on Cooper's Lane and bought, with coins from his own pocket, a second notebook identical to his field ledger, and a bottle of the cheap violet ink the Bureau used, and he spent the evening at his kitchen table practicing his own handwriting—loosening it, sloppying it, teaching his disciplined hand to make the small honest errors of haste—because Friday would come, and Harrow would demand his report, and Elias Wren intended to deliver one: complete, signed, meticulously inaccurate. A false trail drawn with a true pen, leading the Bureau away from a shuttered shop on Lantern Street and a girl who trusted lines more than people, and toward a district on the far side of the harbor where, by remarkable coincidence, a vacant warehouse had recently changed hands.

It was, he reflected, dipping the pen again, the first map he had ever drawn. And like all maps, it was an argument about who belonged—and who had better run.

He was sealing the finished forgery in an envelope when the knock came at his own door: three knocks, pause, two more. Harbor rhythm. Marta's rhythm.

He opened it on Ada Sandoval, hood up, face pale as tide-foam, and behind her the fog swallowing the streetlamp whole.

"They took the plates," she said, before he could speak. "But that's not why I'm here." She glanced past him into the room, at the ledger open on the table, at the violet ink still wet on the nib, and something in her eyes sharpened from fear into calculation. "Marta says the Bureau moved against the boathouse within a day of your visit to Sub-Level Three. She wants to know what you told them." A breath, fog-white between them. "I want to know what they made you promise."
