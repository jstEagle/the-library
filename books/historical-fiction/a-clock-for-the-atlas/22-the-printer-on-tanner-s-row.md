## 22. The Printer on Tanner's Row

The printer's name was Hollis, and he worked at the bottom of Tanner's Row, where the street narrowed and the smell of tanning lye gave way to the sweeter rot of ink and lamp oil. His shop was the last before the drainage ditch, wedged between a bone-boiler's yard and a shuttered chandlery, and it had no sign hanging above the door—only a faded rectangle of lighter wood where a sign had once hung and been taken down. Odile said that absence was the first good thing about him. A man who removed his own name from his own door, she said, was a man who understood discretion as a trade rather than a virtue.

They went to see him in the gray middle of a Wednesday morning, Ada and Marta, leaving Elias and Tam behind on the pretext of mending nets. It had been Odile's insistence: never bring more people than a meeting requires. *Every extra body is a witness,* she had said, propped on her pillows with her seal resting on the blanket like a sleeping animal. *And witnesses have a way of becoming confessions.*

Ada had expected a printer's shop to look like Corvane's map room turned sideways—order, precision, everything squared to an invisible grid. Hollis's shop was nothing of the kind. Type was everywhere, in cases stacked three deep and in wooden frames piled against the wall like firewood; paper hung from lines strung across the ceiling, drying in sheets that brushed their heads as they walked; the floor was a drift of paper trimmings, misprints, and spent wicks. In the middle of it all sat a press the size of an ox, black with decades of ink, its iron screw gleaming where a hundred thousand hands had worn the rest dull.

Hollis himself was smaller than Ada expected—a compact, balding man with ink worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that his hands looked older than his face. He did not rise when they entered. He was setting type by hand, plucking letters one by one from a case with the absent speed of long habit, and he finished his line before he looked up.

"Closed," he said.

"We haven't asked you anything yet," Marta said.

"Whatever it is, I'm closed."

Marta set a coin on the nearest flat surface—not gold, only silver, but enough of it—and let it sit there shining. Hollis glanced at it the way a hungry man glances at bread he has told himself he cannot afford.

"I know what you're going to say," he said. "You'll say it's honest work. Printing's honest work. Set the type, pull the lever, take the money, don't ask." He wiped his hands on his apron, which only moved the ink around. "I printed pamphlets during the war. Both sides. You know what happened to the printers who guessed wrong? There's a list. I've seen it. My name was on it for six years, and then some clerk died and a new clerk didn't care, and now I print hymnals and customs manifests and auction catalogs, and nobody remembers me, and that is worth more to me than every coin in Vellamare harbor."

"Then we won't ask you to remember us," Ada said.

It was the first thing she'd said since entering, and Hollis looked at her properly for the first time. She had thought hard about how to do this—had rehearsed it in front of the boathouse window until Marta told her to stop frightening the gulls. Not pleading. Pleading invited contempt. Not bargaining. Bargaining invited suspicion. Just the plain statement of a fact, laid out like a line on a chart.

"You don't need to know what you're printing," Ada went on. "You don't need to know who we are. You need to know three things. One: it's maps and text, forty pages, an edition of two hundred. Two: it will be distributed by other hands, not yours—you'll never see where it goes. Three—" she paused, and made herself say it plainly— "you'll be paid for risk, not just work. We're not pretending this is safe. We're paying for the danger, up front, because we can't pay for it after."

Silence. From the lines above them, a sheet of drying paper turned slowly on itself, like something listening.

Hollis took the silver coin off the table, weighed it, and set it back down.

"That's a deposit," he said. "Not an answer. Come back tomorrow."

* * *

He named his price the next morning, and it was ruinous.

"Three hundred crowns," he said, without any change in his voice, as though naming the cost of a loaf. "Half before I set the first line. And I keep my name off it—the colophon stays blank. And if anyone asks, and anyone will ask, I printed it for a merchant consortium out of Callow's Reach, and I never saw your faces."

"Three hundred," Marta repeated flatly. "That's a boat. That's two boats."

"That's my life, madam, priced honestly. The Bureau pays informers ten crowns a head for names on pamphlet lists. I've seen men hanged over printing that mattered less than whatever it is you're hiding under your cloak right now." His eyes flicked, just once, to the wrapped bundle beneath Ada's arm, and away again with the practiced blindness of a man who survived by not seeing. "Two hundred and fifty, and I'll run it nights, myself alone, and burn the type afterward. That's my floor. Below that, go find a fool."

They found themselves standing outside in the thin rain, and neither spoke for a moment.

"He's not cheating us," Marta said finally. "That's the worst of it. He's pricing it fair."

Ada looked down Tanner's Row, at the wet cobbles shining brown as old ink, and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for a week. They had gathered, between the five of them, a little over sixty crowns. Marta's hidden savings, meant for her old age. Odile's pension, hoarded out of a bitterness that now seemed prophetic. Elias's salary, offered with a shame he could barely speak around. Tam had given eleven crowns in coins saved across ten years of silence, wrapped in oilcloth as though the money itself were guilty.

One hundred and ninety crowns short. Nearly two hundred.

There was exactly one place left to find it.

* * *

The silver had sat in the map shop's back room for as long as Ada could remember—since before her time, since before the war perhaps. A tea service that Master Corvane never used: a great tray, a coffeepot with a swan-neck spout, a sugar bowl with a hinged lid, four cups, cream jug, tongs, all of it stamped with a crest that was not Corvane's crest. Someone else's inheritance, sold or lost or abandoned generations back and absorbed into the shop the way the shop absorbed everything—quietly, completely, without asking questions.

Ada had dusted it every month for nine years. She had never once used it.

She took it down from the high shelf on a Friday evening, when the light through the shutters had gone the color of tarnish, and laid it out on the worktable on a square of velvet she used for cleaning lenses. Piece by piece. The tray last, because it was the heaviest and because she kept finding reasons to handle the smaller things first.

Her fingers knew the weight of each piece better than her memory did. Nine years of dusting will do that—teach your hands an object while your mind keeps its distance. The coffeepot balanced perfectly when lifted by its handle, no wobble, the casting true. The sugar bowl's hinge still moved without a sound. Whoever had made these things had cared about hinges, about balance, about the small invisible virtues that only revealed themselves in use. It had been made to be used, and passed on, and used again, and instead it had sat in a dark room above a map shop through a civil war and a flood and the slow erasure of a district, gathering dust in the shape of its own engraving.

"There you are," she said softly, to it, to the room, to the dead man whose silence filled it. "I'm sorry it took me so long to find a use for you."

Because that was the truth underneath everything else she felt: Corvane would have wanted this. Not the sentiment of it—he had been incapable of sentiment before noon—but the logic. Silver sitting idle in a cupboard while truth went unprinted was a misallocation of resources, and Corvane had held misallocation to be very nearly a moral crime. He had taught her that a map unused was a lie by omission. He would have carried the tray down Tanner's Row himself, grumbling about the weight.

But there were other things. Smaller things, and harder.

Her mother's ring—or the ring she believed had been her mother's, salvaged from the one bag that survived the workhouse years, a thin band of soft gold with a garnet gone cloudy with age. She had never worn it. Wearing it would have meant admitting it was hers to wear, and she had spent her whole adult life admitting to nothing.

Her tools—no. Not the tools. She drew the line at the tools; the ruling pens and the burnisher and the fine German dividers stayed, because selling those would be like selling her hands. But the little brass instrument case with the ivory scales, the one Corvane had given her the day she finished her apprenticeship charts—that could go. That should go. An apprentice's set, superseded, duplicating tools she already owned. Practical. Sensible. She repeated the words until they almost stopped sounding like excuses.

And the last thing, the one she handled longest: a single loose plate, copper, etched with the coastline from Hallow Point to the Saltmarsh seawall as it had looked *before*. Her master's finest work, she had always thought. Every wharf, every lock gate, every stair that ran down to the water, drawn with such tenderness that the plate itself seemed to grieve. It was one of a kind. It was also, Odile had told her weeks ago, worthless to the cause—the Bureau had the plates from the raid, and this one had never been part of the master atlas anyway. It was beautiful, and it was past, and past was precisely the currency this enterprise required her to spend.

A copper plate weighs nearly two pounds. Ada learned that afternoon that two pounds is very heavy, if you carry it far enough.

* * *

The assayer on Cordwainer Lane weighed everything twice, wrote figures on a slate, rubbed out one figure, wrote it again, and pushed the total toward her without ceremony. Ada watched him handle her mother's ring—turning it under his lens, checking the garnet with a jeweler's probe—and felt nothing, and then felt the nothing arrive all at once, delayed like thunder after lightning.

"Forty-two crowns for the lot," the assayer said. "The copper I'll give you scrap price—it's got sentimental value to somebody, I can see that, but sentiment doesn't melt. Thirty-eight without the plate."

"The plate goes with everything," Ada said. "Or nothing."

He shrugged, which in assayers is a signature. Forty-two crowns. Added to the group's sixty-odd, it made barely half of what Hollis demanded.

It was Marta who solved the rest, and she solved it the way she solved everything: by boat. "I've got a cargo hold's worth of goodwill scattered from here to Callow's Reach," she said, when Ada came back to the boathouse pale with the day's spending. "People owe me favors the way the tide owes the moon. Some of those favors can be converted." She counted out coins onto the table, coin by coin, deliberately, letting each one click. "Don't look at me like that. I've been meaning to spend myself down for years anyway. What's the point of dying rich in a city that lies to poor people?"

By Sunday night the full sum lay in a canvas sack on Odile's blanket, and Odile, too weak to sit up but too invested to sleep, counted it twice with a shaking forefinger and pronounced it sufficient.

"Three hundred crowns," she said, settling back against her pillows, "to commit treason in forty pages." A dry rattle that was her version of laughter. "In my day we subverted the state for the glory of the archive. Now it costs capital. Everything degrades, even sedition."

* * *

They paid Hollis on a Tuesday, in his back room, counting the coins out onto the same scarred table where he set his type. He counted it too—all three hundred, twice, with the fluency of a man who trusted no one's arithmetic but his own—and then he swept it into a drawer and looked at Ada with the first directness he had shown her.

"Now," he said. "Show me what I'm printing."

She unwrapped the copy-master they had prepared—the fair copy of the atlas, page proofs of the testimonies, the whole architecture of the truth arranged in sequence. She had expected him to leaf through it the way merchants leafed through invoices: quickly, professionally, seeing paper and not content.

Instead he read.

He read the title page for a long time. He turned to the first map—Saltmarsh entire, streets named, wharves named, every stair and lock gate restored to existence in ink—and his thumb came to rest at the edge of the page, very still. He turned pages. The survey notes reproduced in facsimile. Tam Osei's sworn statement. The chain-of-provenance documents bearing Odile Ferrant's seal. The second map, showing the levee cut from the landward side, the cut marked in red, annotated in a hand the colophon would identify only as *the cartographer's*.

When he reached the end, he sat looking at the closed cover for the space of ten breaths.

"I printed customs manifests for the grain ships," he said quietly, "the winter after the flood. Grain shipped *out* of Vellamare that year. Record quantities." He tapped the atlas copy once, with one ink-stained finger. "I always wondered who ate, that winter, while the quarter drowned. Now I know who starved, and why, and whose arithmetic did it." He stood, and buttoned his apron, and became briskly professional again, as though the previous minute had been a misprint he had caught and corrected. "Come back in twelve days. Two hundred copies. Blank colophon, like we agreed." He paused at the door to the pressroom. "The type gets melted after. No reprints. If you want more, you come back and pay me again, and pray I'm still alive."

* * *

Twelve days. Odile, hearing the number, closed her eyes and performed a calculation of her own, lips moving silently. When she opened them there was something new in her face—not hope exactly, but the cold bright neighbor of hope.

"The official history ships in nineteen days," she said. "Elias confirmed it Thursday—the crates are being crated at the ministry bindery now. Nineteen days, minus twelve for printing, leaves seven for distribution." Her eyes found Ada's. "Seven days to nail the truth to every door in this city, child. Seven days before the lie arrives dressed in leather and gold stamping and sits on every respectable shelf in Vellamare." She pulled the blanket higher, as though against a draft that came from the future rather than the window. "Which means we begin planning tonight. Which means, Ada—" and here her voice dropped into the register she used for lessons, the archivist's voice, the one that had survived purges and disgrace and now illness itself— "which means we must build the map of doors. And I think it is time you told me exactly how many friends you believe this city truly has."

Ada looked at the canvas sack, empty now, folded small beside the bed. Everything they had was spent. Everything that remained was them.

"More than you think," she said. "Fewer than we need. Let's find out which."
