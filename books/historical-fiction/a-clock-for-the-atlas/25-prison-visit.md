## 25. Prison Visit

The tide did not care what the Bureau had taken. That was the first mercy of the morning, and Ada clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood: unreasonably, gratefully, with salt in her throat. She stood at Marta's window overlooking the harbor basin and watched the water rise against the pilings exactly as it had risen every day of her life, indifferent to raids and prison walls and the fact that Tam Osei had been in the Custody House on Cordon Street for three nights now because he had signed his name to the truth.

Three nights. She had counted them the way she once counted hachures on a hillside, one stroke after another, precise and useless.

"He's not eating," Marta said behind her. She came into the room carrying two chipped cups of chicory coffee and pressed one into Ada's hands without ceremony. "I paid the turnkey's wife to look in on him. He takes the bread. Leaves the fish. Says it smells like the flood."

Ada's stomach turned. Of course it did. Everything in Vellamare smelled like the flood if you had been standing on Pier Nine when the water came through, and Tam had been standing there. Ten years of silence had bought him nothing but this: a cell, a fish he couldn't eat, and a signature that made him dangerous.

"Then we get him out fast," Ada said.

"Faster than fast." Marta lowered herself onto the bench by the cold hearth. In the grey morning light her face looked older than it had at any point since the boathouse raid—drawn around the mouth, bruised under the eyes. Guilt sat on her like a coat she couldn't take off. It was her old partner, Selly Wick, who had sold them; her debt, her history, her mistake. She had not said so aloud. She didn't need to. It was in every pot of stew she'd made since, every extra blanket she'd dragged up from the hold of the *Perpetua*, every hour she spent at the harbor talking low and fast to people whose names she wouldn't give even in a whisper.

"I've been round to the fishwives," Marta said now. "Half the women who sell on the south steps have husbands or brothers or sons who've done a stretch in Cordon Street. There isn't a wall in that building doesn't have a crack in it, and I don't mean in the mortar." She blew on her coffee. "Messages go in. Not often, not clean, but they go in. Bread with a note baked into the crust. Laundry lists that aren't laundry lists. A warder who owes me from 'sixty-nine and hates his supervisor more than he fears the ministry. It can be done."

"Then we send word tonight."

"We send word tonight," Marta agreed, "and you write it, because my hand shakes and yours doesn't, and whatever goes into that cell has to be steady enough to keep him silent."

Ada set down her cup. Her hands *didn't* shake—that was true—but only because she had spent ten years training them not to, ten years of holding a pen over vellum while her heart did whatever it pleased underneath. Steadiness was a craft like anything else. You practiced it until it became the shape of you.

She took paper from her satchel—the good rag paper, the kind that wouldn't dissolve if it got damp in a guard's pocket—and thought about what a man three days into silence needed to hear.

Not everything. That was the first rule of small maps: leave out what will get the reader lost. She could not tell him about Elias, or the proof of the official history, or the printer on Tanner's Row waiting on their coin. She could not tell him about the sketch of her own face circulating through the wards, or the safehouse they'd moved to twice since the raid. Every word was a risk not only to Tam but to whoever carried the paper past the warders, and carriers broke, carriers always could break.

So she wrote six lines.

*They did not take the true thing. The copies are safe and multiplying. Your statement stands; no one can unmake it but you. Hold your silence as you held the sluice gates—not forever, only until relief comes. We have not forgotten how to open doors in this city. — A friend of the atlas.*

She read it twice. Then she burned the draft in the hearth and copied the final version out clean, in a hand that was not hers—a round, clerkish hand she had learned by forging harbor manifests for Corvane during the embargo years—and folded it along the grain so it would sit flat inside a loaf.

Marta watched her work and said nothing until the wax was cooled on the fold. Then: "'A friend of the atlas.'" A ghost of a smile. "You're getting romantic in your old age."

"I'm getting careful in my old age," Ada said. "It looks similar from a distance."

* * *

The loaf went in at noon, carried under a fishwife's shawl by a woman named Bree Halloran who had lost a brother in Saltmarsh and asked no questions, which made her, in Marta's arithmetic, the safest kind of courier alive.

Ada did not go to Cordon Street herself. She wanted to—with an ache that sat in her chest like a splinter she could not work loose, she wanted to stand outside those walls all day if that was what loyalty cost—but wanting was not doing, and doing was this: staying hidden, staying useful, keeping the face from the Bureau sketches off the street where a warder's wife might see it and remember. So she stayed in the safehouse attic above the rope works while Marta ran the errand, and she worked, because work was the only prayer she knew.

The copied atlases lay in pickle barrels in a cellar two streets away, but the master's plates—the ones the raiders had missed, the early coastlines Corvane had cut before the war ended and everything went wrong—were here, wrapped in oilcloth under the eaves. She unwrapped them one at a time in the slanted light and checked each for damage, and as her fingers moved over the incised lines she talked to him in her head, the way she always did when the work got heavy.

*They took your finished plates, Master. The beautiful ones, the ones you pulled proofs of on Sunday mornings when the shop smelled of beeswax. But you taught me never to put the whole truth in one drawer, didn't you. You hid things the way other men hide sins. I'm only doing what you'd have done.*

The light moved across the floor. Somewhere below, the rope works groaned and hissed through its afternoon run. Ada inked a correction onto a proof sheet of the outer shoals, and her hand was steady, and she hated it a little—hated that grief and fear had never once managed to reach her hands, that they had to make do with her chest, her sleep, the spaces between sentences.

When Marta came back at dusk, Ada read the answer in her walk before she read it on her face: something had gone through.

Bree Halloran's cousin on D-ward had passed the bread. And three hours later, a scrap of paper no bigger than a thumbnail had come back out, tucked into the seam of a returned shirt collar.

Ada smoothed it flat on the table with both hands, the way she flattened a stubborn chart, and read:

*Held the gates before. Can hold again. Tell the girl from the seawall: page nine was always true. — T.O.*

For a long moment she could not see the words for the water in her eyes.

Page nine. She knew it by heart. Every map in the atlas had its argument, but page nine—the Saltmarsh quarter, surveyed street by street in Corvane's smallest, finest hand, every drowned house named—was the page Tam's statement anchored. *Was always true.* Not *is*. As if the map existed somewhere whole and permanent already, and all any of them were doing—all any cartographer had ever done—was uncovering it.

"Tell the girl from the seawall," she repeated aloud, and her voice cracked on the last syllable, and Marta, who never remarked on anyone's tears, turned tactfully to bank the fire.

* * *

Elias Wren had learned that the Bureau of Records ran on paper the way a mill ran on water, and that the paper could be dammed.

He sat at his desk in the census annex with his fourth report of the season half-drafted before him and the gaslight buzzing overhead, and he performed, for the benefit of anyone glancing at his ledger, the small theater of diligence: columns ruled, households numbered, marginalia neat. His supervisor, Undersecretary Corbett, liked neat marginalia. Corbett liked to walk the rows of desks after lunch and read over shoulders, harvesting small disloyalties the way other men picked pockets, and so Elias gave him a harvest of nothing—accurate counts, punctual filings, and not one word about a mapmaker's apprentice whose file Corbett had flagged eleven days ago with a red seal that meant *pursue*.

Eleven days. Elias had lain awake most of them, staring at the ceiling of his rooms on Chandler Lane, doing sums that would not resolve. Because he knew now—he had known since the raid—what the red seal had purchased. Corbett had used Elias's report as a compass point. *An undocumented apprentice in the late Corvane's shop, skilled, evasive about provenance.* Two weeks later, agents had kicked in the shop door and carried off a dead man's art. Elias had drawn the map. They had marched.

His brother had gone into a records purge twelve years ago with nothing but a name and a wrong address attached to it, and had come out of it nowhere at all—no cell Elias could visit, no grave Elias could tend, a man subtracted. Their mother had died still setting a place for him at table. And Elias had joined the Bureau afterward believing, in the way of young men who cannot bear chaos, that the problem had been sloppiness—that if the records were kept *well*, kept truly, no one would ever be subtracted by accident again. He had spent fourteen years being precise in the service of a machine that was not sloppy at all. It subtracted people on purpose.

The gas hissed. Corbett's footsteps creaked along the row, paused—Elias felt the pause between his shoulder blades like a hand—and moved on.

When the office emptied at seven, Elias did not leave. He sat in the blue dusk with his collar undone and worked out, for the hundredth time, the geometry of what he was about to do.

Tam Osei's intake papers had crossed the annex counter on the second day, misfiled—deliberately misfiled, Elias suspected, by a junior clerk whose sister sold mackerel on the south steps—into the civil-detention drawer rather than the security one. That misfiling was a door left open, and Elias intended to walk through it quietly and close it behind himself. Civil detainees were entitled to a documented interview within a fortnight: a census officer's confirmation of identity, household, and dependents, entered into the record. Routine. Bureaucratic. The sort of thing nobody read.

Except that the record was the one country Elias Wren still governed, and he had decided, in the small hours of the fourth sleepless night, to legislate there.

He drew a fresh form. *Interview of detainee re: household enumeration, pursuant to Statute 41.* He wrote Tam Osei's name in the header, and then, in the space meant for *dependents and persons claiming connection*, he wrote—in the dry, unimpeachable language of his trade—a sentence that would live forever in the Bureau's own archive, copied, indexed, bound, immune to burning because there were too many copies already:

*Detainee attests prior residence, Saltmarsh quarter, Pier Nine district; attests presence at said location on the night of 14 Ventôse, Year II of the Union; attests knowledge of persons unknown operating the landward sluice mechanisms; documentation pending.*

It was not much. It was not a confession extracted, not testimony sworn. But it was ink in the machine's own blood, a fact wearing the Bureau's uniform, walking freely through its halls—and someday, when the atlas was on every church door and some future honest clerk went looking, that entry would be sitting in the files like a lit lamp, saying: *this was known. This was written down. Someone inside these walls wrote it down.*

Elias read it over four times. Then he signed it, dated it, and slid it into the tray for routine processing, where a tired night clerk would stamp it without reading and bind it by Friday.

His hands were shaking now. Of course they were. They had not been trained to steadiness the way hers were; they had only ever trained at counting.

He put on his coat and hat, nodded to the doorman, and walked out into the lamplit rain feeling lighter than he had any right to feel—as though betraying his employers were simply a chore he had finally gotten round to, like clearing a desk. At the corner of Cordon Street he stopped. The Custody House rose black against the wet sky, three storeys of shuttered windows, and somewhere behind the third window on the left, if the fishwife's information held, a lockkeeper was refusing his supper on principle.

"I'm coming," Elias said to the building, under his breath, in the voice he used for figures too large to say aloud. "However long it takes. I am coming with paper, which is worse."

* * *

The message reached Ada by the strangest route yet: not through Marta's smugglers but through the Bureau itself, delivered by Elias Wren in person, pale as milk, standing in the doorway of the rope-works attic at an hour when no respectable clerk should have known the address.

"How did you—" Ada began.

"Marta told me. After the kitchen." He came in slowly, dripping, hat in his hands, looking around the attic—at the plates, the inks, the proof sheets—as though he expected the room to arrest him. "I've filed an interview record on Tam. Statute 41. It puts his account of the levee into the Bureau's permanent archive, under their own stamp, before spring."

Ada stared at him. The gaslight popped. Outside, rain ran down the skylight in silver ropes.

"You put his *statement*—"

"Not the statement. An attestation that it exists and is pending. Enough." He swallowed. "It's done and it can't be recalled, so if you mean to be angry, be quick about it, because I also came to tell you the rest. Corbett's flagged file—it isn't only the notes anymore. There's a list. Targeted materials, priority seizure. Your atlas is on it, by description. And beneath it—" his voice dropped, and Ada saw that his hands, hanging at his sides, were trembling, "—beneath it is a line item for *apprehension and deposition of associated witnesses.* Plural. Osei. Ferrant. Quill." He met her eyes at last. "You're not on it yet. Only because they still think you're a boy."

The rain ticked against the glass. Ada found that she was not afraid—or she was, but the fear had gone quiet and structural, load-bearing, part of the building now. She thought of Tam's scrap of paper. *Held the gates before.*

"Then we know the shape of it," she said. "Thank you for the record, Mr. Wren. It was well drafted."

Something in his face broke open—relief, or gratitude, or the specific astonishment of a man thanked for treason. "I'll come again," he said. "When there's more."

"Do," Ada said, and closed the door softly behind him, and stood a long while in the lamplight with her hand flat against the wood, as if she could feel through it the pulse of the city—every hidden carrier, every misfiled page, every small brave act ticking away out of sight like the works of some vast patient clock.

Four days later, Bree Halloran brought out the next scrap from D-ward, and Ada read it by candle flame in Marta's kitchen, with Odile's cough rattling in the room above and Marta sharpening a boning knife that did not need sharpening.

*Word reaches me even here,* Tam had written, in a cramped hand that had clearly been written lying down. *Someone inside is keeping a record under their own name. Whoever he is, tell him: that is the bravest thing a man can do with paper, and I have seen men do brave things with less. Hold. I am holding. The gates open from the sea side, in my experience. Find the sea-side man. — T.O.*

Ada read it twice, then passed it to Marta, and watched the widow's eyebrows climb.

"The sea side," Marta said slowly. "The Custody House backs onto the old naval canal. Deliveries, coal, laundry barges." The knife stopped. "And the barge contracts for Corden Street went, three years back, to a man named Aurel Dann. Who owes me money, owes me *more* than money—" she smiled, and it was not a warm smile, it was the smile of a woman remembering she had been very good at something once, "—he owes me a favor with a hull and a cargo manifest, and a laundry barge goes through that sea gate every Thursday at dawn."

Ada looked at the fire. Looked at the scrap of paper in Marta's fist. Somewhere across the dark city, Tam Osei was lying on a plank bed holding silence like a breath; somewhere else, Elias Wren was binding his treason into the archives; and here in this kitchen, out of almost nothing—a debt, a barge, a tide table—something was assembling itself, line by line, the way a coastline assembles out of survey points that seemed meaningless until you joined them.

"Thursday," Ada said. "How many men fit in a laundry barge?"

Marta began, grimly, to laugh.
