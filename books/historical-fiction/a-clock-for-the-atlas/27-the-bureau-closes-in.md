## 27. The Bureau Closes In

The sketch went up on the fish market board on a Tuesday, between a notice about cholera quarantine and the price of salt herring.

Ada did not see it first. Tam's granddaughter did—a girl of nine with quick eyes and quicker feet, who came pelting up the seawall stairs to the garret where Ada had been sleeping for eleven nights, and burst through the door without knocking, which no one in Marta Quill's network ever did.

"There's a picture," the girl panted. "On the fish board. Of a woman."

Ada was already reaching for her coat. "Describe it."

"Dark hair, short-ish. A scar—" the girl hesitated, hunting the word— "here." She touched her own cheekbone. "And under it says *apprentice to the traitor Corvane*. There's money."

How much money, the girl didn't know, but she knew the shape of the face, and when she said *it looks a bit like you, miss*, she said it without fear, only curiosity, and that innocence cut worse than the notice itself.

Ada went down anyway. She had to see it. A hunted thing that refuses to look at its own likeness is already half-caught.

The market at mid-morning was all noise and brine and bargaining, and the paper on the board might have been any official notice if not for the drawing pinned above the text. It was done in charcoal by someone competent—not an artist of talent, perhaps, but an artist of accuracy. The hair was wrong, longer than hers had been since Odile took the scissors to it three weeks ago in Marta's kitchen. But the jaw was right. The set of the mouth, grave and slightly down-turned at one corner, was right. And there, high on the left cheekbone, was the scar—the thin white seam she'd carried since she was six years old and fell against a drafting-table corner in Master Corvane's shop, the one mark she had never once thought to hide because who notices a scar? Everyone, apparently. Someone had described her. Someone who had stood close enough to see her face and remembered it well enough to give it to a Bureau draftsman.

She made herself read the text beneath. *Information regarding the whereabouts of the unnamed apprentice of the late Edouard Corvane, wanted for questioning in matters of sedition and theft of state property. Reward: two hundred crowns. Report to any Bureau office or constable.*

Two hundred crowns. She thought of Marta's boat, of Odile's medicine, of the printer on Tanner's Row waiting for the last third of his payment. Two hundred crowns would buy all of it and leave change enough to buy a neighbor's silence too. That was the arithmetic the Bureau understood, and they were right to understand it. Not out of malice. Out of hunger. Out of the ordinary desperate arithmetic of a port city where half the population owed rent to the other half.

A fishwife beside her squinted at the sketch, then at the crowd, then at the sketch again. Ada turned away before the woman's gaze could travel far enough, and walked—slowly, unhurriedly, a woman with an errand and nothing to fear—out of the market square, past the customs house where another copy of the notice was already going up on the public boards, and did not run until she reached the alley behind the chandlery, and even then she only walked faster.

* * *

Marta heard the news before Ada finished telling it, which was usual; Marta heard most news twice over before it happened.

"Two hundred crowns." She said it the way other women said a curse word. "They've never posted more than fifty in my memory. Not for smugglers. Not for the men who burned the customs sloop in 'sixty-eight." She was packing while she talked—bread, cheese, a flask of the raw plum spirit she kept for emergencies and drank from rarely enough that its existence was itself an emergency. "It means they're frightened. You don't pay two hundred crowns for a mapmaker's apprentice unless the maps are worth ten thousand, and you don't believe they're worth ten thousand unless your own historians told you so."

"The official history ships in spring."

"And every week between now and then, you are worth more alive-and-lost than dead. Which is good news, in its way." Marta thrust the bundle at her. "Bad news is that they've got your face now, and faces don't need warrants. Any constable, any busybody, any man who owes the Bureau a favor—that's your face being looked at in every market, every ferry landing, every church porch from here to Greyhaven."

"I'll move tonight."

"You'll move within the hour." Marta's voice had the flat finality of a harbor bell in fog. "There's a room behind the ropewalk, over the sailmaker's. Old Nan has it. She doesn't ask questions; she can't hear them anyway, which is better." Then she paused, and something moved behind her weathered face that was not quite tenderness but lived next door to it. "Sit down a minute."

Ada sat. Outside, gulls argued over the gutting tables, and somewhere a ship's bell rang the hour, and none of it stopped.

Marta took Ada's chin in a hand rough as tarred rope and turned her face to the light, studying it with a professional eye—the eye of a woman who had spent thirty years moving things past people whose business was noticing. "The scar stays. Can't be helped, and trying to cover it looks worse than showing it. The hair goes shorter. Shorter than a widow cuts it—short as a boy's. Boys get ignored; women get remembered. You'll bind what's left and wear the cap." She let go. "And you'll stop walking like her."

"Like who?"

"Like yourself. Like a girl who grew up bent over a drafting table and thinks with her eyes before her feet. You look at doorways like you're measuring them. You stand square to things, front-on, the way you'd face a map. From tomorrow you stand angled. You slouch a little. You let people pass you; you never pass close behind anyone. And you don't count things anymore."

Ada blinked. "I don't—"

"You count. Windows, doors, exits, heads. I've watched you do it since the day we met—you can't help it, it's how your mind works, everything gets measured and placed. It saved your life once or twice, I'll grant. But a girl scanning a street is a girl looking for something, and looking-for-something is a shape the Bureau teaches its men to spot. So from tomorrow, you look at nothing. You look tired. Tired women are invisible, child. The whole city is full of tired women nobody sees. Be one of them."

It should have been easy. It was the hardest thing anyone had asked of her yet. Harder than hiding the notes in the pickle barrels, harder than lying to the Bureau agent in her own shop with her heart hammering like a fist on a door. Because invisibility had been her craft for ten years—she had perfected it, polished it, worn it like her master's old apron—and now she was being told that even that was gone, that the self she hid inside had been sketched and priced and nailed to a board among the herring, and whatever she built next would have to be built out of nothing, out of a stoop and a shuffling gait and a deliberately unseeing gaze.

"They've taken my master's plates," Ada said quietly. "They've taken the shop. They've taken my name off the census rolls and my face onto their boards. What's left of me that they haven't drawn?"

"Your hands," Marta said. "They can't draw what your hands are doing. So keep them busy and keep them moving, and when this is over, you'll sign your work in front of the whole city." She shouldered her own basket. "Ropewalk. Within the hour. And Ada—" she used the name deliberately, one of the few places it could still safely exist, between these walls— "the girl on that poster isn't you. Don't you go mourning her. She was always a disguise too."

* * *

The sailmaker's room over the ropewalk smelled of hemp and hot pitch, and its single window faced the long covered shed where workers paced back and forth all day laying out cordage, so that the floor trembled faintly with their footsteps like the deck of a ship at anchor. Old Nan, deaf as a bollard, showed Ada the cot, the chamber pot, the bucket of rainwater, and the view, by pointing, and then left her alone with a lamp and a lock on the inside of the door that was more gesture than iron.

Ada cut her own hair that night, because Marta had offered and Ada had refused, though she couldn't have said why. Some small stubborn ceremony, maybe. If the Bureau was going to take her face, they would not also take the act of surrendering it.

She knelt before the little cracked mirror propped against the wall—Corvane's mirror, salvaged from the shop after the raid, along with his silver pens and one drawer of instruments the agents had dismissed as junk—and worked the shears through the dark length of it herself. It fell in heavy ropes into her lap, into her hands, and she held it a moment before letting it go: ten years of a life, measured in hair. When it was done, the stranger in the mirror had a narrow face, sharp at the jaw, all eyes and cheekbone and that pale seam of scar. A boy, almost. A tired apprentice of no particular family, of no particular city, of nowhere in particular at all.

She bound the remainder, tugged Nan's borrowed cap low, and practiced.

That was the word for it—practiced, the way she'd once practiced lettering until her wrist ached. She walked the length of the room angled, shoulders rolled, chin dropped. She let her gaze slide past the window instead of fixing on it. She shuffled. In the mirror, the stranger slouched and dragged one heel and became, slowly, someone the fishwife in the market would look straight through.

Somewhere below, the ropewalkers' footsteps kept their patient rhythm, laying out thread after thread of cordage, each strand worthless alone, thousands of them twisted together into rope strong enough to hold a ship against a tide. She thought of Odile saying it months ago, in the garret that smelled of spilled gin and old paper: *No single document survives, girl. Only chains of them. Provenance is just rope—witness tied to witness tied to witness.* She thought of Tam's signature drying on the sworn statement. Of Elias carrying the Bureau's list of targeted papers in his coat like a coal he couldn't put down. Of the copies in the barrels, the plates hidden, the printer waiting.

They had her face. Let them. A face was one strand.

Near midnight, a soft knock came in the pattern Marta had taught her—two, pause, three—which meant friend, news, open quickly. Ada lifted the latch and Elias slipped in, still in his Bureau coat, gray-faced, snow melting on his shoulders.

"They've widened it," he said without preamble. "The notice. It's not just Vellamare—it went out on the coastal packets this morning. Greyhaven, Port Merrow, the fishing villages all down the Sound." He pulled off his gloves finger by finger, and she saw his hands weren't steady. "And there's more. The description didn't come from the market. It came from inside. Someone gave the draftsman the scar, the build, the age. That level of detail means someone who's seen you close. Someone in—" He stopped.

"In our circle," Ada finished.

"No." He said it too fast, and then again, slower, forcing himself to mean it. "No. I've been turning it over the whole way here. It could be the print shop. The printer knows a description crossed his counter. It could be the turnkey Marta bribed. It could be the landlord at the last safehouse. It doesn't have to be—" He couldn't finish, and the unfinished sentence filled the little room like smoke.

Ada looked at the stranger in the mirror—at the cropped hair, the cap, the slouch she hadn't learned yet—and felt the last of her invisibility go out of the world like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. For ten years she had survived by being no one, and the Bureau had spent a winter making her into someone: a name, a face, a price. Hunted things cannot hide. They can only move, and trust, and endure.

"Then we assume it's everyone," she said at last, evenly. "We assume every stranger is watching and every friend might be followed, and we keep working anyway. Because stopping is the one thing that hands them the win." She met Elias's eyes. "What else?"

He hesitated. She had learned to read his hesitations the way she read coastlines—the dangerous shallows just visible under the surface. "My supervisor requested the original file on Corvane today," he said quietly. "The closed one. The one from the year of the flood. He's never cared about the case before. And tomorrow morning I'm assigned to the search detail—they're sweeping Tanner's Row."

The printer's row. Where the atlas was, sheet by sheet, coming into being.

Ada looked down at her own hands—ink-stained, steady, the one part of her they hadn't managed to draw—and began, without a word, to fold Nan's blanket into a bundle small enough to carry at a dead run.
