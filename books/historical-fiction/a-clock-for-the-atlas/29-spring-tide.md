## 29. Spring Tide

Ada had imagined many kinds of bad news in the months since she'd first pried open her master's locked drawer—raids, arrests, fire, flood—but never this: Odile Ferrant sitting upright at Marta Quill's kitchen table with a printed broadsheet in her hands and something almost like laughter in her eyes.

Not joy. Ada had lived long enough beside grief to know its counterfeit when she saw it. It was the laugh of a woman reading her own obituary and finding it elegantly written.

"Sit down, girl," Odile said.

Marta pushed a stool toward her without looking up from the pot she was stirring, though Ada noticed her knuckles were white around the spoon's handle. The kitchen smelled of bay leaf and lamp oil and, beneath it all, the brackish breath of the harbor that no amount of scrubbing ever quite banished from Marta's house.

The broadsheet was a bookseller's notice, the kind pasted in shop windows across Vellamare every season. *By arrangement with the Ministry of Restoration: THE HISTORY OF THE FLOOD OF SALTMARSH, Being a True and Complete Account of the Rebellion and Its Suppression.* Below the title, in smaller type, the author's name—Hollis Vane, Historiographer Royal—and below that, the line that had stopped Odile's breath:

*On sale to the public in nine days.*

"Nine days," Ada said. The words came out flat, as if someone else had spoken them through her mouth.

"Spring tide," Odile said. "They timed it deliberately, of course. Vane is nothing if not theatrical. The anniversary falls on the highest water of the year, and what better moment to publish your lie than when the whole city is already looking at the sea?" She tapped the notice with one crooked finger. "I'd wager my last bottle they mean to have commemorative readings on the seawall itself. Stand on the bones of Saltmarsh and read aloud that its own defenders drowned it."

Ada sat very still. Outside, gulls argued over something in the gutter, and a cart rattled past toward the fish market, and the ordinary morning went on with its ordinary noises while the floor of her plans quietly tilted beneath her feet.

Nine days. Their printing was not finished. Their printing was barely *half* finished—the atlas plates done, the sworn testimonies set in type, but Tam's statement still locked behind prison walls where he'd been held three weeks now, and the final signature pages unprinted because Tam's name could not go on them until he stood before a magistrate or a notary and spoke his piece. They had planned for spring. For weeks yet. For the slow, careful margin Odile had always insisted on: *provenance before publication, publication before provocation.*

"They've moved early because they're frightened," Marta said from the stove. She ladled stew into three bowls with the economy of a woman who wasted nothing, not motion, not food, not words. "You don't rush a book to print unless you've heard it might be contradicted."

"The raid on the boathouse," Ada said. "They found nothing in the barrels, but they know something exists. And Elias said—"

"Elias," Odile cut in, "is either in a cell by now or walking a razor so thin he can't send word. We plan as if we'll hear nothing from him." Her hands were shaking—it was the palsy that had been getting worse all winter, the reason her copying days were over—but her voice was steady as a plumb line. "So. Nine days. Let us do arithmetic."

And that was how the race began—not with trumpets, but with an old woman counting on her fingers at a widow's kitchen table.

* * *

The boathouse smelled of pitch, pickle brine, and hot lead, and by the third night of the new schedule it smelled also of sweat and singed candlewick and Ada's own fear, which she had learned to work through the way one works through a cramp—acknowledging it, breathing around it, refusing to let it steer her hands.

The press itself was a marvel of Marta's procurement: a secondhand flatbed press bought off a bankrupt almanac printer in Corvenna, smuggled upriver under a load of salted herring, and reassembled in the loft above the boat slip where the barrels of copies already sat sealed against discovery. It groaned like a living thing when it worked. Sometimes Ada thought she could feel it thinking.

She stood at the composing stone, setting the last columns of testimony, when Marta came up the ladder with the particular tread she used for carrying bad news lightly.

"Tanner's Row sends word," Marta said. "Your printer wants his money tonight. All of it. He says the Ministry men have been asking after paper orders along the row—someone's buying up stock, and he doesn't care to be caught holding ours."

"He agreed to be ignorant."

"He agreed to be paid. Ignorance was a courtesy we extended him." Marta set a lantern on the barrel head so its light fell across the half-finished stack of printed sheets—Saltmarsh restored, street by drowned street, the names of the dead inscribed along the quays in letters small enough to fit but large enough, Ada prayed, to be read by candlelight on a church door. "Can you get it?"

Ada thought of the little strongbox beneath the floorboards of the shop—the silver her master had left, and her mother's ring, and the few coins of her own hoarded across seven invisible years. She had told herself when she carried them to Tanner's Row that she was spending the past to buy the future. She had not known then how much past there would prove to be.

"I'll get it," she said. "Tonight, before the shops close. But Marta—" She hesitated. This was the part she had rehearsed and dreaded in equal measure. "If I'm seen going in or out of the Row, they'll have me. The sketch is posted in every customs house. It looks enough like me that even I flinch at it."

"So you won't go as yourself." Marta's grin was sudden and wolfish. She reached into the chest by the ladder and pulled out a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapping it to reveal a widow's weeds—black bombazine skirt, gray shawl, a bonnet with a veil fine enough to blur a face into shadow. "My sister-in-law's. God rest her, she was broader than you, but grief hides a multitude. Every woman on Tanner's Row will look at a veiled widow and see exactly what she expects to see: someone coming to sell her dead husband's things. Half the silver in this city changes hands that way."

"And the money?"

"In the muff." Marta pressed the fur muff into her hands, heavy as a sleeping cat. "Walk slowly. Widows are never in a hurry; hurry belongs to the living."

Ada dressed in the loft's dark corner, pinning her cropped hair out of sight beneath the bonnet, and when she caught her reflection in the black glass of the window she did not recognize herself—which was, she understood, precisely the point, and precisely the loss. Seven years of learning to be no one, and now the skill had a use she'd never wanted.

At the top of the ladder she paused. "If I'm not back by the midnight bell—"

"Then Odile burns the composition blocks and I scuttle the barrels in the channel, and the truth goes to the bottom to wait for better divers." Marta said it the way she said everything, plain as bread. "But you'll be back. You're too stubborn to drown on dry land."

It was, Ada would reflect later, the closest thing to a blessing anyone had given her in years.

* * *

Tanner's Row at dusk was a river of smells and noise—tallow and ink and roasting chestnuts, printers' apprentices shouting the last delivery times, the great wheels of paper carts grinding cobbles worn slick by a century of commerce. Ada moved through it inside her veil like a fish inside a bell jar, seeing everything, touched by nothing.

The printer's shop was shuttered but not closed. She knocked the pattern Marta had taught her—two, pause, three—and the door opened on a chain, and one eye, red-rimmed and wary, looked out.

"You're late," said the printer, whose name she had deliberately never learned. "Money."

She opened the muff. He counted by lamplight, lips moving, and Ada stood in the cold and watched her master's silver disappear into another man's apron pocket and felt—nothing, or close to it. Grief, she was discovering, could be spent like coin too. There simply wasn't time to feel each piece leave.

"The sheets are ready?" she asked.

"All but the signature pages for your man Osei. Those I hold till you bring me the text final." He glanced up and down the emptying street. "And I'll tell you for nothing, widow, since you paid me honest—you want to be quick. There's a Ministry clerk been to three shops on this row this week asking who's ordered double weight rag paper. Rag paper's for books meant to last." His mouth twisted. "Somebody in this city is printing something they expect people to keep."

He shut the door before she could answer.

Ada walked back toward the harbor slowly, as widows do, and let those words settle in her chest like swallowed coals. *Something they expect people to keep.* Yes. That was the whole war in a sentence. Vane's history would be printed on rag paper too, bound in calfskin, sold in shops with brass fittings, shelved in libraries with marble floors. Everything about it announced permanence. And their atlas would hang on church doors in the rain until some verger tore it down—unless it was kept. Unless ten thousand hands copied it, memorized it, read it aloud to children, folded it into coat linings and memory. Permanence, she understood suddenly, was not a property of paper. It was a property of *witnesses*. Odile had been trying to teach her that for months, and only now, in the veil, in the cold, did the lesson land.

Two versions of truth, racing for the same shelves. One backed by the Ministry, the other by five people, a stolen press, and whatever the city itself decided to believe.

She lengthened her step despite the disguise. Widows, she decided, could hurry just this once.

* * *

Odile was waiting in the boathouse loft, propped in the good chair Marta had stolen—borrowed, she insisted—from a Customs House waiting room, with the proof copy of the official history open on her knees.

Elias's theft. Three nights ago he had come to them pale and rapid, still smelling of the Bureau's corridors, and laid the advance proof on Marta's table like a man laying down a live grenade. *Ships Friday,* he'd said. *Every bookseller in the city gets copies Friday morning. Read page ninety-four.* Page ninety-four, where Hollis Vane blamed the levee breach on Saltmarsh's own militia—where the men who died holding the line were named as the men who broke it. Ada had read it once and could not read it again. Odile had read it four times, pencil in hand, annotating the margins the way she annotated everything, cataloguing every lie the way a surgeon charts a wound.

"Well?" Ada asked, pulling off the veil.

"It's elegant," Odile said, and spat, with archivist's precision, into the spittoon. "That's what makes it dangerous. Vane has citations—forged ones, but forged well, I know the hand, it's a Bureau workshop style I saw during the purges. He has dates. He has a witness list, all dead or disappeared or invented. A hundred years from now, if nothing counters it, scholars will call it definitive." She closed the book. "Which is why our timing matters more than our typography now. Listen. Friday morning, his books arrive. Friday night is the anniversary eve. The city will be full of mourners and soldiers both. If our atlas goes up Thursday night—before his lies are even on sale—then when people stand in the booksellers' queues and read Vane's account, they'll already be holding ours in their heads. First impression is a fortress. After that, every reader of Vane reads him *against* us, checking, doubting. Doubt is all we need. Truth does the rest."

"Thursday," Ada said. "That's six days. The signature pages aren't set. Tam—"

"Tam," said a voice from the ladder.

Marta climbed up into the lantern light, rain steaming off her shoulders, and her face wore the expression Ada had learned to dread most: hope held on a very tight leash.

"Word from my turnkey friend. Tam's been moved. Not freed—moved. From the holding cells to the magistrates' wing. There's to be a preliminary hearing." She let the leash slip, just slightly. "Thursday morning. Day after tomorrow. Before the anniversary crowd fills the streets and before Vane's books hit the shelves—they mean to bury his testimony quietly, one clerk, one room, no audience. But Ada—" Marta turned to her, and in the lamplight her eyes were bright and hard as harbor water. "A hearing means a public record. It means he speaks his statement *on the record*, before a magistrate, with a clerk taking it down in triplicate. Provenance. Witness. Seal. Everything Odile said we needed—handed to us by the Ministry's own machinery, if we can get him heard properly and get the record copied."

Silence, except the rain and the press ticking as it cooled.

"If he testifies," Odile said slowly, "and the record survives, we don't even need to smuggle his signature page. We cite the hearing transcript. The state becomes its own contradictor." She began to laugh again, that terrible dry laugh, and this time Ada understood it better. "Oh, it's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've heard in twenty years of watching them burn paper."

"But he has to actually say it," Marta said. "In a room full of Bureau men. Alone. With no promise we can protect him after." She looked at Ada. "And someone has to make sure the record gets copied before it 'goes missing.' Someone inside."

"Someone inside," Ada repeated softly.

Elias. Who had not sent word in three days. Who had walked back into the Bureau carrying a stolen proof in his coat, knowing what would happen if they searched him, knowing his supervisor already suspected him of exactly the incompetence he'd confessed to. Who had lied for her once already and was now, somewhere across the dark city, deciding whether to lie again—or whether this time the lie would cost him everything.

Ada looked down at her hands. They were stained to the second knuckle with ink that no scrubbing would fully remove, her master's ink and her own, and she thought: *every map is a promise that the territory exists.* Somewhere out there was a courtroom, a frightened lockkeeper, a compromised census taker, and six days in which two versions of the truth would run side by side through the streets of Vellamare like rival tides.

"Then here's what happens next," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, the way her coastlines were steady, drawn one patient point at a time. "Tomorrow I finish setting the testimonies. Tomorrow night, Marta gets word to Tam that we know about the hearing and that the city will be listening. And someone finds Elias Wren before the Bureau decides what he is."

Outside, the rain thickened over the harbor, and far off, the buoy at the mouth of the channel began its slow, patient tolling—the sound, sailors said, that meant the spring tide was building.

Four more nights, it seemed to say. Four more nights, and the water would decide everything.

Ada bent back over the composing stone, picked up her tweezers, and began, letter by letter, to set the truth in type.
