## 30. Freeing Tam

The money smelled of the blackmailer.

Marta had noticed it the first night, counting it out on her kitchen table by candlelight: forty crowns in mixed coin, wrapped in oilcloth, left in the hollow of the breakwater post where Doss Halloway had collected payments for twenty years. It was good coin—Vellamare mint, heavy in the palm—but it carried a stink beneath the metal, a smell like wet rope and old lies, and Marta Quill had spent long enough around both to know them at a distance. Hush money. The kind a man paid when he could not be bothered to keep his mouth shut honestly.

She had given it up once already—the boat, the *Speranza*, her husband's last living thing—to buy silence from that same mouth. And now she sat at her kitchen table turning one of the crowns over and over with her thumb, and thought: *I have paid this man twice for nothing.*

Not nothing, said the colder voice in her head, the one that sounded like her husband Bram and always had. You paid him and the family is still whole. The copies are still in the pickle barrels. Tam is still breathing in the House of Detention, which is more than some men can say after ten days inside.

Ten days. She had made inquiries through channels that did not officially exist—a fishwife here, a customs clerk there, a turnkey's sister who sold mussels off a barrow on the Quay of Weeping Saints—and what came back was this: Tam Osei had not been charged. He had been taken in the boathouse raid as a material witness, held under a writ signed by someone in the Bureau of Records who wanted very much to have a quiet conversation with him about the night the levees broke. They were waiting, the mussel-seller said, for a man to come down from the capital. An examiner. Tam was not being punished; he was being *kept*, the way one keeps a cask of gunpowder dry until the day one needs it.

That was worse than punishment. Punishment ended. Keeping did not.

Marta pushed the coins into a pile and looked at them until they stopped looking like money and started looking like what they were: leverage. Forty crowns was what Halloway had demanded for his silence about the barrels, the boathouse, the girl with the ink-stained fingers. But Halloway was a greedy man, and greedy men kept ledgers in their heads, and somewhere in that ledger there would be an entry marked *turnkey*—because no contraband moved out of the House of Detention, or a prisoner moved out of it, without grease on the hinges, and Doss Halloway had been greasing hinges since before Bram Quill drowned with his hold full of sugar.

She knew the hinge. She only had to find it.

His name was Piet Vos, and he worked the dawn shift on the north gate of the detention house, the gate that opened onto the canal where the prison barges tied up. He was a wide, soft-spoken man with a bad knee and a widowed mother in the Marsh End, and eleven years ago—before the flood, before everything—he had owed Bram Quill six months of quiet landings at a stairway behind the salt warehouses, and Bram had never once had reason to complain of him. Piet Vos had gone straight after Saltmarsh drowned, the way a lot of honest men went straight when the water showed them what the city was. Straight men made the best bribes, Bram used to say. They hated themselves for taking, so they took carefully, and they never, ever talked.

Marta found him at the Mussel & Anchor on the second evening, eating a bowl of eel stew alone by the window, and sat down across from him without asking.

He looked up. Recognition moved across his face slowly, like weather.

"Mistress Quill," he said.

"You knew my husband."

"Everyone knew your husband." He put down his spoon.

"Everyone owed my husband, too, but not everyone remembers it." She kept her voice low, pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing fish prices. "I need a man walked out of the north gate at dawn change, three mornings from now, and I need it never to have happened."

Piet Vos went very still. A big man, and stillness on a big man is louder than shouting. Around them the tavern clattered on—dice, dishes, a drunk singing something about the lighthouse—and Marta watched the fear arrive in his eyes and did nothing to soften it, because softening it would have meant lying to him, and she respected him too much for that.

"They hanged a turnkey in Corvein province two years ago," he said finally. "For less."

"They hanged him because he was caught and alone." Marta reached into her bag and set the oilcloth bundle on the table between them, but did not unwrap it. "You will not be caught, because you are careful, and you have been careful for eleven years, and I chose you for it. And you will not be alone, because if anything goes wrong—if so much as a hair of that man's head is touched going out that gate—then certain papers reach certain desks describing exactly how Doss Halloway has been paying you to move prisoners' letters in and out of the north wing these past three years. Letters from men awaiting examination. Letters that name names."

Piet's face did not change. His hands, folded on the table, whitened at the knuckles.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," Marta agreed. "You don't. Nobody ever does. That's rather the point." She tapped the oilcloth once. "Twenty crowns now. Twenty when I see him on the far side of the canal. And Piet—" she waited until he looked at her, really looked, and let him see, for one unguarded moment, the grief underneath the iron, "—that man in your north wing kept the levee gates at Pier Nine for nine years before the flood. He pulled children out of the water while soldiers stood on the wall and watched. Whatever they tell you he is, he is the last honest man in that building, and I am asking you to be the second."

The tavern noise filled the silence while he decided. It took longer than any bribe should have, and Marta understood that this was the payment he was making in return: not the money, but the pause, the dignity of choosing rather than being bought.

"Dawn change," he said at last. "Third morning. The relief comes at half past five; the gate is open four minutes while the watch lists pass. After that I cannot help you. If he is not at the coal stairs by quarter past, he is yours to lose."

"He'll be there," Marta said, and rose, and left the twenty crowns wrapped in oilcloth beside his untouched stew.

* * *

They planned it the way Odile Ferrant taught Ada to plan a provenance: every link in the chain proved, every hand accounted for, nothing trusted to luck that could be built instead.

"Luck," Odile said from her chair by the stove, propped on pillows though she insisted she was recovering, "is what incompetent people call the residue of other people's preparation. We are not going to be lucky. We are going to be thorough."

So they were thorough. Ada drew the route as a map, because that was how she thought now—everything had become cartography since the night of doors, since she had learned that a street was an argument and a doorway was a position to be defended. She drew the north gate, the coal stairs, the towpath along the canal, the point where the towpath passed under the Customs Bridge and a man standing upright in a boat's hull would be invisible from the bridge for precisely eleven strides. Eleven strides. She had paced it herself at dawn, twice, wearing a fishwife's shawl and carrying a basket of nothing.

"The barge goes through at first light," Marta said, laying a stub of charcoal on the map at the sluice. "Coal barge, out of the east yards, low in the water. It passes the coal stairs at seven minutes past six, every morning, because the tide does not negotiate. Bram timed it for eleven years." Her voice did something small and complicated on Bram's name, and then went on. "Tam steps off the bottom stair into the barge's shadow, walks its length crouched, and steps off again at the slip below the rope-walk, where a skiff will be waiting."

"Whose skiff?" Elias asked.

"Mine," said Marta, "which is to say nobody's, which is to say the harbor master's records show it was sold for scrap in the autumn and sunk. Paperwork, Wren. The one thing your Bureau understands."

Elias Wren sat at the corner of the table with his census ledger closed in front of him, and he had not touched it all evening, and everyone had noticed and no one had remarked on it. He had brought something else instead: a single sheet in Bureau format, copied in his own fair hand.

"The examination order," he said. "The examiner from the capital arrives Friday. After that they move Tam to the ministry cells, and no widow's money reaches the ministry cells." He looked around the table—at Marta, at Odile, at Ada, whose hair was still short from the safehouse weeks and whose face, thanks to the sketch circulating through every guardpost in Vellamare, was known now to every man paid to remember faces. "It has to be Thursday dawn. Tomorrow. I want to say that plainly, because I've heard you plan around risks all night, and this one isn't a risk, it's a certainty. If we wait, we lose him."

No one argued. Ada looked at the map she had drawn and saw it whole: the gate, the barge, the bridge, the stairs, the skiff, and at each point a name. Piet Vos. The coal barge. The rope-walk slip. Marta at the oars. And at the Customs Bridge, one more name, the one she had fought against and lost.

"Me," Elias said, meeting her eyes. "I stand on the bridge. If the watch is alerted, if the gate alarm sounds, I am a census official with credentials who happened to be walking to work, and I can delay them—badly, plausibly, with questions about ward boundaries. I'm good at being ignored, Ada. I've practiced."

"It puts you in it," she said. "Past the point of pretending back."

"I filed a false report ten days ago." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I've been past the point of pretending for a while. I'd just like to be useful in daylight for once."

Odile, from her pillows, pronounced it like a sentence: "Then it's provenanced. Every link witnessed, every hand known. Move."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and the canal lay flat as poured lead, and Tam Osei stood in the dark of his cell with his ear against the door, counting.

He counted everything now. Ten years of keeping silence had taught him that counting was a form of prayer: he had counted the strokes of the levee hammer the night the water came, counted the children he lifted into the boats (nine; he could name them; he did name them, every night, like a litany), counted the ten years since, counted the days of his captivity by the sound of the keys. Fourteen keys on the day turnkey's ring. Two locks on his door. The dawn shift turned them at half past five, and the man who turned them was slow on the second lock because of his knee.

Tonight—the last night, though he did not know it yet—he had counted something new: footsteps overhead that paused at his ceiling at midnight and again at three, too regular for a wandering guard. Someone was keeping watch over him. Someone outside still knew he existed.

That was the thought he carried to the door at half past five, when the keys came jangling up the stair: *someone knows I'm here.* Ten years he had lived as a silence, a closed ledger, a lockkeeper who happened to be elsewhere the night Pier Nine needed him. Even taken, even written into a Bureau writ like an item of evidence, he had assumed he would end the way Saltmarsh ended—efficiently, administratively, with no one to mark the entry.

But the footsteps overhead had said otherwise.

Piet Vos opened the door and did not look at him, which Tam understood; and spoke the watch-list formula in a voice pitched flat, which Tam also understood; and then, in the corridor between the first cell door and the second lock, while the other dawn-shift men were at the far end with the bread carts, Piet said, so quietly it barely disturbed the air:

"North gate. Four minutes. Walk like a man going to work, and whatever you hear behind you, do not run."

Then the big man's hand closed on Tam's shoulder—one turnkey, one prisoner, the oldest sight in any prison in the world—and steered him down the stair and along the passage to the north gate, which stood open against the grey light, and through it.

The canal air hit him like a resurrection. Coal smoke, weed, tar, the wet stone smell of water that had been waiting all night for the tide—it was the smell of Vellamare, and Tam Osei stood on the towpath for one full second simply breathing it, while behind him the gate began its slow swing shut and the relief watch crunched gravel somewhere out of sight.

Four minutes. He walked.

At the coal stairs the barge was already there, riding low, its hull a black wall between him and the rising light, and he went down the slick steps and along the barge's shadowed flank crouched low, hand on the wet timbers, eleven strides, fifteen, twenty—and stepped off at the slip below the rope-walk where a skiff nosed the stones and Marta Quill sat at the oars with her headscarf pulled down and her jaw set like a woman late for market.

"In," she said. "Sit low. Breathe later."

He got in. The skiff slid away from the slip with hardly a ripple, and Tam, crouched in the bilge with cold water seeping through the seat of his trousers, watched the underside of the Customs Bridge slide overhead, felt the stroke of the oars steady and unhurried above him, and began—for the first time in ten days, perhaps in ten years—to count something other than losses.

One stroke. Two. Three.

Above, on the bridge, a thin man in a census coat stood at the parapet consulting a ledger, and when two watchmen came puffing up the far ramp drawn by some sound or instinct, the thin man intercepted them with the weary courtesy of officialdom—*boundaries, gentlemen, the new ward lines, if you could spare a moment's opinion*—and the watchmen, being men who had survived Vellamare by avoiding exactly such conversations, gave their opinion briefly and moved on, and Elias Wren remained at the parapet with his heart going like a drum and his face composed into bureaucratic tedium, watching the skiff's wake flatten into the general glitter of the canal, and seeing nothing, officially, at all.

* * *

The safehouse was a sailmaker's loft above the ropewalk, borrowed from a woman who owed Marta three favors and asked no questions about the fourth. By the time the skiff's passengers climbed its ladder, the others were already there: Odile wrapped in two blankets despite the stove, and Ada at the one window with the shutter cracked to a blade of light, watching the street.

Tam came up the ladder stiff and slow, and stopped at the top, and stood there blinking in the lamplight like a man surfacing from deep water.

Ada had imagined this moment for chapters of her life, it seemed—since the memorial, since the market, since the night she had first pressed his statement toward him and he had neither signed nor refused it. She had imagined gratitude, reunion, the loosening of the thing in her chest that had tightened the day they took him. What she had not imagined was how ordinary he would look: a tired lockkeeper in a prisoner's coat, grey at the temples, hands hanging empty at his sides as if he no longer quite knew what they were for.

"Mistress Quill," he said hoarsely. "You shouldn't have spent the boat on me."

"The boat was sinking anyway," Marta said. "Bram built her heavy. Sit down before you fall down, you stubborn man."

But Tam did not sit. He crossed the loft instead, past the offered chair, past the bowl of soup Odile was pushing toward him, and stopped in front of Ada, and studied her face the way a man studies a chart of a coast he thought he knew.

"They told me in there," he said, "an examiner was coming from the capital. To ask me about the levee. And I lay awake deciding, the whole ten days, whether I'd keep the silence one more time or finally—" his voice caught, steadied— "whether ten years was enough. And then I saw the footsteps stop over my ceiling at midnight, and I thought: somebody out there hasn't forgotten Pier Nine. Somebody's coming."

"We were coming," Ada said.

"Then I owe you the thing I was saving." He put his hand into the breast of the prisoner's coat and drew out a scrap of paper folded small and soft as cloth, handled so many times its creases were furrows. On it, in a laboring hand, was a line of figures and a date, the tenth night of the spring flood, and beside the figures two words: *landward side.*

"My log," he said. "The gauge readings from Pier Nine, that last night. Water rising from the landward side, before the sea ever touched us. I tore it from the book and sewed it in my coat and I've carried it ten years, and I couldn't make myself sign a statement, because signing felt like dying. But handing it over—" he pressed the paper into Ada's hands, closing her fingers around it with both of his, cracked and trembling and steady all at once, "—handing it over felt like living. Write it however it needs writing. I'll swear to every word. In front of the magistrates, in front of the Bureau, in front of God and the whole wet city if that's what it takes."

Ada looked down at the paper. A number, a date, three words in a dead man's hand—no, a living man's hand, here, warm against hers—and she thought of survey notes half-burned in a locked drawer, of a chain of custody built line by line, of Odile's lesson: *every link witnessed.* The chain had another link now, forged in a cell by a man who had chosen it over his own safety, and it would hold.

"Welcome back," she said, which was inadequate, and was all there was.

They ate the soup. Odile drank a medicinal amount of brandy and declared Tam's log admissible, corroborated, and beautiful, in roughly that order. Marta laughed for the first time in weeks, short and rough, like something unlocking.

It was Elias who broke the circle. He had gone to the window to take his turn at the shutter, and now he stood very still, the ledger-fair copy of the examination order forgotten in his hand.

"Ada," he said quietly. "Come look at this."

She joined him at the blade of light. Below, at the corner of the ropewalk, a boy was chalking something on the wall of the net-loft—not graffiti, not play. A mark she recognized, because she had designed it: the little tide-symbol, spiral within a wave, that the couriers had chalked on church doors the night of the atlas. A message. The boy finished, glanced up and down the street, and ran.

And beneath the symbol, in a hurried hand Ada did not know, were three more words, and the light through the shutter crack fell across them plainly:

*They know Friday.*
