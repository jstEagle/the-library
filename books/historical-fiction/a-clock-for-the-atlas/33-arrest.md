## 33. Arrest

The press on Tanner's Row had been running since before dawn, and the whole shop smelled of ink and hot metal and the tallow candles that had burned down to their saucers overnight. Ada stood at the sorting table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her fingers black to the second knuckle, collating the last signatures of the final gathering. Forty copies. Not the five hundred they needed for the doors — those had gone out into the dark hours ago in satchels and fish barrels and Marta's own determined arms — but forty clean copies bound in grey boards, the first fruits of the run, kept back for a purpose Ada had not entirely confessed even to herself.

She opened one and turned to page forty.

It was not the finest map in the atlas. The engraver had been rushed, and the coastline of the drowned quarter wavered in places where it should have held true, the ink pooling slightly in the low spots of the plate. But it was accurate. That was the thing no one could take from it. She had drawn Saltmarsh as it had been on the ninth of October, 1864, from the tide tables and the survey notes and Tam Osei's halting, hard-won testimony, and beneath the streets she had printed their names — every name Odile could verify, every name the survivors had whispered to her at the memorial and after — set small and upright along the lanes where the people had lived, so that reading the map was like pressing your ear to the ground of a buried city and hearing it breathe.

*Saltmarsh,* the title read, in letters she had drawn herself, *as it stood the day the levees were cut.*

The press groaned through another impression behind her. The printer's boys were gone — paid off and sent home before light, told nothing — and only old Hessel remained at the machine, his forearms slick with grease, pretending with great dedication not to know what he was printing. He was a widower with a granddaughter in the Saltmarsh orphanage. He had asked exactly one question when they brought him the plates: *Will it hang me?* And Ada had answered honestly: *It might.* He had nodded and begun setting type.

The bell above the shop door rang.

Ada did not startle. She had known this moment was coming the way she knew where a river would meet the sea — not by guessing but by reading the ground it must cross. She set down the copy in her hands, squared its edges against the table, and looked up.

Four men came in. Three wore the grey coats of the Bureau of Records, and the fourth — the youngest, still soft about the jaw, his hat held slightly wrong, too far forward, like a man wearing armor he hadn't been fitted for — was an officer of the city watch, which meant someone in the Bureau wanted this done with legal trimmings today. Their boots were wet. It had begun to rain again, the thin persistent drizzle of a Vellamare spring, and water beaded on the shoulders of the lead agent's coat.

He was not the agent who had come to the shop offering coin for Master Corvane's papers, all those months ago. This one was older, thicker through the chest, with the unhurried manner of a man who had done this many times and had stopped needing to hurry. His eyes went around the room the way a customs officer's eyes go over cargo: the press, the stacked paper, the drying racks, the table, and finally Ada herself, standing very still with her black fingers folded in front of her apron.

"Close early," he said to Hessel. It was not a request.

Hessel disengaged the press with a clank, wiped his hands slowly and thoroughly on a rag that was already black, and shuffled toward the back room without looking at anyone. A brave man shows courage in strange ways; Hessel's was to leave the door to the alley unbolted behind him.

"Name," said the agent.

"You have it," Ada said. "Or you wouldn't be here."

"Say it anyway."

"Sandoval. Ada Sandoval."

Something shifted in his face — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. Somewhere in a Bureau file, a sketch of her face was pinned to a report, and he had compared it against the living woman across the table and found the match sufficient. She wondered if he saw what she had seen in that circulated drawing herself: that it made her look harder than she felt, older, dangerous, when all along her danger had been nothing but paper and patience.

"Ada Sandoval," the agent repeated. "Apprentice to the late Ambrose Corvane, cartographer. You are detained under the Statute of Seditions Publication, article nine, for the production and distribution of unlicensed documents bearing on matters of state record." He said it fluently, a paragraph worn smooth by repetition. From his coat he drew a pair of iron cuffs, not brandished, merely produced, the way one produces a receipt. "You'll come quietly."

"I will," Ada said.

And she meant it, and the meaning of it was the strangest thing she had ever felt. Ten years of careful invisibility — ten years of no surname spoken aloud, of answering to *girl* and *apprentice* and *the Corvane woman*, ten years of walking through Vellamare like a pencil line drawn faint enough to erase — and now that it ended, now that they had come for her by name, she found there was no terror left in her chest to feel. There was a heartbeat, steady as a pendulum. There were her hands, steady too. And there was the stack of forty grey-bound atlases on the table between them, and somewhere out in the rain-soaked city, five hundred more, nailed or nailing, pasted or pasting, going up on doors while the Bureau's men stood here counting one small woman.

"Turn around," the officer said, and lifted the irons.

"One moment." Ada picked up the topmost atlas from the stack.

The agent's hand came up sharply. "Put that down."

"It's yours," she said. "Consider it evidence. In fact—" she turned it so the cover faced him, then opened it to the marked ribbon, "—consider it *read*. Page forty. Before you lock me up. That's all I ask, and I think you'll find it's a lawful request. You're officers of record. Reading the record is your profession, isn't it? Or is it only keeping some records and burning others?"

The youngest watchman made a small sound, quickly suppressed. The lead agent looked at the book without touching it for a long moment, and Ada watched him weigh it — not the weight of the boards, but the weight of the thing itself. He was not stupid. Men who rise to lead raids on print shops are rarely stupid; stupid men are used, not promoted. He knew what the book was. He knew, because his Bureau had spent all winter buying up private papers, because they had ransacked her shop and taken the plates, because they had taken Tam Osei and interrogated Elias Wren and raided the boathouse — they had known for months exactly what was coming off this press. They had simply believed, until this morning, that they would stop it in time.

He took the atlas. Carefully. That was the detail Ada would carry with her through everything that followed: he took it carefully, holding it by the boards the way you hold something that might be worth something, and he did not put it away, but left it open in his hands at page forty, and his eyes went down.

Ada let him read.

She had composed that page the way she composed all her work, line by line, argument by argument. At the top, the title. Beneath it, the map: Saltmarsh whole, its four hundred houses rendered in fine stipple, the lock gates drawn in section, cutaway to show their timber hearts. And in the lower margin, in lettering small enough to require bending close, the caption she had written at three in the morning six days ago with Odile's voice in her ear correcting her grammar and Marta's stew steaming at her elbow:

*This quarter stood below the high-water mark of no flood. The levee gates show cuts made from the landward side, outward-facing, such as rising water cannot make. Surveyed by A. Corvane, 1864–65. Witnessed by T. Osei, lockkeeper. Certified authentic by O. Ferrant, formerly First Archivist of the Bureau of Records. These names are given freely, under seal, and may be verified.*

Three names. Her master's, dead and beyond their reach. Tam's, already in their cells. Odile's, given from a sickbed with a shaking hand pressed flat on the wax to make it take. And beneath them all, unspoken, hers — the apprentice who had finished the survey, drawn the plate, ruled the lines. She had not signed the atlas. She had signed only the colophon, in the back, where printers sign: *Printed at Vellamare, in the year 1875, by many hands.* But everyone in this room knew whose hands led them, and the agent looking at page forty knew it too.

"The distribution," he said, raising his eyes. "Where are the rest?"

"Gone," Ada said, and could not keep it entirely out of her voice — not fear, but something adjacent to joy, which startled her more than fear would have. "Hours ago. You're standing in an empty room, Captain. Whatever you burn here, you'll be burning empty crates."

"We'll see." He closed the atlas — gently, again, that terrible gentleness — and nodded to the watchman. "Cuff her. Take the stock. All of it. And the plates."

"There are no plates," Ada said. "You took the plates in the autumn. Don't you remember your own raid?" She watched that land too, watched the small muscle move in his jaw. They had taken the finished plates in November and believed that was the end of it, and the copies had been made from new plates cut in the boathouse by lamplight, by her hand and Odile's instruction and Marta's stubborn arms working the screw press. The Bureau had confiscated the past and missed the future entirely.

The young watchman stepped forward and took her wrists. His hands were shaking, she noticed. He was perhaps nineteen. He did not look at her face; he looked at the floor, at the task, at the mechanics of the thing, and the irons closed around her wrists with a sound like a lock gate swinging shut, and she thought, absurdly, of Tam Osei's hands on the great wheels at Pier Nine, and of how a mechanism built to hold back water could be turned to drown a city if the wrong orders came down the chain.

"Mind the step," Hessel said from the doorway to the back room, gruff as a man reminding a customer of the weather. The agent shot him a look but said nothing. What was there to say to a man who had printed sedition all night and would claim till his grave that he'd been hired to print hymnals?

They walked her out into the rain.

* * *

Tanner's Row at dawn was nearly empty — a milk cart, a sleepwalker of a dockhand, the shutters coming down off a bakery across the way, filling the street with the smell of warm bread that seemed almost obscene under the circumstances. The Bureau had brought a closed carriage, black, with curtains drawn, and Ada understood the choice: they had wanted her gone before the city woke, wanted her vanished the way people had been vanished for a decade, quietly, between one census and the next, leaving a gap in the record that no one was permitted to ask about.

But Vellamare wakes early, and word travels faster than carriages.

It began with the baker's boy, who came out to flip the shutter and stopped dead, flour on his hands, staring at her cuffed wrists. Then the milkwoman, reining up her cart in the middle of the row. Then two women from the fish market with baskets on their hips, and a lamplighter going home off shift, and by the time the carriage door was open Ada counted fifteen people in the street, and more windows opening above.

Nobody shouted. That was what unnerved the agents — she could see it unnerving them, the way the silence pressed on them from all sides. No mob, no stones, just the whole street turning to watch, faces pale in the drizzle, and somewhere down toward the harbor a church bell beginning to ring. Not the alarm. The hour. Seven o'clock, rung by a sexton who had, perhaps, already been to his own church door that morning and seen what was nailed there.

"Move," the lead agent said, low.

Ada moved. But at the carriage step she paused — deliberately, one beat, two — and turned her head to the street, and said, in a voice pitched not for the agents but for the bakery and the upper windows and the fishwives with their baskets: "Read page forty."

Then she ducked into the dark of the carriage, and the door slammed, and through the curtain-gap she saw the baker's boy already running — not away, but up the row, toward Saint Aldric's, toward whatever was posted there, toward the seawall where the crowds would gather by noon. She saw the milkwoman hand her reins to the lamplighter and follow. She saw the lead agent stand a moment in the rain with the atlas still under his arm because he had not put it down, had not handed it to an underling, had carried it himself out of the shop like a man carrying something that might yet matter.

Then the carriage lurched, and Vellamare slid away behind the curtain, and Ada Sandoval sat alone in the dark with the irons cold on her wrists and began, methodically, to memorize the route.

That was the mapmaker in her, and she smiled at it despite everything. Left onto Cooper's Lane — no, the ruts said Cooper's, but the turn was too wide; they'd doubled back through the tannery yard to shake any followers. Right at the cistern. The long descent toward the Customs House, where the wheels rang on stone flags, which meant the lower quay road, which meant — her heart gave one hard knock — they were taking her not to the watch house but to the Bureau's own annex, the grey building with the iron stairs, where people went in through doors that did not always open again.

She let the fear come then, alone as she was, because it deserved its hour and honesty was a kind of craft too. She let it show her the cell, the questions, the long grey years. She let it remind her that Odile was abed with fever, that Tam was already inside these walls somewhere, that Elias had walked into his own interrogation two weeks ago and come out changed in ways she hadn't dared ask about. She let it enumerate everyone she loved and everything that could still go wrong.

And when it was done, when it had shown her the whole of the country it ruled, she did what she had learned to do with every wild coastline she ever charted: she drew the boundary around it, named it, and shrank it to scale.

They had the woman. They did not have the truth. Five hundred atlases were on church doors in the rain. Tam's statement was sworn and witnessed and sealed. Odile's certification stood under archivist's authority, and no Bureau decree could un-certify what a certified archivist had certified — Odile had drilled her on that until Ada could recite the statutes in her sleep. The record existed now outside her body, outside anyone's body, reproduced and dispersed, and that was the entire design. A truth kept in one person can be arrested. A truth nailed to forty doors requires forty arrests, and forty trials, and forty public rooms full of citizens reading aloud.

Let them try it, she thought, watching the grey wall of the annex slide past the curtain gap. Let them read page forty in court. She would ask for nothing else. She would refuse to answer anything but the atlas, question after question, until the whole proceeding became a public reading of the thing they meant to suppress — and she would do it politely, precisely, line by line, the way her master taught her to draw a coast that resisted the pen.

The carriage stopped. Boots on gravel. The door opened onto rain and the iron staircase of the Bureau annex, and the young watchman reached in to help her down, and his hands were still shaking, and this time he did look at her face.

"I read it," he said, barely above the rain. "In the carriage. Page forty."

Ada held his eyes.

"Then you know," she said, "exactly what you're arresting."

His throat worked. He said nothing. But he did not grip her arm as he led her up the stairs — he guided her, the barest touch at her elbow, a courtesy so small it might have been invisible, and she understood that it was not invisible at all, that it was the first crack in a wall that had stood ten years, that walls made of frightened men come down one courtesy at a time.

At the top of the stairs, the annex door opened, and a voice she did not know called out from within, clipped and cold:

"Send her to holding. And get word to the Ministry — the hearing is set for tomorrow morning, before the full bench."

Tomorrow. Not weeks. Not months. They were afraid too, then — afraid of the doors, afraid of the bells, afraid of the crowds she could hear, even here, even now, beginning somewhere far off across the rain to gather at the seawall.

Tomorrow morning, the full bench.

Ada lifted her chin, shook the rain from her hair like a woman stepping off a boat onto solid land, and walked in to meet her record.
