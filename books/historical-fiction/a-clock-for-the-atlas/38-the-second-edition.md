## 38. The Second Edition

The proofs arrived on a morning so clear that the harbor looked drawn rather than real—every mast inked sharp against the sky, every rooftop ruled in place, as though Vellamare itself had been laid down by a careful hand and would hold its lines forever. Ada Sandoval stood at the counter of the reopened shop with the first bound copy of the second edition in her hands, and she did not open it for a long while.

She had learned that some moments were like maps: best read slowly, from the edges inward.

The book was heavier than the first edition. The secret printing—the one nailed to forty-one church doors on the anniversary of the flood—had been a lean, urgent thing, printed fast on cheap stock, its pages already browning at the margins like old skin. This one was different. This one was legal. The printer on Tanner's Row had run it openly, his apprentices whistling as they worked, the type set in full daylight without a single shutter drawn. The magistrates' suspension of her sentence was not quite an exoneration—it was a government's way of pretending it had never been afraid—but it was enough. It was paper that permitted other paper.

Ada turned the cover at last.

The title page bore two names now, and she still caught her breath at the sight of them, after all these weeks: *Sandoval & Ferrant, Cartographers.* Beneath, smaller: *Being a Complete Atlas of the City and Coast of Vellamare, with the Quarter of Saltmarsh Restored.*

*Restored.* One word doing the work of ten years.

She heard the bell over the door and did not look up. She knew the tread—Marta Quill walked the way she'd sailed, heel-first, as if daring the floor to argue.

"Well?" Marta said. "Is it fat enough?"

"Four hundred and twelve pages."

"And every one of them true?"

"Every one we could prove." Ada smoothed her palm across the open page. "The rest is marked as unproven. Odile insisted."

"Odile," Marta said, "would insist the tide account for itself line by line." But there was no scorn in it. She came around the counter and stood beside Ada, looking down at the book with the expression of a woman inspecting cargo she intended to trust with her life. "Show me the streets."

Ada turned to the foldout.

It was the plate they had all worked hardest over, the one that had cost Tam Osei three evenings of trembling dictation and cost Ada more sleep than she cared to count. Saltmarsh, as it had stood before the water. Every lane rendered in Corvane's original survey hand—she had traced his linework faithfully, wobble for wobble, because Odile had taught her that fidelity included imperfection—and then, along each drowned street, in a smaller italic face the printer had cut specially, ran the names.

Not the names of the dead. They had tried that first, and it had failed; there was no complete roll of who had drowned, and the gaps gaped like missing teeth. Instead, at Odile's suggestion, they had inscribed the survivors—the living witnesses of Pier Nine and Rope Walk and the Lantern Steps—each name set against the street where they had lived, so that the map read less like a memorial than like a muster. *Here stood Tam Osei, lockkeeper. Here stood the Widow Bren. Here stood the children of the Saltmarsh school, forty-one souls accounted for.*

Grief given geography. Memory made permanent. Odile had said it once, half-drunk, half-serious, and Ada had stolen it for the preface without shame.

"There," Marta said quietly, and touched the page with one blunt finger. Her own name was there—*Marta Quill, née Ferro, Rope Walk*—set among the neighbors she had lost and the ones she had somehow kept. She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. Ada had learned that about Marta: the widow kept her griefs the way she'd kept contraband, sealed tight, moved only at night, but she let you stand next to them.

The bell rang again. Elias Wren came in carrying a crate of books on his shoulder, which was absurd—he was a registrar now, a man of ledgers, not freight—but he refused to let anyone else haul the first delivery, and no one argued with him anymore. He set the crate down, straightened, and looked at Ada with the particular carefulness he still used when something mattered.

"The booksellers took the whole first consignment," he said. "Harrow's on the Quay sold eleven copies before noon. There's a queue."

"A queue," Ada repeated. For an atlas. For testimony.

"For the foldout, mostly." He hesitated, then added, in the lower voice he reserved for things that hurt: "People are going out to the seawall with it. Standing where their streets were. Reading the names aloud."

Ada looked down at the map again—at the blue wash she had laid over the drowned quarter, a blue so pale it was almost a memory of water rather than water itself—and felt the strange double weight of the work: that a thing made to endure could also be alive, moving through the city's hands, changing as it went.

"That's what it's for," she said.

* * *

Odile arrived at midday, carried up the shop steps in the wheeled chair that Tam had built for her himself, with rope handles and iron fittings salvaged from a lock gate. Her illness had settled into something chronic rather than mortal—a banked fire, the physician called it—but her mind burned as clean as ever, and she had appointed herself quality inspector of the entire edition, a role she performed from a corner armchair with a magnifying glass and merciless standards.

"Page two hundred and six," she announced, before Marta had even finished settling the blanket over her knees. "The depth soundings off the old customs pier. Compare them to my corrected figures."

Ada had the proof ready. She had learned to anticipate Odile's audits the way sailors learned to anticipate weather. "Corrected. Your figures, your initials in the margin, exactly as you marked them."

Odile held the magnifier over the page for a long moment. Then she grunted—which, from Odile Ferrant, was a laurel wreath.

"You know what this is," she said, tapping the atlas's cover. "Do you? Truly?"

"A book."

"It is a *record of custody*," Odile said. "Ten years from now, fifty years, some clerk or scholar will want to know how we know what we know. And every answer will be in here. Corvane's field notes, reproduced. My authentication, signed. Osei's sworn statement, witnessed. Wren's Bureau correspondence, published entire—including the parts that embarrassed him." She glanced at Elias, who was shelving books and pretending not to listen, and whose ears had gone red anyway. "That is what separates an archive from a pamphlet. A pamphlet shouts. An archive *answers*."

"I know," Ada said. "You taught me."

"You repeat it back prettily. Whether you know it—" Odile closed the magnifying glass with a snap, "—we shall see when the historians come. And they will come, girl. The official history is dead; no one buys it except collectors of embarrassments. Which means the next generation will build on *this*. On your lines." She fixed Ada with the look she used for examinations. "Are your lines worthy?"

Ada thought of the nights in the boathouse, many hands copying one truth. Of the drawer, the half-burned notes, the levee cut from the landward side. Of her master's voice saying, *the ink outlives us, girl; make sure it says something worth outliving.*

"Yes," she said. No hedging. Odile had cured her of hedging.

"Then sign the colophon properly," Odile said, "and stop standing about looking astonished. You're a publisher now. Astonishment is for amateurs."

* * *

In the afternoon, the Saltmarsh children came.

They came most days now—that was another thing the atlas had done, another current it had set flowing—but today there were nine of them, the eldest perhaps twelve, filing into the shop with the solemnity of a delegation. Their teacher, a thin, fierce woman named Ilse Bren who had lost a father and a house to the water and gained a vocation, herded them to the big table where the open foldout lay under glass.

Ada watched from behind the counter while Ilse taught. Watched the children lean over the map of streets they had never seen, pointing at the places their parents spoke of in lowered voices. Watched one small boy—Tam Osei's neighbor's grandson, she thought—put his finger on the Lantern Steps and ask, "Was this really a road?"

"It was really a road," Ilse said. "Your grandmother climbed it every day of her life until the water came. And do you see the name written beside it?"

He squinted. "'Sera Osei.'"

"My grandmother," said a girl beside him, unexpectedly, "is named for her."

The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when the past walks into them wearing new clothes. Ada found she had to grip the counter's edge.

That was the thing no one had warned her about—not Odile, not Marta, not even her own imagination. She had expected the atlas to be an argument, a weapon, a correction entered into the record. She had not expected it to be a *cradle*. That names set down in ink would be picked up and handed forward, generation to generation, like lamps passed down a dark stair. That a map could be a promise: *you were here, you counted, you will be counted again.*

When the children left, waving, Ilse lingered at the door.

"They're asking to learn drafting," she said. "Real drafting. Survey work. I can teach letters and numbers, but not the craft." She met Ada's eyes with the directness of someone who had survived the loss of everything soft. "I'm told you take apprentices."

"I take one at a time," Ada said. "And I'm strict."

"Good. So am I." Ilse almost smiled. "Send word when you have a bench free."

After the door closed, Marta, who had heard everything from the back room, emerged drying her hands on her apron. "Well," she said. "Corvane took you in off the street. Now look what you're doing."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You agreed the moment your face did that thing it does." Marta tapped her own cheek. "There. That thing. Save it for the magistrates; it doesn't work on me."

* * *

Evening came down over the harbor the way it always did, gold going to copper going to violet, and Ada stayed late alone in the shop, as she had in the old days—though nothing about these days was the old days, and she no longer needed to keep the shutters drawn. She lit the lamp over the drafting table and opened the atlas to the last plate.

It was the one she had fought hardest to include and hardest to draw: not Saltmarsh restored, but Saltmarsh *now*—the seawall, the grey water, the channel markers, the empty space rendered honestly as empty. Beneath it, a single line of text, approved by all of them after long argument:

*This page intentionally records absence. Absence, too, must be surveyed.*

She had drawn it the night after the hearing, working by feel more than sight, her hands remembering what her eyes refused to measure. Tam had come to check on her around midnight, stood behind her chair for a long time, and finally said only: "You've got the depth right. Where the lock gates were. Nobody gets that right." Then he had gone home and slept, he told her later, better than he had slept in ten years.

A knock at the door. Through the glass she saw Elias, hat in hand despite the cold, holding a letter.

He came in stamping the damp off his boots. "From the ministry," he said. "Officially addressed to 'the publishers Sandoval & Ferrant.' Officially." He set it on the counter between them. "Unofficially, I pulled strings to see it early. Read it."

Ada broke the seal. The language was ministerial, circular, dense with self-protection—but the substance was simple. The Bureau of Records hereby acknowledged receipt of the second edition and requested, *in the interest of completing the national archive*, that the publishers deposit copies of all source materials, survey notes, and sworn statements, such materials henceforth to be held *in perpetuity* under official custodianship.

"They want the originals," Ada said.

"They want control of the originals," Elias corrected. "Deposit copies, keep your duplicates, and it means nothing—they'll shelve it and forget it. Refuse, and they'll spend years trying to compel you, and the fight will drown the book in procedure." He rubbed his jaw. "Or there's a third road."

"Which is?"

"Odile's old post. Keeper of Deposits. It's been vacant since the purge—no one wanted it, too much shame attached. If one of you holds the office, the collection comes *with* the office. Official custody, official funding, official protection." He paused. "And official access for any historian, any student, anyone, forever. No minister can seal it without making noise about why."

Ada stared at him. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about little else since the hearing." His mouth twisted. "I spent seven years counting people for a machine that erased them. Let me spend the rest of my career making sure no one can erase them again. But I won't take it unless the collection goes in whole—with Corvane's notes, with Tam's statement, with everything. And I can't offer that unless the publishers agree to deposit."

Ada looked at the letter, then past it, through the dark window toward the harbor, where the seawall stood invisible but certain, holding back the sea above the streets where her master had died for the truth and where the truth now lived in four hundred printed pages.

Her master had worked in hiding. She had worked in fear. And now the work was being invited inside the walls of the state itself—to be locked, protected, and *kept*, the way a clock keeps time whether anyone watches it or not.

"We'll need to convene the others," she said. "Marta. Odile. Tam. It's their testimony as much as mine."

"I know. Take your time." Elias gathered his coat, then stopped at the door, and something younger broke through the registrar's gravity. "Ada. Eleven copies before noon. At Harrow's. People are reading your names aloud on the seawall." He shook his head slowly, as if the arithmetic still wouldn't balance. "I just wanted you to hear it from someone who counts."

The bell chimed behind him. Ada stood a long moment in the lamplight, the letter in one hand and the atlas open before her—absence surveyed, presence recorded, a city arguing with itself about who belonged and, for the first time in ten years, losing the argument to the truth.

Then she reached for a fresh sheet, dipped her pen, and began to draft the terms of deposit—in her own hand, under her own name, for the record.
