## 3. A Knock After Curfew

Curfew in this city is a peculiar kind of politeness. The guns agree to stop at dusk, the patrols agree to march instead of shoot, and the rest of us agree to pretend we are asleep behind our shutters, all of us lying in the dark like a city of bad actors. I have lived under three curfews now—Russian, then theirs, then whoever's it is this month—and I have learned that the only honest hour is the one just after the lamps go out, when even the liars run out of energy.

I was doing the accounts. That is what I call it when I sit in the dispensary with a candle stub and add up the day's small failures: two men feverish, one man gone, forty bandages boiled when we needed sixty, and Sister Anneli's eyebrow rising at my request for more laudanum as though I were asking for brandy for my own pleasure. The hospital's official ledger sat open on my left. My own ledger did not exist at all, because a thing that does not exist cannot be found, and I have made a religion of that arithmetic.

My rule is simple, and I recommend it to anyone intending to outlive a war: carry nothing you cannot run with. No photographs. No letters. No keepsakes except what fits against the ribs without printing through a coat. When I came to this posting I owned one bag, and I have kept it that way deliberately, the way other women keep their figures. It has made me unpopular at gift-giving occasions, which suits me fine.

The knock came at half past ten.

Not on the main doors—no one knocks on the main doors of a military hospital after curfew unless they are drunk, dying, or delivering trouble, and often all three. This was on the dispensary's side door, the one I leave unbolted because the orderlies forget to check it and because once, in November, a man crawled to it holding most of his hand in his cap, and I have not had the heart to bolt it since.

I put out the candle first. Habit before thought; thought arrived a half-second later and approved. Then I stood in the dark and listened.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. Pause. One knock.

That pattern meant nothing to me, which frightened me more than any pattern would have. A signal you recognize belongs to someone; a signal you don't belong to no one yet, and no one is the most dangerous thing knocking at your door in a occupied port city in December.

I opened the door the width of a hand.

Cold came in first, the kind of Baltic cold that feels personal, and behind the cold, a woman. Hood drawn low, wool coat stiff with frost where her breath had frozen on it, one gloved hand braced against the frame. She was young under the hood—younger than me by some years—but she stood like someone who had stopped being young somewhere in the last month and hadn't been informed formally.

Behind her skirt, half-hidden, a boy.

"Are you the night nurse?" she said. Her accent was from inland, the flat vowels of the farming coast, scrubbed nearly clean by effort.

"I'm a nurse," I said, which was true and also a lesson in how much truth can be made useless by subtraction. "The hospital takes wounded soldiers. Not civilians. Not—" I looked at the boy again. He was perhaps six, wearing a coat built for a bigger child and a scarf wound around his neck so many times it reached his eyebrows. He shivered in a way that had gone past violent into something steady and mechanical, a shivering that had become simply how he existed. "Not children."

"I know what it takes." Her voice dropped. "Please. Five minutes."

Every sensible instinct I own lined up at the door of my skull and refused entry. I am not a sentimental woman. I have watched men die of gangrene with better manners than most bishops show at dinner, and I have buried four colleagues in two years and grieved each of them precisely as long as the work allowed. Sentiment, in a hospital, gets people killed at roughly the same rate as incompetence, and it is harder to court-martial.

But the boy looked up at me. Just that. And there was something in his face—not pleading; he was too far past pleading, the way the worst patients are too tired to ring the bell—and I thought, with the strange clarity that sometimes visits me between one breath and the next: *this child has already been told he doesn't exist.*

I know that look. I keep a ledger full of it.

"Inside," I said. "Quickly."

* * *

She would not come past the doorway. That should have told me everything, and it told me most of it. She stepped one boot onto the stone and no further, as though the threshold were a border post, and she pulled the glove off her right hand with her teeth—a gesture so practiced it broke my heart in a small, efficient way, the way competence in desperate people always does.

In her palm was a folded paper, small as a communion wafer.

"His name is Emil," she said. "Don't write it down anywhere. Don't say it loudly. Don't tell him you know anything about—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "He's had fever twice this winter. If it comes again, the left ear, the side of his neck, it goes to the chest. You'll know what to do. No one else will."

"Where is his mother?"

"You're looking at her." Flat. Not bitter. A woman reporting weather.

"And his father?"

For a moment I thought she would strike me. Then I saw it wasn't anger holding her still; it was arithmetic, the same arithmetic I do every night, weighing what can be said against what can be survived. Whatever number she reached, it was: nothing.

"He's dead," she said finally. "Officially."

There is a whole education in the word *officially*, if you've been paying attention these last years. I had been paying attention. I felt the floor tilt very slightly under my feet, the way it does when a case turns, and I made myself keep my voice at the pitch of discussing linen inventories.

"You want me to keep him here. In a military ward. During an inspection season."

"I want you to keep him alive until spring." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and they were not begging eyes. They were assessing eyes. "They said—the people who told me where to come—they said you were careful."

"Careful," I repeated. "That's a flattering word for what I'd call it."

"They said you write things down." And here her voice went so quiet that I had to lean into the cold to catch it, which I suspect was her intention, because a woman leaning into a doorway is a smaller target than a woman standing back from one. "Names. Names that got taken off the reports."

The candle I had extinguished might as well have been blazing behind me. Every hair on my arms stood up under two layers of wool.

"I don't know what you've been told," I said, "or by whom, but this is a military hospital and I am a nurse with one bag to my name and no interest in—"

She pressed the paper into my hand. Folded small, thrust forward with the efficiency of a woman passing contraband, which is exactly what she was and exactly what I suddenly, horribly, was becoming. Then she crouched, which took her below my sightline and level with the boy, and said something to him in a language I didn't catch—low, fast, the tone unmistakable everywhere in the world. *Be good. Be brave. Be quiet.*

Emil said nothing. He looked at her the way soldiers look at departing officers: understanding perfectly that the order is final and hating it anyway.

Then she stood, pulled her glove back on with those same capable teeth, and was gone down the alley before I had assembled a single sentence worth saying. I heard her boots on the frozen mud, quick and even, already a memory. Some people are born to make exits. I have spent my whole life rehearsing them and still fumble the hem.

The boy and I regarded one another across the threshold while the wind considered us both and found us wanting.

"Well," I said. "You'd better come in before the patrol does."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding a child in a hospital: it is absurdly easy, and that is the frightening part. A ward full of broken men generates so much legitimate noise, so many legitimate shadows, that one small extra shadow vanishes into it like a drop into the sea. I took his hand—it was a piece of ice with fingers—and walked him down the corridor at the pace of a woman fetching linens, and no one looked at us, because no one looks at anyone here. Looking is a luxury. We are all saving up.

The linen store sits at the corridor's end, windowless, smelling of carbolic and starch and the ghost of decades of fever. There is a gap behind the lowest shelf where the wall bows away from the plaster—I found it my first winter and filed it away the way I file everything, under *someday*, never guessing someday would arrive wearing a six-year-old's boots.

"There's a blanket on the second shelf. Take it and get behind there. If anyone comes in, you're asleep and you heard nothing, which shouldn't be hard, because you'll be asleep."

He looked at me with an expression I would learn well in the coming weeks: the look of a child deciding whether an adult is a fact or merely a rumor. Then he took the blanket, folded himself into the gap with the boneless economy only children and cats possess, and disappeared. Thirty seconds, and the room contained one nurse and a great deal of linen.

I had barely re-straightened the shelves when the boots started.

Patrols pass the hospital on a schedule I could recite drunk: the pair at eleven, sloppy and bored; the sergeant's rounds at midnight, less bored; the two o'clock sweep that is either thorough or theatrical depending on the officer's ambitions. But these boots were early, and they were not sloppy. Four men, the measured tread of men who have been told tonight matters.

I picked up a stack of bandage rolls I did not need and began counting them, because a woman alone in a linen store at night must be doing something countable, and the door opened.

Lieutenant Kessler. Brandt's aide. I knew him by the way he stood in doorways—squarely, taking up the frame entirely, a man practicing for larger rooms. Behind him, two military policemen with lanterns, and behind them, the night orderly, Halversen, whose face was performing innocence so badly it should have been arrested on its own merits.

"Sister." Kessler's voice was pleasant. Everything about Kessler was pleasant the way a scalpel is pleasant: clean, precise, designed for opening. "Late for laundry."

"The boil schedule runs overnight, Lieutenant. Bandages for the morning changes." I counted another roll into the stack. Fifty-one. "You're welcome to inspect the store, but I'd caution you against touching the carbolic shelf. The third bottle leaks."

One of the MPs lifted his lantern and swept it along the shelves. Light crawled across starched white rows, across buckets, across the bowed wall where a boy was breathing so quietly I could hear my own heart interfering with the count. Fifty-two. I discovered that terror, properly channeled, is excellent for arithmetic. Perhaps that is why the army loves its paperwork.

"The Colonel's adjutant staff will conduct inspections beginning Thursday," Kessler said. "Ward rolls will be checked against admission registers. Discrepancies will be reported." He let that sit in the air between us like a coal. "You keep the registers current, Sister?"

"To the minute." Fifty-three. "The army taught me that much."

Something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly; assessment. A man pricing goods. "See that it stays so. Good night."

The lanterns withdrew. The boots receded in their measured fours. Halversen shot me a glance on the way out that contained, I believe, an apology, or possibly a warning; with Halversen the two arrive in the same envelope.

I set down the bandages. My hands, now that they were no longer needed for counting, elected to shake, and I let them, briefly, in private, the way one lets a fever break. Then I knelt at the gap in the wall.

Two eyes looked out at me from the blanket. Wide. Perfectly silent. A child who had been taught this silence so thoroughly, and so recently, that it was still fresh paint.

"They're gone," I whispered.

He uncurled slowly, and I saw that in one fist, held so tightly the knuckles had gone white, he clutched something I hadn't noticed him carrying. A wooden horse, carved small enough to hide in a child's hand, its mane worn smooth by love. He saw me see it, and his whole body flinched around it, guarding it, ready to lie for it.

"It's safe with me," I said. "Everything in this room is safe with me."

He didn't answer. Six years old and he already knew better than to believe sentences that begin that way. God, what we do to children, and call it strategy.

I unfolded the note then, by feel, holding it close so no stray light could find it. Small, precise handwriting, the letters of someone educated above her station:

*Forget this face.*

Three words. No name, no date, no destination, nothing so useful as a plea. Just an instruction, and instructions are the most revealing documents in the world if you read them backwards. You don't tell someone to forget a face unless the face is known to somebody dangerous. You don't leave a child with a stranger unless you know the stranger's habits. And you don't know a stranger's habits unless you've been watching her—

Unless someone has told you what the stranger writes in a ledger that officially does not exist.

I sat back on my heels in the dark, with the boy breathing behind me and the patrol's echo long faded, and understood that the door I had opened tonight had not been a dispensary door at all. Somewhere in this city, someone knew about my ledger. Someone had pointed a mother and her son at me like an arrow at a target, and now the thing I had built to be invisible had a six-year-old sleeping inside it, warm and alive and utterly undeniable.

Evidence, I thought. That's what I'm keeping behind my lowest shelf. Living, coughing, fever-prone evidence of everything the reports say never happened.

I should have been afraid. I was—I was afraid down to the marrow, in the practical way, the way of a woman calculating distances to checkpoints. But underneath the fear, something else had shifted its weight and settled, the way ice settles on a river just before it becomes a bridge.

The boy's name was Emil. His father was officially dead. And somewhere between those two facts lay a story the army had signed its name to in invisible ink.

I blew on my hands, banked my fear with the rest of the coals, and began, quietly, to plan.

Tomorrow I would need to find out whose son he was.

I had a suspicion I already knew—and I had written the name down myself, in a ledger full of men the papers insisted had never been born.
