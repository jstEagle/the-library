## 4. The Boy Who Wasn't There

My hands are too small.

I know this because Papa's hands were big enough to cover my whole face and still leave room for him to see between his fingers when he played the bear game. Bear game was where he put his hands over my eyes and said, *where is Emil? Where has he gone?* and I had to say nothing because if you say anything the bear finds you, and then he took his hands away and said *there he is* and I always laughed even though I knew the whole time where I was. That is the joke of the bear game. You know where you are. The bear doesn't.

Now nobody knows where I am. Not even me, really. There are beds in rows like teeth and men in the beds and they cough in turns like a song nobody wants to learn. It smells sharp, like when Mama cut onions but never stops cutting them, forever, everywhere.

The woman who found me at the door smells different from that. She smells like soap that has been used a lot, which I think means she works hard, and underneath it something warm like bread crusts. Her name is Freya. She said it twice so I would remember. I remembered.

* * *

Here is what I am not supposed to do. I am not supposed to say my surname.

Mama told me on the road, walking fast in the dark, holding my wrist so tight I thought my hand might fall off and then I could be two boys instead of one and one of them could go home to bed. She crouched down in the snow by a fence and held my face in her cold hands and said, *Emil. Listen. If anyone asks your family name, you don't have one. Boys who have no name are invisible. Invisible boys can't be caught.*

I asked what happens to boys who get caught.

She didn't answer. When Mama doesn't answer it's worse than when she does.

So I practiced being invisible. It's easy in the daytime if there are lots of things to look at, because people look at things and not at boys. It's harder at night. At night I say my surname inside my head over and over like counting stairs, and it keeps me from falling down. I can't tell you what it is. Even now. Even here. Maybe especially here. But I say it. Every night before sleep takes me, I climb the stairs of it, all the way up, and at the top is Papa saying *there he is.*

* * *

Papa had a way of holding things. Not just me — everything. He could hold a whole egg in one hand and crack it one-handed into the pan and not get any shell in, and I tried for one hundred days to do it and got shell in every time and he ate the eggs anyway, every time, shell and all, and said *mm, crunchy, my favorite.* He let soldiers hold his arm like that too. He was a sergeant, which I thought meant he was in charge of the sarge, like a potato. He laughed so hard when I said that. He laughed until he had to sit down on the chopping block and wipe his eyes, and Mama came to the door with flour on her hands to see what was funny, and he couldn't even tell her, he just pointed at me, and she smiled the small smile she kept for when Papa was being ridiculous, which was often.

His hands smelled like leather and woodsmoke and sometimes horse. He had a scar across one knuckle shaped like a river going nowhere. I asked about the scar once and he said, *a door bit me,* and I believed that for a long time, longer than I should have, probably, but doors do seem like they could bite. Ours had a latch that snapped.

Then our house burned down, and after that I stopped believing most things.

* * *

I will tell it wrong. Grown-ups always want you to tell things right, in order, with reasons, but my insides don't keep things in order. My insides keep things like pockets keep things. So: fire first, or maybe the shouting first, or maybe the boots. Boots came before fire. Boots on the road, many boots, and Mama pulling the curtain shut and telling me to play quietly, which is a thing you say to boys, but her voice was doing the thing where it's flat and careful like ice that might break, so I knew quiet wasn't playing anymore.

Then light through the curtain seams, orange, wrong color for morning.

Then heat. You don't forget heat. Heat is a hand that pushes you even when nothing touches you.

We went out the back and the barn was already roaring and the sky was full of sparks like angry stars that had come down to complain. And Papa wasn't there. Papa had gone out earlier, before the boots, before anything, and Mama kept saying *he'll find us, he knows the road, he'll find us,* and we walked, and walked, and the snow got into my boots and melted and froze again, and somewhere along that road she started calling me by a different name. Not my real one. A borrowed one, like wearing another boy's coat. *Emil,* she'd say, but then other names for other people, quick as dealing cards. I got confused about which boy I was supposed to be. Sometimes I answered to the wrong name and she squeezed my wrist, which meant *wrong boy, be the other one.*

And then the hospital steps, and her face above mine, and the note pushed into my coat, and her mouth against my ear saying *forget this face,* which is a strange instruction because how do you forget a face? Which face? Hers? Mine? Whose face does she want forgotten?

I didn't ask. When Mama doesn't answer questions it's worse than answering, so I've learned to save my questions like coins, and spend them only when I'm sure of the shop.

* * *

Freya found me on the steps. I don't remember much of that part because I was mostly asleep standing up, the way you can when you're very cold and very done. But I remember her hands. They weren't Papa's hands. Nobody's are. But they did the thing where they checked me all over, quick, like she was reading me, and she made a sound low in her throat when she felt how cold my feet were, and that sound was an angry sound but not angry at me. Angry on my behalf. I didn't know people could do that. Be angry for you instead of at you.

She carried me inside. I'm nearly seven and getting heavy, Mama says, and I offered to walk, I think, or meant to, but my legs had resigned without asking me. So she carried me past all the beds of coughing men and someone whistled low and someone else said something about curfew and she said, *he's a patient,* in a voice that closed like a gate, and everyone believed her.

I decided to believe her too. That's the arrangement, I think. She pretends I'm a patient. I pretend I'm invisible. Between us we make one boy who exists just enough.

* * *

The linen store is where I live now. It's a little room full of folded things, sheets stacked like clouds that gave up, and it smells like everyone in the building at once, which sounds terrible but is actually company. Freya brings me food on a tray and teaches me signals. One knock means stay quiet. Two knocks means it's her. Three knocks means hide behind the lowest shelf and pull the sheet over yourself and breathe through your mouth, slow, like a sleeping bear.

I asked her why three knocks would ever happen, if one means danger and two means her.

She looked at me for a while. She has eyes that do sums; I could see the sum happening. Then she said, *because the world is badly organized, Emil, and we must be better organized than it.*

I liked that answer so much I repeated it to myself four times before bed.

At night the ward goes quiet except for coughing and moaning and once a man calling for somebody named Lotte over and over, softer each time, until Lotte was only a breath and then not even that. I lay in the dark of the linen room and listened to Lotte go out of the world like a candle, and I did the stairs of my surname, up and up, faster than usual, and at the top Papa said *there he is,* and I cried a little into a pillowcase that smelled like forty strangers, and that was fine. Crying in the dark is allowed. It's crying where people can see that costs questions.

* * *

Yesterday — or some day, the days here are all wearing the same coat — a man with one arm taught me to fold bandages.

He sat in his chair by the window with his sleeve pinned up and showed me how to make the folds even, edge to edge, like making paper soldiers. His name I'm keeping quiet, because names are dangerous now, but he let me practice on a whole basket and he inspected each one like a general and said *acceptable* or *no, again,* and when I finally made three perfect ones in a row he nodded like I'd been promoted.

*You fold better than half my old company,* he said.

*I had a good teacher,* I said, because Papa taught me paper soldiers first, and the words came out before I remembered about the coins, about saving things. The one-armed man looked at me sideways. Grown-ups do that sideways looking when they think you've said more than you meant to.

But he didn't ask. He just said, *good teachers are worth their weight in winter,* and we folded bandages until my arms were tired, and it was the best afternoon I've had since the boots.

That's the strange arithmetic of this place, which I am learning even though I'm not in school anymore: everything is smaller here — the food, the light, the hope, probably — and yet somehow the afternoons are bigger. Folding bandages with a man who has one arm felt bigger than whole weeks of before. I don't understand it. I'm writing it down anyway, in my head, in the pocket where I keep things that don't fit.

* * *

Tonight Freya brought me bread with actual butter on it, which she pretended was normal, but butter hasn't been normal anywhere for months, so I knew it was a present disguised as dinner. I ate it slow to make it last and she sat on the crate across from me and watched, and I watched back, because that's fair.

*Emil,* she said. *Do you know your father's full name?*

The question dropped into the room like a coin into a well. I heard it falling for a long time.

Because here's the trouble: yes, I know it. Of course I know it. I've been climbing those stairs every single night. But Mama said no name, and Mama's rules are stitched into me deeper than my own voice, and also — also — Freya asked it in a particular way. Not *what is your father's name* but *do you know it.* As if knowing it mattered. As if something depended on whether I knew.

Grown-ups' questions are doors. Some open onto rooms you want. Some open onto cold.

Freya waited. She didn't push. She did the thing where her eyes did sums and her mouth stayed kind, and I thought about Papa cracking eggs one-handed and eating the shells and saying *my favorite,* and I thought about Mama's wrist-grip in the snow saying *invisible,* and I thought about the one-armed man saying *again,* patiently, until I got the fold right.

I opened my mouth. I don't know yet what was going to come out — the name, or the silence, or the question I've been saving about whose face I'm supposed to forget.

Before any of it could arrive, there came footsteps in the corridor. Heavy ones. Boots, in fact, and more than one pair, and Freya stood up so fast the crate scraped, and she put one finger to her lips.

One knock's worth of meaning, delivered without knocking at all.

I slid behind the lowest shelf and pulled the sheet over myself and began to breathe through my mouth, slow, like a sleeping bear — while out in the ward, a man's voice, flat and important, asked the nurses where they had been keeping the boy from the steps.
