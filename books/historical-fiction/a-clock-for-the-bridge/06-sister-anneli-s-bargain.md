## 6. Sister Anneli's Bargain

I have been a head nurse for nineteen years, and in that time I have discovered exactly three things hidden in the linen store: a bottle of aquavit belonging to Dr. Møller, which I confiscated and then drank; a litter of kittens, which I confiscated and then kept; and now, folded into the space between the winter blankets where no one folds anything, a boy.

He was asleep when I found him, which was considerate of him. The kittens at least had the decency to mew. This child lay with his fists drawn up under his chin like a small boxer who had lost on points, breathing in that thin, careful way children breathe when they have taught themselves not to be heard. I knew that breathing. I had done it myself once, in a root cellar in '63, while soldiers argued above us about how much hay they were owed.

I stood in the doorway of the linen store with my lantern held high — an idiot's posture, frankly. If anyone had walked past they would have seen Sister Anneli Holm illuminating a six-year-old boy like a painting she intended to appraise. But the corridor was empty. The ward beyond was sleeping its drugged, groaning sleep. And so I lowered the lantern and looked at him properly.

Small. Pale in the way of children who have stopped eating regularly but are too polite to mention it. Boots two sizes too large, cinched at the ankle with string. Someone had loved those boots onto his feet; nobody ties string like that out of indifference.

There was a note in Freya's handwriting pinned to the blanket — *he is mine to worry about, say nothing, I'll explain* — which told me two things. First, that Freya Iversen had finally found something worth breaking her rules over. Second, that Freya thought a note could stop me from asking questions, which meant she did not know me as well as she believed.

I am sixty-one years old. I have buried a husband, two brothers, and a great many illusions about the honesty of men in uniform. A note does not stop me. A note merely tells me where to begin.

I put out the lantern, sat down on the crate beside the shelf, and waited for morning like a woman waiting for a train she already knows will be late.

* * *

Here is what the record will say about me, if any record survives this century, which I doubt: Anneli Holm, head nurse, Second Military Hospital, competent, unmarried, difficult. They use "difficult" about women who ask why eleven men can die on a Tuesday and appear in no report by Friday. I have been difficult since 1871. It is the only distinction I have ever pursued deliberately.

My brothers — Torvald and Eirik, if you want their names, and you should always want names, names are the whole game — died within four months of each other in a campaign whose name we were later instructed to pronounce differently. Torvald came home in a box with no letter. Eirik did not come home at all. When my mother wrote to the regiment asking after him, she received a reply so courteous it might have been composed for a bishop, informing her that no soldier of that name appeared on the rolls of the engagement in question. No soldier of that name. My mother read that sentence perhaps forty times aloud, as though repetition might dissolve its arithmetic. She died still believing she had misremembered her own son's existence.

I did not believe it. I was twenty-nine and furious, and fury is the only fuel that burns clean at that age. But belief without position accomplishes nothing, and I had no position. I had a broom and a bedpan and a ward full of boys whose names I learned and whose deaths I watched get subtracted, year upon year, from the official sum of things. So I did what careful women do. I stayed. I became indispensable. I made certain that every man under my care received one extra measure of kindness than his paperwork entitled him to, because paperwork is a liar and kindness is not, and I called that a bargain with my conscience and considered the interest paid.

And then Freya Iversen arrived, five years ago now, all long silences and steady hands, and within a month I saw her doing sums behind her eyes that I recognized. You cannot watch a girl copy casualty lists by candlelight — and yes, I knew about the ledger almost from the beginning; I am the head nurse, nothing happens under these floorboards without my knowing, including the floorboards — you cannot watch that and mistake it for anything other than what I had wanted to do at twenty-nine and had not dared.

I never asked her directly. There is a courtesy among conspirators even before the conspiracy begins. But I began leaving things where she would find them: the discharge file with the suspicious gap, the requisition form signed twice in different inks. If she was going to build her little archive of the disappeared, she would do it with better materials than most. Call it my contribution. Call it, if you like, an old woman paying installments on a debt thirty years overdue.

Now there was a boy in my linen store, and the note said *mine to worry about*, and I sat in the dark doing the one calculation I had sworn off decades ago: what is this going to cost, and who pays?

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of coal smoke, as mornings here do, and with it came Freya, moving down the corridor with her tray of dressings and her face arranged into its usual mask of mild competence. She is good at that face. I have watched her wear it through inspections, through deaths, through Colonel Brandt's visits, which are inspections with better tailoring. But I have also watched her hands, and hands confess everything. That morning her hands were wrong — too deliberate, setting each instrument down as if it might detonate.

She reached the linen store door, saw me standing beside it, and stopped. For one full second her face simply fell, the way a dropped thing falls, and then caught itself and reassembled. Nineteen years of wards teaches you to read that second. Guilt, fear, and something underneath both that looked, absurdly, like apology.

"Sister," she said. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early. It's the rest of the world that sleeps in." I opened the store room door and stepped aside so she could see past me to the shelf, where the boy sat up blinking in the light, hair flattened on one side, clutching a folded blanket to his chest as though he'd been caught stealing it. "Your note said he was yours to worry about. I've worried. He needs feeding, his boots need inner soles, and he flinches at footsteps, which tells me more than your entire note did."

Freya closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them she was, I think, preparing a lie, and I confess I felt a flicker of hurt at being considered lieable-to. Then something in my expression must have changed her mind, because the lie visibly collapsed mid-throat and what came out instead was true, or near enough.

"His father was Sergeant Anders Brandt-Voss. Emil Anderssen. He's listed nowhere, Sister. Not on the wounded rolls, not on the dead ones. His father isn't listed anywhere either — not anymore." Her voice stayed level, but only just. "The mother left him at our door three nights ago with instructions to forget his face."

"And instead you hid him among the sheets."

"Instead I hid him among the sheets."

I looked at the boy again. He was watching us with the particular attention of children who have learned that adults' conversations determine whether they eat. Six years old. Father erased. Mother fled. Face officially forgettable. I ran the numbers the way I used to run them in '71, standing over my mother's letters, and got the same total: nothing owed to this child by anyone with the power to pay.

"Well," I said. "He can't stay in the linen store. Møller counts the blankets now that the aquavit went missing — he blames the orderlies, poor devils — and he'll find him inside a week." I took the tray from Freya's hands, because if we were going to be fools we would at least be organized fools. "He'll be my sister's boy, come from the country. Greta's grandson. She lives near Viborg; no one here has met her and no one ever will. He helps with linens. Children fold badly, but no one inspects a child's corners."

Freya stared at me. It occurs to me now that in five years she had probably catalogued every possible response I might make — report, blackmail, cold refusal — and had not included *inner soles*. Good. Let the young be surprised occasionally. It keeps the heart supple.

"You knew," she said slowly. "About the ledger. You've known."

"Since the week you started it. You hum when you copy names. You think you don't, but you do. It's the same tune every time." I set the tray down on the crate. "I knew because I nearly started one myself, thirty years ago, and lost my nerve, and have regretted it in the manner of a toothache ever since. Two brothers. Torvald and Eirik. Neither appears on any roll you'll find in this building, or any other. My mother died believing she'd invented one of them."

The ward noise rose and fell somewhere beyond the door — coughing, a moan, the scrape of a trolley — the ordinary liturgy of the place. Freya didn't speak. She had gone very still, the stillness she uses at bedsides when a man is deciding whether to fight or let go.

"So," I continued, before either of us drowned in it, "here is my bargain, and I'll state it plainly because plainness is all I have left. You keep your ledger, and you keep that child alive, and I look the other way at whatever those two enterprises require. When you need a quarantine sign forged, I hold the stencil. When you need an hour unaccounted for, I account for it. In exchange—" and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized, a slight crack, like ice underfoot, "—in exchange you put my brothers' names in it. Wherever the book goes, whatever becomes of it, Torvald and Eirik Holm go with it. I don't care if I'm alive to see it. I care that they exist somewhere on paper that tells the truth."

"They're already in it," Freya said quietly.

I confess I sat down on the crate rather abruptly. Nineteen years of composure, undone by a nurse half my age holding a tray of dressings.

"I found them in the old campaign files last spring," she went on, looking at the floor now, embarrassed, as though kindness were the indecent act in the room. "Two entries, struck through in the same hand as the others. I copied them the same night. Page fourteen." She finally looked up. "I should have told you. I didn't know how to tell you without explaining why I knew."

Page fourteen. I have not looked at page fourteen since, and I will not describe to you what it cost me to learn that my brothers had been sitting beneath the floorboards of my own hospital for a year while I stepped over them daily. Some gifts arrive so late they feel like accusations. But that is the nature of debts this old; you take the payment and you weep in the linen store about it privately, which, for the record, I did, after Freya left, with the boy patting my arm awkwardly and offering me half of his bread.

Half his bread. The child who owned nothing, whose own mother had instructed the world to forget him, divided his breakfast with a crying old woman he'd met an hour prior. I have nursed colonels. I know which of God's creatures shows better character under deprivation, and it is not the officers.

* * *

We established the routine that same week, and I will grant Freya this: she thinks like a quartermaster. The boy — Emil, though publicly he answered to "Nils," my fictional grandson, a name chosen in honor of no one, selected precisely because it was so dull no one would remember it — slept in my quarters, rose before the ward, and spent his days in the linen store folding, badly, then less badly. Freya taught him letters in the afternoons under cover of teaching him inventory. By December he could write *sheet, blanket, pillowcase*, and one word more that I pretended not to notice him practicing: a longer word, written slowly, tongue between his teeth, on the backs of discarded requisition forms. I did not read it upside down across the table. All right — I read it. It was his father's surname. The one he'd been ordered never to say.

I let him practice it anyway. Orders to forget are the only orders I have ever taught a child to disobey.

It was, I admit, almost pleasant, those first weeks. There is a particular warmth that gathers around a secret shared by people who have each already lost what mattered, a warmth the young mistake for happiness. Freya laughed twice that I counted — genuinely, unexpectedly, startling herself both times. The one-armed sergeant from bed nine taught Emil to whistle through his fingers. I caught myself humming, which Freya informed me I also do, tunelessly, and which I declined to dignify with a response.

But I am sixty-one, and I have survived this long by refusing the comfort of pleasant. Every evening I stood at the ward window and watched the garrison lights and did my sums again. A boy hidden. A ledger accumulating. A colonel whose career rests on the fiction that men like this child's father never drew breath. Those three facts were circling each other like dogs on a chain, and I had been a nurse long enough to know that when dogs circle, eventually someone leaves the gate open.

On the fourth Thursday of December, the summons came. Not for Freya, not yet — for me. An invitation, printed on garrison stationery, informing me that Colonel Brandt would conduct a routine inspection of the Second Military Hospital's facilities and staff on Saturday morning, and that all records should be made available.

All records. I read the phrase four times by lamplight while Emil slept a cot's length away, and I thought of floorboards, of coal dust, of a ledger that hummed when it was copied. Routine inspections do not specify records. Inspections that expect to find nothing do not bother announcing themselves at all.

I burned the invitation in the stove, fed the fire until the corner bearing the garrison seal curled black, and went to wake Freya.

"We have two days," I told her, "and I suspect neither of us believes it's routine."
