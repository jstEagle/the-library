## 7. Brandt Inspects the Ward

Colonel Brandt came to inspect the ward on a Tuesday morning in early December, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day we changed the worst dressings and I had long ago concluded that the war ran on a schedule of deliberate cruelty.

Sister Anneli brought the news at half past seven, while I was coaxing porridge into Private Lund. "He's coming," she said, low, not looking at me, smoothing a sheet that did not need smoothing. "The Colonel. With his aide and two staff officers. They'll want the full tour. He's expected within the hour."

The porridge spoon went very still in my hand. Lund, who had one leg and a philosopher's patience, said, "Bad news, Sister? You've the look of a woman reading her own obituary."

"Only a colonel," I said.

"Ah," said Lund, who had been at the front. "Worse than bad news."

Here is what you must understand about my situation that morning: hidden in the linen store, forty feet from where Brandt would begin his tour, slept a six-year-old boy whose existence was, on paper, an impossibility, and whose father's name appeared nowhere in any official record because I had made certain it appeared in mine. Emil had been with us four days. Four days of learning to fold bandages and being fed by stealth and sleeping like a small warm secret between stacks of sheets — and now the man who erased his father from history was walking into the building to count noses.

I finished Lund's porridge with a steady hand, which surprised me. The body is an obedient animal. It learns to do the work while the mind runs screaming in circles somewhere behind the sternum.

"Anneli," I said in the corridor, "I need the linen store counted this morning. Officially. I'll do it myself before the tour."

She looked at me for exactly one second longer than necessary, which in our language was a whole conversation. "Take your time with the counting," she said. "I'll put the fever cases nearest the door. Staff officers hate fever. They'll keep to the far side."

God preserve Sister Anneli. She had lost two brothers to reports signed by men like Brandt, though she never said so aloud; she only arranged wards, quietly, so that the powerful were kept comfortable and the inconvenient were kept alive.

* * *

The linen store is a narrow room off the east corridor, windowless, smelling of starch and carbolic and — since four days ago — faintly of child. I went in, locked the door behind me, and knelt among the shelves.

"Emil." I kept my voice at the pitch one uses for telling secrets to bread dough. "Emil, wake up. We're going to play the quiet game."

He surfaced from under a folded sheet like something from a winter pond, hair standing up, one sock missing as usual. "Is it the loud game?"

"No game today is loud. Today the best players don't make a single sound. Not a cough, not a sneeze, not a whisper. Can you do that?"

"How long?"

"Perhaps an hour."

His face did arithmetic. Six-year-old arithmetic takes a while. "An hour is very long."

"An hour is nothing," I said, "if you win it." I pulled out the bottom shelf — it slid on its runner, a trick I'd oiled myself — revealing a cavity against the wall where the floorboards hadn't been true since before the war. Not a cellar. A gap. Deep enough for a boy if he folded up like a letter. I'd lined it with blankets, and I will confess here what I confessed to no one: I'd lined it the first night, before I'd even decided to keep him. My hands had known what my head was still arguing about.

"In here? All the way?" His voice wavered on the edge of protest.

"All the way. And Emil — listen. If anyone opens that door and finds this shelf moved, you are no one's child and no one's guest. You are a lost boy who wandered in from the cold, and you don't know your surname. You've never known it. Say it back."

"I don't know my name." He said it flatly, the way children recite things they have decided are simply true now, and something in my chest folded up smaller than he did when he climbed into the gap and drew the shelf shut over himself, leaving a finger's width of air.

Then I stood in a room full of linen and listened to my own heart behave badly.

That was when I heard them — boots in the corridor, several pairs, one pair heavier than the rest, and a voice I knew from casualty reports the way you know a dog that has bitten you: unhurried, pleasant, entirely without warmth. Brandt had arrived forty minutes early. Of course he had. Punctuality is the last virtue of ambitious men.

* * *

What follows I relate as calmly as I can, though I ask you to remember that calm, on that morning, was a garment I wore over a woman shaking so thoroughly inside that I could feel my ribs humming.

They entered through the main doors. Through the linen store's closed door I could hear everything: Anneli's greeting, pitched perfectly between deference and efficiency; the murmur of aides; the squeak of the Colonel's gloves along a bed rail. He began, as he always began, with statistics. Beds occupied, beds vacant, mortality for the month, supplies expended versus supplies requisitioned. He had a way of saying *expended* that made the dead sound like ammunition.

I could not stay in the linen store. A locked linen room during an inspection is an anomaly, and anomalies attract colonels the way open wounds attract flies. So I did the only thing available: I became furniture. I took a stack of folded sheets in my arms, unlocked the door from the inside, and walked out into the corridor directly into the path of the entire party, wearing the expression of a woman mildly inconvenienced by linens.

"Sister — Iversen, isn't it." Brandt's eyes found my name without effort. Men like him memorize the names of subordinates the way generals memorize terrain: not out of respect but to know what they own.

"Colonel." I inclined my head the correct degree. Behind him, two staff officers and — slightly apart, slightly ahead, which I noticed then only dimly — a lieutenant with fair hair and a notebook he wasn't writing in.

"The ward looks orderly," Brandt said. "Orderly is cheap. What does it cost me?"

"It costs us the usual, Colonel. Carbolic, lint, and the sisters' sleep."

Someone laughed — one of the staff officers, briefly, before remembering his rank permitted nothing so human. Brandt smiled the way frost forms. "And your mortality figures hold?"

"They hold where medicine holds, Colonel. Where medicine fails, we write letters." I said it evenly. I had learned long ago that the safest lie told to dangerous men is the truth, provided you choose which truth.

He moved on down the ward. I followed at the distance appropriate to a nurse attending an inspection, and I will tell you the strange thing about terror performed as calm: after some minutes, the performance and the feeling begin to blur, and you cannot quite locate the frightened woman under the composed one. This is either a mercy or a warning. I have never decided.

Brandt stopped at the bed of Corporal Voss, who had a chest wound and the grey complexion of a man auditing his own accounts. "How long has this one?"

"Nine days," I said.

"Will he return to duty?"

"He'll return to his mother's kitchen in Rostock, Colonel, if the trains run."

Brandt wrote something. I understood then, with the cold clarity that visits at the worst moments, that whatever he wrote, Voss had just become a figure in someone's ledger — returned to depot, unfit, or *died of wounds*, whichever suited the arithmetic better. I thought of my own ledger under the floorboard three rooms away, the names inked in my small careful hand, and I wanted to say something unforgivable. Instead I adjusted Voss's blanket, which was the only rebellion immediately available, and Voss — good Voss — winked at me when the Colonel wasn't looking.

The tour proceeded. And then, at the far end of the ward, near the window where the light came down like something rationed, Brandt paused.

"This store room," he said. "It's kept locked?"

My blood performed a maneuver I can only describe as dropping through a trapdoor. "All stores are locked, Colonel. Inventory discipline."

"Open it. I like to see how a hospital keeps its house."

There is a moment — everyone who has lived through anything knows this moment — when time goes thick as glycerine and every possible future arranges itself around you and waits to see which you choose. In one future I fumbled the keys. In another I refused and was arrested by lunchtime. In the third, the only livable one, I said, "Of course, Colonel," with the mild boredom of a woman asked to show her pantry, and walked thirty feet that felt like thirty years, and turned the key.

The shelf stood shut. The oil on its runners gleamed innocently. The room smelled of starch and carbolic and nothing else whatsoever.

Brandt stepped inside, gloved fingers trailing a stack of pillowcases, lifting a corner here, weighing a bolt of bandage there. The staff officer made notes. The fair-haired lieutenant — Kessler, I would learn his name soon enough — remained in the doorway and did not look at the linen at all.

He looked at me.

Not the glance a man gives a nurse. Not the glance given to a woman, either, though there was some of that, and I noted it the way one notes weather. No — this was inventory. He was reading my face the way Brandt read ledgers: for discrepancies. For expenditure that didn't match requisition. I gave him back nothing. I thought about bandage widths. I thought about the price of carbolic. I let my expression settle into the mild, tired competence that was my uniform under the uniform, and after a moment he glanced down at his notebook and wrote nothing in it, which somehow frightened me more than if he had written everything.

"Adequate," Brandt pronounced, and left the room, and I relocked the door behind five living men and one silent child with hands that trembled only once the key had turned, because even fear understands choreography.

* * *

They should have left then. Inspection concluded, statistics gathered, the Colonel's car waiting. But Brandt had a habit — I had heard Anneli complain of it — of doubling back. He liked to arrive unannounced and leave unpredictably, on the theory that institutions show their true faces when they cannot prepare for him. So instead of departing through the main hall, he turned abruptly toward the surgical annex, and the party flowed after him like water finding its level, and I, released from attendance, made for the linen store to check on Emil —

— and heard, behind me, the boots turn too.

"Show me your isolation arrangements," Brandt was saying to Anneli. "The fever cases. Where do you segregate?"

And Anneli's voice, smooth as a well-laundered sheet: "This way, Colonel. Though I warn you, typhoid respects neither rank nor curiosity."

But the route to typhoid lay past the linen store. Of course it did. Everything that morning lay past the linen store; I began to suspect the building had been designed by my enemies.

I reached the door first. There was no time for cleverness and no need for it — cleverness is for problems with margins, and I had perhaps eight seconds. I went in, crossed to the shelf, and said, very softly, "Emil. Stay absolutely still. Whatever happens. Absolutely still." Then I dragged a carton of soiled dressings — bound for boiling, reeking convincingly of the week's honest work — directly in front of the shelf's seam, threw a tarpaulin over it, and unlocked the door just as boots arrived.

"Nurse." Brandt again, framed in the doorway, the staff officers behind him, Kessler at his shoulder. "This room twice in one morning. Either you're very diligent or something in here interests you."

The sentence had teeth in it, casually displayed, the way a man taps a riding crop against his boot. I felt the fear rise in me like floodwater and I let it pass through and keep walking, the way you let a cramp pass through so your hands stay useful.

"Diligence bores me, Colonel, so I'll claim the second. There's a damp problem in the north wall. If the plaster goes, we lose a hundred yards of stored linen and the quartermaster loses his temper, and I know which of those the army can spare."

He considered that. Genuinely considered it — I watched him weigh the answer against his expectations and find it heavier than anticipated, which is the only kind of surprise a suspicious man enjoys. "Show me."

So I showed him. I showed him the faint bloom of damp in the plaster, which existed, because I am nothing if not thorough, and which I had privately blessed every night since discovering it. I showed him the tarpaulined carton, which he did not ask me to move, because soiled dressings announce themselves through every sense a gentleman possesses. Behind me I heard Kessler shift his weight, once, and I did not turn around, because turning around is an admission.

Behind the shelf, in a gap of air one finger wide, a six-year-old boy held his breath because I had told him to, and I thought, with a ferocity that startled me: *he is braver than every man in this room.*

"Have it seen to," Brandt said at last. "Damp rots more than linen." Which was, I suppose, the most truthful thing anyone said all morning.

* * *

They left finally, near noon, the car grinding away over frozen cobbles. I waited a decent interval — ninety seconds, an eternity — then opened the shelf and lifted Emil out. He unfolded stiffly, blinking, and looked up at me with the terrible composure of children who have done as they were told in the dark.

"I didn't cough," he said.

"You didn't cough," I agreed, and my voice betrayed me slightly, and he noticed, because children notice everything except the things adults wish they would.

"There was a man with a voice like a door," he said.

"Yes."

"He said *adequate* about the pillows. Papa said soldiers shouldn't say adequate about things people sleep on."

I crouched down to his level. Somewhere down the corridor Anneli was already moving, restoring the ward to its ordinary lies. "Emil, you did perfectly. Better than perfectly. But I need to tell you something hard, and I need you to hear it." I waited until his eyes settled on mine. "That man may come again. And until we leave this place, whenever I put you in that gap, you go without asking why. Can you do that for me?"

He thought about it with his whole face, the way he did. "Is it because of Papa?" he asked. "Because of what he did? Mama said —"

"Your father did nothing wrong," I said, faster than wisdom would have allowed. It was the first time I'd said it aloud to anyone, including myself, and it hung in the carbolic air between us like something newly born.

Emil nodded slowly, accepting it or shelving it, and went off to fold bandages with the wounded men who had adopted him, and I stood alone in the linen store amid the smell of starch and damp plaster and told myself the morning was over.

Except.

As I relocked the door, I saw Lieutenant Kessler at the end of the corridor. He had come back for his gloves, presumably — he stood holding them. But he was not leaving. He was standing very still, watching me lock a linen room, and when our eyes met he did not look away as manners required. He looked at me the way a man looks at a sum that refuses to come out right — and then he smiled, faintly, courteously, touched two fingers to his cap, and walked away whistling something almost cheerful.

I stood in the empty corridor with the key in my hand and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after the danger has passed, that the inspection had not been the test.

I had been.
