## 9. Instructions to Forget

The note had been living in my apron pocket for three days, folded into a square so small it might have been a prayer. I had taken to touching it the way some men touch a talisman—briefly, guiltily, hoping it would do its work without requiring anything of me.

*Forget this face.*

Three words in a woman's hand. The ink had feathered slightly at the *f*, as though the writer's fingers were cold or shaking or both, and she had pressed hard enough on the downstrokes to leave ridges in the paper you could feel with a thumbnail. I know what that means. I have written enough letters I didn't want traced back to me. You press harder when you're afraid of being illegible. You press as if force could substitute for courage.

For three days I had read it only in snatches—in the linen store while Emil slept under a mound of blankets, between the second and third dressings of the evening, once by accident when I reached for my pencil and found the paper instead. And every time I read it, the same thought presented itself with the tiresome insistence of a debt collector: *you cannot forget a face you have already memorized.*

Because I had memorized it. That was the trouble.

Emil had his father's brow—I was nearly certain of it now, certain in the way one is certain of a diagnosis before the tests confirm it, which is to say: not at all comfortable. Sergeant Anders Voss. Entry forty-one in my ledger. *Reported drowned during retreat across the marshes; body never recovered; struck from rolls 14 March.* The official language was very tidy. Drowned men make no widows' claims and no awkward questions, and dead sergeants cannot testify about whose order sent them into the marshes in the first place.

If Emil was Voss's son, then Emil was evidence. A small, warm, freckle-nosed piece of testimony that walked, ate porridge, and asked entirely too many questions about horses. And evidence, in this city, in this winter of our Lord 1899, has a way of being burned.

So it was not sentiment that made me sit up past midnight in the dispensary with the note flattened under the lamp glass and the hospital's archive box open before me. It was arithmetic. I told myself that firmly. Sentiment gets people shot; arithmetic merely keeps them awake.

* * *

The discharge files lived in a cabinet in the records room that no one visited after the autumn reorganization, when the new adjutant—a young man with the moral flexibility of wet rope—had declared half the old filing "surplus to administrative need." Surplus meant destined for the stove. I had rescued two drawers' worth on the pretext that old discharges were still useful for verifying pension disputes, which was even true. Sister Anneli had watched me carry the boxes past her office and said nothing, which by then I understood to be her native language of consent.

I went down at half past midnight, when the ward was settled and Matron's lamp on the third floor had gone dark. The records room smelled of damp paper and the ghost of pipe smoke, and the candle made the frost on the window look like a map of somewhere kinder.

Here is what nobody tells you about handwriting: it is a face. A person can disguise their words, lie in their content, sign another woman's name—but the hand betrays them the way a gait betrays a man walking away in the dark. The angle of the *t*-bars. Whether the loops close or hang open like mouths. Where the pen hesitates, because hesitation lives in ink as surely as confidence does.

I had spent four years reading officers' hands—their signatures on casualty reports, their initials in margins, the little flourish Colonel Brandt added to his *B* that always reminded me of a man tipping his hat to himself. Reading hands was how I caught the erasures in the first place. A report rewritten in a clerk's neat copy over an original in the colonel's own slant; a death list where the ink of the amendments was fresher than the ink of the record. Paper keeps honest hours even when its authors don't.

So I laid the note beside the discharge papers and compared, page by page, drawer by drawer, and I will confess that my hands were steadier than my heart.

The fourth folder. Discharge authorization, regimental auxiliary corps, dated three years back. A widow's claim form attached, filled out in a hand that pressed hard on its downstrokes and feathered at the cold.

*Katrine Voss,* née Lindqvist. The loops closed. The *K* had that same stubborn forward lean, like a woman walking into wind.

I sat back on the stool and let the candle gutter, and for a moment the whole room tilted the way a room does when you stand up too fast after blood loss.

She hadn't been illiterate and desperate, which was my first assumption and shames me now. She had been *organized*. The claim form was complete, correct, witnessed—filed properly through channels that had no intention of honoring it, because you cannot claim a widow's pension for a man who officially never existed. She had written to the rolls office twice; both letters were paper-clipped to the file, unanswered, marked *no record of service*. Bureaucracy's favorite hymn.

And then, on the last sheet, in the same hand but hurried, the pen digging trenches: a request for certification of service *for purposes of relocation documentation*, addressed to the harbor commission, stamped received—the date was fourteen months ago—and beneath the stamp, in a clerk's bored pencil: *referred to garrison command.*

Referred. Which in this city meant flagged. Which meant someone above the clerk had looked at the name Voss and done the same arithmetic I had done in reverse: a wife asking questions is an annoyance; a wife asking questions near shipping manifests is a problem; and problems travel toward coasts, where ships are.

Except she hadn't gone to the coast. Not yet. Because six-year-old boys slow a flight down terribly, and because a woman who presses that hard on her downstrokes does not abandon her child unless she believes—with better information than I had—that keeping him will kill them both.

*Forget this face.* It wasn't a mother's cruelty. It was a mother's tradecraft. She had left him where he'd be fed, hidden him among strangers whose profession is keeping people alive, and asked the only thing she could ask: that he become invisible. She had tried to erase her son the way the army had erased her husband, and for the same reason—to keep him breathing.

I put my forehead briefly against the cool edge of the cabinet. Weary vigilance, Anneli called my expression. Tonight it was weary something else.

* * *

There was more in the folder, and here the hope arrived, and hope, in my experience, should always be searched for weapons before being admitted.

Folded behind the claim forms was a half-sheet in a different hand—clerical, cramped—and a coastwise permit stub. Someone in the auxiliary corps office had issued Katrine Voss a worker's passage chit for the coastal steamer line, valid for ninety days from issue. The issue date was eleven months past. Expired, then. But the chit named the route: down the sound, stopping at Havnør, Marvik, and ending at Ålborg Point, where the fishing fleets wintered.

A trail. Faint, cold, eleven months stale—but a trail nonetheless, pointing exactly where trails in stories point: toward the sea.

And pointing at a clock, too, because nothing in this city points anywhere else. Jonas had come by two evenings ago with his daughter's check-up behind him and his conscience visibly arguing with his fear, and had said, in the roundabout manner of a man offering to commit treason: "You know they mean to clear the archives at New Year. Everything of the old administration. Bonfire on the customs quay, official decree—*obsolete records of the prior dispensation.*" He had smiled the way one smiles at a euphemism. "Which includes, I happen to know from the survey office copies, the garrison's duplicate discharge registers. The originals are already marked with red tags. Red tag means burn."

Red tag means burn. So the file in front of me—the proof that Katrine Voss existed, that she had a legal claim, that her husband's service was documented before it was disappeared—was already condemned paper, sitting in my rescued drawer like a man in a ward scheduled for transport. If I did nothing, it would burn with everything else, and the last independent record of the Voss family would be whatever I carried in my head and my apron pocket.

But there was a worse clock under the better one, and I found it when I turned to the permit stub again. Because permits of that kind required garrison countersignature, and in the corner, small and bored and damning, was an aide's initial: *R.K.*

Robert Kessler. Brandt's own shadow.

He had signed her permit. Eleven months ago, before I knew his face, before he began watching me across ward floors with those patient gray eyes, Lieutenant Kessler had processed the paperwork of the woman whose husband his colonel had erased. Perhaps it meant nothing—clerks sign thousands of papers. Or perhaps it meant that somewhere in Brandt's headquarters there was a file with a red tag on it that said *Voss*, and Kessler had read it, and remembered, the way I remembered. Men like Kessler collect leverage the way other men collect debts. A surviving witness to Brandt's marshes, and a child who could prove the witness had lived—that was not nothing. That was possibly everything.

Which meant the boy asleep upstairs under the blankets was not only evidence against Brandt. He was bait that might already have a hook in him.

* * *

I climbed back up through the hospital's bones carrying the folder inside my shirt, flat against my ribs like a second ledger, thinking—as I seem to think constantly now—*carry nothing you cannot run with.* The folder weighed perhaps four ounces. It weighed considerably more than that.

At the linen store door I paused, listened past my own heartbeat, and heard the small honest noise of a boy breathing in his sleep. I went in.

Emil had migrated in the night until only his boots remained under the blanket, and his hair stood up off his forehead like frost. He had tucked the carved horse—one of the orderlies had whittled it for him, badly, with great love—into the crook of his arm, and he was talking to it in his sleep, giving orders, I think. Children dream in commands. Nobody has taught them yet that orders can be wrong.

I crouched down and looked at the face I was instructed to forget.

"Katrine," I said softly, trying the name out loud for the first time. It fit the shape of the handwriting somehow. A woman leaning into wind.

What frightened me—not the guards, not the burning, not even Kessler—was the arithmetic of the deadline. The bonfire was set for New Year's Eve, seventeen days out. The coastal steamers ran on schedules Jonas could draw me blindfolded. But a woman traveling alone, moving under expired papers, avoiding garrison eyes, does not wait at Ålborg Point like a character in a novel. She may have passed through months ago. She may be in Marvik, in Havnør, in a grave, in a new life with a new name pressing lightly on her pen. Eleven months is a long time for a trail to stay warm in a Baltic winter.

And if I wanted to find her—if I wanted to deliver a boy to his mother and a truth to the world before the world held its bonfire—then I would not be hiding a child anymore. I would be moving one. Across checkpoints. With papers neither of us properly possessed. Past a lieutenant who had perhaps signed this very trail into existence and was waiting to see who followed it.

Hope with a deadline attached. That is the most dangerous kind. Hope without a deadline you can indulge forever, the way you indulge a chronic cough. Hope with a date on it makes you stand up, pack a bag, and do something stupid.

Emil stirred and opened his eyes halfway, unhurried, unafraid—he had decided sometime in the past week that I was a fixed feature of the universe, like walls and porridge.

"You're sitting in the dark," he observed.

"I'm reading," I said.

"There's nothing to read."

"There's always something to read." I tucked the blanket up over his shoulder. "Go back to sleep, Voss."

He went very still. Then, in the smallest voice: "That's Papa's name. I'm not supposed to say it."

"I know," I said. "You're allowed to say it to me. I'm keeping all the names."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, decided in my favor, and rolled over. Within a minute he was asleep again, ordering his wooden horse forward into whatever battle children fight on behalf of sleeping adults.

I stayed crouched in the dark a moment longer, with the condemned file against my ribs and the note in my pocket, and made the only resolution available to a woman in my position: before this century dies, Katrine Voss's name goes back on a page that cannot burn—or I go down trying, and someone else writes mine underneath it.

In the morning, I would need to see Jonas about the steamer schedules.

It was while I was blowing out the candle that I heard, faintly through the floorboards overhead, the front bell ring once—curfew hours, no visitors—and then the muffled voice of an orderly admitting someone important, someone whose boots I already knew the sound of, coming to the hospital at one o'clock in the morning for reasons no honest man requires.
