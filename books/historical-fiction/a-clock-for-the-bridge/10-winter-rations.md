## 10. Winter Rations

The supply wagon was due on the ninth of December. It arrived on the twenty-third, or rather half of it arrived—the other half having been requisitioned somewhere between the depot and us by an administrative miracle that no one could explain and everyone understood.

Sister Anneli read the manifest twice, folded it into a square small enough to crush, and crushed it.

"Potatoes, forty sacks," she said. "We received eleven. Flour, twenty. We received four. Salt—" She stopped, because there was no comic conclusion to that line. There was simply no salt.

"So we eat blandly and die healthy," said Corporal Voss from his cot by the window, where he had spent six weeks being cheerfully indignant about a leg that would not decide whether to heal. "There are worse fates. I've seen most of them."

He had, too. The ward hummed its low agreement—the sound of thirty men deciding collectively not to complain before they'd even heard breakfast's verdict.

I did the arithmetic at the washstand, because arithmetic is a nurse's second prayer. Eleven sacks of potatoes, four of flour, a ward of thirty-one patients, nine staff, and one boy who officially did not exist and therefore consumed nothing on paper, which made him the only person in the building whose appetite cost the army anything less than a scandal.

It came to roughly three weeks. The war, in my experience, rarely cooperated with three-week horizons.

* * *

Rationing began the next morning with a speech so short it could have been carved on a coin. Anneli stood at the head of the ward and told the men the truth: portions would shrink, kitchens would close early, and anyone caught hoarding or trading would answer to her personally, which was a worse prospect than answering to any tribunal in the province. Then she sat down at the little table and ate the same thin barley porridge as everyone else, from the same dented bowl, and that was the whole of the law.

What surprised me—though I suppose by then it shouldn't have—was what happened afterward.

Voss went first, as he went first at everything. He called Emil over to his cot with the gravity of a general summoning a courier and presented him with half of his evening bread, already buttered, which meant Voss had somehow bribed or charmed extra butter out of the kitchen, which meant Voss was probably running a small economy under our noses that Anneli had chosen not to notice because it kept morale alive.

"You're too thin," Voss announced. "You're embarrassing the ward. A boy your age should look like a turnip—round and smug."

Emil took the bread with both hands, the way he took everything, as though it might be withdrawn at any moment for reasons he hadn't been told about yet. Six years old and he already knew that food given freely is food you examine first. I turned back to my bandage cart and let him have the moment without my eyes on it. Some kindnesses can't survive being watched.

By the end of the week the whole ward had organized itself around the boy without a single word being spoken about it. Halvorsen, who had a shoulder wound and a face like a closed ledger, taught him a card game played with a deck missing the sevens. The man in bed twelve—a farmer named Kessel whose lung had taken shrapnel at the river crossing—saved the soft centers of his bread because Emil's teeth were loose. Even old Lindqvist, dying by inches of something the surgeons argued about, would wake from his dozing to make sure Emil had moved away from the window drafts.

They never said *he reminds us of someone*. They never said anything at all. But I have watched enough men to know when a ward has adopted a child: it happens the way a body adopts warmth, invisibly and completely, and it happened here over the course of about four days.

Anneli noticed it before I did. She always noticed things before I did; she just preferred to pretend otherwise.

"The boy needs work," she said, cornering me at the medicine cabinet. "Not chores—work. Something that's his. A child with nothing but time watches the rest of us carry things, and it eats at him."

"He's six."

"He's six with a soldier's eyes. Give him a job, Freya, before he decides he's a burden. Children believe what we let them believe about themselves. You know this."

I knew it. I had been six once too, in a house where believing yourself useful was the difference between being fed and being forgotten.

So I sat him down on the linen store floor, among the bales, with a stack of clean bandages in front of him, and I showed him the fold. Lengthwise first, then thirds, then tuck. The hospital fold. Every nurse in the province could do it blind, and every nurse in the province knew exactly which officer would send a bandage back for being folded wrong, which gave the whole business a certain solemnity.

"Lengthwise," I said.

"Lengthwise." His hands were small and red from cold and absolutely serious.

"Thirds."

"Thirds." The fold wandered slightly toward the diagonal. He looked up at me in alarm.

"It counts," I said. "Bandages don't have to be beautiful. They have to be ready. That's better than beautiful."

He thought about that with his whole face, the way children do, and then bent over the next one.

By the third day he could do forty without dropping a single crease. By the fifth day he had opinions about other people's folds, which he expressed only to me, in a whisper, with the discretion of a man reporting irregularities up the chain of command. Voss's bandages were "crooked like sleeping dogs." Halvorsen's were acceptable. Mine, after close inspection, earned a single grave nod that I confess I treasured more than several compliments I have received in print.

He began carrying them. That was the transformation Anneli had predicted. He would load his small arms with rolled bandages and walk the ward like a quartermaster, distributing them to men who received them with the solemnity of medals. *For the shoulder, Herr Kessel. For the leg, Corporal Voss.* Nobody laughed. I want that recorded somewhere: thirty grown men, wounded and hungry and frightened in ways they didn't discuss, never once laughed at him. They straightened when he approached. They thanked him by name.

A boy who had been carried out of a burning house in the dark, told to forget his own face, now had a job, a title, and thirty men who needed him. If you had told me in October that the most functional family in this city would assemble itself out of barley porridge and correctly folded linen, I would have written it down as too sentimental for fiction. Life, as usual, ignoring the standards of the trade.

* * *

The cold came hard that second week of December. The stove in the east bay drew badly and the windows wore a skin of frost on the inside, so that the ward existed in a kind of white murk, lamps glowing through ice like candles behind wax. We hung blankets across the worst panes. The men breathed steam. Somewhere below, the coal cellar held perhaps ten days' worth, and above us the war continued to consume everything within reach except, for the moment, us.

Hunger changed the acoustics of the ward. Quieter men got quieter. Louder men got louder. Voss told stories—long, ridiculous ones about his aunt's dog and the customs officer and a wheel of cheese whose details grew more elaborate each telling—and the ward let him, because a story fills a stomach partway.

On Thursday, Kessel's wife managed to get a message through with a fishmonger's cart: she was alive, the farm was standing, the cow had calved. He read it four times and then put it inside his shirt, over his heart, and lay facing the wall for an hour, and when he turned back his eyes were wet and he was smiling and nobody looked at him directly, which is how men grant each other privacy in rooms that offer none.

That night he called Emil over and cut an apple into seven pieces—one for himself and six for the boy, which was most of an apple, which was wealth beyond counting.

"From my orchard," Kessel said. "The apples keep till spring. This one traveled better than I did."

Emil ate it slowly, holding two pieces in reserve in his coat pocket, and I watched him learn abundance and thrift in the same hour.

I contributed what I had. My mother's ring stayed where it always stayed, but my pantry at the nurses' quarters yielded a jar of honey bought in August from a woman at the market who had claimed—falsely, as it turned out—that her bees had a view of the sea. I diluted it with hot water and carried the pot through the ward at evening, a spoonful each, and the men treated it like communion. Lindqvist, who could no longer manage solid food, took his slowly and said it tasted like his mother's kitchen in Riga, and whether that was true or merely what honey does to memory, it counted as medicine, and I entered it in the book as such.

Anneli found me later, washing the pot.

"Where's yours?"

"I had some while I poured."

"Liar."

"Thick liar, then. It's very good honey."

She took the ladle from my hand, filled it, and held it out with an expression that permitted no negotiation, and I drank my spoonful, and it did taste faintly, impossibly, of the sea.

* * *

There was one bad evening, near the solstice, when the arithmetic nearly broke.

The kitchen reported the flour gone faster than projected—mice, or theft, or the ordinary entropy of armies—and Anneli stood at the cupboard doing sums aloud with her jaw set, and the numbers came out wrong in the direction that numbers had come out wrong all year. Two weeks became ten days. Ten days became, if the wagon was late again, something we didn't say out loud.

The ward felt it the way animals feel weather. Conversations thinned. Voss's story ran dry mid-sentence for the first time in living memory.

It was Emil—I still don't know fully how—who turned it.

He appeared at the head of the ward during supper with his bandage cart and announced, in the clear carrying voice of officialdom, that bandage supplies were adequate but folding standards had slipped, and inspections would resume in the morning, and there would be consequences.

Silence. And then Voss, bless him forever, sat bolt upright and demanded to know who had reported him, and Halvorsen said it was obviously the man in bed twelve, and Kessel denied it with his mouth full, and within four minutes the entire ward was arguing about imaginary bandage infractions with the passionate grievance of men who had been handed, by a six-year-old quartermaster, permission to feel normal for one more evening.

Anneli caught my eye across the room. She didn't smile. Anneli considered smiling a waste of professional capital. But she nodded once, slowly, the nod she reserved for treatments that worked against expectation, and went back to her sums.

Ten days. We would make it ten days. After that—

After that was a problem wearing different clothes every day, and I had stopped pretending I didn't know which costume it would wear next. Because the flour wasn't the only thing running out. Patience at headquarters was. Questions about the boy had been asked once already, softly, by Lieutenant Kessler, in that pleasant voice of his that filed everything it heard. The sergeant's name had surfaced once in a dying man's mouth. Things that surface once tend to surface again.

But that was tomorrow's ration. Tonight there was honey water and a card game missing its sevens, and Emil asleep on a pallet between Voss's cot and the stove with a folded bandage tucked under his cheek like a talisman, and the frost on the windows glittering like something celebratory rather than lethal, and thirty men breathing slow in the dark, full-ish, warm-ish, safe-ish.

Fragile. All of it fragile as spun sugar, one hot hand away from ruin. I have since learned that this is the only kind of domestic peace there is; we had simply reached it earlier than most.

I sat by the stove past midnight with the ward ledger open on my knee, entering quantities, temperatures, pulses. Under *consumables: bandages*, I wrote *adequate — inspection passed,* and beside it, in the margin, because no one audits margins: *folded by E.*

Then I blew out the lamp and listened to the ward breathe.

In the morning, Jonas the mapmaker would come asking for me, hat in his hands, debt in his eyes, and everything would begin to move. But the morning was hours away yet, and the stove ticked, and the boy slept, and for one whole night the arithmetic held.

* * *

Jonas arrived while the frost was still on the glass, stamping snow off his boots in the doorway with the manner of a man who has rehearsed being casual and knows he's failing.

"Fräulein Iversen. You look well."

"I look tired, Jonas. Flattery from a man who draws coastlines for a living is wasted on me."

"Yes." He glanced down the ward, at the men, at the small figure folding bandages with monastic concentration, and his voice dropped. "It's about the bridge. The checkpoints. What I owe you." He swallowed. "I've drawn something. And Freya—you need to see it before the new year. Before the fires."

He reached into his coat, and my chest went tight, because I had been waiting for that word without letting myself hear it.
