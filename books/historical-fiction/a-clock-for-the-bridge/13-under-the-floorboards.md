## 13. Under the Floorboards

There is a particular sound a hospital makes in the hour before an inspection, and I had learned it the way one learns a storm by the ache in old scars: not with the ears first, but with the spine.

It was the boots. Boots in a hospital have a grammar. The shuffling slippers of the night orderly meant nothing. The quick, light tread of Sister Anneli on her rounds meant order and small mercies. But heavy heels, arriving in threes—three men, or two men and an officer, walking in step because they had been taught that unison was authority—that grammar spelled only one thing: *someone is about to ask you questions you must pretend never to have heard.*

The boots came at half past four in the morning, which was either very rude or very clever of them, depending on how charitable one felt toward Colonel Brandt at any given moment. I was not feeling charitable. I was feeling the cold sweat of a woman who has been awake since midnight re-reading a dying soldier's murmured name in her own handwriting and wondering exactly how many minutes she had left.

The answer, it turned out, was forty.

I had spent those forty minutes doing arithmetic that would have horrified my mother, who believed arithmetic should concern itself with flour and thread counts and never with things like *how long does it take to lift a floorboard* and *how fast can a six-year-old be woken, silenced, and hidden without crying.* Emil, to his enormous credit—and I do not say this lightly, because children that age are generally constructed entirely of noise and sudden movement—did neither. When I shook him gently awake in the linen store and told him we were going to play a game called Quiet as Stones, he looked at me with those grave dark eyes, nodded once, and held up his arms to be carried.

That nearly undid me. It is always the trust that does it, never the danger. Danger merely sharpens; trust makes soft places in you at the worst possible moments.

* * *

The coal cellar had been my second thought; the floorboard under the third bed in the east ward my first; and the linen store—where Emil actually slept—my last resort, which is to say the place I used anyway, because desperation is an excellent arguer and always wins against prudence.

The ledger could not stay where it lived. That was the first certainty. Petersen the orderly had sold more than gossip—I was sure of it now, sure the way one is sure of weather coming—he had sold *routines*, and routines point directly at floors. Any search worth its officer would start at the boards beneath my cot.

So: the coal cellar. Not because coal cellars are clever hiding places—they are terrible ones, damp and drafty and full of the exact sort of loose surface that records footprints—but because of what Brandt himself had ordered done with the century's records. Burning. Ash. Dust to dust, paper to paper. If a thing was going to be erased from the world, I reasoned, it should learn the trick from the masters. I would bury the ledger in coal dust and let it practice being nothing until spring.

I carried it down wrapped in an old pillowcase, past the boiler that breathed like a consumptive giant, and knelt in the black drift at the corner farthest from the stairs. My hands knew the book better than my eyes did—the leather gone soft as a dog's ear, the corners rounded from years of my thumb, the pages inside holding sixty-one names, sixty-one men whom the army had decided, with the tidy finality of a clerk crossing out a line, had never existed.

Sergeant Anders Lindqvist. Third name on the ninth page. Emil's father. The reason all of this had stopped being principle and become something with teeth.

I pressed the ledger into the coal dust face-down, dragged the black grit over it until no edge showed, then hauled a broken crate atop the spot and sat on that too, for one full minute, breathing, listening to the boiler, feeling like a gravedigger who has buried the wrong body—or perhaps exactly the right one.

Then I went back up, washed my hands twice, black still ghosting from under the nails, and prepared to commit treason.

* * *

They came into the east ward at five o'clock precisely, which told me they had scheduled it like a dinner party. Colonel Brandt did not lead them—he never led anything so vulgar as a search; he arrived afterward, the way rot arrives after the wound—and so it was Lieutenant Kessler who stood in the doorway with a folded paper and two military policemen behind him looking like furniture that had learned to walk.

Kessler's eyes found me before the paper found his voice. That was the detail I filed away and did not enjoy filing: he looked at me *first*, at my hands, at my face, with the unhurried attention of a man checking whether a trap he had set had caught anything yet. Petersen's notebook had made its journey. I understood this only dimly then—a coldness in his courtesy, a patience—but I understood it.

"Sister Iversen." He did not use my title often enough for it to feel respectful; he used it rarely enough that it felt like handling evidence. "Routine inspection of staff quarters and storage. You'll forgive the hour."

"Forgive it?" I said, and gave him the smile I kept for difficult patients and worse officers. "Lieutenant, fever does not keep office hours. Neither should you."

One of the policemen almost smiled. Kessler did not. He gestured, and the search began.

I want to describe it honestly, because there is a temptation, in memory, to make such scenes operatic—shouted orders, overturned beds, drama. There was none of that. They were thorough the way frost is thorough. Mattresses prodded with bayonet points through the ticking. Bandage cupboards emptied shelf by shelf, the contents re-stacked by hands that had never stacked a bandage in their lives and left the ward worse than fever ever had. My own cot lifted, turned, set down. The floorboards sounded with a knuckle-rap, one by one, along every wall of my sleeping cubicle, and I stood folding a sheet that did not need folding and watched them do it and thought, with a clarity that frightened me more than the searching did: *I am watching them look for the book and I am not afraid enough.*

Somewhere in that minute, the line was crossed. I could not see it and cannot draw it now. But I knew I had crossed it when Kessler rapped the board beneath my cot and I felt nothing in my chest at all except a kind of professional interest in his technique.

"Your floor is honest," Kessler said.

"It has little choice," I said. "I mop it daily."

* * *

Emil was under the bench in the linen store.

Not hidden—there is no true hiding place in a linen store, I had known that when I put him there—but *concealed*, which is a different discipline. I had built it myself in the previous forty minutes: a low shelf stacked with blanket bolts pulled slightly forward, and behind them the gap between shelf and wall where a small boy could sit with his knees to his chest, and in front of the whole arrangement, the crowning argument, the piece of theater I was most proud of and most ashamed of: a laundry basket of soiled dressings, lid off, positioned so that anyone opening that alcove would have to reach past bloodied linen to find anything worth finding.

Men will search a room for papers. Men will not, if they can help it, put their bare hands into a basket of typhus dressings. I had counted on that, and counting on another man's squeamishness felt, at that moment, like the most cynical act of my life, which considering some of my recent acts was saying something.

And I sat on the bench above him.

That was the plan—not a plan, plans are written down and have contingencies; it was a desperate improvisation wearing a plan's coat—but it was what I had. If they opened the alcove, they would find a nurse resting, and behind the nurse, presumably, blankets. Children are small. Blankets are thick. And a nurse sitting down in her own linen store during a search is performing innocence with her whole body, which is worth more than any lock.

Emil's hand found mine in the dark under the bench-edge and gripped it. Small fingers, cold, absolutely still. I have held the hands of dying men who moved less. I squeezed once—*Quiet as Stones*—and felt him go stone.

Then the door opened and Colonel Brandt walked in.

* * *

He did this sometimes, I had learned. Appeared where the real work was happening, unannounced, because Brandt's genius—if one grants the word—was for being present at other people's risk and absent from his own. He filled the doorway of the linen store the way he filled casualty reports: completely, and with a great deal removed.

"Sister Iversen." His voice was mild. Mildness, from Brandt, was the dangerous register. "You're up early."

"The ward doesn't nurse itself, Colonel."

"No." He stepped inside. The room was narrow; Kessler waited at the door behind him like punctuation. Brandt looked at the shelves, the blankets, the basket of dressings with its honest stench, and then down at me, and smiled the way winter smiles. "Diligence. I've always admired it in your profession. So much depends on what you choose to notice. A temperature. A change of color. Small things." He picked up a folded towel, weighed it in his hand, set it down. "A name spoken by a dying man, for instance. Small thing. Easily missed."

My heart did something athletic. My face did nothing at all. This, I discovered, is what training is actually for—not the bandaging, not the lectures on antisepsis, but this: the ability to stand in front of a man who could end you and arrange your features into polite boredom.

"I hear many names," I said. "Most of them mumbled. Dying men talk to their mothers mostly, in my experience. And to God, but He has better hearing than I do."

Something flickered at the door—Kessler, watching, and I had the sudden vertiginous sense that I was not performing for Brandt at all, that the true audience stood behind him taking notes. I filed that away too. My file was getting full.

"A sergeant's name," Brandt said softly. "Lindqvist."

The hand in mine went rigid. Six years old, and he knew his father's name even forbidden it; the small fingers clenched around mine hard enough to hurt, and I thank God for the pain, because it anchored me to the moment instead of letting me float away into panic where mistakes live.

"Lindqvist," I repeated, and let my brow fold, and performed the most important lie of my life with the flat competence of a woman reciting a stock list. "The one they shot for the retreat at Kalnen? Before my time here. The men still mention him now and then—it was a popular opinion he was made to carry the blame for someone else's map. Soldiers love a grievance, Colonel. It seasons the rations."

Silence. The boiler's breath came up faintly through the floor. Emil did not breathe; I did not breathe; the dressing basket did its malodorous duty.

"You think he was blamed unfairly," Brandt said. Not a question. A probe, slid in gently, seeking the depth of the wound.

"I think," I said, "that sergeants make convenient arithmetic. But then I'm only a nurse. Arithmetic above my station."

He studied me for a long moment. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: I could not tell, even then, whether he believed me. He was too good. He let the silence stretch until it creaked, and I let it stretch, and the ward clock somewhere below ticked us both through it, and finally Brandt inclined his head a fraction—the smallest possible unit of concession—and said:

"Staff quarters are being searched tonight. Yours included, thoroughly, while you're on shift. Anything improper found will be treated with the severity the times demand." A pause. "I would hate for diligence to be misapplied, Sister. You're too useful."

"I've always hoped so," I said.

He turned and was gone, Kessler after him, and the door closed, and I sat on that bench with a child's hand crushed in mine and did not move for eleven minutes by the ward clock, because I had learned in the war that the brave thing and the foolish thing are distinguished only by whether you survive the next quarter hour.

When I finally lifted the blankets, Emil looked up at me and whispered: "Was I quiet?"

"You were stones," I said, and gathered him up, and my hands shook only after I was certain he could feel it.

* * *

They searched my quarters that evening while I changed dressings in the east ward with perfect, murderous composure. They took nothing, found nothing, and left my Bible—genuinely mine, genuinely read—sitting square on the pillow like an acquittal. Kessler supervised personally. Through the doorway I watched him run his palm along the skirting board of my cubicle and knock twice on the wood, and I thought: *he knows the routine too. He's done this before, or he intends to do it again.*

Which meant the search had not been a search at all. It had been an announcement: *we know you have something. Show us where you keep it.*

Well. They had shown me theirs. Coal dust and a laundry basket were now the least of my assets; my true asset, newly minted that morning, was this: Colonel Brandt had looked me in the eye and asked me about Lindqvist, and I had answered, and there was no longer any version of events in which I was an innocent woman who might yet walk away. The lie had done what lies do. It had signed me.

I finished the dressing, checked a pulse, noted a temperature—all the small diligent things Brandt admired—and when Anneli passed me in the corridor and touched my sleeve, a touch light as ash, I realized she had seen all of it. Every minute. She had stood at the medicine cabinet with her back to the search, sorting vials that needed no sorting, present and silent the whole day long.

"He asked you about Lindqvist," she said quietly. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And you lied to his face."

"Yes."

Anneli looked at me for a long time, and there was something moving behind her face—something old and salted with grief, something I did not yet understand and would, soon, when she chose to give it to me.

"Then you'll need to sleep sometime," she said at last, "and eat, and watch the boy, and get that book across the river. You can't do any of it alone." She turned back to her cabinet. "Come to my office after lights-out, Freya. There's something you should know about me before you decide whether to trust me. You deserve to know it *before*, not after."

She said it like a woman laying a debt on the table, face-up.

And I stood in that corridor, coal dust under my nails, a child asleep on stolen blankets three doors away, a forged innocence cooling behind me like a fired bullet, and understood that whatever Anneli was about to confess might be the knife in my back or the arm at my elbow—and that I no longer had the luxury of choosing ignorance.

I went to her office.
