## 16. Emil Learns His Father's Name

The nurse with the grey eyes has a rule that nobody is allowed to die before breakfast, and mostly the ward obeys her, which I think means she is more frightening than God.

I know this because I have been living in the linen store for eleven days, or maybe twelve, and I have counted everything there is to count twice. There are forty-one folded sheets on the second shelf if you don't count the one with the brown stain, which Sister Anneli says doesn't count either. There are six candle stubs in the tin. There are nine men in the ward who let me fold bandages now, and one of them — Corporal Voss, who is missing an arm and says he traded it for a bad card hand, which is a lie because I have seen his file, I read upside down very well — taught me a song about a miller's daughter that has a rude word in it, and when I sang it back Sister Anneli dropped a bedpan.

But this chapter of my life, as Freya would say, though she would never say *chapter*, she would say *this business* — this business begins with my name. Or rather, with the name I am not allowed to say.

I am not allowed to say my surname. The woman who left me at the door told me so, pressing her cold thumb hard into my cheekbone like she was stamping wax. *Not your name. Not Papa's name. Forget this face.* She wrote it on the note too, forget this face, which was strange, because how do you forget a face by writing it down? That is the opposite of forgetting. Even at six years old I understood that much about paper. Paper remembers. Paper is the most stubborn thing in the world. My papa said so once, when he was filling out forms at our kitchen table with his tongue between his teeth, saying, *Emil, mind what you put on paper, because paper outlives men.*

He was right about that. He was wrong about almost nothing else, except maybe that turnips could be made tasty with enough salt, which they cannot, I have tested it here in the hospital where we eat them every single day.

* * *

It happens on a Thursday, because afterwards I ask Corporal Voss what day it is and he says Thursday, why, and I say no reason, and he looks at me the way grown-ups look at you when they already know the reason but are being polite, which is its own kind of kindness.

Freya comes to the linen store after the evening medicines. I know the sound of her steps now, better than my own heartbeat probably. She walks like someone counting her money inside her shoes — quick, quiet, careful. She brings the lamp turned low and a piece of bread with real butter on it, which is contraband, and we split it sitting on the floor among the sheets, and for a while neither of us talks, because that is also something we do now, sit and not talk, and it is not lonely the way not-talking used to be at the farmhouse.

Then she says, "Emil. I want to tell you about your father."

My stomach does the thing it does, like a bird got in there.

"You don't have to," I say quickly, because part of me wants it more than butter, more than anything, and another part knows that wanting things is how you get hurt. The woman with the cold thumb taught me that too.

"I know I don't have to." She turns the lamp up a little, just a little, so the light makes a small yellow room inside the dark room. "I'm going to anyway. Because you're old enough, and because it isn't fair, what's been done to him, and because—" she stops, and starts again, which she almost never does, "—because names shouldn't be buried with the people who owned them. Not good ones."

"Was he good?" I ask. It comes out smaller than I meant it to. "Papa?"

"He was a sergeant," Freya says. "Do you know what a sergeant is?"

"A man who shouts at lieutenants," I say, because Papa shouted at everyone below him and saluted everyone above him and I once asked him who shouted at him and he said, *the war, son, the war shouts at all of us.* I didn't understand it then. I understand it better now, from the linen store, listening to the ward cough all night.

Freya almost smiles. It's a real one, small and crooked. "That's approximately correct. Your father was a sergeant in the Third Regiment. And he was — Emil, listen to me, because this is important and I will only ever tell it to you true — he was a good soldier. Not the kind they make medals for. The other kind. The kind who carried a private with frostbitten feet four kilometres on his own back. The kind who gave his ration to a boy of nineteen who was crying for his mother, and told him it was extra, that the kitchen had sent extra, so the boy wouldn't feel beholden. The kind the other soldiers followed not because they had to but because wherever he stood felt safer than anywhere else."

I don't say anything. My throat has closed like a drawbridge.

"I know because I've talked to men who served under him," she goes on, quieter. "Corporal Voss. The one with the arm he lost at cards."

"He didn't lose it at cards," I say.

"No," Freya agrees. "He lost it the same day your father was killed. And that day, Emil, was not your father's fault. Something went wrong that went wrong much higher up. Much higher up than any sergeant. But the report that was written afterward needed somewhere to put the blame, and reports cannot blame colonels, only sergeants. So the report blamed your father. And then the report did something worse. It erased him. As if he had never stood anywhere at all. As if no one had ever felt safer because of him."

"That's lying," I say. "On official paper."

"Yes," Freya says. "It is exactly that. Lying on official paper. And it works, most of the time, because most people believe paper over people. But I keep a record of my own. Of every man they've struck off. Your father's name is in it. Written properly. Spelled correctly. With the truth next to it."

I look at my hands. They are small hands, bandage-smelling hands. "What's the truth next to it?"

"That he died holding a position he'd been ordered to hold, so that others could get out. That he saved eleven men doing it. That the order itself was the mistake, and the mistake belonged to a man whose name I will tell you when you're older, or when you're angry enough to need it, whichever comes first."

I think about being angry enough. I am already quite angry, in the way I imagine a stove is angry, all the way through, quietly, with nowhere for the heat to go.

"What is his name?" I ask. "His whole name? All of it?"

And Freya tells me.

I can't write it here the way she said it, because she said it slow, syllable by syllable, the way you teach a bell to ring, and writing it fast would be disrespectful. But I will tell you what I did with it.

* * *

She gives me paper. Real paper, not the backs of medicine lists, though I have practiced on those too, secretly, writing letters that aren't words yet, just shapes waiting to be promoted. She gives me a pencil stub worn down to the size of an acorn and says, "Start with just the first name. Get it steady. Then add the rest."

The first name is easy. Four letters. I have known how to make those letters since before the fire, since Mama used to hold my finger inside hers and move it across the flour dusted on the table. *E.* Then *m*. Then *i*, which is just a stick standing up straight and pretending to be brave. Then *l*, which is the same stick again, and I remember thinking even at five that it was funny, two sticks in a row, like the name ran out of ideas halfway through.

Then the surname. Three parts to it, like a wagon with three wheels and somehow still rolling. The middle part has a letter in it that looks like a snake that changed its mind. I make the snake wrong three times. On the fourth try I press so hard the pencil snaps its point, and I sit there holding the broken stub and hating myself in a hot, sudden way, and Freya — who has been folding sheets ten feet away, pretending not to watch, which is a skill nurses have — comes over without a word and sharpens the stub with a little knife she keeps in her apron, and puts it back in my hand, and goes away again.

The fifth time, the snake comes out right.

After that something happens that I don't have a word for. It's like the name stops being a row of letters and becomes a door, and behind the door is our kitchen, and Papa is filling out forms with his tongue between his teeth, and Mama is burning the onions on purpose so she has an excuse to send me to the neighbour's, and the dog — I haven't thought about the dog, I haven't let myself — the dog is under the table stepping on my feet with his heavy polite paws. All of it is behind the door and the door is the name and the name is mine to open whenever I want, forever, and nobody can burn it, because I have written it, and paper outlives men.

So I write it again. And again. Down the page, row after row, until the rows stop looking like practice and start looking like a regiment. A whole regiment of Papas standing at attention in their ink, shoulder to shoulder, none of them erased.

"You'll fill the page," Freya says eventually.

"I'm going to fill two pages," I inform her.

"Two pages," she repeats gravely, as though I've announced a military campaign, and perhaps I have.

By the time the lamp oil runs low, the page is full edge to edge, and I start the second one, and my hand aches in a good way, the way legs ache after running somewhere that mattered. Corporal Voss leans over from his cot — he can see into the linen store doorway from where he lies, which is not an accident, I think, I think they arranged it — and squints at the page.

"What's all that, then?" he asks.

"Names," I say.

"Whose?"

"My father's," I say. Out loud. In the ward. Where anyone could hear.

There is a silence with a lot of weight in it. Voss looks at Freya. Freya looks at Voss. Some message travels between them over my head, silent as smoke.

"Good name?" Voss asks me.

"The best," I say.

"Well then." He settles back against his pillow with his one arm. "Fill both pages, lad. Fill three. Men like that, you can't write them down too many times."

* * *

Later — much later, past the hour when even the lamps are supposed to be sleeping — Freya sits beside me on the floor again and doesn't talk for a long time. When she finally does, her voice has a different texture, like cloth turned to its reverse side.

"When I was recording your father's name in my ledger," she says, "I wrote the date he died. Do you want to know it?"

I nod against her sleeve.

"It was the ninth of March. Six years ago this spring." She pauses. "You were born in April."

I do the arithmetic, which is my best subject after reading upside down. "So I was almost one."

"You were almost one," she says. "Which means he held that position knowing you were at home. Knowing there was a version of the world with you in it. Men fight differently when they know that. Better, usually. Your father fought the best way there is."

I think about that for a while. The lamp gutters. Somewhere out in the city a patrol passes, boots on frozen cobbles, and we both go still the way we always do, two animals in a burrow, until the sound fades.

"Freya," I say.

"Mm."

"If paper outlives men. Then the bad paper outlives them too. The report that lied."

"Yes," she says slowly. "It does."

"And it's going to be burned. After New Year's. Jonas said. All the old records, everything, burned in the square."

"That's the plan, yes."

"So if the bad paper burns," I say, working it through, feeling the idea come together in me like ice forming, solid and sharp, "then the lie dies. But only if somebody's already copied the truth out first. Otherwise it all dies together and nobody ever knows which was which."

Freya is quiet for so long I think maybe she's fallen asleep sitting up, which she does sometimes, and I have started keeping watch over her the way she keeps watch over me, which seems fair.

"You," she says at last, and her voice sounds strange, thick, "are six years old."

"Almost seven," I say. "In April. Like Papa's battle, nearly."

"Almost seven," she agrees, "and you've just explained the entire reason I'm risking my life."

"Somebody has to," I say, shrugging, because it seems obvious. "Paper doesn't copy itself."

She laughs then — really laughs, a short startled sound she claps her hand over, because of the ward, because of the patrols, because laughing is also contraband here — and pulls me against her side, and I let her, because some things you're allowed to want.

Before she leaves, she takes the finished page — the full one, the regiment of Papas — and looks at it a long moment in the last of the light.

"May I keep this?" she asks. "With the ledger. So he's in it twice. Once in my handwriting, once in yours."

"Yes," I say. "But I want it back after. When it's safe."

"When it's safe," she promises, and folds the page into the ledger, into the place where the true names live, and I watch my father slide in among all the other fathers and sons and brothers that the reports pretended never existed, and I think: *now there are two of us remembering you, Papa. Soon there will be hundreds. I'm going to write you so many times they'll run out of fires.*

I fall asleep smiling, which I hadn't done since the farmhouse.

I wake to shouting.

Not ward-shouting, not the ordinary night noises of men dreaming their battles over again. This is downstairs-shouting. Boots on stairs. A voice I have learned to fear without ever having heard it raised — the aide, Kessler, the one who watches Freya too closely — saying something about a list, about staff quarters, about *tonight*.

Freya is already awake. Of course she is. She is putting the ledger into its oilcloth, her fingers moving fast and sure, and when she looks at me her face is calm, perfectly calm, and that is how I know it is very bad indeed.

"Emil," she whispers. "Linen shelf. Now. Take the horse."

I grab the wooden horse from Christmas and climb into the dark between the sheets, and pull the topmost ones down over me, and through the weave I watch the door, and wait for whatever comes through it.
