## 17. The Aide's Offer

The boots stop outside the door.

I have learned to read boots the way other women read faces. The orderlies wear their heels down at the outside edge, from shuffling. Anneli walks flat and quick, a woman perpetually late for something she does not want to attend. The guards march on their toes when officers watch and on their heels when they don't.

These boots are patient. They stop square. They do not shift weight. Whoever wears them has been told to stand somewhere and is prepared to stand there until the building falls down around him.

Then a knock. Two raps, measured, courteous—the knock of a man who has never once had to break down a door because doors open for him.

"One moment," I call, as though I have been caught mid-task, as though my heart is not trying to climb out through my throat. I slide the oilcloth bundle back beneath the floorboard with two fingers, press the plank down, and nudge the coal scuttle half an inch so its shadow lies where it should. In the linen store, six feet away, Emil is a shape of stillness under old sheets. If he is breathing, I cannot hear it. Good boy. Best boy in the Baltic.

I open the door.

Lieutenant Kessler stands in the corridor with his hands clasped behind his back and an expression of mild, reasonable regret, like a man arriving to collect a small debt he is embarrassed to mention. He is younger than Brandt by twenty years and better looking by any measure, which in an army is the same as being more dangerous. His coat is buttoned to the throat. His cap is under his arm. He has even removed his gloves, and I understand this immediately for what it is: theater. A man who comes armed comes gloved. A man who comes bare-handed wants you to look at his empty hands and forget his holstered pistol.

"Sister Iversen." He says my name correctly, all three syllables, which tells me he has been reading files. "Forgive the hour."

"It is nine o'clock, Lieutenant."

"Forgive the *place*, then." He glances past my shoulder into the ward with the unhurried interest of a man pricing furniture. "Might I come in? It is a matter of some delicacy."

Everything in that sentence is true and none of it is honest, but there is no refusing him without explaining why one refuses, so I step aside and let the wolf into the sheep barn and hope he finds the sheep uninteresting.

* * *

He sits on the wooden chair by the stove as if invited to tea, and I remain standing because sitting across from a man suggests equality and I would rather keep the arithmetic clear between us. The stove ticks as it cools. Somewhere down the corridor a man coughs, twice, and stops.

"You've been with us since the spring of 'ninety-seven," Kessler says, consulting nothing. He doesn't need to consult anything; it is a performance of memory meant to frighten me with its accuracy. "Recommended by Matron Hesse. Commendation for typhus duty. You turned down the transfer to the convalescent home in—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel— "the south."

"The south was far," I say.

"It was safe," he says, and lets that sit between us like a coin on a table.

I fold my hands. Under the floorboards, perhaps four feet from his polished boot heel, lie the names of forty-one men who officially never existed. I calculate the distance the way I used to calculate doses—exactly, and with a prayer attached.

"What is the delicacy, Lieutenant? I have patients."

"Yes. You always say that. It's noted." He smiles to show the notation was friendly. Then the smile is put away, folded up like his gloves, and what remains is simply intelligent and cold. "Sister Iversen, I am going to speak plainly, and I ask you to hear all of it before you answer. What you do afterward is your affair."

"I haven't answered a question yet."

"No," he agrees pleasantly. "You're good at that."

The stove ticks. Down the hall the cougher coughs again, and someone hushes him.

"There was an advance in October," Kessler says. "The one at the mill race. You may remember the wounded it produced, or failed to produce. The official report places casualties at nineteen. The Colonel's draft, before revision, placed them at thirty." He watches me the way a card player watches not the cards but the other man's wrists. "Eleven names were removed. Administrative correction. Clerical error, we called it. The clerks were very sorry."

My face is doing whatever faces do. I feel nothing in it—that particular anesthesia of terror where the muscles decide, without consulting you, to be smooth as pond ice. Inside, I am running through the ward inventory of things he could know. Does he know about the ledger or does he only suspect? Suspicion is weather; knowledge is a verdict. Men like Kessler do not knock on doors over weather.

"Clerks are often sorry," I say. "It is the whole of their profession."

Something flickers at the corner of his mouth—not quite approval, but the recognition of a fellow professional. And there it is, the first wrong note, the thing I will turn over in my mind for hours after he leaves: he is not here to catch me. A man conducting an arrest does not compliment the arrestee's wit.

"The sergeant who led that advance," Kessler continues, "was named Voss. Erhardt Voss. He was blamed for the failure. Posthumously, of course—he was obliging enough to die. The report says he advanced without support due to his own misjudgment. The truth, which lives in a draft folder the Colonel believes was burned, is somewhat different." He pauses. "The truth has a son. About so high. Dark hair. Currently, I'm told, recovering from a bad cough."

The floor does not tilt, though I give it full permission to. I think of Emil under the sheets, six years old, gripping a carved horse, and I think: *he knows, he knows, he knows,* and then I think, more slowly, the way one thinks while drowning: *then why am I not already in a cell?*

"I don't know what you're describing," I say. "We treat many children."

"We treat exactly one," Kessler says mildly. "And he has been treated for exactly one bad cough, which Sister Anneli logged, and which you personally saw to at two o'clock in the morning on the twelfth. Your devotion is remarkable. It is also," and here his voice softens into something almost kind, almost warm, the cruelest register he owns, "a great deal of rope."

* * *

Let me be honest, since honesty costs me nothing now and everything later: in that moment I hated him, and in the next moment—and this is the shameful part—I believed him.

Because that is how temptation works. It does not arrive wearing horns. It arrives carrying your own best argument, dressed for church, speaking in the voice of the sensible friend who only wants what you want but has thought through the logistics. Kessler did not threaten Emil. He did not mention cells or firing squads or the bonfire scheduled for New Year's Eve, where the old regime's records are to go up in smoke along with every name in my ledger. He sat by my cold stove with his bare hands folded and offered me, in essence, a lifeboat, and only afterward mentioned that the sea was full of drowning.

"Here is my proposal," he said. "You will leave. Both of you. Before the year turns. There is a train on the thirtieth—I can put you on it. Papers, travel authorization, a letter of introduction to a hospital in Rostock that owes me a favor. The boy goes with you as your nephew. No one stops a nurse and a child."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, the ledger comes to me. All of it. Every name."

"I don't—"

"Sister." For the first time he leaned forward, and I saw the real man under the courtesy, and the real man was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Do not insult us both. You copied eleven names off the Colonel's own desk in October. I know because I held the candle. I have watched you write in that ledger for weeks—I knew about it before the boy ever appeared at your door. Half the garrison suspects; nobody can prove it; proving it serves nobody, least of all me."

"Then why—" I stopped, and made myself finish. "Why do you want it?"

And he laughed, short and bitter, a laugh with no humor in it anywhere, and stood, and walked to the window where the frost had grown ferns on the inside of the glass, and stood looking out at the dark harbor with his back to me.

"Because the Colonel is going to burn it," he said. "On the thirty-first, with everything else. Because in five years' time Erhardt Voss will be a footnote that says *coward*, and the Colonel will be a general, and I will have spent those five years standing behind him holding his hat. Because a man in my position has exactly one kind of currency, Sister, and it is not medals." He turned around. In the stove light his young face looked older than Brandt's. "It is paper. Names on paper, in the right hands, at the right time. Your ledger is worth more than my whole career, and my whole career is worth more than your conscience. That is the entire arithmetic of this war."

"And if I refuse?"

He picked up his gloves from the chair and worked them on, finger by finger, unhurried.

"Then I wish you a happy new year," he said. "Truly. From the bottom of whatever I have instead of a heart. But I would think hard about the thirtieth. Trains are punctual things. Bonfires are punctual things. Opportunities—" he tapped his cap against his leg— "are not."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not look back.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I believe every word you wrote in that ledger. Every name. I simply intend to make them pay rent." A beat. "Good night, Sister. Give my regards to the boy. Tell him his father advanced exactly as ordered. Tell him whoever taught him not to say his surname was wiser than she knew."

Then the door closed behind him, and his boots went away down the corridor—patient, measured, unshifting—and stopped at the far end just long enough for me to understand that he had been heard by someone waiting there, and then went on, and were gone.

* * *

I got Emil out from under the sheets and he came with the horse clutched to his chest and asked nothing, only searched my face with those grave eyes, reading my terror the way children read weather. I sat him on my knee by the dead stove and gave him the last of the Christmas honey on a spoon and did not tell him a single true thing.

Anneli found me there an hour later, still sitting, the honey spoon dry on the table.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me a way out."

Her face did the complicated thing it does—the relief and the suspicion arriving together and fighting for space. "And the price?"

"The ledger. To him. Not to burn—to *keep*. Leverage against Brandt." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud for the ward, and a patient stirred, and I brought my voice down to a whisper. "Do you see the joke of it, Anneli? He wants to rescue the truth. Rescue it, and lock it in a drawer, and feed it crumbs, and bring it out when it can do him the most good. Forty-one men, and he wants them for collateral."

"That's not the joke," Anneli said quietly. She sat down across from me, and took my cold hands in her warm ones, an intimacy we had never managed before. "The joke is that it might work. The joke is that you'd live. The boy would live. You'd be in Rostock by spring, folding bandages, and the truth would be safe in a drawer instead of ash in a brazier, and everyone would call that a victory."

"And you?" I said. "What would you call it?"

She was silent a long time. Through the window, across the frozen harbor, I could see the great pile they had begun stacking on the far bank for the burning—cordwood and crates and old files, the century's paperwork, growing day by day into a dark mountain that would make, for one night, a great light.

"I'd call it surviving," she said at last. "Which is not the same as winning. And I'd ask you one question, Freya, and then I'll let you not sleep in peace." Her grip tightened. "If you give him the ledger—your copy, the true one, the one under these boards—what happens to it when Kessler no longer needs it? When Brandt falls, or rises, or retires? Paper in a powerful man's drawer is paper until he decides otherwise. Those men trusted you with their names. Not him."

I looked down at the floorboards between my feet. Four feet of pine and coal dust and forty-one men who had died twice—once at the mill race, once on the Colonel's desk—and were trusting a nurse with a third chance at staying dead honestly.

"He knew about the ledger all along," I said. "Weeks. Since October. He stood there holding the candle while I copied, and he said nothing, and tonight he knocked politely and offered me a train."

"Then ask yourself what else he's been watching," Anneli said, rising. "A man who waits weeks to make an offer is a man who wanted you desperate first."

She left me there with the dying stove and the sleeping ward and the mountain of wood across the black water, and I did not sleep, and near dawn I did the arithmetic again, slower this time, and arrived at the number I had been avoiding all night:

Kessler wanted the ledger delivered into his hands by the thirtieth.

Jonas had told me the burning was moved forward one day.

Someone had moved it—and I was suddenly, coldly certain that the polite knock and the generous train and the bare hands were not separate events at all, but two ends of the same closing fist.
