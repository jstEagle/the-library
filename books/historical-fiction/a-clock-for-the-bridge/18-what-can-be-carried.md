## 18. What Can Be Carried

The rule came to me years ago, back when I was still a nurse who believed paperwork was a nuisance rather than a weapon: *carry nothing you cannot run with.* I learned it the way most people learn their important rules — by breaking the other kind first. I had once owned a trunk. A real trunk, brass-cornered, with a false bottom my father had built for smuggling brandy during a war before this one. I loved that trunk. It loved me back by being impossible to lift, and when the retreat came through our sector in '96, I stood in the road and watched a soldier pry it open on the cobblestones, help himself to my winter coat, and leave everything else scattered like the contents of a gutted bird.

So now, three nights after Kessler's offer sat in my chest like a swallowed nail, I pulled the rule out, blew the dust off it, and obeyed it properly for once.

I did it at night, after the ward's breathing settled into its various rhythms — the shallow ones near the end, the heavy ones near the beginning, Corporal Voss's snore cutting through all of it like a saw through wet wood. Anneli kept the night lamp low and pretended not to watch as I knelt in the corner of the linen store with my one decent bag open on the floor like a mouth waiting to be fed.

"You're sorting," she said. Not a question. Head nurses develop a tone that turns statements into diagnoses.

"I'm tidying."

"At midnight."

"I'm a woman of unusual habits."

She let it go, which was her way of saying she'd already sorted her own bag weeks ago, in her head, and had simply been waiting for me to catch up. That is the thing about Anneli nobody writes ballads about: she arrives at every disaster early and waits politely.

* * *

The sorting itself was brutal in a way I hadn't anticipated. I have amputated fingers. I have told mothers things over telegraph wires that no wire should have to carry. And yet nothing prepared me for the peculiar cruelty of holding up each object I owned and asking it, aloud, in a whisper, whether it was worth my life.

My second uniform dress — no. It went onto the pile for the ward, because Marta down in recovery had grown two sizes since the shelling and refused, on grounds of dignity, to be seen in a hospital gown past Christmas. My good shawl — no, though this one hurt. It went to Sister Greta, whose cough had turned the corner from annoying into ominous sometime around Advent. The photograph of my parents, taken the year before the first war thinned our family's ranks — this one I held for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about photographs: they weigh nothing and they weigh everything. A print of stiff-backed people squinting into a sun that has since set on all of them, and there I was, kneeling on cold stone, trying to decide whether love could survive being left behind or whether leaving it behind was simply another word for killing it.

I put it in the bag. Then I took it out. Then I sat there feeling ridiculous, an experienced nurse brought low by cardstock, until common sense arrived wearing its usual work clothes: if I was caught, the photograph would tell whoever caught me exactly who I was. And who I was, increasingly, was someone the Colonel's men were being very thorough about learning.

It went to the pile. I told myself my mother lived in my hands — in the way I folded bandages, in the stubbornness of my spine — and not in any picture. I did not entirely believe it, but belief, like rations, can be stretched thin when necessary.

* * *

Three things earned their place in the bag.

First, the ledger. Of course. Forty-odd pages of names the Empire had decided never existed, wrapped in oilcloth and carried flat against the bag's frame where a search would find it last, if at all. I had sewn the oilcloth sleeve myself, badly — Anneli's hands do needlework, mine do surgery, and neither of us pretends otherwise anymore. Every time I lifted the bag I felt that small hard weight and thought: *eleven men Brandt erased in one report alone.* Multiply by his career. Divide by the truth. Carry the remainder.

Second, Emil's boots.

This requires explanation, because boots seem an odd heirloom for a child who already owns boots — or rather, who owns the idea of boots, since his current pair had more holes than leather and let the cold in like invited guests. In the linen store's deepest shelf, wrapped in a pillowcase, I had been keeping a pair I'd traded half a month's tea ration for: sturdy, slightly too large, oiled, with soles that would hold up to frozen streets and checkpoints and whatever distance stood between here and safety. They were meant for later. For the night of.

But rules are rules. If the boots couldn't run with us, they didn't belong to us, and I wasn't going to be caught at the eleventh hour fumbling through shelves while soldiers kicked doors. So into the bag they went, and that night, instead, I made a ceremony of the smaller thing: I sat Emil down on a stool between beds, cut away the laces of his ruined pair with my scissors, and put the new ones on his feet right there under the ward's dim lamp.

"They're big," he said, wiggling his toes inside them like a swimmer testing water.

"Feet grow," I said. "Boots don't. We're planning ahead."

He looked at me with that terrible seriousness children deploy when adults say something true by accident. "Are we going somewhere?"

"We're always going somewhere," I said. "That's what feet are."

He accepted this, which is the mercy of six-year-olds: they take your evasions at face value and file them away, and you only discover how much they understood much later, at inconvenient moments. He practiced walking in the boots up and down the ward, and Private Ostrau — one-armed, foul-mouthed, secretly gentle as bread — declared them regulation-issue and demanded Emil salute him, which Emil did, wobbling, and the whole ward laughed in the low careful way wards laugh, so as not to pull stitches.

Third, my mother's ring.

It lives on a cord around my neck, where it has lived since she pressed it into my palm at a train station and said words I have never repeated to anyone. Thin gold, worn nearly smooth, worth almost nothing to anyone but me — which is precisely why it must come. A bribe guard takes money. A pawnbroker takes gold. But if everything else failed, if the ledger burned and the names dissolved and the century ate its own dead, the ring would remain as proof that a woman named Ida Iversen had existed, had married, had buried two children and raised one badly, had died with her hand held. Some evidence matters even when no court will ever read it.

I counted the ring twice, the way you do with irreplaceable things, and tucked it back under my collar where it lay against my sternum like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Everything else went to the ward, and here is where the chapter of my life turned strange and warm, because giving things away turns out to be its own peculiar arithmetic.

Marta got the dress and cried, then pretended she hadn't, then wore it the next morning belted with rope and called it fashion. Greta got the shawl and immediately began knitting it into something else, because Greta believes all wool yearns to become socks. The medical texts went to Anneli, who received them without comment and shelved them in her office where a searching officer would assume they belonged to the hospital all along — which, legally speaking, they now did. My spare spectacles went to old Halversen, the cartographer's patient, whose eyesight had surrendered somewhere around the Battle of the Narrows and who had been pretending to read the same newspaper for a week.

Even the small indignities became gifts. My hairbrush — cracked, missing teeth — went to a boy of nineteen in the far bed who had been combing his hair with his fingers before his mother's letters arrived, because he wanted to look like a man who had a mother worth combing for. My tin of sugar, hoarded against emergencies since October, I divided into six paper twists and dealt them out like playing cards, telling each recipient it was medicinal. Technically, joy is medicinal. I stand by the prescription.

Emil watched all of this redistribution with narrowed eyes, and finally climbed onto my stool while I was wrapping my sewing kit — the one object I couldn't decide about — in cloth.

"You're giving everything away," he said.

"I'm making room."

"For what?"

"For the things we're keeping." I tied off the knot. "There's a trick to traveling light. You only carry what you'd grab if the house were burning."

"What if you're carrying me?" he asked, perfectly reasonably, and I had to turn my face toward the window until my voice worked again.

"Yes," I said. "You're the heaviest thing I own. And the only thing I wouldn't trade."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then took the wooden horse Ostrau had carved him from the shelf and placed it carefully in the pocket of his new boots' accompanying coat. "Then I'm carrying him," he said. "He's what I'd grab."

And there it was — the entire philosophy of my life, articulated by a child holding a horse the size of his fist. *Carry nothing you cannot run with.* He had understood it in four seconds. It had taken me thirty years and a gutted trunk on a cobblestone road.

* * *

By the third night, the bag stood packed by the linen store door, innocent as a schoolbag: oilcloth-wrapped ledger flat against the frame, boots broken in by a week of ward-parades, ring at my throat, sewing kit, two days' rations compressed to the density of sin, Jonas's checkpoint maps copied onto tissue paper and folded into the lining of my coat rather than the bag, because coats don't get set down. Everything I would grab if the house were burning. Everything, I reminded myself nightly, that could be grabbed *for* me if the house were burning and I weren't given the courtesy of doing the grabbing myself.

That thought should have frightened me more than it did. Instead it produced a strange calm, the calm of a solved equation. I knew now exactly what my life weighed. It fit in one bag with room left over for a child's hand.

Anneli found me on the last night of sorting, sitting on the floor with my back against the shelves, watching the lamp gutter. She lowered herself beside me with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions, and looked at the bag for a long moment.

"It's ready," she said.

"It's ready."

"And you?"

I thought about lying to her, out of habit, and decided we were past it. "I've been ready since '96," I said. "I just didn't know what for."

She nodded slowly. Then she reached into her own apron pocket and pressed something into my palm: a small paper packet, folded with hospital precision. Inside, dried bread, a twist of sugar, and a single copper coin.

"Bribes and calories," she said. "The two currencies."

"When did you—"

"Night you started talking to your shoes," she said. "Don't argue with a head nurse. We're worse than generals and better informed."

We sat there a while longer, two women on a cold floor, surrounded by everything we'd chosen to lose. Through the wall came the ward's breathing, Voss's snore, Emil's small even sigh from the cot Anneli had officially assigned to "a convalescent nephew" on paperwork that would fool no one and had fooled everyone, which is the best kind of paperwork.

I should have gone to sleep satisfied. The bag was packed. The plan had edges. Even Kessler's offer, that swallowed nail, had been converted into fuel — every reminder of it sent me back to the maps, back to Jonas's careful pencil marks, back to rehearsal.

Instead, what woke me before dawn was smaller and colder than fear. It was arithmetic again — the ledger kind, the dangerous kind.

Because I had spent three evenings giving things away, and I had watched every gift travel across the ward, and it occurred to me, lying rigid in the dark, that a ward is a small city, and cities talk. Marta's new dress. Greta's shawl. Halversen's spectacles. Six paper twists of sugar. Kindness leaves fingerprints everywhere it passes, and I had spent a week pressing mine into forty pairs of hands, some of whom had families outside these walls, some of whom had visitors, some of whom — and here my stomach turned over with the slow certainty of a body found in snow — had laundry to deliver, errands to run, reasons to pass through the gate and speak to people who paid for small pieces of information the way other people paid for bread.

Someone had sold the searchers 'the nurse and the boy' once already. I had assumed it was suspicion. Now, listening to the ward breathe around me, I understood it might simply have been commerce — and that I had just spent a week advertising my departure to the entire marketplace.

The bag by the door suddenly felt less like a solution and more like a confession with handles.

I lay there until the bell rang for morning shift, doing sums I didn't want to do, and when I rose I did two things before anything else: I moved the bag from the linen store to the space beneath the loose floorboard where the ledger used to live — better a hiding place they'd already searched than a fresh one — and I went looking for the orderly who ran the laundry detail, smiling at him the way you smile at a locked door, wondering all the while what price he'd already named, and to whom.
