## 19. Betrayal in the Laundry

There is a particular smell to a hospital laundry that no amount of lye can kill: it is the smell of everything the ward wants to forget. Blood that has dried too long, sweat that has soaked through wool, the sour breath of fever baked into linen until the fibers themselves seem to have given up. I had washed a thousand bandages in that room over four years, and I had come to think of it as the most honest place in the building. The wards performed recovery for visiting officers; the operating theater performed miracles for itself. The laundry told the truth. It took what we could not look at and turned it white again, or tried to.

I should have paid more attention to who else was listening to honesty that winter.

* * *

It began with soap. Everything that winter began with something small — an extra ration of bread, a candle stub, a spoonful of sugar hoarded like contraband. Soap was mine. Sister Anneli kept the lock on the supply cupboard and I kept the key hidden in my boot, and between us we had arranged a system of such delicate corruption that I sometimes thought we should both be decorated for logistics rather than shot for theft. A bar of soap went into Emil's hands twice a week whether his hands needed it or not, because a child who has slept in ditches has a horror of dirt that no adult can properly imagine, and because the ritual of it — lather, rinse, the small ceremony of clean fingernails inspected under lamplight — was the closest thing to mothering I had to offer him.

On the Tuesday before Christmas I went down for our bar and found the cupboard already unlocked.

I stood very still in the corridor and listened to myself think, which is a thing one learns to do in a hospital. Fear is loud. It wants to run shouting down the halls. But I made it sit quietly while I counted possibilities. Anneli, taking supplies for the ward, forgetting her discipline in the holiday rush of dying men. A laundress with cold hands and no patience for keys. Or — and here the fear stood up and refused to sit again — someone who had watched me take my bar of soap three times a week for two months and had simply learned my habit the way a hunter learns the path a deer takes to water.

The cupboard held its inventory perfectly, which was somehow worse. Nothing stolen. Only the lock, hanging open like a mouth mid-sentence.

I took my soap. I locked the cupboard. I walked back up three flights of stairs at exactly the pace of a woman with nothing on her mind, which is the slowest pace there is, and I did not let my hand shake until I was behind the screen of the linen store where Emil sat folding bandages into squares with the grave precision of a man defusing artillery.

"Freya." He looked up. He had learned my name the way he learned everything that winter — carefully, testing its weight, as if names might be taken back if mishandled. "Corporal Weiss says my corners are better than yours."

"Corporal Weiss is missing two fingers and half his judgment." I crouched down and straightened his collar, mostly so I could look at the door behind him. "Emil. Do you remember what we said about the game? About being quiet when I tap the shelf twice?"

"Like mice," he said. "Mice don't have corners."

"Mice don't have corners," I agreed, though I hadn't the faintest idea what he meant, and I filed it away among the hundred small mysteries of him that I intended, someday, to have time for.

* * *

The orderly's name was Piet Sorel, and I want to be fair to him, insofar as fairness is owed to a man who sells children. He was not cruel. That is the part that took me longest to forgive — not the act, but the smallness of it. He was a widower of perhaps forty with a bad knee and a mother in the eastern quarter who needed medicine more expensive than his wages, and he changed bedpans and hauled linens and complained about the coffee with the rest of us, and if you had asked me in November to name the staff least likely to matter to my story, I would have pointed at him within the first ten names.

He was in the laundry that afternoon. I know now that he was not doing laundry.

What I knew then was smaller and stranger: that evening, when I came off shift, Corporal Weiss caught my sleeve in the corridor — the intact hand, always the intact hand, he was scrupulous about it — and said, in the tone of a man remarking on weather, "Sister Freya. That boy of yours. He folds a neat bandage."

"He does."

"You're teaching him letters too. I've seen the slate."

"He's six," I said. "Six-year-olds learn letters. It's practically the law."

Weiss nodded, slowly, and looked past me down the corridor toward the stairwell, where — and this I only registered later, replaying it the way one replays a card game after losing — Piet Sorel was standing with a crate of folded sheets, not moving, not delivering them anywhere, simply standing in the way a man stands when he is memorizing something.

"Sister," Weiss said quietly, and now his voice had lost its weather, "there's talk going around the orderlies' room. Talk about a nurse with a secret nephew. Talk about questions being asked upstairs. I don't know whose mouth it comes from. But you were good to me when I came in, and I'd rather you heard it crooked from me than straight from someone worse."

I thanked him. I said something light. I walked back to the ward with my spine made of broomstick and my mind made of ice, and behind my composed face I was doing the arithmetic that had become my life: how many hours until morning, how many steps from ward to linen store, how long it would take Brandt's men to search a building if they started at the top.

* * *

Anneli heard me out without interrupting, which is her gift and her weapon both. We stood in the dispensary among the brown bottles, out of earshot even of the dying, who are the only truly discreet people in a hospital.

"A nephew," she repeated. "Someone invented a nephew for you. That's sloppy work. Sloppy inventions are how inquiries begin — not with evidence but with a story that doesn't quite hold."

"So we give the story somewhere to hold." I had been turning it over all through supper. "A sister in the western provinces, widowed, unable to feed the boy. It explains his accent, his lack of papers — papers burned in the farmhouse fire, which is even true. It explains why he's here instead of an orphanage: I'm family, I took him in, no paperwork required in wartime, everyone shrugs."

"It requires your sister to exist."

"My sister died of scarlet fever in 'ninety-four. She exists beautifully. There's a grave."

Anneli looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her weigh it — not the plan, but me. Whether the woman in front of her was clever enough or merely desperate enough to believe she was clever. "And when they check the grave?"

"Then we're past stories anyway." I kept my voice level. "Anneli. You and I both know where this ends. Stories buy weeks, not acquittals. What they buy us is Christmas."

She closed the dispensary cabinet with a click that sounded like a decision. "Then we spend them well."

* * *

For three days afterward I conducted an experiment in being watched, and I recommend it to no one.

I moved through the hospital performing ordinariness the way an actress performs virtue — thoroughly, and with constant awareness of the audience. I took Emil's meals at regular hours. I scolded him publicly, gently, for tracking slush across the ward, establishing before witnesses that he was a nuisance and a chore and nothing rarer. I let him be seen folding bandages, because hiding him entirely would have confirmed whatever whisper was circulating; a hidden child is a mystery, but a working child is only poor relations. And everywhere I went, I catalogued.

The orderlies' room door, left open a suspicious inch. Sorel carrying linens to wards that had requested none. Sorel's eyes finding me at the end of corridors and sliding away with the studied carelessness of a card cheat. Twice I caught him near the linen store. Twice I smiled at him — a flat, friendly smile, the smile of a woman thinking about soap — and twice he smiled back with a warmth that curdled me, because it was the warmth of a man who believes he has already settled accounts with you.

Here is what I understood, standing in the laundry on Friday amid the steam and the honest stink: he had sold us already. Whatever Weiss had heard was not rumor gathering courage. It was merchandise that had changed hands. The only question left was price and timing, and timing, in a garrison city in the last week of a dying century, had a shape I could guess.

After Christmas. Of course after Christmas. Even betrayal keeps the holidays. No colonel spends the birth of his Savior hunting a nurse through a hospital ward; the season demanded its truce of appearances. But the day after — the twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh, when the city swept up its pine needles and the garrison returned to its ledgers — the search would resume. Perhaps with Kessler's careful discretion. Perhaps with soldiers.

I stood there with a bar of soap in my hand and realized I was memorizing the room. The window latch. The chute that fed down to the cellar. The trestle tables, the copper boiler big enough to drown a man. I had walked into this room a thousand times as a nurse. Now I walked through it as a fugitive surveying an escape route she prayed never to use, and I thought: this is what paranoia is. It is not fear. Fear is a guest; it arrives, it shouts, it leaves. Paranoia moves in. It rearranges your furniture. It makes you count the exits in your own home and weigh your friends by the pound of their desperation.

Piet Sorel came in while I stood there. He stopped. For a heartbeat neither of us moved, two people in a room full of other people's filth, and I watched him decide what his face would do.

"Sister Freya." Warmth again. "Cold day for the laundry."

"Every day is a cold day for the laundry, Piet. That's rather the point of it." I lifted the soap. "Blessings of the season to you."

"And to you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on a table. "And to the boy. He's looking well. Growing."

"Yes," I said. "Children do that. It's their one reliable quality."

I walked past him out into the corridor and did not let my knees inform the rest of me until I reached the stairwell. Behind me I heard him begin to sing — some carol, tuneless, contented. A man at peace with his ledger.

* * *

That night I sat on the floor of the linen store with Emil between my knees and combed his hair with my fingers, which he permitted because it had become our custom and because he was six and tired and the world's arrangements were beyond him.

"Freya. Is someone angry with us?"

The question landed exactly where questions from children land — in the unprotected place. "Why would you ask that?"

"Weiss talks to me about horses and then stops talking when Sorel comes. Sorel looks at me like I'm on his list." He held up an imaginary paper. "I'm on lists. I was on a list before. Papa said don't say the surname because of lists."

I rested my chin on the crown of his head and looked at the shelf above us, at the stacked linens going pale in the lamplight, and I decided — as I had decided once before, in another dark room, holding another impossible truth — that he would have as much of it as he could carry.

"Yes," I said. "I think someone may be angry. Or something worse than angry — I think someone may find us useful. Those are different troubles, and the second kind is harder, because you can't apologize your way out of usefulness." His head tilted against my collarbone, listening. "But listen to me, Emil. After Christmas we're going to play the quiet game for real. The whole game. All the rules. Can you hold all the rules?"

"I held them before."

"You did. Better than I did, probably." I turned him around to face me. "And Emil — the surname. Your father's name. The one you practiced writing."

"I remember it."

"Don't say it anymore. Not here. Not to anyone, not even in whispers, not even asleep. Keep it where you keep the mice. After the bridge—" I stopped myself, but the word had escaped and hung in the air between us, small and enormous. *Bridge.* His eyes went wide and knowing, the way children's eyes do, having been told all winter that bridges were for spring, someday, when things were safer.

"After the bridge," he repeated solemnly, "I can say it anywhere."

"After the bridge," I said, and kissed his hair, and put out the lamp, "you can shout it from the rooftops."

I did not sleep that night. I lay on my cot in the nurses' room and listened to the building breathe — the tick of cooling pipes, a patient coughing two floors up, footsteps in the corridor that paused, just slightly, outside our door, and moved on. Somewhere below me, in the laundry or the orderlies' room or some smoke-filled tavern in the eastern quarter, a price was already written down in someone's book with my name beside it and a six-year-old's description underneath. *The nurse and the boy.* We had become an entry. I had spent years making entries of my own about men erased, and now the machinery had turned its attention around, and I understood, with the clarity that only comes at three in the morning, exactly how the men in my ledger had felt in their last hour: named, priced, and hunted by paperwork.

Christmas Eve was thirty-six hours away. Christmas Day would be a borrowed warmth — candle stubs, a carved horse, a stolen hour of joy in the linen store. And then the twenty-sixth would come, grey and punctual, and the search would come with it.

I got up in the dark and felt under the floorboard for the ledger, and held it against my ribs, and began, in earnest, to count the days.
