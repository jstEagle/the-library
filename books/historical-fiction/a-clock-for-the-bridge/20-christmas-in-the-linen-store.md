## 20. Christmas in the Linen Store

There is a particular arithmetic to a military hospital at Christmastime, and it is not the arithmetic of carols. It goes like this: forty-one beds, thirty-nine occupied; eleven candle stubs salvaged from the chapel bin before the sacristan noticed their absence; one sack of dried apples contributed by the wife of a man in Bed 12, who had walked nine kilometers through sleet to deliver it and stayed only long enough to count her husband's fingers twice. Subtract from all this any official celebration, because the garrison has decreed that festive observances strain coal reserves, and you arrive at the sum total of Christmas, 1899: almost nothing.

Almost nothing is, as it happens, my specialty.

I had been planning it for two weeks the way I planned everything now—in fragments, in the margins of other tasks, the way the ledger lived under the floorboards and the plan for Emil's future lived somewhere behind my sternum where no inspection would ever find it. A holiday could be smuggled the same way contraband moved through this city: in small pieces, carried by many hands, assembled in secret by people who understood that joy, like gunpowder, was confiscated at the gate.

The linen store was the obvious venue. It sat at the end of the east corridor, windowless, warm from the boiler flue that ran behind its wall, and stacked to the ceiling with sheets that smelled of lye and cold air. More importantly, it was the one room in the hospital that Sister Anneli entered without inspecting, because inspecting linens required an emotional investment she refused to make. "I have buried enough this year," she told me once. "I will fold what is clean and burn what is not and think about neither." It was the closest thing to a philosophy the ward possessed, and we had all quietly adopted it.

Emil, of course, knew nothing. This was the difficult part—not the candles, not the apples, but the lying. The boy had a talent for reading faces that should have alarmed me more than it did; children who survive by being overlooked develop the eyes of customs officials. Twice I caught him watching me count bandage rolls with an attention that had nothing to do with bandages, and twice I redirected him with chores, which he accepted with the solemnity of a man receiving orders he intends to follow exactly until the moment following them becomes inconvenient.

"Are we doing anything for the holiday?" he asked me on the twenty-third, folding gauze into squares with the precision of a much older person. He had learned to do this from Private Halloran in Bed 9, who had lost his left hand at the failed advance in November and had decided that teaching a six-year-old to fold dressings was the highest purpose remaining to him. "At home we had a tree. Papa cut it himself."

The word *home* landed the way it always did, somewhere soft and unprotected.

"Here," I said, "we do things differently. Here we celebrate quietly. Very quietly. Can you keep a quiet celebration?"

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Is it a secret?"

"The best kind of secret. The kind nobody knows about except us."

"Then they can't take it away," he said, and went back to his gauze, and I stood there holding a tray of instruments and understanding, abruptly, why the woman at the door had written *forget this face*. Some things you protect precisely by making them invisible. I had been practicing that art for years myself. I simply hadn't expected to be outdone by someone who still needed a stool to reach the basin.

* * *

The conspiracy assembled itself over three days, in the manner of all good conspiracies: nobody said anything outright, and everybody knew.

Sister Anneli's contribution arrived without comment—a small paper of sugar almonds, the kind sold at the market stall by the cathedral, appearing in my apron pocket with no note. When I raised an eyebrow at her across the dispensary she looked at me with the expression of a woman who would deny the existence of her own hands if asked under oath. "I found those," she said. "In the corridor. Abandoned property."

"You walked past the market stall yesterday. Frau Bergmann says you bought out her almond supply."

"Frau Bergmann," said Anneli, sorting vials with tremendous concentration, "is a gossip and possibly a liar, and if either of those things reaches a tribunal I will testify she sells watered milk." She thrust the last vial into its rack hard enough to make the whole shelf ring. "Midnight. After the night orderly makes his round at half past. Not before. And Freya—" she stopped, and for a moment the armor slipped and I saw the girl who had once been somebody's little sister, before the reports started coming in envelopes. "Make it good. Whatever it costs. Make it good."

Private Halloran, one-armed, irrepressible, was the second recruit, and the easiest. I found him whittling at his bedside, which was against regulations and therefore constant.

"Halloran," I said, "what are you making?"

"A horse," he said, offended I'd had to ask. "It's obviously a horse. What did you think it was, a very ambitious dog?" He blew shavings off the blade. "For the boy. Don't tell him. Men in beds have no secrets from boys in wards; he'd smell it on me inside an hour. He watches everything, that one. Like a little customs man." He turned the carving in his remaining hand. It was, in fairness, unmistakably a horse—rough-maned, short-legged, with a head cocked at an angle of permanent skepticism, as though the horse itself had doubts about the century ahead. "My brother carved these. Back home. He got the farm when Father died; I got the army and the knife. Seems only right the knife should finally produce something useful."

"It's beautiful," I said, and meant it, and he grunted and hid the horse under his blanket as the day orderly passed, the way men hide cards, or letters from home, or hope.

Others gave what they could, which is to say they gave everything, because scarcity does not shrink generosity; it concentrates it, the way frost concentrates blood. Sergeant Dahl in Bed 3, who could no longer stand without assistance, spent an afternoon teaching three convalescent men a round song whose lyrics had been carefully laundered of anything a passing officer might construe as seditious merriment. Frau Lindqvist, whose husband occupied Bed 12 and whose apples anchored our feast, sent up a twist of salt. The chaplain, a mild man who had long ago concluded that God's work on this ward was done primarily by women, developed a sudden and convenient deafness regarding the whereabouts of four candle stubs from the chapel box.

By the afternoon of the twenty-fourth, the linen store held: eleven candles of varying lengths and histories; a sack of dried apples; sugar almonds; a twist of salt; half a loaf of the dark bread Anneli had traded two jars of carbolic to obtain; one wooden horse wrapped in a square of clean gauze, because we were nurses to the bone and wrapped everything; and, at the center of it all, one small fir branch, cut by Jonas the mapmaker from a tree on the harbor road and carried the length of the ward corridor inside his coat, so that he walked in smelling extravagantly of pine and guilt.

"It looks," I said, propping the branch in a bottle on top of a stack of pillowcases, "like the world's smallest and most criminal Christmas tree."

"It looks," said Jonas, adjusting his spectacles, "like evidence. But then most beautiful things do, in this city." He checked his watch, then checked the corridor, then checked his watch again, because a cartographer's faith is placed entirely in schedules. "Half past eleven. The night orderly is eating. He always eats at half past; I've mapped it." Of course he had. Somewhere in Jonas's coat lived a hand-drawn chart of every man in this hospital's bladder, appetite, and conscience, cross-referenced by hour. It was possibly the most dangerous document in the building after mine, and I have never loved a piece of paper more.

I fetched Emil from the ward, telling him there was a spill in the linen store that needed his help, which was even true—there was a spill, and it needed his help, and it was made of candlelight and apples, but I have never claimed my lies lacked ambition.

* * *

I will try to describe his face when I opened the door, and I will fail, because I am a writer and therefore professionally obligated to admit when language quits.

He stood in the doorway in his too-large boots—he had grown into Halloran's spare socks by then, rolled twice at the ankle—and he looked at the candle stubs burning on the pillowcase stacks, and at the fir branch in its bottle, and at the sugar almonds arranged in a spiral because Anneli, it turned out, could not bring herself to merely pour them, and he did not say anything at all. He just looked. And then he looked up at me, checking, the way he always checked, whether this was real or whether it was one of those things adults said was real, and I knelt down so we'd be level and said, "Merry Christmas, Emil," and something in his face unclenched.

Later, much later, I would come to understand that this was the first thing anyone had prepared *for him* since the farmhouse burned. At the time I only understood that a six-year-old's smile, arriving after long absence, is a violent event, and that I had to turn and fuss with the candles immediately or embarrass us both.

We ate apples and bread with salt. We ate sugar almonds one at a time, slowly, because Anneli kept counting them with her eyes and redistributing them toward Emil's side of the circle with a furtiveness she believed was undetectable and was not. We sang the laundered round, badly—Halloran's bass had survived the loss of his arm intact and he deployed it with punitive enthusiasm—and Emil sang too, knowing perhaps half the words, supplying the other half with tremendous confidence and none whatsoever correctly. The candles guttered and were replaced from our reserve. The boiler ticked behind the wall. Outside, somewhere beyond the windowless dark, patrols walked their circuits and the city counted down its last week of a century, and in here a boy held a wooden horse.

Because of course Halloran couldn't stay away. He appeared at the door at midnight, having talked his way—or rather, having limped persuasively—past the duty nurse, with his arm in a sling and his blanket over his shoulders like a disreputable general's cloak, and he presented the horse with a formality that collapsed halfway through into embarrassment.

"It's not finished," he said. "The mane needs work. I'll want it back to finish the mane."

"He'll return it," I translated for Emil, "the moment he needs an excuse to visit you."

"That's blackmail," said Halloran.

"That's nursing," said Anneli.

Emil held the horse in both hands the way you hold something you have been taught the world can take from you. He turned it over. He ran his thumb down the rough mane. And then he did something I did not expect: he lifted it close to his mouth and whispered to it, briefly, privately, before tucking it inside his shirt against his chest.

"What did you tell him?" Halloran asked, gruff, looking anywhere else.

Emil considered whether to answer, decided against it, and then reconsidered, because he was six and secrecy sat poorly on him. "I told him about Papa," he said. "So somebody knows. Besides me."

The candles made their small sounds. Anneli looked at the ceiling. Halloran's jaw worked, and finally he said, "Good. That's what horses are for," in a voice that had gravel in it, and Emil nodded, satisfied, as though this confirmed a long-held theory of equine purpose, and ate another almond.

I sat on a pillowcase stack with my back against the warm wall and watched it: the boy, the men, the sister who had lost brothers singing softly in the dark, the mapmaker keeping time, the one-armed carver, the stolen light. And I thought: *this* is what Brandt signs away on those forms. Not names on paper. This. Candle stubs and salt and a boy whispering to a wooden horse so that somebody besides himself will know his father existed. They call it trimming reports. They call it protecting reputations. Nobody has ever once called it what it is, which is walking into a linen store and blowing out the candles while everyone is still singing.

Well. Let them come with their matches. We would simply steal the flame back. We were getting rather good at it.

* * *

The party broke up past one o'clock, in the conspiratorial manner of all such gatherings: candles pinched out one by one, apples hidden, the fir branch returned to its bottle for next year, because we were optimists now, apparently, planning ahead like people with futures. Halloran reclaimed the horse with theatrical reluctance—"for the mane, mind"—and returned it within ten minutes, mane improved, having used the excuse exactly as intended. Anneli herded everyone back to bed with the brisk tenderness of a sheepdog who reads poetry. Jonas consulted his watch and pronounced the evening, in his dry way, "well mapped."

I tucked Emil into his corner behind the linen shelves, where he had slept since Anneli had officially Not Noticed his presence, and pulled the blanket to his chin.

"The horse stays with you tonight," I said. "Halloran's finished the mane. It's yours."

"I know," he said, already drowsy, the wooden shape a lump against his heart. Then, surfacing: "Nurse Freya. Is Christmas over now?"

"Not till morning."

"Good." His eyes closed. "Because I want to remember it twice."

I sat beside him a while in the dark, listening to the boiler tick and the building settle, and let myself have the feeling, fully, the way you drink water when you've been rationing—joy, plain and stolen and paid for in sugar almonds. That was my mistake, if you want to know. That is always the mistake. You sit down in the warmth for one hour and you forget that the cold has been organizing outside the whole time.

I was hanging up my apron in the dispensary when I heard voices at the end of the corridor—low ones, unhurried, the worst kind. Lieutenant Kessler stood talking with the night orderly near the stairwell, and whatever the orderly was saying, he was saying it with his eyes down and his hand out slightly from his side, palm half-open, the gesture of a man delivering goods.

Kessler glanced my direction. He smiled the way winter smiles.

"Merry Christmas, Sister," he called pleasantly. "Lovely evening for it. The whole city smells of pine and candle smoke. One wonders where everyone finds the candles."

I wished him a merry Christmas in return, in a voice I did not recognize as mine, and walked back down the corridor with my spine straight and my mind already moving—floorboards, coal dust, the weight of paper against wood—while behind me, in his corner of the linen store, a boy slept with a carved horse over his heart, guarding the only witness statement that mattered.
