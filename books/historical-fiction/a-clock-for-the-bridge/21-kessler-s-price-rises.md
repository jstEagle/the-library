## 21. Kessler's Price Rises

Lieutenant Kessler chose the dispensary for it, which I thought was a mistake on his part. A man who wants to threaten a woman should not do it in a room where she keeps the laudanum.

He came in just after the evening bell, when the ward beyond the door had settled into its coughing, snoring dusk, and he closed the door behind him with the softness of a man who has practiced closing doors. He looked at the bottles on the shelf. He looked at the mortar and pestle. He looked everywhere except at me, because Kessler believed—wrongly—that delay made him seem thoughtful rather than rehearsed.

"Sister Iversen," he said at last. "You look tired."

"It's winter, Lieutenant. We're all tired. Some of us have the decency to be tired quietly."

That earned me half a smile. He had a good smile; that was the whole trouble with him. Brandt's face was a locked drawer, but Kessler's was an open one, and you could read everything in it—ambition, charm, and the particular hunger of a man standing on a ladder someone else built.

"I'll come to the point," he said, which was a lie, because he then spent two full minutes discussing the coal situation. The coal situation. As if we were two matrons comparing pantries rather than conspirators deciding the fate of a book of dead men. But I let him talk. It gave me time to notice his boots were wet to the ankle, though it hadn't snowed since noon, which meant he'd walked the long way around—from the garrison gate, perhaps, or from wherever one goes to arrange things one doesn't want traced back.

Then he said, "The original ledger."

Not *is there* a ledger. Not *I've heard rumors*. The article, definite and singular, as though we were discussing a delivered parcel.

"You'll want to be more specific," I said. "We keep many books here. Rations. Dressings. Deaths."

"Don't." He said it gently, which was somehow worse than if he'd said it with teeth. "Please don't make this more difficult than it needs to be. You've been careful, Sister. I'll grant you that. But careful is not invisible. There is a boy in your linen store whose existence exists nowhere in any record of this city, and there is a woman who has been asking questions about casualty reports from the autumn advance, and there is a colonel who has begun to sleep badly. Those three facts are converging, whether you will them to or not."

I kept my hands busy among the bottles. It is easier to lie when your hands have honest work. "I don't know what you're describing."

"Of course you don't." He picked up the mortar, weighed it in his palm, set it down again. "Here is what I'm offering. Give me the original ledger—all of it, every page—and I can guarantee your passage across the bridge before the first of January. Papers, timing, a guard on the toll house who owes me. You, the boy, whatever else you're carrying. Clean through."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," he said, "you forget my name."

I turned then and looked at him properly, because I wanted to see whether he understood what he was saying. He did. That was the terrible part. He stood there in the lamplight, handsome and reasonable and utterly unashamed, and he knew exactly what he was asking me to bury.

"You want the ledger," I said slowly, "not to publish it."

"Publish it?" For the first time something genuine crossed his face—surprise, almost offense. "Sister, think. If those pages surface under my name, what happens? An inquiry. Officers questioned. And every officer questioned will ask who helped the nurse, who opened which gate, who let which blanket through the customs house. Do you imagine the answers would stay quiet? Men hang for less than what I've already done by speaking to you tonight."

"So your concern is the truth getting out."

"My concern," he said, "is *surviving* the truth getting out. There's a difference. One is idealism. The other is arithmetic."

There it was. I had spent weeks telling myself a story about Lieutenant Kessler—that beneath the aide's polished boots there lived a man sickened by what Brandt had done, a man who wanted the erased sergeant restored, the eleven names read aloud, the record set straight. It is astonishing, really, how generously a lonely woman will interpret a man's attention. Every glance he'd lingered too long, every question asked too precisely—I had filed them all under *ally*. What I should have filed them under was *collector*. He wasn't gathering evidence against Brandt. He was gathering leverage over him, and my ledger was simply the heaviest coin in the drawer.

"You'd hold it over him forever," I said. "Every promotion, every command. He'd spend his career knowing you had it."

"He'd spend his career behaving himself." Kessler shrugged. "Isn't that better than a scandal? Nothing changes in the files, no one is disgraced, the boy stays alive, you reach the coast. Everyone keeps what they need."

"And Sergeant Voss keeps a traitor's name on his grave."

The silence after that lasted long enough for me to hear the ward breathing through the wall—the small wet symphony of forty damaged men.

"You know," Kessler said finally, and his voice lost some of its polish, "I have read those reports. The real ones. I know what happened at the crossing, and I know who signed what afterward. I am not proud of the uniform's arithmetic, Sister. But I watched a captain disappear last spring for writing a letter to his wife that was too honest, and I have a mother in Danzig and a brother in the academy, and I have concluded—correctly, I believe—that one man cannot pull down a colonel. He can only pull down himself, and take whoever is standing near him along."

"So you'd rather stand on him."

"I'd rather *stand*," he said, and there was something raw underneath it, something almost like grief. "Yes. I would rather stand. That is what the century has taught everyone I know."

He set a folded paper on the dispensary table between us. Passage papers, or something dressed up to look like them—a travel permit, a stamp, a signature that wasn't quite his own. He had prepared it all. He had walked here through the wet snow with these papers in his pocket and the offer arranged like a surgeon's tray, and he expected gratitude.

"The original," he said. "By Friday. Leave word at the pharmacy window with the word *cough syrup* and I'll know it's done. After that, you never saw me, I never saw you, and the past belongs to whoever holds the paper."

"And if I say no?"

He put his gloves back on, finger by finger, unhurried. "Then I go on being an excellent lieutenant, and eventually the search resumes, and eventually it succeeds, and I will have done nothing to stop it—because, you understand, I never saw you either." He paused at the door. "You've been very brave, Sister. Braver than anyone I've served under. Don't spend it all on principle. Principle doesn't keep children warm."

Then he was gone, and I stood alone in the dispensary among the honest bottles, and I discovered that my hands were shaking—not from fear. From disappointment. Fear I knew how to carry. Disappointment sat differently, heavier, lower, like wet wool against the ribs.

I had wanted him to be what I needed. That was the whole confession of it. I had looked at a man's clever eyes and convenient hatred of his colonel and constructed an ally out of wishful thinking, the way a starving woman constructs a meal out of the smell of bread. And all the while he had wanted exactly what Brandt wanted—the truth off the table—merely by gentler means. Brandt burned names. Kessler bought them and locked them in a drawer and called it prudence. Between the fire and the drawer there was, I now understood, very little difference worth dying for. Both ended the same way: Sergeant Voss forgotten, Emil fatherless twice over, and eleven men staying eleven men no longer counted anywhere but under my floorboards.

He was right about one thing, damn him. I couldn't give him the original. And I could no longer trust anything he offered.

Which left me holding a copy I'd already surrendered once in spirit—half my sewn pages abandoned at the customs house—and a passage that now came with a muzzle attached, and a boy asleep in a linen store who practiced his father's name in charcoal when he thought nobody was looking.

Friday. He wanted it by Friday.

I blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, doing the arithmetic Kessler so admired, and arrived at a sum he had not anticipated: if his price was silence, then silence was the one currency I still owned outright, and I intended to spend it loudly.

* * *

Anneli found me an hour later in the laundry, wringing sheets that were already dry. She took the wringer handle from my hands without comment, worked it twice, and said, "Either tell me or break the machine. Choose quickly."

So I told her. All of it—the offer, the papers, the word *cough syrup*, the drawer he meant to lock the truth inside. She listened the way she listens, without moving her face, and when I finished she was quiet for the length of two more sheets.

"I told you," she said at last. "Men like that don't want the war to have been wrong. They only want to have been on the right side of it." She folded a sheet with sharp, economical motions. "My brothers' names are in your book, Freya. Two of them. If you hand that book to anyone—colonel or lieutenant—and it disappears into a drawer, then they died twice, and the second death lasts longer."

"I know."

"Then say what you're going to do, because you have the face of a woman who has already decided and is merely waiting for permission."

I hadn't known until she said it. But she was right; I had decided somewhere between the laudanum bottles and the wet snow, in the moment Kessler set his papers on the table like a man laying down a winning hand.

"He wants the original by Friday," I said. "So Friday he receives an original. My hand, my ink, my pages—" I took a breath. "Every name true, and every date wrong by a day, and every report quoted just crooked enough that when he lays it beside the real files, it indicts him instead of Brandt. A forgery of my own ledger. Let him keep his leverage in a locked drawer and discover, whenever he finally opens it, that he has been sitting on a bomb with his own fingerprints all over it."

Anneli stopped folding. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something she almost never did—she laughed, briefly, low, like a match struck in a cellar.

"And the real one?"

"The real one goes where he isn't looking. Which means," I said, "that everything now depends on Jonas's map, and on how fast a boy with new boots can walk, and on whether the ferryman keeps his patience past Thursday."

She handed the last sheet to me, folded, and her voice went flat and serious again. "Then you'd better tell Jonas tonight. Because I heard something at supper you won't like—the garrison's been posting extra men at the customs house since morning. Double watch, starting tomorrow."

Tomorrow. Not New Year's Eve. Tomorrow.

I put the sheet down on the pile and felt the whole careful architecture of our plan shift under me like ice at thaw, and somewhere down the corridor a child's voice was singing a marching song wrong on purpose to make the soldiers laugh, and outside, the snow had begun again, filling in every footprint we'd left.
