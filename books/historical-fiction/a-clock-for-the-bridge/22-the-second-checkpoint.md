## 22. The Second Checkpoint

There is a particular way a customs house smells in winter: coal smoke and wet wool and the sour breath of men who have been standing too long in one place, being paid too little to care and just enough to pretend otherwise. The customs house on the river road smelled of all three that morning, with an undertone of tallow candles gone soft in the heat of the stove, and I walked into it carrying a blanket full of treason and tried to look like a woman thinking only of her feet.

Which was not difficult. My feet were genuinely the thing I thought about most.

The blanket was Anneli's work, though she'd have denied the artistry. She had a seamstress's patience and a conspirator's hands—she'd unpicked the hem of the grey wool blanket three nights earlier while I sat across from her pretending to inventory laudanum, and sewn forty-one pages of the ledger's copy inside it, folded into strips no wider than my finger. Forty-one names. Eleven of them belonged to men erased after the failed advance in October; the rest went back two years, back to entries made when my hand was steadier and my anger newer. Jonas had drawn me the customs house interior twice, once in ink and once in charcoal from memory, marking where the tables stood, where the stove stood, where the inspector with the bad eye liked to warm himself at midmorning.

"The bad eye is your friend," Jonas had said. "He looks at everything twice because he can't trust either look. By the second look he's bored."

"And the other one?"

"Brandt's man rotates through every third week. Young. Gloves. He'll pat down anyone who looks like they're hiding something, and he'll enjoy it." Jonas had tapped the charcoal sketch. "So don't look like you're hiding something."

"Thank you," I had said. "I'll simply be honest instead."

Jonas had given me the look of a mapmaker confronted by someone determined to walk off the edge of his paper. It was, I am told, the same look he gave his daughter whenever she proposed anything, which is perhaps why his daughter preferred me.

So there I was, midmorning, third day after Christmas, with a blanket over my arm as if I were delivering it to the hospital annex on the far side—a plausible errand; we were always short of blankets; half the city knew our shortages by heart—and a basket of medicine bottles in the other hand to give the blanket a reason for existing. Bottles rattle. Rattling bottles are the most honest sound in the world. No one searches a nurse whose basket audibly contains what she says it contains.

The queue moved the way queues move in winter: grudgingly, in shuffles, with much stamping and cursing of the cold. Ahead of me a fishmonger argued about a stamp on his crate; behind me two women discussed whether the new year would bring bread rationing, concluding cheerfully that it would. Emil was not with me. That had been the hardest decision and the easiest argument. "Not this time," I'd told him. "This time you stay with Sister Anneli and fold bandages." He'd accepted it with the grave nod of a child who has learned that adults' logistics are weather—you don't argue with weather, you just hope it holds.

The bad-eyed inspector waved through the fishmonger without looking up. Then it was my turn.

"Nurse," I said, before he could ask. "Hospital annex. Blankets and medicines. You'll want to see the bottles, I expect."

It is a strange arithmetic, volunteering the search before it's offered. Men like him expect resistance; give them cooperation and half their suspicion starves. He looked into my basket with his good eye, then his bad one, then made a small gesture that meant *go*, and I felt the pages against my forearm through the wool, forty-one little heartbeats, and I was already composing the sentence I'd say to Anneli when I got home—we did it, it was nothing, I'll laugh about it later—

"Gloves," said a voice behind the table. "Stop her. The one with the blanket."

Gloves. Brandt's man. Three days early, or rotated late, or simply placed there by a God who had never once in my experience shown aptitude for timing.

* * *

He was younger than Jonas had guessed, or perhaps Jonas had flattered youth out of habit. Fair, clean-shaven, with the particular neatness of a man who irons his own gloves. He lifted the blanket off my arm as if lifting something from a laundry line, unhurried, and I understood that the performance had already begun and my part was a woman mildly inconvenienced by procedure.

"Where is it going?" he asked.

"The annex on Kirchstrasse. We're short. We're always short." I let a fraction of weariness into it—not complaint, exactly, just the truth of a woman whose feet hurt. "If you need to unfold that, please refold it along the creases. It took Sister Anneli an hour."

A small lie, offered like bait. Liars offer details; honest people offer grievances. He shook the blanket out. Once. Twice. Ran his gloved hand along the hem—the hem, God help me, the hem—and stopped.

Or didn't stop. This is the part I have replayed four hundred times since. His fingers came to the end of the hem and rested there a moment, and I could not tell, then or now, whether he felt the thickness of forty-one folded pages beneath the stitching or whether his hand simply paused because hands pause, and he looked up with those flat courteous eyes and said:

"Open the basket again. All the bottles."

And while I unlatched it, hating how carefully I unlatched it—he watched carefulness the way cats watch birds—he set the blanket across the table and drew a knife from his belt, a small one, penknife-sized, and I thought: *this is where it ends, this is where forty-one names die on a customs table between a fish crate and a stove.*

He cut the twine at one corner of the blanket. Not the hem. A corner, where a mending patch covered a genuine repair—I had insisted we patch it genuinely; Anneli had thought me mad, wasting effort on authenticity. He picked at the patch, found old darning, found nothing, and his shoulders came down a centimeter, the centimeter of a man deciding not to work harder than his pay requires.

Then the other inspector—the bad eye—called across the floor. There was a dispute at the far table, crates and stamps and a merchant's rising voice, and Gloves hesitated between two kinds of tedium, and chose the louder one. He pointed at my basket.

"Go," he said. "Leave the blanket."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The blanket stays. You go." He was already turning away. "Regulations. Textiles get inspected separately during—" and here he named some directive, some circular number, delivered in the tone of a man reciting scripture he doesn't believe but has never bothered to doubt.

I could have argued. Arguing is sometimes the right move; a woman who argues convincingly is a woman with nothing to hide. But his hand had been on the hem. His hand had *paused* on the hem. And somewhere under that grey wool lay eleven names that Colonel Brandt had personally erased, including the name of a sergeant whose son slept in my linen store and practiced his letters on the backs of medicine labels.

Some things you abandon to save them. Some halves you surrender so the whole survives.

"Fine," I said, with the exact pettiness of a woman who has lost a blanket and intends to mention it to everyone she meets for a week. "Sister Anneli will want the requisition slip stamped, then. Since it's confiscated."

That annoyed him. Being handed paperwork by the woman you've just robbed is a special insult, and he stamped the slip badly, half off the table's edge, and I gathered my rattling bottles and walked through the gate with my spine straight and my heart going like a loom.

* * *

I did not breathe properly until Kirchstrasse. I did not speak properly until the linen store, where Anneli took one look at my face and put down the bandage she was rolling.

"They took the blanket."

"You sewed forty-one—"

"They took the blanket." I sat down on a stool that seemed farther away than stools usually are. "All of it. Every page. Under inspection regulations he invented on the spot or read out of a circular I'll never find."

Anneli sat down across from me, slowly, the way she does when something must be absorbed before it can be answered. Emil was somewhere in the ward beyond, laughing at something—one of the patients, probably; Private Halloran had been teaching him card games with a deck missing the eights, and losing to him anyway, generously.

"How many copies existed?" Anneli asked.

"One," I said. "That was the point of the copy. To move it. Not to keep it."

"So the original—"

"Is under the floorboards with the coal dust and the spiders, who are now the best-read spiders in the Baltic." I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. "Forty-one names, Anneli. I carried them in my arms like a baby and handed them to a man with ironed gloves because he told me to."

"Yes," she said. "And?"

I lowered my hands. She was looking at me with that expression she keeps for patients who announce they are dying of a cold.

"And what?"

"And you're still sitting here. And he's still reading bottle labels in a queue somewhere, and the ledger still exists, and you still know every one of those forty-one names by heart—which you proved last Tuesday when you recited them in your sleep, quietly, but loud enough that I stopped darning." She took the requisition slip out of my pocket, examined the sloppy stamp, and almost smiled. "You lost a copy. Copies exist to be lost. That's why we make them. Now tell me what you actually learned today."

What I actually learned today. I turned it over the way she meant, the way she'd taught the ward to turn a wound over to see its whole underside.

"He knows the hem," I said at last. "His hand went to the hem of a stranger's blanket without being told. Which means either he's searched hems before—many times—or someone told him to search hems this week." I heard my own voice drop. "Someone told him. Kessler knows I'm moving something, even if he doesn't know what. And he didn't stop me at the gate. He let me carry the bottles through."

Anneli was quiet a moment. "The bottles matter more than the blanket?"

"The bottles were real. Medicine, sealed, hospital-stamped." I stood up, and my legs remembered their business. "Either Kessler wanted me to succeed a little—to keep me moving, keep me believing there's a lane open he can close later—or he wanted me to think I'd slipped past him so I'd try again, bigger, with the original in hand." I looked at the floorboards, at the invisible seam beneath the shelf where the true ledger slept. "Either way, he's not hunting the copy anymore. He's hunting me into making another one."

"Then don't make another copy," Anneli said.

"No," I agreed. "We make two. And we send neither of them anywhere near a customs house."

Emil appeared in the doorway then, holding the mutilated deck, wanting to know if I'd seen Private Halloran cheat, and I picked him up—even six-year-olds can be picked up if you're determined and they're cooperative—and held him hard enough that he complained, which is the correct amount. Over his head I looked at Anneli, and she looked back, and neither of us said the obvious thing aloud: that the blanket was sitting on a customs table, or already in an inspector's locker, and that sometime soon—tonight, tomorrow—a gloved hand would take a knife to a hem and find forty-one names folded like a moth's wings, and read them, and understand exactly who had written them and exactly whom to tell.

We had won our partial victory and mailed its receipt to the enemy.

That evening Jonas came, summoned by a boy from the laundry who knows which doors to knock on. I showed him the stamped requisition slip and described Gloves in detail—the neatness, the knife, the hand on the hem—and Jonas listened with his eyes closed, mapping me as I spoke.

"Not the garrison gate, then," he said when I finished. "Not the bridge road. If they know your textiles, they'll know your face by Friday. You'll need a route nobody inspects because nobody expects anything to cross there." He opened his eyes. "How do you feel about ice?"

And before I could answer, the ward door opened and one of the orderlies leaned in, breathless from the stairs, and said that there was a man at the front entrance asking for the head nurse—a civilian, unshoveled boots, and he wouldn't leave a name, only a message: *tell her the ferryman's price has changed.*
