## 23. Anneli Covers the Ward

There is a particular kind of paper that frightens me more than gunfire ever did.

I learned this late in life, which I resent. In my thirties I believed fear came wearing uniforms and carrying rifles. Now I know it comes on a Tuesday afternoon, folded into your apron pocket, smelling of cheap ink, waiting for you to pick up a pen.

The notice lay on my desk between the linen inventory and a letter from the supply office complaining—again—about our requisitions of carbolic soap, as though cleanliness were a personal indulgence of mine. The notice was from the medical directorate. Standard form. The kind that crossed this desk forty times a winter: quarantine declarations, fever classifications, transfer authorizations. Paper with the power to open doors and close them. Paper that had never once lied to anyone, because paper doesn't have to. People do the lying on its behalf, in their own handwriting, which is then filed away as truth.

I had been looking at it for eleven minutes.

I want that on the record somewhere, if there is a record after all this: Sister Anneli Sørensen, head nurse of the garrison hospital, stared at an official document for eleven minutes before committing her crime, and during those eleven minutes she thought of exactly three things, none of them noble.

She thought of her post. Forty-one years old, unmarried, unpropertied, and employed by the army since she was nineteen. This hospital was not merely where I worked; it was the entire architecture of my life. My room with its narrow bed and the water stain shaped like Norway on the ceiling. My authority over four wards and eleven subordinates who feared me in the healthy way. My pension, accruing with the slow dignity of something that assumes you will live long enough to collect it. All of it balanced on the continued assumption that I was a woman who followed rules, because I had spent two decades making that assumption unshakeable. I had built my safety out of predictability the way other women built theirs out of dowries.

She thought of the cells beneath the provost's office. Not dramatically—I did not imagine damp walls or chains. What I imagined, precisely, was the chair. A wooden chair under a lamp, and a man across a table asking questions in a reasonable voice while another man wrote everything down, and the slow horror of realizing that every answer you gave was becoming a document, and documents outlive courage.

And third—this is the one I am not proud of, so naturally it goes in the book—she thought of her brothers.

Not their faces anymore; I have lost the faces, God forgive me, I kept the names instead, which seemed the more durable part. Torsten. Mikkel. Eirik. Three names struck from three different reports in three different years, each time with the same bureaucratic elegance, each time accompanied by some variation of *lost during the action, circumstances under review*, and each review concluding, as reviews do, that no further action was warranted. My mother died believing Eirik had deserted. That was the version the paper told her, and she was too broken to doubt paper. She went into the ground ashamed of her youngest son, who had died holding a position nobody would admit existed, in a battle nobody would admit had happened, under an officer whose career required that neither admit be made.

So when I say I thought of my brothers, understand what I mean: I thought of how easy it had been. How the machinery of erasure ran so smoothly that even grief couldn't jam it. And I understood that the machine had one weakness, one only—it depended on people like me feeding it clean paper.

I picked up the pen.

* * *

Let me explain what Freya needed and why, because I know she will never tell it plainly herself. She has a novelist's habit of turning herself into weather—present everywhere, described by everyone, responsible for nothing.

Three days. That was all she'd asked for, standing in my office the night before with her hands folded like a schoolgirl requesting extra arithmetic practice, which is how Freya asks for things that would get us both shot. The boy needed to be moved out of the city before the searches resumed—the laundry betrayal had reached her ears faster than it had reached mine, which tells you something about how well she listens and how poorly certain orderlies whisper—and moving a child through three checkpoints required timing, a cart, Jonas's schedules, and above all a window in which nobody came looking into Ward C.

A window. In a military hospital. During a manhunt. She wanted me to manufacture a window.

"You could refuse," she said. "I've planned around refusal."

"Have you."

"I plan around everything. It's less painful than hoping." And there it was, the whole of Freya Iversen in one sentence, offered without self-pity, as if it were a recipe.

I told her I'd think about it, which we both knew meant yes, and she left, and I sat with my ledger—my honest one, the one that balances—and added nothing to it for a long while.

Here is what I decided, in the dark hours, doing the arithmetic I suspect she'd already done for me: a quarantine notice buys three days absolutely. Typhus fears nothing and negotiates with no one. Mark Ward C for suspected typhus, post the yellow card, and even Colonel Brandt himself will stand at the door rather than cross it, because officers are brave men about artillery and superstitious cowards about infection. Quarantine also explains any unusual movement—supplies carried in, a stretcher carried out, staff in double aprons. It is, in short, a perfect lie, which is why I knew it had to be mine alone. If it failed, it would fail onto one head, and I preferred that head to be attached to a body that had already lost everything worth losing.

I did not tell Freya this reasoning. She would have argued, and I am very good at winning arguments, and some mercies must be administered without consent.

* * *

The forgery itself took twenty minutes and was, I confess, the easiest part. Twenty years of filling out forms teaches you their grammar. The directorate stamp sits in the second drawer, locked, but locks in hospitals exist to slow down visitors, not staff—we all have keys to everything, because at three in the morning when a man is bleeding, hierarchy is a luxury. The form number I copied from last February's scarlet-fever declaration. The signature I did not attempt; attempting Director Halvorsen's hand would be vanity, and instead I used the standard delegation line—*by order of, per authority delegated under emergency provisions*—which is the bureaucratic equivalent of a locked door with the key hanging beside it. Nobody reads delegation lines. Delegation lines are where documents go to stop being questioned.

The date I set back two days, so the quarantine would appear already established, already routine, already boring. Boredom is the best camouflage ever devised. Nothing survives scrutiny like paperwork that looks tedious.

Then I posted the yellow card on the door of Ward C myself, at shift change, when the corridor was fullest, because a lie announced quietly invites curiosity and a lie announced loudly becomes furniture. "Typhus suspicion," I said, in my ward-round voice, the one that has quelled more insubordination than any matron's cane. "No entry without my authorization. Double aprons inside. You—" I pointed at young Petra, who startles beautifully, "—new linens go nowhere near that ward until cleared."

Petra went pale and obedient. The corridor absorbed the information the way corridors do, half attention, full gossip. By evening mess the whole hospital believed Ward C was dying of typhus, and by morning, I'd wager, two or three soldiers' wives in town believed it too. Rumor is the cheapest disinfectant. It keeps everybody at a distance.

Freya watched me post it from the linen store doorway with Emil's shadow behind her skirts—a small shadow, a six-year-old shadow, learning already to stand where walls are. She mouthed *thank you* and I scowled at her, because gratitude between colleagues should be conducted like contraband, silently and with embarrassment.

* * *

The cost arrived sooner than expected, which is the custom of costs.

On the second day of our manufactured plague, Director Halvorsen sent for me. I want to describe the walk down to his office accurately, because accuracy is all I have left to offer this story: I walked it at my normal pace. This took discipline. Fear makes women hurry, and hurrying reads as guilt to every man who has ever sat behind a desk waiting to read it. So I walked as though I were going to discuss soap requisitions, carrying the actual soap file as a prop, and if my heartbeat was irregular, well—forty-one-year-old women are entitled to irregular heartbeats.

Halvorsen is not a wicked man. I want to state that clearly, because wickedness would simplify him and he does not deserve simplification. He is a tired, decent administrator who has spent the war signing papers he didn't write, and he looked up at me over his spectacles with genuine distress.

"Sister Sørensen. This typhus declaration." He turned it toward me. "It bears the directorate stamp."

"It bears the delegation clause," I said. "Under emergency provisions. The outbreak protocol requires immediate posting; waiting on your signature risks the whole south wing. I can show you the provision."

"I don't doubt the provision." He rubbed his eyes. "I doubt the case. Our laboratory confirmed no typhus cultures from Ward C."

"Culture results take nine days," I said. "The protocol doesn't wait nine days. That is why the protocol exists. Or has the directorate revised it since autumn?" I let the question sit. Bureaucrats flinch at implications of procedural ignorance the way horses flinch at snakes.

He flinched. Then he leaned back and studied me, and for a moment—not long, but long enough to count—he stopped being tired and became careful, and I understood I was in the presence of the most dangerous thing in this building, which is not cruelty but competence.

"Who reported the suspected cases?" he asked.

"The duty nurse. Symptoms consistent with the prodrome. High fever, rose spots, delirium in one patient." I delivered it flatly, clinically, and here my forty years of nursing paid their dividend: I could describe typhus prodrome in my sleep, having nursed two epidemics of it, and the description carries its own authority. You cannot invent rose spots unless you have seen them, and I had seen hundreds.

"And these patients now?"

"Improving under isolation, which argues against typhus, frankly, and supports enteric fever of a milder strain. I expect to lift the quarantine within the week." I paused, and then—because the best lies contain their own small confessions—I added, "I may have posted early. I'd rather apologize for a false alarm than preside over a wing of funerals. You may reprimand me formally if the regulations require it."

And there, gentlemen and ladies, is the trick I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me: offer the small sin to hide the large one. A reprimand for excessive caution is a paper wound; it heals into a commendation the moment anyone looks back at it. Halvorsen actually smiled, wearily, the smile of a man grateful to be dealing with mere overzealousness in a year that had shown him so much worse.

"A verbal note, then. Keep your protocols current, Sister." He signed something else entirely, some other matter, and I collected my soap file and walked out at my normal pace, and in the corridor I permitted myself exactly four seconds of shaking before folding it away, because the ward round started in six minutes and Sister Anneli Sørensen does not shake on ward rounds.

* * *

That night I went down to Ward C after lights-out, ostensibly to check the isolation patients—there were two genuine fevers in there, which had made the whole construction sturdier; I recommend always building your lies on a foundation of truth—and found Freya sitting on an empty bed with Emil asleep against her shoulder, his boots on the floor beside her, laced together by their strings the way children lace things, hopefully, as if boots might wander off on their own.

"He ate everything at supper," she whispered. "Even the barley. He said it tasted like wallpaper but he ate it."

"Good. Wallpaper builds character." I checked the fevers, adjusted a blanket, performed the small liturgy of night rounds. Then I said, without turning around, because saying it facing her would have made it heavier than it needed to be: "The quarantine lifts Friday morning. Whatever Jonas has arranged, it happens Thursday night or it doesn't happen."

"I know. He says the customs house guard rotates at eleven."

"Then eleven it is." I straightened, and finally looked at her. She was watching me with that expression she has—the one that takes your sacrifice and holds it up to the light looking for the flaw, the invoice, the price tag. "Stop it," I said.

"Stop what?"

"Auditing me. I'm not doing this for absolution, and I'm not doing it for you specifically, and I'm certainly not doing it because I'm brave. I'm doing it because I once believed three pieces of official paper about three boys who deserved better, and belief is a debt like any other." Emil stirred against her shoulder and settled. In sleep his face went soft and terribly young, and I saw—God help me—I saw Torsten at seven, before everything, before any of it. "Besides," I added, because sentiment unattended curdles, "you'll ruin it all. You're a terrible liar, Freya. Your face announces everything. It's why I handle the paperwork."

Something crossed her face then that might have been a smile, quickly governed, the way she governs everything. "Friday morning," she said. "I'll have the ward spotless. No evidence."

"There's never any evidence," I said. "That's the entire problem with this war. There's never any evidence."

* * *

Thursday afternoon, two days before the window opened, a clerk from the provost's office came through the wards conducting what he called a routine census of admissions. Routine censuses are not routine. They are what happens when someone upstairs wants numbers that match other numbers, and I escorted him personally, cheerful as a parish visitor, steering him past Ward C with a brisk "typhus, you'll want to note it and move along, the quarantine protocols prohibit lingering"—and he lingered exactly as long as fear permitted, which was not at all.

But at the corridor's end he paused at the noticeboard, where the general orders hang, and read something twice, and made a small mark in his notebook, and I felt the temperature of the day change.

After he'd gone I read the board myself. Most of it was the usual sediment—fuel rationing, chapel hours—but pinned at the bottom, fresh ink, was an amendment to the New Year's arrangements. The ceremonial burning of the old registry archives—the great century-end bonfire on the far bank, the one the whole town talked about with that mixture of reverence and appetite people reserve for sanctioned destruction—had been advanced.

Moved up. From the thirty-first to the thirtieth. One day earlier, by order of the garrison commandant, reasons unspecified, effective immediately.

I stood in front of that notice for a long moment, longer than a head nurse reading fuel rationing ought to stand anywhere, and I thought about what burns in those archives. Discharge files. Old regimental rolls. The paper ghosts of men whose names the current regime would prefer to keep dead—including, if Jonas's intelligence was right, the file that could prove a certain sergeant had been blamed for a colonel's blunder. Including, perhaps, the mother's trail. Everything Freya was racing toward, scheduled now to end a day before her race did.

They hadn't done it randomly. Orders like that come from ambition, and ambition in this garrison wears a colonel's coat and has recently suffered a nurse-shaped irritation it cannot locate.

I took the notice down under pretense of replacing a curled pin, memorized it, and repinned it. Then I went to find Freya and tell her that the calendar had just been cut out from under us—and found her already standing at the ward window, pale, watching smoke rise from the direction of the commandant's offices, holding a scrap of paper that someone had slipped her, on which, in a clerk's hurried hand, the new date was written and underlined twice.

"They've moved it," she said. Not a question. Her voice was steady in the way ice is steady.

"Yes," I said. "Which means your three days are now two, and Thursday is tonight."
