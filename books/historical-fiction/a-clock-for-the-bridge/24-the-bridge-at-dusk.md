## 24. The Bridge at Dusk

Jonas had drawn me three maps, and I had memorized two of them and burned one, which felt wasteful until I reminded myself that paper with your escape route inked on it is not paper. It is a confession with a border around it.

The third map was the bridge.

I went to look at it at dusk, because that was when I would have to cross it, and because dusk forgives a woman standing still. At noon, a person who lingers is a loiterer, a spy, a nuisance. At dusk, a person who lingers is simply someone watching the light go off the water, and there is no law against that yet. Give the century another week.

I wore my grey coat, the plain one, and carried a market basket with turnips in it, because nothing says *harmless* like root vegetables. Emil had wanted to come. I told him no, and he took it the way he takes most things now—without argument, with a look too old for his face, as if he were filing my refusal somewhere to be understood later. Six years old, and already he has learned that adults say *no* in the tone people use for weather. I hate that he has learned it. I hate more that I taught him.

*Stay with Anneli,* I had said. *I'm buying turnips.*

*I don't like turnips,* he said.

*Nobody likes turnips,* I said. *That's what makes them convincing.*

He didn't laugh. He is learning to laugh again, but slowly, the way frost leaves a window—one corner at a time.

* * *

The old bridge crosses the river at the narrowest point of the harbor mouth, and it has crossed it, according to a plaque I could not read from where I stood, since before anyone now alive was born. It is stone, four arches, wide enough for two carts and a crowd and no more. On our side: the toll house, squat and slate-roofed, with a lamp burning behind its window like an eye that never quite closes. On the far side: another lamp, another toll post, and beyond them the road that unspools toward the coast road, the ferry landing, and everything I have promised Emil without quite promising it aloud.

Between the two lamps: perhaps six hundred paces of open stone, lit at intervals by gas lamps that someone, in some municipal office, had decided were essential to civilization and would remain so through martial law, insurrection, and the end of a century.

Six hundred paces. I walked bridge lengths in my head all the way there. The ward corridor is forty paces, end to end. Fifteen corridors. I have paced that corridor ten thousand times with basins and bandages and bodies, and I know exactly how long forty paces takes when you are carrying something heavy and pretending you are carrying nothing at all.

Three checkpoints on the crossing itself—that was what Jonas's map said, and the map did not lie. First, the garrison gate at the near approach, where they checked papers going out of the city proper. Second, the bridgehead at the far end, where they checked faces, which is worse; papers can be forged but a face remembered is a face remembered. Third, the toll house itself, mid-bridge, where the city collected its penny and the army collected its looks.

I found a spot against the sea wall of a fishmonger's shuttered stall, close enough to see, far enough to have a reason—the turnips, the waiting, the general stupidity of women—and I watched.

The garrison gate first. Two sentries, changing at intervals I counted against my pulse, which is a poor clock but always available. One of them—young, tall, a moustache he was clearly growing for the army rather than the army having been grown for him—took his work seriously. He stopped everyone. He turned back a man with a barrel cart and made him open it, and the barrel held nothing but herring, and the man swore about it for a full minute after passing. The other sentry was older, thicker, with the particular posture of a man who has decided the war will end before it reaches his post. He looked at papers. He looked past faces. When a well-dressed merchant offered something folded small between fingers, the older sentry examined his boots with great interest until the transaction concluded.

There it was, then. The whole architecture of empires, reduced to two men: one who takes pride and one who takes coins. You cannot bribe pride, but you can exhaust it, bore it, arrive in front of it at the exact moment it wants most to be elsewhere. And coin—coin is simpler. Jonas had said the older one's name was Pfeil, and that Pfeil owed nobody anything except a landlord, and landlords make a man reasonable.

I watched the gate for an hour. I learned the change of watch, the angle of the lamplight, the fact that the tall sentry walked his post to the left and the lazy one to the right, and that between the half-hour and the hour there was a stretch—perhaps ninety seconds—when both their backs were turned toward the toll house lantern at once, drawn by some ritual of reporting I couldn't hear from the fishmonger's wall.

Ninety seconds. I have set bones in less.

* * *

The bridgehead was harder. Faces there, not papers, and the man checking them had a face worth remembering himself: grey, seamed, patient in the way of men who enjoy waiting for people to make mistakes. He held each traveler a beat longer than comfort allowed—not suspicious, exactly. Curious. A collector. I watched him stop a woman with two children and study the children as though children might be smuggled goods. Perhaps, to him, they were.

Emil could not pass that man. Not twice, not once if the man was paying attention, and he would be paying attention, because attention was clearly the only pleasure his life afforded him.

Unless Emil was not interesting. Unless Emil was a nephew with a cold, tired, ordinary, wrapped in a blanket, sniffling on schedule. I thought of the blue wool blanket on the linen store shelf, the one Anneli had mended twice, and I thought: *the copies are gone out of it, sewn away through the customs house at such cost, and now the blanket itself must work again.* Objects earn their keep in this war or they don't get kept.

And then I saw, standing second-post at the bridgehead, half in shadow, a man I knew.

Private Halloran. Ward nine, for eleven weeks, with a thigh wound that should have killed him and didn't because he was too stubborn and I was too irritated to let it. I had changed his dressings forty times. I had listened to his theories about cards, horses, and his mother's cooking, and by the end I could recite them in order. He'd been discharged fit a month ago, reassigned to garrison duty, and there he stood, stamped with boredom, guarding a bridge in the last week of a century.

Debts, Jonas had told me once, are the only currency that doesn't rust. He meant it about maps. But there was Halloran, alive because of bandage changes and stubbornness, and I filed him under *perhaps* and moved on before he could look my direction and be made curious.

* * *

The toll house sat mid-bridge like a full stop. Its keeper was visible through the glass—a fat man reading by lamplight, collecting pennies without rising from his stool. Around him, the evening foot traffic: dockworkers going home, a pair of officers, women with parcels, a knot of boys running the planks of the pedestrian edge and being ignored by everyone official. Where there are crowds, I reminded myself, there is cover. And New Year's Eve would be the largest crowd this bridge ever sees—everyone in the city pouring toward the water for the fireworks, the bells, the spectacle of time itself changing over.

A mob of revelers is either the best disguise in the world or the worst, depending entirely on whether the checkpoints are reinforced or thinned for the holiday. That was the question I couldn't answer from a fishmonger's wall. That was the question that had brought me down here in the dark in a coat smelling of carbolic and turnips.

That, and the ferryman.

* * *

Jonas had given me the name reluctantly, the way he gives everything—first refusing, then surrendering with a sigh that suggested I had personally ruined his afternoon. *Marek,* he'd said. *Runs the night ferry from below the bridge, the old landing on the north side. We go back years. I mapped the channel for him when the silting nearly put him aground, and I never sent him the bill. He'll grumble. Grumbling is how he says yes.*

The old landing was a plank stair down the embankment, ice-slicked, with a boat tied at the bottom that looked like it had been built the same decade as the bridge and maintained with roughly the same philosophy. A man sat in it, mending a net, wearing enough layers to constitute a small building. Grey beard, hands like tree roots, eyes that took my measure from the top of the stair and found it wanting.

"Marek?"

"Nobody," he said, which I took to mean yes.

"Jonas Ahlqvist sent me."

"I owe Jonas nothing."

"He said you'd say that."

"He would know." The needle paused. "What does the mapmaker want now? I already voted how he told me."

I came down the stair carefully, one step at a time, holding the rail with my free hand, keeping the basket level. Turnips are surprisingly load-bearing. I stood at the edge of the planks and did not step into the boat, because I had learned in the ward that you do not lean over a man who has decided to be difficult; you wait where he can see your whole face.

"He wants nothing," I said. "I do."

"Then Jonas sent you because he owes *you*, not the other way round." Marek snorted, a sound like ice cracking. "That man keeps accounts in his head worse than a customs clerk. Who are you, then? What do you want ferried?"

"A woman and a child. Tomorrow night. Across, after midnight, without passing the toll house."

"After midnight tomorrow." He resumed his mending with great violence. "New Year's Eve. You want me on black water, in the dark, on the one night every drunk in the city is throwing fireworks at my head, moving a woman and a child past three checkpoints—" He stopped. Looked up. Really looked, this time, and I watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes: the coat, the hands, the way I scanned the embankment top before speaking. "You're her," he said. "The nurse. Jonas's nurse."

"I'm a nurse," I said. "Whether I'm anyone's is a matter of accounting."

"Jonas's girl was sick all winter. Fevered near to death. He talks about the nurse who sat up three nights running and wouldn't take payment." The needle stilled again. "Wouldn't take payment. From *Jonas*. Do you know what that does to a man like him? Now he owes you forever and you won't let him clear the debt. It's eating him alive. He re-draws coastlines to cope."

"It was three nights of sitting," I said. "Anyone would have."

"No," Marek said. "Anyone would not have." He put down the net. He looked out at the water, black and moving under its skin of ice plates, and then back at me, and whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle something in his. "Tomorrow night. After the bells. There's a fog comes off the water after the fireworks die—burns off nothing, sits on the channel like a lid. If you're at the north stair at quarter past twelve, I'll take you across. Woman, child, one bag, no questions."

"One bag," I said. It wasn't a condition. It was a prophecy.

"One bag," he repeated. "And I'll wait one night only. Understand me. New Year's Eve, the patrols double, then they thin at the bells because even soldiers are men, then they thicken again by one o'clock when the officers sober up enough to count their absent men. Your window is the width of a fog. Miss it and I'm gone, and I won't be back, and you can stand on that stair until spring growing moss."

One night. One window the width of a fog.

"Why?" I asked him, because I am a fool who asks why when silence would serve. "Why risk it?"

Marek picked his needle back up and jabbed it through the netting with unnecessary force.

"Because I had two sons," he said. "Conscripted. Both of them. And I've had one letter in four years and no letters at all since spring, and I know what it means when the letters stop." He didn't look at me. "Jonas says you carry names. Names of men the reports lost." The jabbing resumed. "Somebody ought to. Somebody ought to keep the list. So. Quarter past twelve. North stair. Don't be late, and don't be followed, and don't bring anything you can't hold in one hand while holding the boy in the other."

I thanked him. He told me not to thank him, it was a debt and nothing human, and I climbed the icy stair back up to the living world with my turnips and my hammering heart.

* * *

I stayed a while longer at the fishmonger's wall, because leaving immediately felt like confessing, and I watched the bridge fill with its last honest traffic of the year. Lamplight on wet stone. The toll keeper's penny-clink. Somewhere on the water below me, a man with two missing sons and one net was pretending he hadn't just agreed to become a criminal.

Hope, I have learned, is not a feeling. Feelings are what you get afterward. Hope is an arithmetic: ninety seconds at the gate, one collector at the bridgehead who must find us dull, one toll house best avoided entirely, one fog, one stair, one quarter-hour, one night.

Every term in the sum had to hold. Every single one.

I was turning to go—basket on my arm, mind already walking back through the streets, rehearsing the route, the lies, the timing—when I saw the carriage.

It came down the hill road from the garrison, black, unhurried, with a crest on the door I could read even at that distance because I had spent a season learning to read it from ward rounds and inspection tours and the particular creak of its owner's boots on hospital tile. It drew up not at the toll house but short of it, at the near approach, and Colonel Brandt stepped down into the lamplight with an officer's cloak and an officer's patience, and he stood looking at the bridge.

Not at the traffic. Not at the water. At the *bridge*—its length, its approaches, its toll house, its gate—the way a man looks at a thing he owns or intends to own.

Then he spoke briefly to the tall sentry, who straightened as though yanked by wires, and gestured up the hill, and two more figures came down out of the dark with lanterns and began—of all things, on the last days of the year—to measure. Pacing the span. Counting. Marking something in a book.

Brandt stood in the middle of his lamplight with his hands clasped behind him, and though the distance was too far and the wind was wrong and it is the kind of thing a frightened woman imagines, I will swear to my grave that his eyes traveled along the fishmonger's row where I stood, unhurried, the way a man runs his finger along a shelf, and stopped—or almost stopped—on the turnip woman in the grey coat.

He turned away. The carriage door closed. The measuring party worked on into the dark.

I walked home the long way, by streets Jonas would approve of, and by the time I reached the hospital my hope had acquired a new weight and a new deadline, and I climbed the stairs to the ward with one sentence beating in me like a pulse:

Whatever he is planning for that bridge—he is planning it for tomorrow, too.
