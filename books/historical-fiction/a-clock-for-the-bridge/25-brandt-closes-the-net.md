## 25. Brandt Closes the Net

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept desk, and I have never been ashamed of it. Some men require brandy, or hunting dogs, or the company of actresses who laugh at the correct moments. I require a clean blotter, three pens arranged by nib width, and reports that arrive on time. Give me those, and I can forgive the century almost anything.

The century, as it happened, was not in a forgiving mood either way.

It was the twenty-ninth of December. Snow had been falling since Tuesday with the persistence of a subordinate who wants something and will not say what. I stood at my window in the garrison headquarters and watched it bury the parade ground one centimeter at a time, and I thought: there is a lesson in that. Everything is buried eventually. The only question of any consequence is whether you are holding the shovel or underneath it.

On my desk lay the casualty reconciliation for the autumn campaign — the corrected version, not the sentimental one. Forty-one pages. Eleven men unaccounted for following the action at the eastern ridge, which is the phrase we used in the documents; in plainer language, eleven men had died because I ordered an advance across ground my cartographers had mapped incorrectly, and because admitting the error would have cost more than the men were worth. This sounds monstrous when said plainly. That is why no one says anything plainly. Language is the first fortification of every army.

The reconciliation had a defect. A small one. A sergeant's name kept surfacing in the auxiliary files like a cork you push under water in one basin only to watch it rise in another. Sergeant Aldric Voss. Dead, according to the ridge report; missing, according to the muster; survived long enough to sign a requisition, according to a quartermaster's ledger nobody had thought to purge. And now — this was the interesting part — a dying private in the garrison hospital had murmured the name aloud in front of orderlies, and one of the orderlies, a conscientious boy named Pell, had done his duty and reported it.

I do not blame the dead private. Men dying of their wounds say all sorts of things — women's names, mothers' names, prayers in dialects they claimed not to speak. What interested me was not that Voss's name had been spoken, but where it had been spoken. The hospital. My hospital, in the sense that everything in this city was mine insofar as it might embarrass me.

I sat down, chose the middle pen, and made a list. I find lists clarify the mind the way cold water clarifies the skin.

*Item one.* Determine how many members of the hospital staff had access to personnel records. Answer, per the attached roster: forty-one, of whom perhaps six mattered.

*Item two.* Identify which staff member had motive. This required less work than one might think. Hospitals are full of women who mistake memory for duty, but only one of them had been at the ridge action as a volunteer nurse — a Miss Freya Iversen, whose file showed competence, seniority, no family of consequence, and a suspicious absence of letters home. People who write no letters are either dead inside or hiding something. In my experience the two conditions frequently overlap.

*Item three.* There was also the matter of the boy.

Ah, yes. The boy. Every net has one knot that refuses to sit flat, and mine was a six-year-old child who appeared in the hospital's admission log without a surname, without a next of kin, without — and here Pell's report grew genuinely useful — any record of having arrived through legitimate channels. A woman had left him at the door after curfew on the fourteenth. Pell had found the note in the intake waste bin, or part of it: *forget this face*. He had kept it, God bless his literal soul, folded inside his paybook like a pressed flower.

I held the scrap of paper to the lamp. A woman's hand, hurried, educated. *Forget this face.* One does not write such a thing about a child unless the face is worth remembering — unless it belongs to someone. And whose son would be worth forgetting so urgently? Whose widow would rather abandon the boy than let him be seen?

A sergeant's widow, perhaps. A sergeant blamed for a blunder that was not his. A sergeant whose continued existence in the record — even as a name spoken by a dying man — was a splinter working its way toward my heart.

I put the paper in the second drawer, locked it, and allowed myself the small vanity of feeling, for one full minute, thoroughly misunderstood. History would call this kind of work ruthlessness, if history ever heard of it, which it would not. But from where I sat it looked like tidiness. There was a version of events, the true one, in which I had cost the army a ridge and eleven lives; and there was the recorded version, in which the ridge had been tactically ceded and the casualties modest. Both versions could not survive into the new century. I had simply decided which one deserved to.

* * *

The meeting took place at four o'clock in the map room, which I had chosen because the map room has one door and no windows that open, and because I enjoy watching subordinates stand on the enemy's hypothetical territory while being reprimanded.

Major Rehnke came first, then the provost officer, Captain Dulmen, then Lieutenant Kessler, my aide, who arrived last and closed the door with the softness of a man who believes doors are listening. Kessler had been watching me lately in a way I did not care for. Not insubordinately — nothing so honest as that. He watched me the way a clerk watches a safe: estimating the combination. I have survived thirty years of uniformed ambition by noticing exactly this quality early, and I filed the observation where I file everything.

"Gentlemen," I said. "The hospital."

Rehnke blinked. He is a solid man, Rehnke, dependable in the manner of furniture. "The garrison hospital, sir?"

"There is only one." I laid out the intake anomaly, the dying man's utterance, the note. I did not mention names — Iversen, Voss — because a commander who shows his whole hand deserves the mutiny he gets. "What I require is a procedure. The ward is to be sealed to visitors beginning tomorrow morning. No admissions except surgical emergencies, no discharges without my countersignature, no staff movement between the civilian wing and the military wing. I want the registers audited. I want every name in those books compared against the master rolls, and I want discrepancies reported to me personally, in writing, before the thirtieth."

Dulmen began writing. Rehnke frowned the frown of a man about to volunteer an opinion.

"Sir, sealing the ward before New Year's — the men's families will want to visit. There will be complaints."

"There will be quarantine," I said pleasantly. "Announce a suspected case of typhoid among the orderlies. Typhoid is the perfect instrument, Major. It frightens civilians, it justifies anything, and by the time everyone realizes it was never true, no one will care to have asked." I turned to the map on the wall, to the river, the bridge, the toll house rendered in miniature like everything else in that room. "And the medical staff are to remain quartered within the grounds. All of them. If any member of staff has personal business in the city, she may conduct it after the first of January."

"She, sir?" said Kessler, mildly.

I looked at him. He had a gift for asking questions that were technically innocent, and I gave him a gift in return: a silence of precisely two seconds, which he received without flinching. Interesting. Most men flinch.

"The majority of nurses are women, Lieutenant," I said. "Grammar, not prophecy. Proceed."

He proceeded. And I returned to my window while they worked out the details, because the details no longer needed me. That is the whole art of command: construct the machine correctly and it grinds without your supervision. Somewhere below, a clerk was already typing the typhoid notice on the good typewriter, the one with the clean capitals, because even fear should arrive well-formatted.

* * *

That evening I dined alone on trout and turned-over cabbage, and read through Pell's complete report a second time, and it was there — between the line about the linen store and the line about patrol schedules — that I found the detail which converted my suspicion into certainty.

The boy had been hidden during inspections. Twice, at least, on the day of my last tour of the ward. Pell did not say so directly; Pell is too frightened to say anything directly, which is his principal value. But he noted that Nurse Iversen had requested extra linens on the morning of my inspection, and again an hour later, and that the linen store had been locked on both occasions, and that she had stood in front of the door while I passed. Standing in front of a door, gentlemen and diary, is a confession wearing an apron.

I set down my fork and considered her. Freya Iversen. I remembered her from the inspection itself — one remembers composure the way one remembers weather — a tall woman, tired-eyed, answering my questions about supply shortfalls in a voice that betrayed nothing. I had liked her, in the limited way I like well-made things. Now I understood the fatigue. It takes effort to carry a secret through a colonel's inspection with a child breathing behind a locked door.

And what, I asked myself over the trout, did she intend to do with it?

Not sell it — she had no buyer. Not destroy it — destroyers sleep well, and she did not look like a woman sleeping well. Which left the third possibility, the one I found myself unable to dismiss: she meant to take it somewhere. Out. Across the checkpoints, past the customs house, over the bridge, to someone who would make the erased names public. A newspaper, perhaps. A court that no longer existed. A priest. It hardly mattered. What mattered was the calendar.

Because here was the fact sitting on my chest like a cat that knows it is welcome: on the thirty-first of December, everything was scheduled to burn.

The archive directive had come down from the capital in November, dressed in the usual language about *administrative rationalization* and *the retirement of superseded instruments of record*. In practice: the old regime's registries, muster rolls, discharge files, court minutes — the entire paper memory of the previous forty years — were to be burned in a public bonfire on New Year's Eve, partly as celebration, partly as erasure, mostly as habit. New century, new ledgers. Everyone in authority had understood immediately that this was also, incidentally, a mercy to men like me. The ridge reconciliation, the auxiliary files, the quartermaster's inconvenient requisition — all of it would feed the flames, and Sergeant Voss would become not even a rumor.

My plan, such as it was, had rested on a simple arithmetic: whatever evidence existed would cease to exist on the thirty-first, and whatever nurse carried whatever ledger would find herself carrying ashes. I had intended to let her run. Let her spend her strength on checkpoints and bribes; let her arrive, triumphant, at a destination where nothing remained to be exposed. There is a satisfaction in letting your opponent win the race after you have moved the finish line.

But the private had spoken the name. Pell had reported it. The machinery was stirring, and stirred machinery produces searches, and searches produce discoveries, and discoveries produce awkward questions asked by men senior to me — men who would sacrifice me to the ridge in a moment if the paperwork ever surfaced before the matches were struck. I no longer had the luxury of patience. Patience was for people whose fires were already lit.

So I did the arithmetic differently.

If the records burned on the thirty-first, there were two days for a search to find nothing, and two days for a clever nurse to move something. Too much time. Time is the only commodity that can be stolen from a commanding officer, and she was stealing it from me hourly.

If the records burned on the thirtieth—

I put down my wine glass and looked at my hands, which are steady hands, and felt the small cold joy that visits me at moments of decision. Move the fire forward one day. Seal the ward tonight instead of tomorrow. Search the quarters while the staff stands in the snow being counted. And close the bridge to unofficial traffic from dusk onward, so that whatever she carried, she carried in a shrinking room.

One day. It seemed such a small theft, moving the century forward by a single day. As though history itself were a train and I merely adjusting the timetable for operational convenience.

* * *

At nine o'clock I summoned Dulmen alone and dictated the orders in the order I wished them executed, because sequence is strategy. First, the quarantine notice, effective midnight, posted at every hospital entrance. Second, the sealing of the ward — sentries at the doors, staff confined to quarters, the registers removed to my office under escort. Third, the search of staff lockers, rooms, and effects, beginning at six the following morning, with particular attention — I permitted myself this one precision — to the linen store, the coal cellar, and any loose floorboards in the east wing.

"Floorboards, sir?" Dulman said.

"Nurses hide things under floorboards," I said. "It is in all the novels. We mustn't disappoint them."

He wrote it down without smiling, which is why he remains employed.

Fourth — and here I made him repeat it back to me verbatim — the archival burning was to be advanced to the evening of the thirtieth. The official announcement would cite the weather. Snow, I pointed out, was forecast to worsen; wet records burn poorly; prudence demanded the destruction proceed before the materials deteriorated further. Anyone who objected would be invited to stand in the square and explain to the crowd why the old regime's papers deserved better treatment than the soldiers who had died producing them.

"And the bridge, sir?"

"Closed to unauthorized crossings from dusk on the thirtieth until further notice. Three checkpoints, doubled watches. Any person carrying documents in excess of personal correspondence is to be detained and the documents brought to me." I paused. "Any person. Regardless of uniform."

"Yes, sir." He hesitated, pen lifted. "And Nurse Iversen, sir? If the search of the ward turns up—"

"If the search turns up a ledger, a child, or a discrepancy involving the name Voss, you will notify me before you notify anyone else, including Major Rehnke." I met his eyes and let him understand the shape of the sentence beneath the sentence. "You will especially notify me before you write anything down."

He understood. Dulmen is many things, but stupid is not among them; stupid officers do not survive as provost captains. He gathered his papers and left, and the door closed behind him with the tidy click of a mechanism engaging.

I remained at my desk a while longer, doing what I do best, which is nothing at all with great attention. Outside, the snow went on falling. Somewhere across the city the archive building stood dark, its shelves heavy with other men's truths, waiting one day — soon to be no days — for the flame. Somewhere in the hospital a woman was, at that very hour, probably awake, probably counting her assets: a ledger, a boy, a route, a friend or two. I hoped she was counting carefully. I have always respected a competent adversary; it is the closest thing to affection my nature permits.

She believed, I imagine, that she was racing toward the new century, that the turning of the year was some great door through which truth might slip. She had not yet understood the essential joke of the age: that calendars belong to whoever prints them, that midnight is a movable feast, that the century ends when I sign the order saying it does.

I drew the final sheet toward me — the amended schedule for the burning — and signed it. Thirtieth of December. Then, because I am thorough, I corrected the date at the top of the page as well, so that no future clerk would find the discrepancy and wonder. The document now agreed with itself. The world, shortly, would agree with the document.

There. The net was drawn: quarantine at midnight, searches at dawn, fire at dusk, the bridge sealed between her and the sea. Every exit accounted for, every hour reassigned to me. I rinsed my pen, capped the ink, and squared the papers on the blotter until their edges aligned.

It was as I was turning down the lamp that Kessler knocked — soft, twice, the knock of a man with something to trade — and entered before I answered, which neither of us acknowledged. He stood by the door with his cap in his hand and an expression I had been waiting weeks to see: the expression of a clerk who has finally located the safe's combination.

"Colonel," he said. "You'll want to hear what I learned at the customs house this evening. About the nurse."

I put the lamp back up.
