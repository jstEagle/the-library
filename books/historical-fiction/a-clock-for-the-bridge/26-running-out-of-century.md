## 26. Running Out of Century

The notice was pinned to the garrison gate at nine in the morning, and by ten it had been read aloud in every kitchen, laundry, and sickroom in the city, which tells you something about how a rumor travels when it's wearing an official seal. Rumors in uniform move faster than rumors in rags. People trust authority to tell them the worst.

I heard it from the boy first, which I should have known not to do. Emil came pelting into the ward with his boots half-laced and his cheeks red as winter apples, shouting before he'd even cleared the door.

"Frau Iversen — the fire — they moved the fire —"

"Inside voice," I said, out of pure habit, and then, because he wasn't listening and neither was anyone else: "What fire?"

"The century fire! The big one! Petersen says they moved it! It's not New Year's Eve anymore!"

Sister Anneli appeared behind him with the particular set of her shoulders that meant bad news delivered at speed. She had a copy of the notice folded in her apron pocket — of course she did; Anneli collected official paper the way other women collected buttons — and she pressed it into my hand without a word, which for Anneli was practically a scream.

I read it twice. It didn't get better the second time.

*By order of the Provisional Command, all records of the former administration, including but not limited to registries, rolls, correspondence, and ledgers of whatever description, shall be committed to the public bonfire on the twenty-ninth of December, two o'clock in the afternoon, in the square before the customs house.*

The twenty-ninth. Two days hence. Not midnight on the thirty-first under a sky full of fireworks, when every guard in the city would be drunk or distracted or both — no. A Tuesday afternoon. Broad daylight. With the whole apparatus of the checkpoints awake and sober and looking for exactly the sort of woman who might be carrying a ledger of whatever description.

"They can't do that," I said, which was stupid, because they plainly had.

"They can," Anneli said quietly. "They've done it. Brandt signed it himself. Look."

And there it was, at the bottom, that confident loop of ink I'd watched dictate eleven men out of existence. *H. Brandt, Colonel.* He hadn't even bothered with a clerk's fair hand. He'd signed it like a man signing for a delivery of coal.

* * *

Let me explain what the twenty-ninth actually meant, arithmetic being the one comfort I had left.

Our plan — Jonas's plan, mapped in pencil on the back of a chart of the river estuary, refined over three weeks of careful, cowardly, essential rehearsal — rested entirely on New Year's Eve. On the last night of the century, the city lost its mind. There would be fireworks on the far bank, bells, crowds crossing the bridge in their thousands to see the old year burned and the new one born. Checkpoints thinned. Guards rotated badly. Kessler had promised passage — Kessler, whose promises I trusted precisely as far as I could throw him, which is to say I'd measured the distance and found it wanting, but still, he was a card in the hand. The ferryman had agreed to wait one night only, and the night he'd chosen was the thirty-first, when the river would be black with boats and no one would count them.

Every thread of it hung from that single hook: *the thirty-first.*

Now the hook had been pulled out of the wall and nailed up two days higher, and nothing we owned was long enough to reach it.

Because here was the thing the notice didn't say but said anyway, between its lines, in the cold bureaucratic handwriting between the lines: if the records burned on the twenty-ninth at two in the afternoon, then everything else would burn on a matching schedule. The bonfire wasn't just paper. It was the deadline for the whole campaign. Brandt wouldn't seal the ward on the thirty-first, when everyone was watching fireworks. He'd seal it on the twenty-eighth or the twenty-ninth itself, quietly, early, the way a man closes a ledger before an audit. The search that had paused for Christmas would resume not after the holiday but before the fire, because you sweep the floor before you burn the dust.

"We have tonight," I said. "Maybe tomorrow morning."

"That's what I make it," Anneli said.

"And the ferryman waits until the thirty-first."

"Yes."

"So we go to the bridge on the thirty-first and find either a boat that has already given up on us, or a checkpoint that has been told to look for us since the twenty-ninth, or — most likely — both."

Anneli took the notice back out of my hands, folded it into eighths, and put it away, as if tidiness could shrink the problem. "Then we don't go on the thirty-first."

"When, then? Tomorrow? In daylight? Through three checkpoints with the whole garrison counting its paperwork before it feeds it to the flames?" I heard my own voice climb and stopped it. Emil was watching from the end of the ward, pretending very badly to fold bandages. "We'll discuss it later," I said, in the voice nurses use, the one that means *later exists, I promise you later,* even when it doesn't.

* * *

Jonas came at dusk, which was dangerous, which meant he knew it was worse than dangerous. He came through the coal cellar with soot on his cuffs and his daughter's scarf around his neck — the girl I'd pulled through fever two winters back, the debt that had bought me his maps and his nerve — and he spread the chart out on the linen store table without preamble, the way men do when they've already argued the thing through alone all day and lost.

"It changes everything," he said.

"I know."

"No, you don't. You know it changes the date. It doesn't change the date, Freya. It changes the *river.*" His finger came down on the chart, on the bridge, on the three small crosses he'd penciled there weeks ago like stations of some secular cross. "On the thirty-first, the ice on the lower reach is considered broken — traffic, current, the ferry wake. Nobody walks the ice. On the twenty-ninth, with this cold—" he tapped the margin where temperatures were logged, numbers that made my teeth ache to read— "the lower reach will be frozen hard enough to bear a man. They'll post sentries *on the ice.* There will be a fourth checkpoint. Unmapped. Unbribed. New."

"A fourth."

"A fourth." He straightened. "And the toll house garrison doubles on burning day, because that's where the wagons come through. And the customs house clears its files starting tomorrow morning, which means extra bodies, extra lanterns, extra everything, right up until the moment they start feeding the fire." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. Soot smeared. He looked suddenly like what he was, a man who drew maps for a living and had discovered that maps are only prayers addressed to geography. "I can't fix this one, Freya. I've been at it since dawn and I can't fix it. There is no good evening left. There's only the least bad hour, and the least bad hour is—"

"Don't say it."

"—tomorrow night. Between the changing of the second watch and the loading of the record wagons. Perhaps ninety minutes. Perhaps less. When the garrison is busy counting what it's about to burn, and the new ice sentries aren't posted yet because the order for those won't be cut until the morning of the burn itself."

"Ninety minutes. Three checkpoints. With Emil."

"With Emil." He said it gently, which was worse. "Or without you. You could send the ledger with me—"

"You can't cross the bridge at all, Jonas, you're marked at the customs house, your name is in their book twice over—"

"—then with Anneli—"

"Anneli is already up to her neck, and the boy stays with me. That was always the shape of it. You know that's the shape of it."

He did know. We stood over the chart in the lamplit little room, among the shelves of folded sheets that smelled of starch and carbolic, and the shape of it sat between us like a third person. Ninety minutes. One evening. Everything we'd built across a whole winter of caution — the test runs, the bribed gate guard and his fondness for coffee, the blanket with its sewn seams, poor Halvard at the bridgehead who owed me his leg and maybe his life — all of it compressed now into a window narrower than a surgical corridor.

"I need the exact watch rotations by tomorrow noon," I said, because someone had to say something practical, and practicality was a rope and I was holding onto it with both hands.

"You'll have them."

"And the ice. Where exactly does it hold, where does it not?"

"Freya."

"If the fourth checkpoint posts late — if the order is cut on the morning of the burn — then tomorrow night the ice is *empty,* isn't it? Empty ice is a road. Show me where the road is."

For a long moment he looked at me the way you look at a patient who insists on standing. Then he sighed, picked up his pencil, and began, in tiny careful strokes, to draw me a map of a frozen river at night.

* * *

I put Emil to bed in the linen store among the stacked sheets, under the lamp with the turned-down wick, and he caught my sleeve the way he does, two fingers, barely, so he can pretend he didn't.

"Is it true about the fire?"

"It's true."

"Will my papa's papers be in it?"

I sat down on the crate beside his cot. This is the trouble with children: they ask the question you've been walking around all day like a hole in the ice.

"Some of them, probably. The false ones. The ones that say wrong things about him."

"Then we should keep the true ones."

"We are going to try very hard," I said, "to keep the true ones."

He thought about that, six years old and solemn as a magistrate. "Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow night. Late. You'll need to be brave in a way that means walking quietly and doing exactly what I say and not asking questions even when you want to."

"I'm good at not asking questions," he said, and then immediately: "Will it hurt?"

Walking. Cold. Fear. "No," I said. "It won't hurt. It will just be dark, and fast, and we'll hold hands the entire time."

He nodded and lay back, satisfied, the way children are satisfied by answers that would keep an adult awake for a decade. I tucked the blankets to his chin. His father's face looked back at me out of his father's eyes, and beneath the cot, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the ledger with its sixty-one names, and I understood with sudden terrible clarity that I was about to carry both across the same ice, and that the ice did not care which of us it held up.

* * *

Anneli found me at midnight in the dispensary, counting out laudanum doses with hands that were steadier than I deserved. She didn't ask what I was doing. She'd seen me count that stock a dozen times; it's what nurses do instead of praying.

"Kessler came by," she said.

That got my attention away from the bottles. "Here?"

"Asked for you. I told him you were on duty in the fever ward. He left this." She put a small envelope on the bench between us. No seal. My name on it in his neat, ambitious hand.

I didn't open it while she watched, which she noted, and which I noted her noting, and so we stood there in a small silent contest of professional courtesy until she snorted — Anneli's version of blessing a thing — and said, "Well. Open it, you fool, before I do."

It was four lines long. It said that Lieutenant Kessler regretted to inform me that certain arrangements previously discussed were "no longer operable in their original form," owing to the revised schedule of events; that a new arrangement might be possible; and that he would call again tomorrow at eleven. No signature beyond the initial. Even his treachery was abbreviated.

"He wants to meet the day before the burn," I said.

"He wants to meet the day *before* the burn," Anneli agreed, "which means he knows the burn is coming early for us too. They're going to seal the ward, Freya. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe the same morning. He's selling you a door out of a building he expects to help lock."

"Or he genuinely has one door and is charging me the world for it."

"Which do you believe?"

I thought of Kessler's face, that smooth helpful surface, the way he'd said *leverage* without saying it. "I believe he'd sell the door, the frame, and the house it's in, and torch the lot behind us for the insurance. But I also believe a man like that keeps one exit open for himself, and a man keeping an exit open sometimes lets other people through it if they pay the toll." I folded the note small. "So I'll hear him out at eleven. And I'll believe roughly none of it. And if it buys us even a look at the toll house garrison's disposition tomorrow, it's worth the hour."

"And if he's bait?"

"Then I'm caught in a room with him instead of on a bridge at night, and you say I was with you the whole time folding bandages."

"Folding bandages," Anneli repeated gravely. "My testimony will be ironclad."

She gathered her keys to go, then stopped at the door with her back to me, and I knew before she spoke because I'd learned the shape of her hesitations by then.

"I falsified a quarantine notice," she said. "It holds until the twenty-ninth. After that it's ash, one way or another — theirs or mine." A pause. "Whatever happens tomorrow night, Freya — I want you to understand that I counted the cost in advance. I'm not brave. I've never once been brave. But I have decided, the way one decides to take a bitter medicine, and I would rather you went across that bridge knowing somebody decided it on purpose."

"You could still not decide it," I said. "Nobody would think less—"

"Everybody would think less," she said, almost smiling, and shut the door.

* * *

I slept two hours, sitting up, in the chair by Emil's cot, the ledger against my ribs inside my coat where it had begun to live, rising and falling with my breath like a second heartbeat. Somewhere past three I woke to the sound of the city clock and did the arithmetic again, because apparently my sleeping mind had been doing it all along and wanted me to have the answer fresh.

Ninety minutes. Three checkpoints and a river of ice. Sixty-one names, one child, one nurse, one night — and not even the night we'd planned for, not the generous drunken turning-of-the-century night with its bells and crowds, but a thin Tuesday evening wedged between a wagon-loading and a watch change, a crack in the day barely wide enough for a woman and a boy if she turned sideways and stopped breathing.

They had taken our month and made it an evening. They had taken our evening and made it ninety minutes. And I lay there in the dark listening to Emil breathe and understood that the compression was the point — that Brandt had moved the fire not for scheduling but for suffocation, that a net works not by being strong but by closing faster than the fish can turn, and that somewhere across the frozen city he was at this very moment, I felt sure, asleep the sound sleep of a man whose paperwork is in order.

At four I gave up on sleep, woke Emil gently, and began, by lamplight, to teach him the route.

"First the gate," I whispered, laying out Jonas's pencil-map on the sheets between us like a game board. "You are my nephew. Your name is Matthias. What is your name?"

"Matthias," he said, and then, with his father's stubbornness surfacing like a stone in thawing water: "But at the end — at the other side — I say the true one. Out loud. At the bridge."

"At the other side," I promised him, "you may say every true thing you own."

Outside, the frost tightened its grip on the glass, and two days away, in a square by the customs house, the timber for a bonfire was already being stacked by men who thought they were building it for paper.
