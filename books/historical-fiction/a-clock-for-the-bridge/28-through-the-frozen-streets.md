## 28. Through the Frozen Streets

The coal chute delivered us into a courtyard that smelled of ash and horse and the particular sourness of spilled lamp oil. Emil came down it in a shower of black grit, landed on his backside, and looked up at me with the expression of a boy deciding whether this was still an adventure or had quietly become something else.

I did not give him time to decide. I hauled him up by the collar of his coat—Anneli's coat, three sizes too large, sleeves rolled four times—and pressed my finger to my lips. He copied the gesture so solemnly that I nearly laughed, which would have been hysteria wearing a festive hat, and then a boot crunched on glass somewhere above us and laughter died before it was born.

They were in the ward already. I could hear them—the particular rhythm of a search conducted by men who enjoy searching: drawers slammed not out of necessity but out of appetite, Anneli's voice pitched high and official, reciting her quarantine regulations like a woman laying bricks between herself and a flood. I could not save her from up here. I could only make her sacrifice mean something, which meant running, which meant running now.

I took Emil's hand and we went.

The back gate of the hospital yard was a thing I had studied for six years the way other women study fashion plates. It had a latch that wanted lifting, not turning; a hinge that groaned if you opened it wide but stayed silent if you opened it halfway and slid through sideways, which required exhaling first. I exhaled. Emil watched me do it and exhaled too, a small steam engine of obedience, and then we were in the alley behind the tannery, and the hospital was gone behind us as thoroughly as if it had burned down.

Which, I thought, listening to the shouts begin somewhere in its guts, it effectively had.

* * *

Curfew in this city has always been less a law than a weather condition. You learn to read it the way sailors read cloud. On ordinary nights the patrols moved like slow clockwork—garrison gate at the quarter hour, customs road at the half, a pause by the fountain where the older guard stopped to smoke and pretend his knees didn't hurt. Jonas's schedules lived in my head the way prayers once had, and I confess the substitution has served me better.

But tonight was no ordinary night. Tonight the whole city was wound tight as a watch spring, because Brandt had moved the burning up, because the century was dying ahead of schedule, because a nurse and a boy had made eleven signatures insufficient and one colonel nervous. The patrols were doubled. They walked in pairs. They talked to each other, which meant they weren't listening, which was the single mercy of the hour.

"Stay against the left wall," I whispered. "Where the shadow is thickest."

"Why the left?"

Because right-handed men look right when they're frightened, I almost said, and decided he didn't need the arithmetic of fear just yet. "Because I said so."

He accepted this with the gravity of a man signing a treaty, and we went along the tannery wall, past the frozen gutter where yellow ice hung like old teeth, into the maze of streets behind the fish market where even the city fathers had given up on straight lines.

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing: it is mostly bookkeeping. Not the wild sprint of the ballads—no music swells, no convenient haystacks. There is only the next corner, and after it the next, and between them the constant inventory: boots, breath, child's hand, ledger's weight against my ribs, distance to the bridge, minutes remaining in every sense of the word. I have never been brave. I have merely been thorough under duress, which history keeps mistaking for courage.

Emil kept up. That surprised me. Six years old, up since dawn, fed on Christmas candle-stub joy and terror in equal parts, and he matched my pace step for step, his small mittened fist locked in mine with the tenacity of a burr. Once, when a patrol turned into our street ahead of us, I pushed him into a doorway and flattened myself over him, and felt his heart going like a bird's against my chest, and heard him count under his breath—one, two, three, four—the way the wounded men counted through dressing changes. He had learned it from them. Even his fear had been schooled by soldiers.

The patrol passed. We went on.

Twice we heard the bonfires being built—the scrape of sledge runners hauling records, the drunken good cheer of men assigned to burn paper in January. The new century would arrive tomorrow evening now, a day early for its funeral of documents. Somewhere in those sledges were discharge files, casualty reports, the whole paper memory of the old regime, and Brandt smiling at it all with the satisfaction of a man balancing his books.

Against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth and held by a strip of my own petticoat, the true ledger said otherwise. Eleven names. A hundred and forty more. Every one of them heavier than the paper they were written on.

* * *

We lost the bells for a while in the tangle behind the market, and I will admit to a moment—I am trying to be honest in this account, honesty being the entire point of the wretched object against my ribs—a moment when I genuinely did not know where we were. Curfewed streets all look alike: shuttered, blue with snowlight, hostile. Then Emil tugged my sleeve.

"There," he said, and pointed.

At the end of a crooked lane stood the chapel. Or what the fire had left of it. I knew of it dimly—some sect's meeting house, burned two winters back, never rebuilt because the congregation had scattered or died, and the parish line redrawn so many times that the ruin belonged to no one, which in wartime means it belongs to everyone and no one goes there. Its stone walls stood hip-high and black. One gable reared up against the sky like a broken tooth. Snow lay inside it, undisturbed, a white floor in a house with no roof.

"We can't stop," I said.

"I know," Emil said, which startled me more than the pointing had. But he was already moving toward it, some instinct of his own pulling, and I let him—told myself it was thirty seconds, told myself the boy had earned thirty seconds, told myself the lie that thirty seconds is ever only thirty seconds.

We climbed over the fallen door lintel and dropped into the snow inside.

It was warmer than the street, oddly—the broken walls cut the wind, and centuries of worship seem to leave a heat of their own. The moon had come up somewhere behind the clouds and gave the ruin a washed silver light. Along the base of the east wall, half buried, lay the things the fire had spared and the scavengers hadn't wanted: a melted candlestick fused to a stone, scraps of painted plaster with saints' faces worn to smudges, a hymn board with numbers still slotted in for a service never finished. And a wooden box, charred at one corner, its lid sprung.

Emil went to it the way water goes downhill.

"Emil." My voice came out sharper than I intended, echoing off the ruined walls. "We don't touch—"

But his hands were already in the box, and whatever he found there stopped my correction in my throat.

* * *

What he drew out was small, dark, and dented. He held it up in the moonlight and turned it, and I saw the shape of it before I could stop myself seeing: a regimental identity tag, the stamped tin disc every soldier in this army wore on a cord around his neck, the one object designed to outlast the man it named.

"It's Papa's," Emil said.

"Emil, you can't possibly—"

"He showed me." His voice had gone very quiet and very precise, the way it did when he was holding himself together with both hands. "Before. He said, if there's a fire, this doesn't burn. Tin doesn't burn. He let me hold it and everything. It has his number."

He held it toward me. I took it because refusing would have been crueler than looking, and I read the stamp by moonlight: the regiment, the company, the number. And beneath the number, half worn, the letters that spelled a surname I had written in my ledger by candlelight while an officer dictated it out of existence.

Sergeant Voss. Erased from the rolls. Blamed for a blunder that was never his. Dead in the reports, dead officially, dead so thoroughly that his son had been instructed to forget his own name—and here was his tin disc in a burned chapel's relic box, tin not burning, exactly as a father had promised his boy.

How it came to be there, I cannot say and probably never shall. Chaplains collected tags from the field; perhaps one had reached this chapel before the fire, perhaps a dying man had sent it home to a city that then burned his home and his record both. The war is full of such errands gone wrong. What I knew, standing in the snow with the disc cold in my palm, was that the evidence in my ledger had just grown a face I could hold between finger and thumb.

"Freya." Emil used my name without the honorific, as he always did, having decided sometime in the linen store that titles were for strangers. "Is he really dead?"

The question I had been dodging for weeks, asked in a ruined chapel at midnight, with a curfew closing around us like a fist.

I crouched down to his level. My knees complained; I ignored them; there are more expensive things than joints.

"The reports say he is," I said. "And I believe the reports about as far as I could throw Colonel Brandt. But Emil—" I closed his fingers over the tag, folding his small hands around it the way you fold a letter you intend to keep forever. "Your father was a good soldier. He was blamed for something that wasn't his fault, by men whose careers mattered more to them than his name. That is the truth, and I have been carrying it written down, and now you are carrying it in your hands, and between us we are going to carry it across that bridge tonight. Do you understand?"

He looked at the tag. He looked at me. Snow was settling on his eyelashes and he did not brush it away, and I thought: Anneli, look what you bought with your quarantine notice. Look what it's standing in.

"He can't read the ledger," Emil said at last, practically. "He's dead."

"No," I agreed. "But the city can. The country can. People who aren't dead yet, who'll want to know what happened to their sons and brothers. Names are like that, Emil. You can strike them off a list, but they don't stay struck. They wait. In ledgers. In chapels. In boys." I touched the disc through his clenched fingers. "In tin."

He nodded slowly, and I watched something settle in him—not peace, nothing so gentle, but a kind of orientation, a compass needle finding north. Grief and resolve look remarkably alike from the outside. From the inside, I suspect they are the same substance at different temperatures.

Then he did the last thing I expected. He took the cord of the tag and put it around his own neck, tucked the disc under his shirt against his skin, and buttoned his coat over it.

"Now it doesn't burn," he said.

I had to turn away and pretend to study the lane for a moment. The moon was very bright. The snow was very blinding. I am a professional; I have composure the way other people have lungs. But there are moments when a child out-dignifies every adult in the ruins, and the only honorable response is to check the perimeter.

* * *

The perimeter, when I checked it, was deteriorating.

Lantern light had appeared at the mouth of the lane we'd come by—not searching yet, sweeping, the way you sweep a floor before you find what you're looking for. Two patrols converging, maybe three. Either they had guessed our direction or they were simply combing every street between the hospital and the river on principle, which amounted to the same thing with worse odds.

"Emil. Up. We move."

We went out the chapel's west side where the wall had fallen flat and made a ramp of rubble, and I carried the picture of that lane in my head now, assembled from Jonas's maps and my own six years of walking this city: two streets east, then the passage between the cooperage and the warehouse—too narrow for patrols to walk abreast—then down to the embankment road, and after that the river, and after that the bridge, and after that midnight, and after that either the rest of our lives or the end of these ones.

The passage was exactly as narrow as advertised. Emil went first this time, sideways, breath puffing, and I came behind with my shoulder brushing brick and the ledger's corner digging into my ribs like a reminder. Halfway through, the far end showed us the embankment road, and the embankment road showed us lanterns. Four of them. Moving toward the bridgehead.

"They're early," I breathed, mostly to myself.

"Is that bad?" Emil whispered back.

"It's inconvenient," I said, which is what adults say instead of *yes*. "New plan. We don't take the road. We take the ice road along the bank—the fishermen's track. Slower, darker, and if the ice cracks you must do exactly what I do, instantly, without asking why."

"What if you crack the ice?"

"Then you'll be the first to know," I said, and was rewarded with a small, appalled giggle, which I counted as a victory. Humor under fire: the ward had taught him well. Half my wounded men would have applauded.

The fishermen's track hugged the bank below the road, a shelf of trodden snow between the embankment stones and the black ice of the river. Above us the lanterns crawled toward the bridge. Below us the river muttered in its sleep, thickening toward the middle but treacherous near the banks where the current ran. We went along it bent double, shadows among shadows, and I had time—there is always too much time in flight—to think about the chapel, about the tag warm now against a small boy's sternum, about how the dead keep sending us errands whether or not anyone agreed to run them.

I had promised a ferryman's debt to Jonas. The ferryman was gone; the debt was mine to collect another way. Three checkpoints stood between us and the far bank, and behind us, gathering like weather, everything Brandt could send.

Ahead, the bridge clock showed me its face above the rooftops, pale as a coin: twenty past eleven. Forty minutes to midnight, three checkpoints, two lives, one ledger.

Emil's hand found mine in the dark.

"Don't worry," he said, with terrible kindness. "Tin doesn't burn."

No, I thought, gripping it tighter. Only everything else does. And we stepped off the fishermen's track toward the lights of the garrison gate, where a guard I had bribed in December waited in his sentry box, and beyond him the second checkpoint, and beyond that—if the stories I had heard in a burned chapel were worth anything at all—Colonel Brandt himself, standing in the cold, doing arithmetic of his own.
