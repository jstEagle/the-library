## 30. The Ferryman's Patience Ends

The river told us before the riverbank did.

I heard it in the quality of the silence — or rather, the absence of one. All week I had built our escape around a single sound: the creak of a moored boat, rope against wood, water slapping a hull that was waiting for us. Jonas had sworn the ferryman owed him, and the ferryman had sworn to Jonas he would wait until midnight at the old willow slip below the tannery. One night only. I had repeated it to myself like a prayer with poor theology: *one night only, one night only.*

What I heard instead was wind moving unbroken across open water.

No hull to interrupt it. No rope. No boat.

Emil stopped when I stopped, which was the arrangement we had — my hand tightened meant *stop*, two squeezes meant *run*, and so far in four hours of frozen streets and chapel dust and Kessler's grasping hands, the signals had held. He stood beside me now, small breath clouding, his father's regimental tag warm in his mitten where he kept worrying it like a coin.

"Is he late?" Emil whispered.

"He's gone," I said. There is no kind way to say it, only quick ways and slow ones, and slow ways are crueler. "The ice must have frightened him. Or the patrols. It doesn't matter why."

It mattered enormously why, actually. It mattered because if the ferryman had fled the ice, then the river was freezing solid enough to walk, and if the river was walking-ice, then Brandt's men would know it too, and the bridge would be watched twice as hard on the last night of the century. But I kept that arithmetic behind my teeth. A six-year-old does not need your full ledger of fears. He needs the next instruction.

"Then how do we cross?" he asked. Not *what do we do* — children ask what *they* do; adults ask what *we* do. Somewhere between the linen store and the coal chute, Emil had learned to include me in his sentences, and I found I could not account for how much that undid me.

"We cross the way everyone crosses," I said. "The bridge."

He looked downstream toward it — you could see the toll house lamp from here, a smudge of orange through the fog, and beyond it the long dark spine of the bridge itself, strung with lanterns for the New Year. Crowds were already gathering on the far bank. Even from this distance I could hear them: singing, ragged and drunk and joyful, the sound of people who believed the century turning would somehow turn their luck with it.

"Uncle Jonas said the bridge has three doors," Emil said. "And every door has a wolf."

"He did say that." Jonas had said considerably more than that, over a candle stub and a stolen map, tracing each checkpoint with a finger still ink-stained from the customs house ledgers: the garrison gate with its bribable sergeant; the bridgehead with its proud, unbribable corporal; and the toll house at the far end, where the collector took no bribes at all because he answered directly to Colonel Brandt. Three doors. Three wolves. And midnight — the bells, the fireworks, the crush of revelers crossing to see them from the high bank — was the only hour of the whole year when those wolves would be too busy counting faces to count ours.

I checked the church spire across the water. The clock face there was a pale coin in the dark, its hands just past eleven.

Forty-eight minutes.

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me at that moment, because later accounts made me out to be brave, and bravery implies a choice made calmly among options. What I felt was not calm. What I felt was the specific vertigo of a woman who has spent years carrying nothing she cannot run with, discovering that everything she could not run without was already in her arms: a ledger against my ribs, a child against my side, and a plan dissolving under my feet like river ice.

The despair arrived, fully formed, right on schedule. I let it sit in me for exactly as long as it takes to notice your own hands shaking — no longer. That was Anneli's gift to me, though she'd never know it. In the linen store, weeks ago, she had taken my wrist mid-crisis and said, *Freya. Fear is allowed. Paralysis is not.* She was sitting in a cell somewhere tonight for saying such things to women like me. The least I could do was obey her.

"Emil," I said. "Do you remember what I taught you about the gate?"

"I'm your nephew. My name is Anders. My mother died of fever and you're taking me to my grandmother across the river because Papa is dead too and grief makes you forgetful about papers." He recited it flat as a catechism, then spoiled it by adding, "You squeeze twice if I'm to run."

"And if I squeeze once?"

"Hold still and be small."

"That's the whole of the strategy," I told him. "Be small, be Anders, and believe the story harder than anyone questions it. Can you do that?"

He nodded. Then, because he was six and honest: "My heart is going very fast."

"So is mine. Fast hearts are correct tonight. Slow ones belong to people who've given up." I took his hand. "We haven't."

* * *

The approach to the garrison gate ran along the tannery road, and the tannery road ran through the one part of the city where even curfew relaxed on New Year's Eve. Not officially — officially the orders stood — but the garrison commander had released half his men to drink and the other half to pretend not to notice the first half, and the streets near the bridge filled with the spill-over: families with paper lanterns, young men with bottles hidden in coat linings, old women selling roasted chestnuts at prices that constituted their own form of warfare.

A crowd is a cloak. A crowd is also a trap. Both things at once, depending on whether the wolves are looking for one sheep among many or for two particular sheep whose descriptions are pinned to every gatehouse board in the city. I had to assume the latter. Kessler had seen my face at arm's length tonight; the laundry informer had sold my description days ago; and Brandt — Brandt had guessed my route before I chose it, which meant the toll house would not merely be manned. It would be *his*.

So we became nobody. I unpinned my nurse's veil and wound it into a plain headscarf, smeared a thumb of coal grime — the coal chute's parting gift, still under my nails — across Emil's cheek, hunched my shoulders into the shape of a tired woman with somewhere duller to be. We joined the current flowing toward the bridge and let it carry us.

The garrison gate appeared the way it always did: too fast. Torchlight, timber barricade, six soldiers, and Sergeant Halloran — my bribed guard, my memorized routine — standing at the center with his ledger book open and his expression closed.

I had planned for Halloran. I had coins sewn into my hem for Halloran. I had rehearsed a widow's patter calibrated precisely to Halloran's vanity and his debts.

What I had not planned for was the officer standing beside him.

Not Brandt. Younger, thinner, wrapped in a greatcoat too fine for gate duty, holding a list. A man reading names into the crowd while soldiers pulled certain celebrants aside. I knew that posture from the ward — I had watched clerks do it over casualty rolls for years, striking lines through men who were still breathing. A list of the wanted, read aloud at the last door of the old year.

"—the woman Iversen, age thirty-one, brown hair, may travel with a child—"

Emil's hand spasmed in mine. Two squeezes? No. Running was death here; the street funneled straight onto the bridge, nowhere to scatter. One squeeze: *be small.*

We shuffled forward in the press. Ten bodies ahead of us. Seven. The reader's voice droned on — deserters, printers, a cartographer's name that made my stomach drop (Jonas, they had Jonas's name, God keep him) — and Halloran stamped papers and waved families through, bored, cold, wanting his post to end so he could get drunk like everyone else.

Four ahead. The officer looked up from his list and scanned faces, and I performed the trick I had perfected across seven years of wards: I gave him nothing to look at. Eyes down, mouth slack, a woman whose entire biography read *laundry day*. Beside me, Anders, small, grimy, sniffling — every child in the city was sniffling tonight.

Three. Two. Halloran glanced up. For one terrible half-second his eyes found mine above the scarf, and something flickered there — recognition, calculation — and then the crowd surged, a firecracker went off somewhere down the lane, and Halloran shouted "Move along, move along, don't block the gate!" and stamped us through with the rest of the human river.

The bridge swallowed us.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about the middle of an escape: there is a stretch where your body decides the worst is over and starts celebrating prematurely. The garrison gate was behind us. Lantern light lay on the frost-slick planks. On both sides of the bridge, the crowd thickened — revelers leaning over the rails toward the far bank where the fireworks barges waited, singing songs about the new century that mostly consisted of the word *hurrah* arranged in different orders. Emil began, dangerously, to relax. I felt it in his hand, the grip loosening from terror toward wonder.

"Don't look at the water," I murmured. "Look at your boots. Count the boards."

"Thirty-one," he said immediately. He'd been counting since the gate. Of course he had. Children survive by counting; it's adults who insist on looking at horizons.

The bridgehead checkpoint sat at the bridge's midpoint, where the old drawbridge mechanism used to be — a stone island in the river's center, lantern-hung, manned by Corporal Voss and four men. Voss was the wolf who took no bribes, the one Jonas had warned me about with real regret: *honest, thorough, and promoted for both, which in this army should have been impossible.* If any door had a wolf worth fearing, it was this one.

And standing beside him, checking papers under a hand lantern, was Lieutenant Kessler.

My whole body went cold in a way the night couldn't explain. Kessler — who had cornered me in the dispensary with his offer, *give me the ledger and I'll give you passage*; whom I had fed a decoy copy barely an hour ago in the burned chapel while the true pages rode against my ribs. He should have been opening his prize somewhere warm. Instead he was here, at the narrowest point of the night, and I understood with sick clarity what that decoy had bought me: perhaps an hour. Perhaps less. He would have found the forged entries by now, or suspected them, and there was exactly one place left to intercept the original.

Us.

I turned us sideways, angling toward the rail, buying seconds by pretending to admire the water. Below, the river ice groaned — a vast, grinding whale-song, the sound that had chased away my ferryman. The far-bank crowds were chanting now, some countdown I couldn't make out.

"Freya."

Not spoken aloud. Drawn, low, from the shadow of the drawbridge housing, where a figure in a sentry's greatcoat stood slightly apart from Voss's detail — a figure I knew by his stance before I saw his face, because I had set the bones of that stance myself, splinted that forearm, stitched that scalp after the shelling in October.

Private Lund. Ward nine. The boy — man — I had pulled back from fever with cool cloths and stubbornness and three nights without sleep. Now twenty-two and holding a rifle he was visibly unsure he'd ever have to use.

"You shouldn't be here," he breathed. "They posted descriptions at all posts. Yours first. Kessler has the bridgehead. He's telling Voss to check every woman with a child."

"Then we can't go through."

"You can't go around either. The ice won't hold a child past midstream — the ferryman went through yesterday, did you know? Everyone knows. That's why they're all crowding the bridge." His eyes flicked to Emil, to my ribs where the ledger rode, and I watched him decide something, watch the decision cost him. "Voss trusts my counts. I've done the tally checks all month. Give me ninety seconds at the barrier and walk through on my count, heads down, and whatever happens — however it ends for me — you keep walking."

"Lund—"

"Nurse." He almost smiled. "October. Third night of the fever. You told me if I lived I'd owe you, and you'd collect someday, and I laughed because I thought you were joking." He squared his shoulders toward the checkpoint. "Ninety seconds."

Across the river, the church clock began to gather itself. I could feel it rather than hear it — the whole city leaning toward midnight like a wave deciding where to break.

Emil squeezed my hand once, asking permission to be small. I squeezed back: *small, and walk, and don't stop for anything, not even me.*

Lund stepped up to Voss's table, loud and crisp: "Corporal! Relief tally for the south rail, sir, needs your countersign before the hour turns—" And as Voss turned, annoyed, dutiful, honest to his marrow, the sentry's lanterns swung wide, the barrier chain lifted for the press of holiday traffic, and the crowd carried us forward into the light—

Where Kessler raised his head from the list in his hands and looked directly at us.

* * *

He didn't shout. That was the horror of it — the courtesy of it. He simply handed the list to a subordinate, stepped out of the checkpoint's glow, and began to move along the railing, unhurried, a man strolling toward a colleague, cutting the angle to intercept us ten yards past the barrier. Behind us Lund's voice rose in flawless bureaucratic song — *countersign, sir, the relief tally* — buying us seconds I could feel draining through the planks like heat.

Ten yards. Eight. Kessler's breath smoked. His hand wasn't on his pistol; it was extended, almost welcoming.

"Sister Iversen," he called, pitched under the crowd noise. "A moment. About the documents you gave me tonight—"

Ahead, the far end of the bridge opened onto the final span and the toll house, its lamps burning bright, and in front of it — I saw him then, and my heart did not stutter but went very quiet, the way it does at the worst news — a tall figure in a colonel's greatcoat, waiting with his hands clasped behind him, watching the bridge come to him.

Brandt. At the last door. Having guessed everything, as he always guessed everything, right down to the route and the hour.

Between us and him: Kessler closing at eight yards. Between Kessler and failure: sixty seconds of clock, maybe fewer. The church bell drew its first breath to strike.

Emil looked up at me, and I looked down at him, and in the space of that glance we conducted the entire council of war available to us — a nurse with forty pages of other men's names against her ribs, and a boy with his father's tag in his mitten, standing on a bridge between every lie of the dying century and the truth of the new one.

"Emil," I said, "when the bells start — run for the lights. Don't wait for my hand. Run."

"Why?" he whispered.

"Because the story changes at midnight," I said, "and tonight, we're going to be the ones telling it."
