## 31. First Checkpoint: The Gate

There is an art to being ordinary, and I have spent six years perfecting it. Ordinary walks with its shoulders down. Ordinary hums off-key and forgets to button one glove. Ordinary is so relentlessly dull that the eye slides past it like water off oiled cloth. Tonight I am going to be the most ordinary woman in the Baltic, and if God has any mercy left for nurses, He will let me be boring all the way across that courtyard.

Emil stands on a crate in the chapel ruin while I adjust his cap. He is wearing clothes two sizes too large — Anneli's doing; she swore a boy looks less memorable swimming in wool — and he has practiced his new name forty times. Not Emil of the erased sergeant. Emil, son of my sister in Riga, sent to live with his aunt because his mother's lungs were weak. A story with no edges. Stories with edges are the ones that cut you.

"And your aunt is?"

"Freya," he says. "Just Freya. She's tired because she works at the hospital and people die there."

I nearly laugh. It is, technically, all true, which is the best kind of lie. "And what do you say if a soldier asks about school?"

"I'm seven. Schools are closed. Everyone knows that." He recites it flat as a psalm, then ruins it by adding, "Aunt Freya, my boots pinch."

"Blisters build character."

"That's not true. You said that about bandage-folding too, and Sister Anneli laughed."

"Sister Anneli laughs at everything. It's how she survives. Put your chin down — no, down, like you're sulking. Good. You're excellent at sulking. You've had practice."

He grins up at me, and something in my chest folds itself small and hides behind the ribs, next to the ledger. That is where it lives now, sewn into the false seam of my coat, against my heart like the sentimental object it has become. Sixty-three names. Sixty-three men who officially never drew breath, carried in coal-smelling wool by a woman who officially is nobody at all.

*Carry nothing you cannot run with.* My own rule, chalked into my head years ago. Tonight I amend it: carry nothing you cannot *walk* with — slowly, wearily, at the pace of a woman who has been on her feet since dawn and resents every cobblestone.

* * *

Jonas met us at dusk behind the tannery and drew the gate in the frost with a stick, breathing through his mouth the way he does when he is frightened and pretending to be a man merely thinking hard.

"The garrison gate changes shift at half past eight," he said. "The night corporal is a man called Voss. Fat, chews tobacco, hates the cold more than he loves the army. Your medicine trick worked on him twice in November; it will work once more. He'll wave the cart through first, then pedestrians, then he'll light his pipe against the windlamp and look at nothing for four minutes. Four minutes, Freya. Count them."

"And after him?"

"After him there's a boy they call Hesse. Young. Memorizes faces." Jonas scratched the map away with his boot, erasing our escape into the frozen mud. "Hesse won't be at the gate tonight. He's on the bridgehead. But he talks to Voss. So whatever face you give Voss, keep it. Faces that change between checkpoints are worth ten questions."

Faces that change between checkpoints. As if I had a spare. As if I could peel this one off in the alley and put on another, someone braver, someone whose hands don't shake when she buttons her own coat.

"You could take the ledger instead," I said, not meaning it, or only half meaning it, the way you say terrible things just to hear how they sound outside your skull.

"No," Jonas said simply. "It's yours to carry. Debts should be paid by the person who signed for them." Then he pressed a flask into my hand — aquavit, awful, wonderful — and was gone into the dark before I could thank him, which is his way of making sure he never has to be thanked.

* * *

The queue at the garrison gate is a small, shuffling country of its own: carters with frozen cabbages, a woman with geese that protest like senators, two soldiers on leave walking with the exaggerated straightness of men who intend to enjoy their leave aggressively. The wind comes off the river with knives in it. Above the arch, a lantern swings and makes the sentries' shadows bow repeatedly to no one, which strikes me as the most honest thing the army has done all year.

Emil's hand is in mine. His fingers are ice and his pulse is a bird's. Mine, I note with clinical interest, is considerably faster than the patient's — a diagnostic failure I choose not to record.

"Remember," I murmur, "you're cold and bored and your aunt is tiresome."

"You *are* tiresome," he says, with such convincing gloom that I could kiss him.

We shuffle forward. Ten meters. Five. The cart ahead of us loses a cabbage, and the whole queue watches it roll with the fascination of people who have forgotten entertainment. The carter retrieves it. Civilization holds.

Then we are at the timber barrier, and Corporal Voss is exactly as advertised: fat, red-nosed, tobacco working in his cheek like a second heartbeat, radiating the profound resentment of a man assigned to stand outdoors in January while other men sit indoors signing papers.

"Purpose."

"Pharmacy errand for the hospital ward." I produce the chit Anneli forged, holding it at the angle of a woman too tired to care whether he reads it. "My nephew, come along for the walk. My sister's boy — she's ill, and I mind him between shifts. Say good evening to the corporal, Emil."

"Good evening, Corporal," Emil says, chin down, voice sullen. Perfect. I could weep.

Voss glances at the chit, glances at me, and here it comes — the moment Jonas described — except his eyes don't slide off. They stay. On my face. One second. Two. The tobacco stops.

I have seen that pause before, from men reading casualty lists. It is recognition trying to assemble itself out of fog. Does he know me? Have I passed this gate a dozen times as the invisible pharmacy nurse, and tonight, because my pulse is hammering loud enough to hear, have I made myself visible?

Ordinary, I tell myself. Be cabbage-boring. I yawn. Actually yawn — the body, bless it, understands theater better than the mind — and shift my weight, and let my breath plume upward, and wait with the expression of a woman whose feet hurt.

"Hospital's got you out late," Voss says finally.

"They've got everyone out late. There's a fever ward now. Nobody wants to staff it, so they send whoever complains least." Which is true, and also a small prayer: *complain to me, Corporal. Misery loves company, and I am offering.*

He grunts. Something eases behind his eyes — not trust, but the classification of a familiar species. Tired woman. Low value. He stamps the chit, jerks his thumb through the arch, and turns to the geese with the air of a man escalating a war.

"Good evening," I say again, for the pleasure of hearing my voice sound like gravel and boredom rather than a scream, and we pass under the arch.

Behind us, four minutes early, Voss lights his pipe against the windlamp and looks at nothing.

* * *

The far side of the gate is a lane between the arsenal wall and a row of shuttered warehouses, lit every thirty paces by lamps that have given up. Emil's grip on my hand has gone from ice to vise. We walk. Not fast — fast is a confession. We walk at the pace of the exhausted, which requires no acting whatsoever, past the arsenal, past a cat inspecting a drain with bureaucratic thoroughness, until the gate is behind us and the lane bends and I permit myself one breath that actually reaches the bottom of my lungs.

"Aunt Freya," Emil whispers, "your hand is shaking."

"It's the cold."

"It wasn't shaking before the soldier."

Out of the mouths of nephews. I crouch, arrange my face into something reassuring, and discover I don't have one in inventory, so I settle for honesty, rationed.

"He looked at me because I looked afraid. And I looked afraid because I was. That's all it was. Fear is allowed at checkpoints, Em. It's expected. Only guilty people are calm — haven't you noticed? The calm ones are always hiding something worse than fear." I tuck his collar down. "You were perfect."

"I wasn't scared."

"Liar."

His grin flashes, quick and feral, and for one absurd moment he is his father entire — I have seen the sergeant's photograph, the same grin caught mid-instant, a man laughing at something just outside the frame. The dead keep their appointments in the strangest places.

We cut through the fish market, stalls chained and blind, and climb the steps toward the river road. From here, above the rooflines, I can see it: the bridge, black against the blacker water, its lanterns strung like a rosary across the gap. The bridgehead checkpoint is a smudge of lamplight at the near end, and beyond it, faintly, the toll house, and beyond that the far bank where — God willing and the devil distracted — midnight will find us on the right side of a new century.

Two checkpoints left. The bridgehead, where Hesse memorizes faces. The toll house, where nobody yet knows who waits.

"One at a time," I say aloud, to Emil, to myself, to the century.

"One what at a time?" Emil asks.

"Everything. Everything, one at a time."

We descend toward the lights. And then I stop, because the lamplight at the bridgehead has shifted, and even at this distance I can see why: they are searching bags. Every pedestrian stopped, bundles opened, blankets shaken out in the snow. And moving among the sentries with the unhurried authority of a young man who enjoys his work, a figure I recognize from Brandt's inspection of the ward — the aide who watched me too closely, the one who later offered to buy my silence with a boat ticket.

Lieutenant Kessler is waiting at the bridgehead.

And he is holding a blanket — *my* blanket, blue-grey, hospital-issue, abandoned at the customs house with half my copied pages sewn into its hem.

He lifts it to the lantern and turns it over slowly, looking for seams.
