## 32. Second Checkpoint: The Bridgehead

The blanket betrayed me.

I should have known it would. I had sewn ledger pages into its hem with my own hands, and somewhere between the garrison gate and the bridgehead I had let myself believe that a thing made with love acquires some kind of invisibility. It doesn't. A thing made with love merely has more to lose.

Emil walked beside me with his hand in mine and his chin up, because I had told him on the chapel steps that if he looked like a boy going somewhere, no one would ask where. He was shivering under the blanket—I had wrapped it around his shoulders against the cold, which was maternal of me and tactically idiotic—and the wind off the river carried the smell of gunpowder from the far bank, where they were already stacking crates of records for tomorrow's bonfire like a man stacking his own confessions.

The bridgehead checkpoint stood in the shadow of the second arch, a lantern, a brazier, two soldiers, and a table with a ledger on it. Everyone in this century kept ledgers. The difference was what you did with them.

I recognized one of the guards immediately, and not in any way I welcomed.

He was the one from the customs house—three nights ago, an eternity ago—the young one with the fair lashes and the habit of looking at people as though they owed him an explanation. He had held my medicine parcel up to his lantern for a full minute while my pulse performed drumrolls, and he had let me pass only because the parcel's contents were, that night, actually medicine. Suspicion is a muscle in some men. In him it was clearly exercised daily.

He saw us coming. He saw the blanket.

I watched the recognition arrive in his face the way you watch weather come in over water—a darkening at the edges first, then all at once.

"You," he said. Not *halt*, not *papers*. Just *you*. Which was worse. "The woman with the blankets."

"Nurse," I said. "Yes. I bring them down to the hospital laundry." This was true three weeks ago. Truth is the best material for lies; you simply cut it to fit.

"The woman with the blankets," he repeated, as though repetition were evidence. He came around the table. Emil's fingers tightened in mine—not gripping in panic, thank God, just holding on, the way a boy holds a nurse's hand on a cold night, which was exactly what we were performing. "You went through the customs house. Now the bridgehead. At midnight on the last night of the year."

"I have a sick aunt across the river," I said. "She insists on dying before the century does. Family stubbornness."

He did not laugh. Guards who laugh can be bribed; guards who do not laugh must be survived. Jonas had taught me the distinction over his maps, tapping each name with his pipe: *this one takes eggs, this one takes pride, this one takes nothing—avoid.* This one took nothing.

"Papers," he said, and held out his hand.

I gave him mine. They were Anneli's finest work—forged quarantine passes, a travel writ for a nurse escorting her sister's orphan to relatives on the coast, stamped with stamps that existed only in ink and hope. He read them slowly. Behind him, his partner warmed his hands at the brazier with the studied indifference of a man who has decided that whatever happens next will happen without his signature on it.

"This writ is dated the twenty-eighth," the fair-lashed guard said.

"Yes."

"It's now the thirty-first."

"How observant of you. Travel takes time when one walks with a child. Or perhaps your quartermaster issues calendars to checkpoints but not to nurses."

I heard my own voice doing what it always did under terror—getting drier, getting wittier, sharpening itself like a knife I could hold between us instead of a fear he could see. It is a terrible habit. It has nearly killed me four times. It may yet kill me tonight.

He set the papers down on the table and put his palm flat on them.

"Wait here," he said, and turned toward the sentry box to fetch, presumably, a sergeant, a form, and the end of my life as currently constituted.

And that is when a voice came out of the dark beyond the lantern light, a voice I knew.

"Sergeant said nobody crosses without full search," the voice said. "That's the order, isn't it? Then I'd best do the searching. You're wanted at the toll house anyway, aren't you—relief rotation. Go on. I'll mind the desk."

The fair-lashed guard hesitated. So did I, but for different reasons, because the man stepping into the lantern light was wearing a sentry's greatcoat two sizes too big for him and standing on a leg I had once helped decide whether to take off.

His name was Voss. Private Voss, when I met him; he had arrived in my ward in October with shrapnel through the thigh and a fever that burned his name right out of him for six days. For those six days he had been *bed eleven* in every report—which is to say, briefly, honestly anonymous—and I had fought for that leg twice, once against the surgeon's saw and once against the gangrene, and won both times by margins so thin you could have written on them. When the fever broke he had cried, not about the leg, about a girl in his home village, and I had pretended to be changing a dressing so he could have his privacy.

He was perhaps nineteen years old. He looked forty in the lantern light. His rifle was slung correctly and his salute was crisp and his eyes, meeting mine, were doing something complicated—pleasure and horror and resolve all pressed flat under military blankness.

"Name and business," he said to me, in a voice pitched to carry authority to anyone listening and something else entirely to me.

"You don't know me, Voss," I said quietly.

"No, ma'am," he agreed. "Never seen you before in my life."

"That's the correct answer."

"Yes, ma'am." He picked up my papers. He read them upside down, I noticed, which meant he couldn't read well, which meant he was pretending, which meant he was buying time and hoping I understood that he was buying time. His hands shook slightly. Not fear—or not only fear. The leg. Standing sentry on a leg full of shrapnel scars in the coldest week of the decade, because they were short of men tonight, because everyone else was at the fireworks or the bonfire or the drinking.

The fair-lashed guard hadn't left for the toll house. He stood at the edge of the lantern circle with his arms crossed, watching.

"She's the one from the customs house," he called. "Same blanket. Check the hem. I've been told they hide things in hems."

The world went very narrow and very bright. I am told drowning persons see their lives pass before them; I saw instead my ledger, every name in it, and thought with absurd clarity: *all that, lost to a hem.*

Voss turned the papers over once more. Then he looked at the blanket. Emil had gone very still against my side, and Voss's eyes moved to the boy—to the small, sharp face, the too-big boots, the white knuckles—and something crossed his expression that I recognized, because I had seen it in mirrors of my own: the arithmetic of what a child costs to protect.

"Hem," Voss repeated. And then, loudly, bored: "Right."

He lifted the bottom edge of the blanket maybe four inches, ran his thumb along it where the pages lay sewn flat as a heartbeat, and dropped it again.

"Nothing," he said.

"You didn't feel along the whole—"

"Nothing in it," Voss said, in a voice that had, for the first time, an edge on it. "It's a blanket. She's a nurse. The boy's half-frozen, and unless your orders say we freeze children at checkpoints as a matter of policy, I'm writing them through. You want to countermand me, fetch the sergeant yourself. I'll wait." He produced the checkpoint ledger, licked his pencil with enormous ceremony, and began to write with the slow deliberation of a man carving his decision into stone where witnesses could read it.

The fair-lashed guard stood a moment longer. I watched him weigh it—his suspicion against Voss's certainty, his pride against the trouble of a sergeant and a search and a sentry who refused to cooperate on the last night of a dying century. Suspicion is a muscle; so is knowing when not to use it.

"Your aunt," he said to me, flatly. "I hope she dies on schedule."

"Thank you," I said. "So do we all."

He went back to the brazier. Voss finished writing—he wrote slowly, and I understood that the slowness was mercy, letting us stand in the light a moment longer where no one would question why, letting our hearts climb down out of our throats at a civilized pace—and then slid the papers back across the table.

"Bridge is crowded tonight," he said, official as scripture. "New Year's crowds crossing to see the fireworks on the far bank. Stay with the crowd. Don't stop on the span. Mind the ice on the north walkway." And then, lower, almost under the wind: "Bed eleven remembers."

I looked at him. There are things you cannot say at a checkpoint with a suspicious man ten paces away, and everything I felt was one of them. What I said instead was:

"Get off that leg when your shift ends. If it opens again, find a surgeon who argues before he saws. Arguing surgeons are the only kind worth having."

"Yes, ma'am," said Voss, who would probably never see me again, and who had traded a piece of himself—for that is what vouching for us was, whatever happened next, a piece of him laid on the table beside my forged papers—for a woman and a child whose names he was pretending not to know.

We walked onto the bridge.

* * *

The crowd swallowed us the way rivers swallow rain.

They came from both banks—families with lanterns, boys with paper horns, old men arm in arm, girls with ribbons in the colors of whichever future they believed in—and they all flowed toward the middle of the span, toward the far bank, toward the fireworks that would greet the new century at midnight. Emil and I moved inside that current like two leaves, and for a hundred meters I allowed myself the luxury of being nobody: a nurse, a nephew, a blanket, a blur. Anonymity, which I had spent a decade building like a wall around myself, was suddenly the widest-open door in the world, and I walked through it holding a child's hand.

Then, halfway across, Emil stopped.

"Freya." He never called me anything but Freya. We had agreed that *Aunt* was for guards and strangers; between ourselves, truth. "Someone's following us."

"Don't turn around," I said. "Walk. Tell me without turning."

"A tall man. Gray coat. He was at the checkpoint but he didn't go through it. He came down the river path."

My spine performed a slow, cold arithmetic. The fair-lashed guard had gone for the sergeant after all—or someone had gone for someone, and whoever it was had chosen the river path rather than the bridge gate, which meant he expected us on the bridge but did not want to be seen following us by the men at either end. That was not checkpoint business. That was private business.

"Is he gaining?"

"No. He's walking slow. Like he wants us to keep walking."

Which was worse. A pursuer who hurries wants to catch you. A pursuer who strolls wants you to arrive somewhere—somewhere he already knows about, somewhere ahead. Ahead lay one thing only: the third checkpoint, the toll house, the last door between us and the far bank and the ferryman who might or might not still be waiting.

"Emil," I said. "Do you remember what I told you about the crowd?"

"Stay in it. Crowds are coats."

"Crowds are coats. Put yours on." I swung him up onto my hip—he was heavier than a year ago, lighter than he'd be next year, a boy balanced precisely on the fulcrum of this one impossible night—and pushed deeper into the press of bodies, into the noise of horns and singing, into the smell of lantern oil and powder smoke drifting off the water. Behind us, unhurried as Sunday, the gray coat came on.

Ahead, the toll house lamps burned yellow at the far bridgehead, and beneath those lamps, unmistakable even at this distance even in this crowd, stood a figure in an officer's greatcoat who was not watching the crowd at all.

He was watching us.

Colonel Brandt had not guessed my route. He had known it. He had been waiting at the end of the night the way midnight itself waits—inevitable, punctual, holding its hand out for everything I had.

The church bells, somewhere behind us in the city, drew their first breath to strike.
