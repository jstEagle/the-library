## 34. Midnight on the Bridge

The toll house smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Colonel Brandt sat inside it like a man who had never once in his life been cold, because discomfort was for other people.

He had a lamp lit on the desk. He had a chair waiting. He had, I noticed with the part of my mind that had gone strangely calm—the part that counted bandages and pulses while everything else screamed—two soldiers posted outside the door and a third warming his hands at the little stove. He had expected me. He had *prepared* for me. There is something almost flattering about being worth preparation to a man like that.

"Fräulein Iversen." He said my name the way he said everything: as though reading it from an inventory. "Sit down."

"I'll stand, thank you." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Emil's hand was in mine, small and freezing and gripping hard enough to hurt. Good. Hurt meant awake. Awake meant we could still run if it came to running, though every sensible bone in my body knew it had already stopped being that kind of night.

"You've made things very difficult," Brandt said. He turned a paper on the desk so that it faced me. It was a list. Of course it was a list; this man thought in lists. Eleven names, then more beneath them—names I knew better than my own prayers, because I had written them myself by candlelight in a ledger under floorboards. "Sergeant Halvard Voss. Do you know what happened to Sergeant Voss?"

"He died," I said. "In an advance you ordered and then pretended you hadn't."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting a rumor he'd hoped was only a rumor. "You've been busy."

"Not busy enough. It took me two years to spell your omissions properly."

Emil shifted against my leg. I put my hand flat on the back of his head, the way you quiet a bird. Brandt looked at the boy for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I had done in a linen store weeks ago, the terrible simple sum of a child plus a name equals proof.

"There's no future in what you're carrying," he said, gentler now, which was worse. Gentleness from Brandt was like warmth from a corpse; technically possible, deeply wrong. "A book of dead men's names. What does it buy? A scandal, a court-martial, maybe my resignation—and in ten years a new colonel with new reports and the same war. Or—" he slid a folded document across the desk, "—passage. For you and the boy. Papers, money, a place on the coastal steamer. No questions. No record. You walk out of this century clean."

"And the ledger?"

"Stays here."

"And Voss's name?"

"Stays where it is."

I looked at the papers. Real ones—I could tell by the stamps; I had spent two years learning which stamps were real, God help me. A life, laid out neatly. A coast. A boy who would grow up without looking over his shoulder. All of it priced at one book and one silence.

It would have been so easy to say yes that I frightened myself. That's the thing they don't tell you about corruption—it doesn't snarl. It holds the door.

"No," I said.

Brandt exhaled through his nose. "I offered."

"You did. And I'm going to remember that you did, which should worry you more than the ledger does."

He stood. He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way a verdict occupies a courtroom. "Then give me the bag."

There are moments when your body decides before you do. Mine decided to hand over the satchel—to let go of the strap with fingers that shook just enough to be convincing, because some of the shaking wasn't acting. Inside that bag, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a ledger bound in the same gray board, stained the same way, smelling faintly of the same coal dust. Jonas had found me a blank account book from the customs house and Anneli had aged it with tea and ash while I copied three pages of true entries and left the rest beautifully, damningly blank. Kessler believed he was buying leverage. Brandt believed he was confiscating evidence. Nobody believes a book can lie. People trust books more than they trust each other—that's how we got into this century in the first place.

The true ledger rode against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my coat, thin as a psalm and twice as dangerous.

Brandt opened the satchel on the desk. He touched the cover the way a priest touches a Bible or a thief touches a strongbox—I genuinely couldn't tell which—and for one long moment he simply read, his lips moving over the eleven names, the twenty after them, the forged certainties I had salted through the pages. Let him have it. Let him lock it in a drawer and sleep soundly above it. A man who thinks he has destroyed a thing stops hunting it.

"This," he said quietly, "is sedition."

"It's arithmetic," I said. "You taught the whole ward the difference between the two. You just didn't like our sums."

He closed the book. Outside, distantly, the first firework went up over the harbor—a green starburst, absurd, festive, the century refusing to die quietly—and I saw one of the sentries turn his head toward the sound, and I saw the other check his watch, and I understood that the world outside this little room was coming apart into celebration, and that celebration is the best chaos ever invented.

"The boy goes to the garrison orphanage," Brandt said, folding his hands. "You go to the cells until the inquiry decides what you are. Those are the terms of your refusal."

"Inquiry," I repeated. "On New Year's Eve. With half the administration drunk and the other half burning records." I smiled at him, and I confess I enjoyed it. "Good luck finding a clerk."

For the first time all night, Colonel Brandt looked uncertain—and it was in that exact moment that the bells began.

* * *

They start with the cathedral, always, and the rest of the city answers like a congregation. Twelve strokes for midnight, and the old year dying between the fourth and the fifth, and every church in earshot hammering out the news. The toll house door banged open. The sentries came in asking what to do—crowds on the bridge, sir, hundreds, thousands, everyone crossing to see the fireworks from the far bank, do we close the bridge, sir?

Close the bridge. On New Year's Eve. At the stroke of the century. I could see the order die in Brandt's mouth before he spoke it; even he knew that some orders cost more than they buy.

"Let them cross," he said finally. "Hold the toll house. No one leaves the near side carrying documents."

Carrying documents. As if the crowd were full of women with ledgers. As if I were the only person in the world smuggling anything tonight.

"Come along, Emil," I said, sweet as syrup, loud enough for the room. "We'll miss the fireworks."

And Brandt, holding his confiscated prize, with two soldiers and a stove and a desk full of paperwork, could do precisely nothing—because seizing us would mean explaining, in front of witnesses, why a nurse and her nephew mattered enough to detain at midnight, and explanation was the one weapon I'd spent two years sharpening for exactly this hour.

We walked out into the noise.

* * *

I have crossed that bridge perhaps four hundred times in six years, and I had never once seen it full. Now it was a river standing upright—families, soldiers on leave, girls with paper hats, a man carrying a gramophone on his shoulder playing something jaunty and slightly warped by the cold. Snow underfoot churned to slush. Fireworks cracked green and gold over the water, and every face was tilted up toward the light, and nobody—nobody—looked down at a tired woman and a small boy pushing steadily through the press.

Emil kept hold of my hand and kept his head down, exactly as drilled. But halfway across, at the highest point of the span, he stopped. Tugged me. Pointed up.

The clock. The great old bridge clock, the one that had run slow since before the war and been repaired badly and run slow again, its face lit by lanterns for the occasion. It read five minutes past midnight. It had read five minutes past midnight, I suspected, for most of the year, faithfully wrong, keeping its own stubborn time while empires corrected themselves around it.

"It's late," Emil shouted over the din.

"It's honest!" I shouted back, and he laughed—a real laugh, torn out of him by surprise—and for one suspended second, mid-bridge, mid-century, with the whole sky blooming fire, I felt something I had not permitted myself since the farmhouse: hope, unguarded, ridiculous, enormous.

Then the second checkpoint loomed, and I remembered where we were.

* * *

The bridgehead post was supposed to be manned by four. Tonight there were two, because the other two had drifted to the railing to watch the fireworks like everyone else with blood in them. And of the two who remained, one was Corporal Lindqvist.

Lindqvist. Twenty-two years old, a farm boy from the marsh country, the man whose lung I had listened to through a February pneumonia, whose fever I had broken with smuggled quinine when the dispensary listed him as expendable. He had told me once, embarrassed, that he owed me his life. I had told him debts were nonsense. I had never been so pleased to be contradicted.

He saw me from ten paces. I watched him recognize me, watch his eyes drop to Emil, watch him understand—he was not a clever boy, but he was a good one, and good boys learn fast in hospitals what a nurse carrying a child at midnight might mean. His jaw worked. His hand moved toward his rifle sling and stopped.

"Sister Iversen." Loudly, formally, for the benefit of his colleague. "Happy New Year."

"Corporal. Happy New Year. We're late for the fireworks—my nephew's never seen them from the water."

His colleague glanced over, bored, dazzled by the sky. Lindqvist stepped aside with the exaggerated courtesy of a man performing innocence, and held the rope aside, and said, "Mind the ice on the far steps, Sister," which was a sentence about ice the way a psalm is about sheep.

We passed. That was all. Two steps, four, ten—and the checkpoint fell behind us, and the third waited ahead, and my heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional in my chest.

I owe Lindqvist a debt now. I intend to spend my whole life paying it, and I will consider that a fortunate life.

* * *

The toll house at the far end was dark.

That was wrong. Every instinct I owned stood up and screamed it. The third checkpoint was never unmanned—Jonas's maps said so, the bribed guard's routine said so, six test runs said so. And yet: no lamp, no stamp of boots, no challenge. Just the little stone hut crouched at the end of the bridge with its door ajar and the fireworks painting it gold and shadow, gold and shadow.

"Freya." Emil whispered it. He almost never used my name; it startled me more than a shout would have.

"I see it."

We could have gone around—there was a ferry stair, there was the ice, there were a hundred bad ideas. But the crowd funnelled straight past the toll house, and a woman avoiding an empty checkpoint draws more eyes than a woman walking through one, so I did the brave stupid thing and walked through, Emil's collar gripped in my fist, counting my own heartbeats like a pulse I was taking.

Empty. Truly empty. A cold stove, a logbook lying open, a pen dropped across it mid-word—as if whoever kept it had heard the bells and simply walked out of the old century without finishing his sentence.

And pinned to the doorframe, fluttering in the river wind, a note in a handwriting I knew too well, block capitals, no flourishes:

**THE FAR BANK IS WATCHED. NOT BY HIM. GO TO THE LIGHTS. — K**

Kessler. Helping me again, or pretending to, or herding me—which, with Kessler, had always been three words for the same weather. I took the note down and put it in the stove and did not light it, because there was no time, and because some evidence you keep even when keeping it burns your fingers.

Beyond the toll house the crowd spilled down the far slope toward the water meadows, where the fireworks were being fired from barges, and beyond them, on the low hill above the customs yards, I could see it: the bonfire. Already built. Already lit at its edges, orange teeth showing through the scaffold of crates and ledgers and files—the whole paper corpse of the old regime, waiting for the match that had, apparently, already fallen. Sparks climbed. Ash began to snow upward instead of down.

Somewhere in that fire were the lies. The false reports. The tidy columns where eleven men had been subtracted from existence. Somewhere in it, too, were the last duplicates of the truth—records that could have cleared Halvard Voss without me, now curling and blackening, which meant that the thing against my ribs was no longer a copy of history.

It was the only one.

Emil tugged my sleeve and pointed at the flames, and his face in that light was his father's face, and I understood that we had not escaped the century. We had arrived at it exactly on time.

"Hold my hand," I told him. "Whatever happens next—hold on, and don't say your surname."

He nodded, solemn as a soldier. And below us the crowd surged toward the fire, and above us the slow wrong clock struck a thirteenth stroke that no one else seemed to hear, and I started down the hill toward the lights—toward whatever Kessler wanted, or whoever wore his handwriting—while behind us, across the black ribbon of the river, the toll house lamp finally lit, and a figure stepped out onto the bridge, and began, unhurried, to follow.
