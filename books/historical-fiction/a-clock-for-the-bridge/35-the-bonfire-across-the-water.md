## 35. The Bonfire Across the Water

The far bank of the river was not so much a place as a rumor of one — a smear of warehouses and frozen mud, a customs shed with its windows boarded, a line of poplars stripped bare like men standing at attention for a review nobody had ordered. We came off the bridge into it at four minutes past midnight, and I will tell you honestly: my legs did not believe we had made it. They kept walking out of habit, the way a clock keeps ticking after you've thrown it against a wall.

Emil's hand was in mine. His glove was too big — Anneli's parting gift, one of a dozen small treasons she'd stuffed into our bag in those last frantic minutes — and every few steps he had to shake it back on like a man resettling a loose boot.

"Are we across?" he asked.

"We're across."

"Is that the new century?"

I looked up. The sky over the old city was doing something obscene with color — green and gold bursting above the rooftops where the garrison was firing celebratory shells, because nothing says *happy new century* quite like artillery. The bells were still going, all of them, out of sync with each other, so that the air itself seemed to stutter. Somewhere a band was playing badly. Somewhere people were singing worse.

"That's it," I said. "You're standing in it."

He considered this with the gravity he brought to everything, then said, "It smells like smoke already."

"It does," I agreed. And it did — but that was woodsmoke from the revelers' fires along the quay, honest smoke, the kind that means warmth rather than erasure. The other kind hadn't started yet.

We found a spot behind the customs shed, out of the wind if not out of the cold, and I did what any sensible fugitive does first: I checked my loot. The ledger was still there, flat and hard against my ribs, bound under my coat with the strip of linen Anneli had torn from her own apron to tie it there. Six years of names. Eleven men erased in a single afternoon. A sergeant blamed for a colonel's blunder, struck from the rolls while his son was learning to walk. All of it pressed against my heartbeat like a second, better heart.

Brandt was holding the decoy by now. I hoped it was giving him pleasure. There is a particular satisfaction in imagining a powerful man sitting in a warm room, turning pages of your handwriting, finding in them exactly what he expected to find — and knowing that the moment he acts on it, he has committed himself to a version of events you invented in an evening, by candlelight, with a six-year-old asleep on your knee. Let him build his defense on my fiction. Fiction is sturdier than most people think. It has to be; nobody protects it with an army.

* * *

The bonfire was visible before it was audible — a glow beyond the records office, low at first, like dawn arriving from the wrong direction. Then the sound reached us across the water: a deep, irregular roar, punctuated by cracks as the older shelving gave way, and beneath it, faintly, cheering. They had turned it into a festival. Of course they had. Give people a fire and a reason and they will dance at almost anything, including their own amnesia.

"Is that the burning?" Emil asked. He had climbed onto a mooring bollard to see better, which I permitted because a boy who has spent a month hiding in a linen store has earned the right to sit on things in the open.

"That's it."

"The bad papers?"

"All of them. Every record from the old regime. Every report, every discharge file, every casualty list." I paused. "Every lie."

He watched the glow swell. "Then why aren't you happy?"

Out of the mouths of children, etcetera. I crouched beside him so we were level, and I told him the truth, or the portion of it suitable for midnight: "Because the fire doesn't know which papers are which. It burns the true ones same as the false. That's why we're holding ours." I touched my coat, where the ledger sat. "Somewhere in those flames are the lies. Somewhere in here is the truth. And next year, or the year after, when someone asks what happened to Sergeant Voss's men—" I stopped. Old habit. Even now, even here, I nearly swallowed the name.

But Emil didn't stop. He never stopped. "Kaspar Voss," he said, clearly, the way he'd practiced until the letters filled a whole page in his careful, enormous child's hand. "My papa."

"Yes," I said. "Your papa."

Across the water, something large collapsed into the fire and sent up a fountain of sparks, and the crowd cheered again, and I thought: *they think they are burying him tonight.* Brandt thought so too, presumably, wherever he was — perhaps still holding my decoy, perhaps already drafting the report in which a nurse and a boy slipped through three checkpoints on the busiest night of the century, an embarrassment he would bury under layers of bureaucratic shrugging. Let him draft it. Paper was his religion, and tonight his god was ash.

* * *

We stayed behind the customs shed for a long time. There was nowhere to go, precisely — Jonas's map ended at the bridge, and everything past it was a country I knew only as a set of rumors: a coast road, a convent, a woman who had once left a note reading *forget this face*. I had the note still, folded in with the ledger. I had decided somewhere around checkpoint two that I would never forget the face, whatever its mother had intended, and I was prepared to be unreasonable about it.

A ferryman's dog found us first — a shaggy, indifferent creature that materialized out of the dark, inspected Emil, decided he was acceptable, and lay down against his boots with the entitlement of the well-connected. Its owner appeared ten minutes later, a broad woman in a fisherman's coat, carrying two mugs of something hot and steaming.

"You're the ones off the bridge," she said. Not a question. "Half the far bank saw you come through the toll house at a dead run with half the city's soldiers behind you. The other half was watching the fireworks." She handed us the mugs. It was mulled wine cut with about a gallon of water, which meant it was perfect. "Jonas's friend, are you? The mapmaker sent word ahead. Said if a nurse and a boy crossed tonight, I was to feed them and ask no questions."

"Did he say anything else?"

"He said you'd argue about paying." She smiled. "So don't."

I drank the wine. It scalded all the way down and I could have wept with gratitude, and possibly did, a little, though the steam gives a person plausible deniability. Emil held his mug in both hands and fell asleep sitting up between one sip and the next, the way children do, surrendering consciousness without ceremony, as though the day were simply a door he'd walked through.

The fisherwoman looked at him, then at me, then at the fire across the water, and her face did something complicated.

"My husband's name went into one of those buildings," she said quietly. "Fishing fleet register, 1894. They rewrote it when the new harbor authority came in. One day he was a licensed man with a boat; the next he was nobody, and the boat belonged to a cousin of the harbormaster." She shrugged, a gesture that contained an entire war. "I used to think paper was nothing. Just wood pulp and ink. Now I know it's the only thing some of us ever get to be."

"Then tonight," I said, "we're the only archive left."

She laughed — a short, startled bark — and then sobered. "There's a cart leaving for the coast road at first light. Salt hauler. The driver owes me. You can ride in the salt." She glanced at the sleeping boy. "It's not dignified."

"Neither is running through a toll house at midnight," I said. "I've given up on dignity. It's heavy, and I only carry what I can run with."

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that recorded somewhere other than my own conscience, which keeps poor minutes. I sat against the customs shed wall with Emil's head on my lap and the ledger against my ribs and I watched the fire across the water eat a century.

It was strange, the things the mind chooses to serve up at such moments. Not the ward, not the checkpoints, not Brandt's face at the toll house — smooth, reasonable, offering me freedom the way a spider offers a fly a tour of the web. No. What came was small things. Sister Anneli teaching Emil to fold bandages into crisp white birds. The one-armed patient carving the wooden horse with his teeth clenched, saying it was for his own boy, who was grown and wouldn't mind sharing. The dying soldier murmuring Kaspar Voss's name into the dark, not knowing he was handing me a live coal. Anneli lighting her own false papers to draw the soldiers off, turning to look at me once through the smoke — just once, no farewell in it, only *go* — before they took her arm.

I had left her there. That was the arithmetic of it, and arithmetic does not care that the alternative was both of us lost and the ledger burned unread. She had chosen it — had practically shoved me down the coal chute, had stood in the doorway of the burning laundry like a general holding a hill — and none of that changed the fact that I had gone, and she had stayed, and the last I saw of her was her hand raised, not waving, but pointing toward the chute. Toward the way out.

The fire across the water roared. The sparks climbed. And I made her a promise, silently, in the manner of people who have run out of churches: *I will make them read the names. All of them. Yours included, if it comes to that.*

The fisherwoman's dog lifted its head, looked at me, and put it back down. Dogs, I find, are excellent witnesses. They require no oath and accept no bribes.

* * *

Toward the smallest hours, Emil woke — children wake at the worst moments, it is one of their many talents — and asked for water, and got it, and then did not go back to sleep but sat pressed against my side, watching the fire with me.

"Freya," he said. He had begun using my name sometime around Christmas, tentatively, testing each syllable like ice. "When the papers burn. Does that mean Papa's name is gone?"

"No." I took his hand and put it flat against my coat, over the ledger, so he could feel the hard rectangle of it through the wool. "That's why this came with us. A name isn't where it's written. It's where it's remembered. The fire can have the building. It can't have this."

"And yours? Is your name in there?"

I blinked. Of all the questions. "No. Why do you ask?"

He thought about it with his whole face. "Because you always write everyone else. In the ward, you wrote the names of the men the reports forgot. But nobody writes you." A pause, devastating in its simplicity. "Who writes you, Freya?"

Across the river, the bonfire settled with a groan, throwing up a last storm of sparks, and the crowd noise had thinned to scattered singing, and the bells had finally stopped their quarrel. Somewhere in the flames went the lies — Brandt's tidy reports, the clean margins where eleven men used to be, the sergeant's disgrace, the whole paper architecture of a respectable man's career. Somewhere in my coat went the truth, tied in Anneli's apron linen, warm from my own body.

And somewhere in a cell across that water sat a woman who had written me, in her way — who had looked at a tired nurse breaking every rule she'd ever kept and decided she was worth a quarantine notice, a falsified page, a handful of burning papers and a pointed finger toward the coal chute.

"Someone does," I said finally. "Someone does, Emil. We'll see that she's repaid."

He nodded, satisfied, and burrowed back against my shoulder, and within minutes his breathing went long and even, and the dog sighed, and the sky over the ruined records office began, very slowly, to consider the possibility of morning.

I sat awake until the salt cart came. I had names to keep, and a coast to reach, and a debt across the water that I intended, with the whole stubborn weight of the new century behind me, to collect.
