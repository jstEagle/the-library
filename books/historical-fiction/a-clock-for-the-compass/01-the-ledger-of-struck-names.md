## 1. The Ledger of Struck Names

The rain had been falling since Tuesday, and by Friday it had stopped being weather and become a condition, like grief, or the war itself — something one simply lived inside. It came through the canvas roof of the dressing station in slow brown ropes, pooling along the seams, dripping into the enamel basin Rafael Montrose kept beneath the worst of it with a patience that no longer felt like patience but like breathing.

She sat at the little deal table wedged between the supply crates and the wall where the stretcher cases lay in their long rows, and she wrote.

The lamp was shaded with a tin plate so that its light fell only on her hands, the page, the ledger bound in grey cloth that had once wrapped a parcel of surgical thread. Around her the station breathed and muttered: men sleeping the heavy sleep of morphia, men sleeping the lighter sleep of men who did not trust sleep, one boy near the stove weeping without sound, his face turned to the wall as though the bricks might take the crying from him if he gave it long enough.

Officially, Rafael was completing the ward journal. Unofficially — though she would not have used the word *unofficially*, or any word that smelled of conspiracy — she was doing what she did every night when the last of the orderlies had gone to their bunks and the matron's office showed only the blue glow of a banked stove.

She was writing down names.

Not all names. The official rolls took care of most of them, in their cramped clerical hand, in triplicate, filed away toward some headquarters where the paper grew like mould behind the lines while the men themselves grew thinner at the front of it. The official rolls were diligent. That was what nobody understood until they'd spent a winter among them — that the machinery of record was not careless at all. It was precise. And precision, Rafael had learned, could be a kind of violence, because precision required decisions, and decisions were made by men whose boots never touched this particular mud.

Private Daniel Okafor, she wrote. Age twenty-two. Landed at Boulogne with the 4th Battalion in June. Wounded at the quarry, abdomen, died at half past four this morning.

And then, beneath it, in smaller letters, the thing that made the ledger hers and not the army's: *Struck from the roll 14 November. Cause given: "administrative error." No error. I dressed his wound myself. He was here eleven hours.*

She had begun the ledger in March, after the first name vanished. She remembered telling herself — firmly, reasonably, in the tone she used for anxious boys before an operation — that it was merely habit. A nurse's habit. Nurses counted things; it was how they kept the world from sliding. A temperature, a pulse, a dressing changed, a name. If a man appeared on the admission book at noon and appeared nowhere at all by evening, then someone had to know he had existed between those hours, even if the someone was only a woman at a table with a tin-shaded lamp.

Habit. Not defiance. She held to that distinction the way other people held to prayer.

* * *

The trouble with telling oneself a thing every night for eight months was that eventually one began to hear the telling as a sound separate from the truth, the way one heard the shelling — distant, rhythmic, ignorable — until suddenly it wasn't.

Because it *was* defiance, wasn't it? Somewhere in her, below the reasonable voice, she had known it from the first page. One did not hide a ledger inside the false bottom of a bandage trunk out of habit. Habit did not make one's hand shake. Habit did not require the daily small treachery of closing the ward journal when an officer passed, the smooth professional lie of her posture, pen still moving on the permitted page. Habit did not feel like holding a coal in one's fist and calling it a stone.

But she was not brave, Rafael reminded herself, dipping the pen. Bravery was a shape other people occupied — the men going over at zero hour, the pilots, even Matron Calloway with her spine like a tent pole. Rafael was careful. Careful people recorded things; they did not act upon them. History happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms with maps on the walls, and her business was only to keep faith with the ones it happened *to*, afterward, quietly, in ink.

Outside, a ration cart ground past through the mud with a sound like a great animal dragging its belly, and somewhere eastward the guns kept their patient arithmetic. Three, pause. Two, pause. One that shook the lamp flame and sent a drop of roof-water plinking into her basin.

"Miss?"

She looked up. The voice came from the row nearest the stove, third stretcher, and she knew before she crossed the space who had spoken, because Sister had marked him at noon with the particular gentleness reserved for the certain cases — the ones beyond surgery's reach, moved to the end where they could be reached easily with water, with morphia, with a hand on the forehead.

He was very young under the dirt, or perhaps not young at all; the war had stopped distinguishing. His abdominal wound had been dressed twice. His eyes were open and fixed on the roof beams as if reading something written there.

"You're awake," she said. "Can I get you anything?"

"Are you writing?" He said it oddly — not *writing letters*, not the usual request. His gaze came down from the beams to her table. "Names?"

Rafael felt the small cold turn in her stomach that preceded any risk. But there was no suspicion in his face, only a kind of hunger. Men dying knew things about honesty that the living forgot.

"Sometimes," she said.

"Mine's Corrigan." He swallowed, and she raised the water cup to his lips without being asked, tilting it carefully against the thirst that morphine made bottomless. "Thomas Corrigan. C-O-R-R-I-G-A-N. Not Connolly."

"Not Connolly?"

"They've got me down as Connolly." A ghost of a smile, bitter as the medicine. "Fellow in my section. We joined up together, off the same street in Salford. Some clerk's got us mixed since the summer — post goes to the wrong beds, pay goes astray. I've told them four times." His hand found the edge of her sleeve, light as a moth. "If I don't get a chance to tell them again. You'll put it right? Thomas Corrigan. My mother signs everything *Mrs E. Corrigan*, and she'd want it right on the—"

"Yes," Rafael said. "I'll put it right. I promise you."

She went back to the table and opened the grey ledger, and her pen hovered over the ruled line longer than it ever had. Because she understood now what she was actually promising. Not to remember him — remembering was easy, remembering was free. She was promising to make him exist somewhere the clerk's error could not reach. She was promising that when the casualty list went up in Salford with the wrong name on it, and Mrs E. Corrigan stood in the drizzle squinting at it for a son who had died under another family's name, then at least one person in the world would know, and would have written it, and would keep having written it for as long as the book survived.

It seemed a very small thing. It seemed the largest thing she had ever done.

*Private Thomas Corrigan,* she wrote. *Salford. Not Connolly — struck from the roll as Connolly, 14 November. He asked me himself.*

Then, because her hand wanted to do more than record, she added: *He laughed once tonight, at nothing, and it was a good laugh.*

* * *

He died at ten minutes past two in the morning, quietly, in the interval between two shells, as though he had waited for the gap so as not to interrupt anyone.

Rafael closed his eyes and folded his hands and stood beside him for a moment in the dark that smelt of carbolic and wet earth. Through the canvas she could hear the guns continuing their arithmetic, indifferent, enormous, and she thought — as she thought most nights, but tonight with the weight of a promise in it — *somewhere this is all being decided. Somewhere there is a room where these men are figures moving on a map, and the erasing of them is done with an India-rubber and a steady wrist, and the men holding the rubber believe history is happening to them, to their side of it, to the grand shape of things. They do not know that history is happening here. In a stretcher. At ten minutes past two. To a boy named Corrigan.*

That was the belief she had carried through eight months of nights: that history was a tide breaking somewhere far off, and she a woman on the shore recording the wrack it left behind. It comforted her, that belief. It asked nothing of her but fidelity.

She returned to the table. Her fingers were stiff with cold; she flexed them over the lamp.

And that was when she heard the gate — the creak of it, the muffled challenge of the sentry, voices pitched low in the rain at an hour when no convoy came. She blew out the lamp on instinct, heart tapping strangely, and stood in the dark among the sleeping men, listening to boots approach through the mud.

A stranger's voice, asking for someone. Asking, she would learn within the hour, for someone who *kept accounts*.
