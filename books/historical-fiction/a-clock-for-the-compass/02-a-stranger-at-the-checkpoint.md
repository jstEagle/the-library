## 2. A Stranger at the Checkpoint

The rain had come in off the flats at dusk and did not intend to leave. It fell on the dressing station's sagging canvas roof with a persistence that made conversation impossible and thought nearly so, and Rafael Montrose had learned to be grateful for both silences — the one outside and the one it imposed upon the ward. Rain meant the guns went quiet. Rain meant fewer men carried in on stretchers, their uniforms stiffened to the color of dried clay. Rain was, she had decided sometime in October, the closest thing to mercy this corner of Flanders offered.

She sat at the trestle table behind the surgical tent with the ledger open before her, its oilcloth cover darkened by handling, its pages ruled in her own careful hand. The lamp guttered. Outside, a mule complained somewhere near the latrines, and beneath all of it the rain kept up its patient arithmetic.

*Private Alfred Dunn, 2nd Btn., admitted 14th, died 15th. No entry on regimental roll. Struck.*

That was how she wrote them now — *struck*, as if they were bells silenced rather than men unmade. She told herself, as she had told herself every night since September, that it was merely habit. A clerk's tic. She had been her father's bookkeeper in a shipping office in Hull before the war, and numbers and names had a way of lodging themselves in her fingers whether or not anyone above her wished them recorded. It was not defiance. Defiance required an audience, and she had none. The ledger lived inside her mattress ticking, wrapped in waxed cloth, and no living soul knew it existed.

It was simply that someone ought to know. Not act — knowing was enough, knowing was safe — but someone, somewhere, in some year when the mud had dried and the shouting stopped, ought to be able to say: *these men existed; here are their wounds; here is the hour of their deaths.* History, she had long ago concluded, happened to other people — to ministers in London, to generals in châteaux, to the sort of women who appeared in newspapers wearing hats. Rafael recorded history the way a housekeeper dusted around an explosion: carefully, quietly, without ever believing herself part of what had been broken.

"Miss Montrose." One of the orderlies, Pieter, put his head through the flap, rain streaming off his cap brim. "There's a man at the checkpoint asking after supplies. Sergeant says he's got papers but the sergeant wants someone from the hospital to vouch for him, on account of the light going."

Rafael closed the ledger and slid it into its place among the linen inventory books, where its identical spine would not be remarked upon. "Supplies? We're not expecting anything."

"No, miss."

"Well. Let me look at him, then."

---

The checkpoint stood where the lane met the duckboard road, a barrier of crossed poles manned by two sentries under a tarpaulin strung between posts. A lantern swung there, and in its smeared yellow circle stood a man who looked as though the sky had spent the entire afternoon trying to drown him personally.

He was young — younger than his posture suggested — with a face all angles and rain, dark hair plastered flat, and the particular stillness of someone who has trained himself not to shiver. He wore no uniform she recognized: a civilian greatcoat gone green-black with wet, mud to the knees, and over his shoulder a canvas satchel that bulged in a way satchels carrying bandages did not. His papers, when the sergeant handed them across, were soft at the edges from damp.

"Tomas Reiner," the man said. His accent was hard to place — Dutch, perhaps, or something adjacent to it, worn smooth by other tongues. "Medical stores for the forward station. I understand the convoy was turned back at Poperinghe."

"The convoy was turned back because there is no convoy," Rafael said. "We're not short of lint, Mr. Reiner. What we're short of is dry weather and good news, and I can't vouch for either."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Then I've walked a long way to deliver nothing."

"You've walked a long way, certainly." She turned his papers toward the lantern. The passes were correct in every particular she could check, which was itself slightly wrong; documents that perfect usually had help. "Who sent you?"

"A friend."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only true one I have left." He said it lightly, but his eyes did not match the tone. They went past her, quick and assessing, taking in the sentries, the tarpaulin, the dark shape of the dressing station beyond — a man measuring a room he intended to occupy. Then his gaze came back to hers and settled, and Rafael felt the strange sensation of being read rather than reading.

The sergeant shifted. "Begging your pardon, Miss Montrose, but regulations say—"

"Yes, thank you, Sergeant. Mr. Reiner can shelter in the receiving tent until morning and go back the way he came. That's within regulations if I say it is." She heard herself say it and could not afterward explain why. There was nothing in the man's face that earned charity. There was something in it that *required* it — a coiled, watchful exhaustion, like an animal that had run past the point of running.

Reiner shouldered his satchel. As he did, the flap of it gaped, and by the accident of the swinging lantern light Rafael saw the edge of an envelope inside — cheap paper, water-spotted, addressed in a hand she knew.

Her breath went out of her as though she'd been struck below the ribs.

She knew that hand the way one knows a voice through a wall. Slight backward slant, the crossbars of the *t* struck low and impatient, the capitals tall as fenceposts. Captain Elias Voss had written to the hospital eleven times in the winter of 1915–16 — requisitions, mostly, then letters of thanks, then, in the last months, longer letters that read less like correspondence than like a man talking himself out of despair. Matron Calloway kept them in the office drawer with the stationery. Rafael had transcribed two of them for the file when the matron's eyes were bad, and her own hand had unconsciously borrowed his slant for weeks afterward.

Elias Voss had been dead since spring. Officially. There had been a notice, a date, a regimental stamp. Rafael had entered him in the ledger's front pages under the officers' section, in ink, because he had mattered — because he had once sat three hours with a dying boy from Yorkshire, holding the lamp so the boy could see his mother's photograph, and never once let his hand shake.

"Mr. Reiner." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "That letter in your bag. Who is it from?"

He looked at her for a long moment. The lantern swung. Rain needled the tarpaulin overhead.

"I was told," he said slowly, "to ask at this station for someone who keeps accounts. Not the quartermaster. Not the matron's ledgers. Someone who keeps accounts of a different kind."

The world narrowed to the space between them, four feet of duckboard and lamplight. Every sensible instinct Rafael owned told her to say *I keep no accounts but linen and morphine*, to send him on into the dark, to remain what she had always believed herself: a bystander with neat handwriting.

"Come with me," she said instead.

---

Matron Calloway met them at the entrance of the receiving tent, a small grey-haired force of nature with a lamp in one hand and disapproval already kindled in the other.

"We've no beds for strays," she began.

"He's delivering medical stores, Matron, delayed from the Poperinghe convoy. He'll sleep in the receiving tent and be gone at first light. I've cleared it with the sergeant."

Every word was a lie, and Rafael had not lied to Edith Calloway in fourteen months of shared mud and shared grief. The words came out smooth and terrible, and she watched them land, watched the matron's eyes flick from her face to Reiner's and back — because Calloway missed nothing, and Rafael knew she was being filed away for later examination.

"One night," Calloway said at last. "Dry rations, no spirits, and he touches nothing in the wards." She raised the lamp higher, studying Reiner as a horse-trader studies a suspect animal, then turned to Rafael. "A word, Miss Montrose. In my office. Now."

But in the office she only said, quietly, "You're a poor liar, Rafael. Whatever this is, it will cost you. Sleep on whether you can afford the price." And dismissed her.

---

Later — very late, when the ward was down to its breathing and the rain had softened to a whisper — Rafael came back to the receiving tent with tea she did not drink herself. Reiner sat propped against a stretcher frame, boots off, wringing a sock with the grim dedication of a man performing surgery.

"You saw the envelope," he said, without looking up.

"I saw handwriting."

"And you recognized it." He set the sock aside and looked at her directly. "You recognized handwriting most of Europe believes belongs to a dead man."

Rafael set the tea down. Her hands, she noted distantly, were perfectly steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything she actually felt. "Captain Elias Voss was reported killed in April. I entered him myself."

"In what?"

The question hung there. *In what.* Not *in your records*; he had said *in what*, and the phrasing was precise, deliberate, a fisherman's fly laid gently on still water.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Voss said you'd say exactly that." Reiner leaned forward, elbows on knees, rainwater still dripping from his coat onto the boards. "He described you, Miss Montrose. He said there was a woman at the dressing station near Ypres — quiet, exact, the sort who counts everything twice — who kept a list the army didn't know about. Names that don't appear anywhere official. Men erased. He said if I couldn't trust anything else in this filthy country, I could trust that such a woman existed, because he'd seen the same erasures from the other side, and someone had to be writing them down."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them. Somewhere out in the dark a gun spoke once, distant, perfunctory, like a door closing in another part of the house.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Rafael said again, but the words sounded different now, thinner, and Reiner heard the difference.

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't. But your hands do." He reached into the satchel and drew out the envelope — water-stained, travel-worn, held together at one corner with a strip of surgical tape that made her chest ache for reasons she couldn't name. He did not offer it. He only laid it on the stretcher between them, face up, and folded his hands.

"Eight months dead," he said, "and he writes to you anyway. I'd very much like to know why — but I think you already do."

Rafael looked at the envelope. At the tall capitals. At the low-struck crossbars of the *t* in *Montrose* — her own name, written by a man the war had officially finished with.

Outside, the rain went on counting.
