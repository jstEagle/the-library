## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

The supply room smelled of carbolic and damp sacking, and by the light of a shielded candle Rafael Montrose watched a dead man's hand move across the page before her.

Not the hand itself — that was the stranger's, this rain-soaked Tomas with his canal-water eyes and his soldier's stoop — but the writing. The letter he had drawn from inside his coat was warped with damp, its envelope soft as old linen, and across its face ran a script she would have known anywhere: the tall ascenders, the curious backward loop on every *f*, the way Captain Elias Voss had always crossed his *t*s twice, quick and impatient, like a man signing confessions.

*To whoever keeps accounts,* it began. She had read those four words eleven times. They had not changed.

"Where did you get this?" she asked, though she had already asked twice, and Tomas had already answered twice in words she could not yet make herself believe.

"I told you." He sat on an upturned crate among the crates of lint and iodine, hands hanging between his knees like a man who had learned to take up as little of the world as possible. "He wrote it. Four days ago. He's alive, miss. He's been alive all along."

"The captain has been dead since April." Her voice came out flat, clinical, the voice she used for men screaming on the table. "I saw the casualty wire. I copied the notice into my own hand for the hospital records. *Died of wounds received in action near Langemarck.* I remember it because—" Because she had remembered him. Because everyone remembered Voss; he had written to her hospital for two years, cheerful, absurdly literate letters full of jokes about the mud and careful questions about how many men she'd saved that week. "Because it was official."

Tomas almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, that smile — tired and crooked and lit from underneath by something that might have been hope or might have been madness. "So is everything else they've struck off."

* * *

Tomas had not wanted to come into Belgium at all. That was the first thing he told himself, wading the last stretch of the Yser canal with the letter sealed in oilcloth against his chest, was that he had never wanted any of it — not the safe house with its leaking roof, not Voss's quiet, relentless dictation through the small hours, not this final errand into a country where couriers were pulled off trains and not always heard from again.

But wanting had nothing to do with anything anymore. That was what Voss had taught him, hunched over the copying pencil in the blue dark, saying *write it exactly, Tomas, exactly, because exactness is all we have against them.*

The checkpoint at the bridge had been easy. A bored sentry, a forged requisition, a crate of actual medical supplies so the lie had weight. The second one tomorrow would be harder, and the third harder still, and somewhere in this dressing station there was supposed to be a person who kept accounts — Voss had said it like a password, *find the one who keeps accounts, they'll know what I mean* — and Tomas was beginning to understand, watching the nurse's face as she read the dead man's living handwriting, that the password had worked.

He studied her while she read. Small woman, steady hands, ink on the side of her right forefinger the way clerks and liars both got it. There was a book somewhere in this place, he'd wager his last franc on it. Voss thought there was. *If anyone on that side is writing down the names,* he'd said, *it will be a woman nobody notices.*

"You knew him," Tomas said, when the silence had gone on long enough to ache.

"I corresponded with him." She did not look up. "On behalf of the hospital. It was hardly acquaintance."

"He asked about you. Whether you were still—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her like a rifle shot, and then her face did something complicated — shame and fury fighting for the same ground — and she pressed the letter flat against her chest as if it were evidence in a trial. Perhaps it was. "You cannot ask me to believe this on the strength of paper. Paper lies. I have spent two years learning how well paper lies."

"Yes," Tomas said quietly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

She made him wait in the supply room with the candle guttering while she went to her quarters, and her hands shook so badly getting there that she had to press them against the wall of the corridor, cold plaster under her palms, until the shaking stopped or was at least promoted to something she could carry.

The letters were in the false bottom of her instrument case, beneath the spare scissors. Not only Voss's — she kept others, her brother's last three notes home, the matron's kindest note after — but Voss's were the thickest bundle, tied in surgeon's thread. Two years of them. She untied the knot and laid three sheets on her cot and set the stranger's letter beside them and looked, really looked, the way she looked at wounds: without flinching, without hoping.

The backward loop on the *f*. The double-struck *t*. The peculiar *R* that started its leg too high, like a man perpetually stepping down. Even the pencil pressure — heavy, urgent, the graphite slightly ridged where his hand bore down — even that was the same. And the date at the top of the new letter was October. Nineteen seventeen. Eight months and some weeks after the wire that said he was dust and a wooden marker somewhere with his name cut into it.

She sat on the edge of the cot for a long time.

It was not simply that the handwriting matched. It was what the letter said, once she forced herself past the signature. It listed dates. The eleventh of March. The fourth of April. The ninth of June. Names — Corporal Deane, Private Ostrander, Lieutenant Halloran—

Her breath stopped on that one. *Halloran.* She read it again. A lieutenant, struck from the rolls in June, no unit given, no cause. Not her brother; her brother had vanished in '16 and never worn a lieutenant's pips. But the name landed in her like a splinter anyway, because it was proof of what she had half-known and refused to know: that the list in her hidden ledger and the list in this dead man's letter were not similar. They were the same list. The eleventh of March — she had that date in her own hand, in her own book, hidden behind the third shelf of dressings, entered the night a sergeant's widow came asking why the army had no record of her husband ever serving.

Voss had documented the erasures. From the other side. From inside whatever machinery did the striking-out, he had watched the same names fall and had gathered them up, and now his evidence was sitting on her cot in the handwriting of a man the army itself had erased first and most completely.

The ground did not shift under her so much as it revealed itself to have been shifting for years, and her the one thing standing still.

* * *

When she came back to the supply room, Tomas saw the change in her walk before she said a word. She moved like someone who had taken a wound and decided, provisionally, not to bleed.

"You're him," she said. "The one who verifies. He trusts you to carry it."

"I'm nobody," Tomas said. "I fix boats. I was good at canals."

"And now?"

"Now I'm good at carrying things." He held out his hand, palm up — an offering, or a surrender. "There's more than the letter, miss. There are copies. Forty pages, maybe fifty, sworn statements, roll extracts, the orders themselves — he kept the orders. Signed. Dated. Names of the officers who signed them."

Something crossed her face then that Tomas had seen before, on men just before they went over. He almost reached for her arm and didn't.

"It has to pass three checkpoints," she said. It wasn't a question. He realized she had read past the signature further than he'd thought — to the instructions, the route, the name of the correspondent waiting at the customs house in the neutral quarter.

"Canal. Rail yard. Customs house. Tomorrow night."

"And if you're stopped—"

"I'm a supplies man with a requisition. But papers aren't enough anymore. They took a courier at the second post last week." He paused. "They need to travel on something that can't be searched. Or in the hands of someone who can't be arrested without a scene."

The candle fluttered. Somewhere beyond the canvas walls a motor ambulance ground its gears in the mud, and a voice called for stretchers, and the war went on doing what the war did. Rafael stood very still among the crates of bandages, holding a dead man's letter against her ribs, and felt the whole architecture of her careful, dutiful, elsewhere life groan like ice under weight.

"There's a matron here," she said at last, slowly, as if each word were being lifted separately. "Matron Calloway. If Major Aldous learns a stranger slept one night in my supply room, neither of us sees spring."

"Aldous." Tomas frowned. "That name's in the papers we're carrying."

The lantern flame bent and steadied. Outside, the rain began again on the canvas, patient and endless, and Rafael Montrose understood that she had just heard the name of the man who signed the erasure orders — the man who had dined at her hospital, who had praised her efficiency, who had known her ledger's hiding places better perhaps than anyone alive.

"How," she said quietly, "is it spelled?"
