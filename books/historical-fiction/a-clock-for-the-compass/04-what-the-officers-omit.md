## 4. What the Officers Omit

The candle had burned down to a stub no wider than Rafael's thumb before she opened the ledger again.

She had told herself she would not. She had spent the evening in the ward as she always did — turning men, changing dressings, holding cups of water to cracked lips, writing the small merciful lies the official forms required — and she had come back to her bunk with her hands aching and her mind deliberately, carefully blank. That was the trick of it. Blankness. You could survive this place if you let the days pass through you like water through gauze.

But the letter was still folded inside her apron pocket, and the ledger was still beneath the loose board under her cot, and somewhere across the darkened camp a dead man's handwriting waited on her table like an accusation.

She knelt, pried up the board, and drew the ledger out. Its cover had gone soft with handling, the leather worn to suede at the corners where her thumbs pressed every night. She set it beside Voss's letter on the packing crate that served her as a desk, opened both, and began, for the first time, to read them against each other.

The ledger she knew by heart. Forty-one names now, each entered in her own cramped hand: name, regiment, date of wound, date of death or transfer, and then — the part that mattered — the date the name vanished from the rolls. She had developed a notation for it over the months, a small circle drawn around nothing, an empty ring where an official number should have been. A hole in the record, inked faithfully.

Voss's pages were different. He wrote like a man who had been trained to observe and had then been ordered to forget how, and had refused. His hand slanted hard to the right, the downstrokes heavy, the crossings of his *t*'s slashed like wire. And what he had written — Tomas had shown her only three sheets, but three were enough — were casualty summaries from sectors she knew. Names of men carried to her own dressing station. Men whose wounds she had dressed. Men whose names appeared in her ledger with their little empty rings beside them.

Her breath fogged in the cold of the hut. She read until the candle guttered, matching entry to entry, and the two documents came together in her hands like torn cloth fitted at a seam. His evidence named the orders. Her ledger named the men. Together they were not a habit, not a superstition, not a private grief indulged in the dark. Together they were a record — whole, corroborated, damning.

*History is something that happens to other people.* She had believed that the way one believes in weather. It had never occurred to her that she might be standing inside it.

* * *

Her brother's name had vanished on a Tuesday.

She remembered the day of the week because the laundry came back Tuesdays, and the laundry cart smelled of lye and steam, and she was folding a stranger's shirts when the casualty list was pinned to the board outside the matron's office. April, 1916. The lists went up each morning, typed on thin paper that curled in the damp, and the orderlies gathered around them the way men gather around a card game — casually, too casually, already knowing the odds.

She had not looked for Albie in weeks. That was a discipline too. He was somewhere north with his battery, and his letters came irregularly, cheerful, full of jokes about the mud and the food and a corporal who sang in his sleep. The last one had mentioned a ridge whose name meant nothing to her. So when she saw the *M*s on the list her eye passed over them idly, the way you pass a window you have looked through a thousand times.

Then it stopped.

**MONTROSE, PTE. A. J. — WOUNDED, MISSING.**

Missing. She stood with the folded shirts pressed to her chest and read it perhaps twenty times. Missing was not dead. Missing was a door left open a crack, a word that contained its own opposite. She asked the adjutant's clerk, a boy younger than Albie, whether there was anything further — a sector, a date, a hospital where he might have been carried. There was not. There was a shrug. *These things sort themselves out, miss.*

Two weeks later she went back to the board and the list had changed. The paper was new; someone had retyped it overnight. **MONTROSE, PTE. A. J.** was gone. Not amended. Not marked deceased, which would at least have been a verdict she could grieve against. Gone — lifted out as though the typesetter's key had simply declined to strike him. The line above ran into the line below and no gap remained, as if the page itself had healed over the wound of him.

She complained. That was the word they used afterward, half amused: *Montrose complained.* She wrote to the records office and received a form in reply. She filled in the form and received another form. She walked, on her one free afternoon, to the sector headquarters at Poperinghe, past sandbags and telephone wire, and asked — politely, cap in hand, because she had learned exactly how much politeness bought — whether a Private A. J. Montrose of the 9th Battery might be traced.

It was Major Aldous who received her. She had known him slightly before — he had visited the hospital on quartermaster business, a grey, precise man with kind creases at the corners of his eyes, the sort of officer who remembered the nurses' names and asked after the ones on night duty. He cleared a chair for her himself. He listened to all of it: the list, the retyped list, the forms, the forms answering forms.

Then he said, "Leave it with me," and something in his voice made her believe him.

He should not have been able to do anything. A major of logistics had no authority over missing men. But eleven days later a second letter arrived from the records office, and this one was not a form. It confirmed that Private A. J. Montrose had been wounded on 14 April near the ridge Albie's last letter had named, had been evacuated along the standard route, and had — the phrasing was delicately bureaucratic — *died of wounds subsequent to removal, record previously miscatalogued.*

Miscatalogued. At least there was now a verdict. She wept for a night behind the linen store where no one could see, and then she did what she had begun doing by then for strangers: she wrote Albie into the ledger. First name in her own bloodline. Name, battery, date of wound, date of the record's correction. Around the space where an official confirmation number belonged, she drew her little ring of emptiness.

And she was grateful. That was the thing she could not stop returning to now, sitting alone with two impossible documents spread before her. Grateful — to Aldous. He had done for her what no one would do for others: he had forced the machinery to produce a body, a date, an ending. She had defended him since, quietly, to the nurses who called him cold. When he arranged extra blankets for the convalescent shed, when he looked the other way on requisitions stretched past legality, when he once stood in her ward doorway at three in the morning and said only, "You keep this place together, Sister," — she had counted these as proof that decency survived somewhere in the apparatus, and that he was its keeper.

He had given her brother back to her so that she would stop asking questions. She understood that now, with the clarity that arrives like ice water: the kindness and the concealment were the same act. He had not corrected a miscatalogue. He had manufactured one — a tidy paper death for a boy whose file had grown inconvenient — and he had chosen her, the grieving sister, to receive it, because a grateful woman does not dig.

* * *

Tomas's sheets lay under her lamp now, and she made herself look at what she had avoided looking at all evening: the references, in Voss's slashing hand, to authorization. He had copied fragments of the orders themselves — she recognized the format, the numbered paragraphs, the flat officialese that could unmake a man in a sentence. *Records to be reconciled accordingly. Nominal rolls adjusted at discretion of sector command. No further correspondence to be entered into.*

At the foot of each fragment, a signature.

She had seen that signature hundreds of times. On blanket requisitions and coal chits, on leave papers and burial returns, on the letter that had given her brother his ending. **A. Aldous, Maj.** — the capital *A*s tall and narrow, the final flourish of the surname trailing off like something tired of being written.

Not a subordinate following orders upward from some faceless ministry. Not a clerk swept along. The discretion in those paragraphs — *at discretion of sector command* — was his discretion. The empty rings in her ledger, forty-one of them now, forty-two counting Albie, each one corresponded to a page he had signed. Her secret record of the erased and his authorizing signature were two halves of a single document that had never before existed in one place. She was holding it, here, in a freezing hut by a dying candle.

She thought of him clearing the chair for her at Poperinghe. She thought of his voice in the ward doorway. Betrayal, she discovered, does not arrive like a blow. It arrives like water finding a crack — silently, along lines you did not know were there, and then everything you built on the ground begins to list.

She pressed her palms flat against the ledger until the paper warmed under her hands. The men in it had trusted her without knowing it; she had appointed herself their rememberer in the cowardly grammar of *merely a habit*. Very well. The habit ended tonight. What remained was a choice with edges on it.

Because there was worse, and she made herself think it through to the end: Aldous had protected her hospital for reasons she could now count differently. Every blanket, every blind eye, every kindness — all of it purchased the silence of the woman who kept accounts. And if he learned that the accounts had found their other half — that a dead captain's evidence sat on her crate beside her ringed and faithful holes —

A floorboard creaked outside. Rafael closed the ledger and the letter in one motion, slid them beneath the blanket roll, and was lying rigid, eyes shut, heart hammering against her ribs, when boots stopped at her door — deliberate, unhurried, familiar boots — and Matron Calloway's knuckles sounded twice against the wood.

"Sister Montrose." A pause, low and urgent through the planks. "I know he's here. We need to talk before dawn — before Aldous's men search the canal road."
