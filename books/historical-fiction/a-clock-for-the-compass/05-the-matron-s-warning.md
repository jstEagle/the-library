## 5. The Matron's Warning

Edith Calloway had learned long ago that a hospital at night told the truth about the day. The lamps burned low, the moaning settled into rhythm, and whatever lies had been spoken in daylight — *you'll be fine, lad; it's only a scratch; the war will be over by Christmas* — dissolved like salt in warm water. Night was when the ward confessed.

She made her rounds slowly, as she always did after midnight, her shoes making no sound on the duckboards laid over the mud that had been a floor once, before the shelling turned it to porridge. She paused at each cot. She touched a wrist here, adjusted a blanket there, and in the third bay she stopped longer than anywhere else, because Private Ansell — nineteen, both hands bandaged to stumps — was awake again, staring at the ceiling beams as though they might fall.

"Matron," he said. "Do you know my name?"

"Of course I do, Ansell. William Ansell. You're from Worcester, and your mother writes every week, and you have a girl called Mabel who has sent you four photographs." She said it gently, but she said all of it, because that was the point. "You are not a number."

He closed his eyes then, satisfied, and Edith stood a moment longer in the dark, feeling the weight of the ledger in her mind the way other women felt a stone in the shoe. She had known about Rafael's ledger for seven months now. Seven months of watching the girl bend over that second book by candlelight, the one hidden beneath a loose board under the dispensary shelf, recording names that had been struck from the rolls as though struck from existence itself.

She should have put a stop to it in October, when she'd first found out. That was what discipline demanded. Instead she had moved the loose board's covering plank so it would sit more naturally, and said nothing, and thereby become — she understood this with terrible clarity — complicit.

Complicity, she had discovered, did not feel like anything dramatic. It felt like extra sugar in a wounded man's tea. It felt like looking away at exactly the right moment. It felt, most days, like nothing at all, until a night came when it weighed enough to bruise.

* * *

The message arrived at half past two, carried by a despatch rider who smelled of engine oil and rain and who would not come past the receiving tent. He pressed the envelope into Edith's hand and said only: "From Major Aldous's office. He wants it understood it was hand-delivered. No clerks."

No clerks. In Edith's experience, whenever an officer specified *no clerks*, what he meant was *no witnesses*.

She waited until the rider's motorcycle had coughed itself back down the track before opening the envelope under the receiving tent's single lamp. The paper inside bore Aldous's own hand — not dictated, not typed. That alone told her the shape of things before she read a word.

*Mrs Calloway — There is reason to believe certain records are being kept at this station which do not appear in any official register. A courier was detained this evening at the rail yard checkpoint carrying documents of a sensitive nature. The matter is being investigated with discretion. I would be obliged if you would assist me in maintaining the good order of your station. — A.*

Edith read it three times. Then she folded it small and held the corner of it near the lamp flame, watching the paper curl and blacken, because some messages were safer as ash than as evidence, and because burning it gave her hands something to do while her stomach turned over.

A courier detained at the rail yard. The rail yard was the second checkpoint on the road toward the coast — the road that led, eventually, to correspondents, to newspapers, to people outside the wire who might actually *print* something. Which meant someone was already moving papers. Someone besides Rafael.

And Aldous knew.

* * *

She found Rafael at dawn, or rather she found where Rafael wasn't. Not at her bunk. Not at breakfast, such as it was. Edith followed the sound of a pencil scratching and came upon her in the dispensary, kneeling on the cold flags with the loose board lifted and the ledger open across her knees, adding a name in her small, stubborn hand.

Rafael looked up, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The girl had flour-pale skin and ink on the side of her right palm where she dragged it across the page, and she was twenty-three years old and had been keeping dead men alive on paper for the better part of a year, and Edith thought, not for the first time: *she looks exactly like my Margaret would have looked, if Margaret had lived past her fourth winter.*

That thought was the whole trouble. Matrons were not supposed to have daughters of the heart in field hospitals. Sentiment got people shot.

"You've seen the morning post," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a question. News travelled through a hospital the way water travels through chalk.

"I've seen it." Edith lowered herself onto the dispensary stool with a groan her knees had earned honestly. "Shut the board."

Rafael slid the ledger under the floor and replaced the plank, and the two of them sat among the shelves of morphine ampoules and iodine and lint, surrounded by the means of easing pain and none of the means of ending it.

"There was a courier stopped at the rail yard last night," Edith said. "Detained. Whatever he was carrying is in someone else's hands now, and those hands belong to Aldous's people. And Aldous has written to me — his own hand, no clerks — saying he believes unofficial records are being kept at this station." She let that settle. "He used the word *discretion*. When a man like Aldous promises discretion, Rafael, he is telling you how far he intends to go without being seen to go there."

Rafael's face, to her credit, did not crumple. It went very still instead, the way a pond goes still when something large moves beneath the ice. "The courier," she said. "Did they say who?"

"A stranger. Not local. Possibly Spanish or Portuguese by the name — Tomas, they think. He'll be questioned, and men being questioned at rail yards do not generally remain silent out of loyalty to strangers." Edith leaned forward. "Which means the trail leads here within a day. Perhaps two."

"Then I move the ledger tonight."

"And take it where? Into your kit bag? Into a convoy inspected by the very officers whose signatures fill its pages?" Edith heard her own voice rise and forced it down. Around them the hospital breathed — snoring, murmuring, the squeak of a stretcher-bearer's boots somewhere distant. "Child, listen to me. I am going to say something once, and then we will never speak of it again unless you choose otherwise."

Rafael waited.

"You can walk away. Tonight. There is a Red Cross barge leaving from the canal at six o'clock, taking convalescent staff down to Boulogne. I can have you on it. Your papers would show a routine transfer — I have written enough transfers to make one true in every particular except its purpose. By tomorrow you'd be at the coast, and by next week somewhere Aldous's reach thins out to nothing. The ledger stays buried here, and when this station is packed up at the end of it all, the ground keeps its secrets. Ground is good at that."

The silence that followed was very long. Outside, the rain had started again, soft against canvas, patient.

"You've known," Rafael said at last. "All this time. You knew and you never said."

"I knew," Edith admitted, "and I moved the plank so it wouldn't creak. Which makes me as guilty as you, only older about it. Don't mistake me — I'm not offering you the barge because I've grown virtuous overnight. I'm offering it because until last night, the cost of your book was going to fall on you alone. Now it falls on everyone near you. On me. On the orderlies who've carried your tea. Perhaps on the patients whose blankets you'll want for other purposes — yes, don't look surprised, I didn't get to be Matron by being unobservant." She drew breath. "Complicity is a debt, Rafael, and debts get called."

Rafael knelt there with her hands flat on the replaced board, as if she could feel the ledger's warmth through the wood. "If I run," she said, "the names stay erased. William Ansell becomes a number. My brother—" She stopped herself, but too late; the name was already in the room between them, the way the dead are always in rooms between the living.

"Your brother," Edith said softly, "whose name vanished from a casualty list in the spring, and whom you have been writing back into existence ever since, one stranger at a time. I know. I've known since November, when you wrote his name at the top of a page and crossed it out and couldn't bear to leave it crossed." Her voice cracked despite forty years of professional steadiness. "I lost a daughter, girl. I know precisely what it is to keep someone alive against all evidence. But hear me: the dead do not need you to die for them. They need you to live somewhere the rain doesn't fall sideways."

"It isn't only about the dead anymore." Rafael raised her head, and Edith saw with a lurch that the fear in her eyes was already hardening into something else, something that had been coming for weeks and could no more be stopped than weather. "There's a man — Tomas — and Captain Voss—"

"I know about Voss."

That landed visibly. Rafael's mouth opened and closed. "How—"

"The handwriting on his old letters to this hospital is on half the pages you hide under that floor. I can read upside down and backwards, Rafael; it's a matron's sixth sense." Edity sighed, and the sigh took something out of her. "So Voss is alive, and he's been gathering the same proof you have, and now his courier is in an interrogation room and his safe house is a matter of hours from a search party. Which brings me to my last sentence, and then I'm done."

She stood, knees complaining, and put her hand — dry, strong, smelling of carbolic — on top of Rafael's head, briefly, like a blessing or an absolution.

"If you stay, and it goes wrong, you will not have chosen wrongly. I want you to understand that before you choose at all. A woman who gives up everything for the truth is not a fool, whatever they write at her court-martial. But a woman who throws her life away carelessly, out of pride, when a barge was offered — that woman breaks my heart twice." She straightened. "Think on it until dark. Then tell me which kind of brave you intend to be."

She was at the dispensary door when Rafael spoke.

"Matron. If I asked you to help me carry something through the checkpoints — something sewn into a blanket, something that has to reach a correspondent — would you help me?"

Edith stood with her hand on the frame, her back to the girl, and watched the rain run down the tent flap and pool in the mud, and thought of Margaret's small shoes she still kept in a drawer in England, and of William Ansell staring at the ceiling beams, and of the courier in the rail yard whose name nobody official would ever bother to record.

"I'm an old woman," she said, "who has spent this entire war learning exactly how much she can bear to lose." She pulled her collar up. "Ask me again tonight, and we'll find out."

Behind her, faintly, came the sound of the plank lifting once more — and beneath it, the scratch of a pencil beginning a new page.
