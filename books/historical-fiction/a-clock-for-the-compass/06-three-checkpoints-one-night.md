## 6. Three Checkpoints, One Night

Rafael sewed the last stitch by candlelight, her needle dipping through wool and oilcloth and the folded pages beneath, the way a surgeon closes a wound she cannot show anyone.

The blanket belonged to Private Anselm Reyes, nineteen years old, shot through the thigh and feverish but stable enough to travel to the rail yard hospital at Poperinghe. He slept now, drugged with what little morphine the stores allowed, his face boyish and slack. She had chosen him carefully — wounded badly enough to warrant evacuation, not so badly that a doctor might cut the blanket away in a hurry.

"You're certain about the stitching," Tomas whispered from the doorway of the supply tent. "It won't hold?"

"It will hold better than most of the men do." Rafael bit the thread. Her fingers ached; she had been sewing since dusk, first for the living, then for this other purpose. "The oilcloth keeps out rain and blood both. The question is whether you can keep your hands steady."

He almost smiled. In the days since he'd arrived soaked and half-drowned at her checkpoint, she had learned the geography of his face: the scar splitting one eyebrow, the way his jaw set when he lied to matron Calloway about convoy schedules, the flicker of guilt he could not entirely suppress when he looked at Reyes sleeping under evidence that could hang them all.

"Steady hands," Tomas said, "are all I have left."

Outside, the rain had stopped, which was worse than the rain. A clear night meant lamplight at the checkpoints, meant faces seen and remembered. Rafael pulled back the tent flap and read the sky — low cloud shredding over a bruise-colored moon, the smell of wet earth and cordite drifting from the direction of the front like weather from another country.

Three checkpoints between here and the correspondent waiting at the customs house in Hazebrouck. The canal bridge, where military police checked every ambulance against its manifest. The rail yard, where Major Aldous himself had taken to supervising evening inspections — Matron Calloway's warning, delivered that afternoon in a voice stripped of everything maternal: *He suspects a leak. If he finds it, he will not stop at finding it.*

And the customs house, where a man named Corrigan waited with press credentials and a safe-conduct pass and no idea what was coming toward him sewn into a soldier's blanket.

"Say it again," Tomas said. "The order of things."

"The manifest says four wounded, two stretcher cases, bound for Poperinghe on the eleven-forty ambulance train. The blanket travels with Reyes because no one questions a blanket. You ride in the cab as escort. At the customs house, Corrigan's orderly takes the stretchers off, and somewhere in the confusion the blanket changes blankets." She looked at him. "And if it goes wrong—"

"If it goes wrong, I was never in your hospital, and you've never seen me before in your life."

"I've seen you every day for six days."

"Then lie," Tomas said quietly, "the way you lied to the matron. You're better at it than you think. That frightens you more than the checkpoints do."

She didn't answer, because it was true, and they both knew it. Once she had believed history happened to other people, in ministries and staff colleges, in rooms where names were struck from rolls by men who never smelled the gangrene. Now she was carrying history in a blanket, and the men it belonged to were asleep or dead, and there was no version of tonight after which she would be the same woman who had threaded the needle.

* * *

Tomas counted the lamps at the canal bridge: three, burning yellow behind sandbags, and beyond them the black water moving slow as syrup. The ambulance idled. Rain had begun again, fine as sifted flour, beading on the windscreen.

"Manifest," said the sergeant, a heavy man with a waxed moustache and the eyes of someone who had been awake too long. Tomas handed it over and watched him read it the way soldiers read everything — without interest, until something snagged.

"Four wounded. Two stretcher." The sergeant walked the length of the vehicle, lifted a canvas flap. His lamp found the nearest stretcher case, a man moaning softly into his own collar. He moved down. Reyes lay second, breathing in long drugged tides, the blanket tucked to his chin.

Tomas kept his face arranged into boredom. Boredom was armor. He had learned that crossing borders in another life, before the war made every border a wound.

The sergeant straightened. "Escort's name?"

"Tomas Verhoeven. Civilian transport contractor, attached."

"Attached to what?"

"Whatever needs attaching."

It should have been fine. It should have been the kind of tired joke tired men traded at checkpoints every hour of every night along this whole bleeding front. But the sergeant's lamp came up, and behind him a lieutenant stepped out of the sentry box — young, dry under an umbrella held by a private, and looking at Tomas with the particular attention of a man working through a list.

"Verhoeven." The lieutenant said it slowly, tasting it. "There was a courier through the second checkpoint Tuesday. Also attached. Also civilian." He smiled without warmth. "He's being questioned still."

The cold went through Tomas like canal water. They had the courier. Which meant they might have a name, a route, a description — and here was the description, standing in the rain with a manifest in his hand.

"Names are cheap at night, sir," Tomas said. "Half the contractors on this road are called Verhoeven or Van Dam or something Dutch. My papers—"

"Your papers say nothing about Poperinghe. Your chit says Roulers." The lieutenant extended a gloved hand. "Step down, please."

Every calculation Tomas owned ran at once. Behind him, the ambulance driver — a boy who knew nothing — stared straight ahead. On the stretcher, Reyes breathed. Beneath the blanket, eight months of a dead captain's handwriting pressed against a living man's fever-warm skin.

If he stepped down, they'd search him, find nothing, and grow curious about why a contractor carried nothing. If he ran—

He stepped down. It was the only move that bought time, and time was the only cargo he could still protect. "I'll want a receipt for my detention," he said. "My firm is very particular."

The sergeant's fist came fast and unannounced — a short blow to the belly that folded him over the tailgate. Not rage; procedure. First blood, warm in his mouth, salt and iron.

"Load the stretcher back," the lieutenant said, already turning away, already bored again. "Ambulance proceeds. Escort stays for questioning."

Through the ringing in his skull, through the taste of blood, Tomas did the only thing left. As they hauled him toward the sentry box, he stumbled against the ambulance's rear wheel, and his hand found the blanket's edge, and with one finger — quick as a pickpocket, desperate as a drowning man — he tore free the loose thread Rafael had left exactly there for exactly this, the unraveling stitch, the escape seam.

He couldn't take the pages. There was no taking anything now. But he caught the sergeant's eye as they pushed him into the light, and he grinned through bloody lips, and he said, loudly enough for the driver to hear:

"Tell the sister the account's settled. All of it."

* * *

Rafael heard the story from the driver at half past midnight, in the receiving tent, while she pretended to change a dressing that needed no changing.

They kept him. They kept him and they let the ambulance go, and the driver repeated the strange words like a man handing over something hot: *tell the sister the account's settled, all of it.* And when Rafael lifted the blanket from Reyes — who slept on, innocent, nineteen — she found the pages gone, torn away at the seam, taken into the sentry box with Tomas.

No. Not taken. *Left.* She understood it the way one understands a wound's depth, by the cold coming off it. He hadn't lost the evidence. He had given himself up holding it, so that the suspicion would pool around him alone — a courier arrested with contraband reads as a courier caught, not a conspiracy uncovered. The blanket would be searched and found clean. The ambulance would roll on. The trail would end at Tomas Verhoeven, civilian contractor, attached to whatever needs attaching.

And whatever they did to him at that sentry box tonight would buy the next mile.

Rafael stood in the dark behind the tent with the empty blanket in her arms, shaking — not with fear, or not only. Somewhere between here and the rail yard, Aldous was waiting under his lamp, and the plan was broken, and there was no evidence left to carry except the ledger sewn into her own coat lining, the one she had kept for two years, the one with four hundred struck names in her careful hand.

Eleven forty. The train to Poperinghe. One checkpoint gone wrong, one checkpoint ahead where her protector turned executioner waited, and one after that where a correspondent waited for documents that no longer existed outside a detained man's coat and her own hidden book.

Matron Calloway appeared at her shoulder, silent as snowfall, and put one hand briefly on Rafael's arm.

"They'll have him talking by morning," the old woman murmured. "Whatever you're going to do, Sister Montrose — do it before dawn."
