## 7. Aldous at the Rail Yard

The rail yard at Poperinghe smelled of coal smoke and creosote and the sweet-rot stink of the canal two fields distant, a smell Rafael had long ago stopped noticing until nights like this, when fear sharpened everything into its worst version of itself. The convoy of ambulances idled in a line under the great arched lamps, engines coughing, headlamps cut down to slits by the tin hoods the drivers wore over them like blinders. Rain came down fine and constant, not enough to be weather, only enough to be misery.

She stood beside the second ambulance in the line, her hand resting on the canvas flank of the wounded man inside — Corporal Deene, twenty-three, a shrapnel wound through the shoulder that had been dressed twice and would need a proper surgeon by morning. He slept, mercifully, drugged into a shallow boat of sleep beneath three blankets, and beneath the third blanket — beneath *his* blanket, which was the whole point, which was the only reason he was here — lay the oilcloth packet she had sewn into the ticking with a curved needle and forty minutes of shaking hands.

Tomas should have been beside her. Tomas was instead somewhere behind her, detained at the first checkpoint, and she had made herself stop imagining what *detained* meant because every version of it ended badly and she still had two checkpoints to survive.

"Convoy's late," said the driver, Ostrander, pulling his goggles up onto his forehead. His face was grey with fatigue and rain. "They're going to be pleasant about it, I expect."

"They're going to be thorough about it," Rafael said. "Pleasant is beyond them."

It was meant as a joke and neither of them smiled.

The checkpoint at the rail yard was nothing but a wooden barrier across the road, a guard hut with a stove pipe smoking crookedly from its roof, and a siding where four cattle trucks stood waiting with their doors open like mouths. Men moved between the lamp standards with rifles slung backwards, their silhouettes jerking in the way of men who had been standing in the cold too long. A military policeman came down the line of ambulances ahead of hers, checking papers with a torch he shielded against the rain, and Rafael watched his light travel — ambulance one, papers, wave on; then the torch swinging toward her — and her heart did something childish and enormous, filling her whole chest so she could hardly breathe around it.

The light stopped on her face.

"Sister." He was young. They were all young. "Papers."

She handed over the convoy manifest and her own pass, both forged in the sense that they were real documents bearing small, surgical alterations, and watched him read them upside down as though literacy were optional. Behind him, at the barrier, an officer had emerged from the hut and was speaking to Ostrander, and even before the officer turned she knew — some animal part of her, the part that had survived her brother and the casualty lists and eight months of struck names — knew the shape of his shoulders under the wet greatcoat, the tilt of his head when he listened.

Major Aldous walked down the line toward her with an umbrella he was not using, carried closed like a swagger stick, letting the rain fall on him as though weather were something he had outgrown.

"Sister Montrose," he said.

"Major."

He had known her since before she had been Sister anything — since she had been a volunteer scrubbing blood out of floorboards at the clearing hospital and he had been a staff officer with a limp and a reputation for decency, the man who found blankets when there were no blankets, who looked the other way at the brandy the surgeons drank, who had once held a dying boy's hand for an hour because Rafael's own hands were needed elsewhere. She had trusted him the way one trusts gravity. She had built her whole understanding of the war on the certainty that Aldous was the fixed point, the decent man in the machinery.

And then she had seen the orders. *Signed.* Her brother's name in the column of the erased, and Aldous's signature beneath it, unhurried, unmistakable, the same looping *A* he used when he wrote to thank her for the men's letters home.

"You're a long way from your ward," he said.

"The convoy needed a medical escort. The matron sent me."

"Calloway sent you." He said the name the way a man sets a card down face-up. "Yes. I imagine she did."

The MP hovered, uncertain whether he was dismissed. Aldous waved him off without looking, and the boy retreated to the barrier, and for a moment the two of them stood alone in the rain between the idling ambulances while the engines ticked and the lamp above them buzzed like something trapped.

"There is a packet," Aldous said quietly, "moving north tonight. Papers sewn into a blanket, or hidden in a stretcher frame, or rolled inside a bandage — the method changes with each telling, which tells me the people carrying it are improvising. It is going to a correspondent. A Dutchman working under an American dateline." He turned the closed umbrella over in his hands, once, like a man examining a cane. "I have been asked to find it."

*Asked.* The word did its work on her even now, even knowing what she knew. Asked, as though he were a servant. As though someone above him wanted this done cleanly and wanted his name off it.

"And if you find it?" she asked.

"Then it stops. And the men in those papers stay where the army has put them, which is nowhere, which is where they have been very carefully placed." He looked at her then — fully looked, the way he had looked at her across the ward at Bailleul three years ago, when she was nobody and he had treated her as somebody — and his voice dropped to almost nothing, lost entirely under the engines. "Rafael. There is a ledger. I have known about it since autumn. A little black book of names that ought not exist, kept by someone at Calloway's station who believes she is being careful."

The ground did not move. That was the terrible thing; she had expected the ground to move and it simply held, indifferent, while everything on top of it rearranged itself.

"You've known," she said.

"I protect what I can," Aldous said. "Do you imagine the hospital stands because the army is generous? Do you imagine your supplies arrive, your evacuations are scheduled, your ward is passed over when other stations are stripped for men? Someone argues for you in rooms you will never see. Someone has been arguing for you for two years, and that someone is asking you now — as a friend, as nearly a father — to walk away from this."

*As nearly a father.* She thought of her actual father, dead of pneumonia the first winter, and of Jonathon after him, and of the morning the casualty list came and she read it four times and his name wasn't on it, and how she had thought at first — absurdly, hopelessly — *they've made a mistake, they've left him off, that means he's alive,* before the truth arrived in its proper shape: not that the list had omitted him, but that the list had been corrected. Amended. Struck. Private J. Montrose had not died at all, officially. Officially he had never existed, and therefore could not be mourned, compensated, or remembered, and his sister had begun keeping a ledger in a dressing station because grief needed somewhere to go and no one would give it a grave.

She saw his name now, in memory, printed at the top of Aldous's orders — not in accusation, worse than accusation: in administration. A clerk's hand entering her brother into oblivion, and Aldous signing it the way he signed everything, steadily, decently, the fixed point.

"You signed it," she said. "Jonathon's paper. You signed the erasure of my brother."

Something crossed his face — not guilt, she realized, and that was the most frightening thing of all. Regret, perhaps. The regret of a man who has weighed something carefully and would weigh it the same again.

"I sign many things," he said. "If I refused that one, another officer would have signed it, and your hospital would have lost its advocate, and nothing would have changed except everything I can still do for you. That is how the machine works, Rafael. You cannot break it by writing names in a book. You can only lose yourself inside it." He stepped closer, and the rain fell between them, and his voice was gentle in a way that felt like a hand closing. "Give me the ledger and the packet. Tonight, before the third checkpoint. You will be protected — I give you my word, and you know what my word costs me. A transfer to Étaples, a clean ward, a future. Or refuse, and I can no longer hold back what is already moving toward you. There is a name on a list at headquarters, Rafael. Yours. Being erased is not always death. Sometimes it is arrest, trial, a charge of espionage that no inquiry ever overturns. Sometimes it is simply becoming one of the names in your own book."

The engines ticked. Somewhere down the siding a truck door slammed. Inside the ambulance, Corporal Deene slept on, three blankets deep, unknowing, breathing slowly above a packet of truth that would kill him as surely as any shell if it were found — no, not kill him, she corrected herself; erase him. Make him administrative. Turn a boy from Leeds into a clerical error.

Loyalty pulled one way, warm and heavy as a tide: two years of protection, of blankets found and wards spared, of the only adult kindness the war had shown her. Truth pulled the other, cold and clean as the canal water, carrying eight months of struck names and a dead captain's handwriting and Tomas somewhere behind her in the dark, detained, perhaps beaten, perhaps worse, for the same cause she was being offered absolution to abandon.

"Which list," she heard herself ask, "is mine on?"

Aldous hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a fraction of a second, but she had spent years reading officers' faces for the truths they withheld from dying men, and she caught it, catalogued it, filed it where she filed everything now.

"That it exists at all should be answer enough," he said.

She reached up, slowly, and adjusted her veil, and when her hands came down they were steady — steadier than they had been all night, steadier than they had any right to be, and she understood distantly that this was what resolve felt like from the inside: not fire but stillness.

"The ledger is at the hospital," she said. "Under the third floorboard of the dispensary cabinet, beneath the chloroform tins. If you want it, Major, you'll have to take it while I'm not there."

It was not quite surrender. It was not quite defiance. It was the only sentence she could build that contained both the truth and the lie, and she watched him decide to believe it, because he wanted to, because the alternative was searching her here, now, in front of his men, and discovering what she was carrying, and doing in the rain what he did not have the stomach to do.

"Third checkpoint opens at four," he said at last, heavily, turning away. "Don't be on that road, Rafael. For God's sake. Don't make me know you the way I know the men in those papers."

He walked back toward the barrier, opening the umbrella at last, a small black circle against the rain, and the MPs began waving the convoy forward, and Ostrander put the ambulance into gear with a grind that covered the sound of her heart breaking and mending itself into a different shape entirely.

The ambulance rolled past the barrier, past the open-mouthed cattle trucks, onto the dark road north, and Rafael sat with her hands folded and her eyes forward, doing sums she could not stop doing: one hour to the customs house. One correspondent. One chance.

And behind her, shrinking in the mirror of the cab's rear glass, the lamps of the rail yard went out one by one — because Aldous, she was certain, had already telephoned ahead, and the road to the third checkpoint was about to become the most dangerous hundred yards in Flanders.

Unless, she thought, watching the dark swallow the road, he had believed her. Unless the word *friend*, spoken so gently, had cost him more than he knew.

She would find out at four o'clock.
