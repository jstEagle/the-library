## 8. The Canal House Burns

Tomas had learned to measure safety in sounds. The creak of the third stair in the safe house meant a neighbor rising for water. The groan of ice on the canal meant nothing at all. But this sound — boots, many boots, moving without any attempt at quiet along the cobbles of the Langeweg — was the sound of safety ending.

He was awake before he understood why, his hand already closing around the cold iron of the bedframe. Voss was awake too. Tomas could tell by the absence of snoring, by the particular stillness that came from the captain's corner of the loft above the old rope store, where for eight months an officially dead man had slept like a man keeping one ear open for the world's opinion of him.

"Boots," Tomas whispered.

"How many?"

"More than two. Fewer than a regiment."

Voss laughed — a low, terrible, delighted sound, the laugh of a man who had rehearsed this moment so many times that its arrival felt almost like a homecoming. He swung down from the loft with a fluidity that still startled people who had read his death notice. Eight months in hiding had not softened him; it had distilled him, burned away everything but intention.

"The copies left with the milk cart?" Voss asked.

"An hour ago. The Dutchman took them toward the rail yard."

"And the last page?"

Tomas touched his breast pocket. Beneath his coat, beneath his shirt, the folded paper lay against his heart like a second pulse: the final page of Voss's evidence, the one that named names — Aldous among them — in a hand so steady it might have been written by a man signing a dinner invitation rather than a death sentence.

"Good," said Voss. "Then we are no longer important."

The door came down before anyone knocked. That was the way of Major Aldous's men, Rafael had told him once — they did not ask questions because questions created records, and records were the one thing Aldous could not abide. They came through the door and the shuttered window simultaneously, lantern light slashing across the rope store's dusty interior, and the first soldier through the door found the muzzle of Voss's revolver aimed at the space between his eyebrows.

"Captain Elias Voss," the soldier breathed, crossing himself with the reflexive horror of a man meeting a ghost.

"Formerly," said Voss. "Gentlemen, you've caught me at a bad time. I was just becoming posthumous again."

* * *

They had argued about this night twice, and both times Voss had ended the argument with the same sentence: *If they take me, they take nothing.* Now, pressed against the wall beside the trapdoor to the cellar while soldiers hammered up the stairs, Tomas finally understood what the man had meant to do from the beginning.

"They'll want the originals," Voss said quietly, steering Tomas toward the narrow stair that descended into darkness. "Aldous can't afford copies floating about unless he knows what they contain. If I burn everything here, tonight, then whatever reaches your correspondent becomes unanswerable. No original to dispute it against. No forgery claim. Just paper and truth, alone together."

"And you? What happens to you?"

Voss smiled, and in the dimness his face looked carved from something older than flesh. "I died in April, Mr. Reyes. Everything since has been borrowed."

The cellar smelled of canal water and lamp oil. Voss had spent months preparing for this — crates of documents stacked with a hoarder's devotion, eight months of stolen requisition forms, erased casualty lists, letters from mothers whose sons had become numbers, all of it copied in triplicate and scattered into the world through channels Aldous had never managed to close. But the originals remained, and originals were evidence, and evidence could be destroyed or disputed or locked away forever in some staff officer's safe.

Fire could not be disputed.

Voss worked with terrible efficiency. Lamp oil over the crates. A match held thoughtfully, almost tenderly, as though he were lighting candles for the dead rather than burning their testimony. And then flame, roaring upward, filling the cellar with heat and orange light, and the faces of eight months' work curling black at their edges.

"There," Voss said softly. "Now it belongs to everyone or no one."

Above them, the floorboards thundered. Someone had found the loft. Someone shouted about a cellar door.

"Tomas." Voss gripped his shoulder, and his voice changed — became the voice of a commanding officer, which was, after all, what he remained even if no army would admit to owning him. "The back wall. There's a gap behind the coal store where the bricks have crumbled. It opens onto the towpath. You go now, tonight, and you don't stop until you reach her."

"She can't—"

"She can and she will. She's the only person in Flanders Aldous cannot afford to shoot in front of witnesses, and she's carrying half the record in her own memory besides. You were the courier. She is the record." His grip tightened. "Tell her—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, something Tomas had never seen there in all their months of hiding: uncertainty. Tenderness, perhaps. "No. I'll write it down. There's no time. Tell her she was right about me, whatever she accused me of when we knew each other before. Tell her she was right."

Smoke was pouring down the stairwell now. Boots on the cellar steps. Voss stepped out from behind the coal crates into the lantern light with his hands raised and his revolver dangling from one finger, and his voice carried up the stairwell with perfect, unhurried clarity:

"Gentlemen! Captain Elias Voss, Fourth Battalion, reporting as officially deceased. I'd suggest someone send a telegram — his mother will want to know."

The shot that followed was not orderly. It was not a military execution or a formal act of war. It was panic dressed in a uniform — a frightened boy with a rifle making a frightened decision in a smoke-filled room — and Tomas heard the impact of the bullet before he understood the shout that followed it, the clatter of the revolver hitting stone, the sudden terrible quiet.

He was already through the gap in the wall when the second shot came, and he did not let himself think about what it meant.

* * *

The towpath was white with frost, and the canal lay flat and black as poured ink beneath a sky the color of wet ash. Tomas ran. He ran the way he had run through Barcelona during the troubles, the way he had run from things his whole life — but this time he ran *toward* something, and the distinction remade him with every stride.

Behind him the safe house burned. Firelight bloomed against the low clouds, turning the canal briefly gold, and he could hear shouting, and once — cutting through everything, impossible and unmistakable — laughter. Even now. Even bleeding out on the stones of that cellar, Elias Voss was laughing at the absurdity of men who believed that fire could erase what ink remembered.

Tomas's lungs tore. The paper against his chest seemed to burn colder with every step, as though it were drawing heat from his body to sustain itself, as though the last page of a dead man's testimony had its own appetite now, its own will to arrive.

*Don't stop until you reach her.*

She would be at the dressing station, or near it, waiting for word of the convoy. He knew her only by reputation and three brief meetings: the nurse who kept accounts, the woman with the ledger hidden somewhere Aldous's searches had never found, the sister of a name struck from a list. Rafael Montrose, whom Voss spoke of the way other men spoke of weather in countries they'd been happy in — with a kind of helpless fondness, as though she were a climate rather than a person.

He reached the rail yard district as the first grey suggestion of dawn began to separate the rooftops from the sky. Somewhere ahead, a train whistled. Somewhere beyond that, the customs house, and beyond that the correspondent, and beyond that — perhaps — a world in which the dead got their names back.

A patrol crossed the street ahead of him. Tomas pressed himself into a doorway, pulled his collar up, and became for thirty seconds exactly what he appeared to be: nobody, a laborer, a man with no country worth recording. When the boots receded he ran again.

His side had begun to hurt in a deep, wrong way, and when he put his hand to his ribs it came away dark. One of the shots in the cellar — wild, panicked, fired at shadows — had found him too. He noted it the way he noted everything now, with a courier's detachment: *a detail, a delay, an inconvenience between me and delivery.* He had perhaps an hour of running left in him. It would be enough. It had to be enough, because the alternative was that the last words Elias Voss ever laughed on earth would belong to no one.

* * *

He found her at the edge of the dressing station's motor yard, standing in the blue pre-dawn light beside an ambulance with its engine already running, and he understood in one glance that she had known — that some wire in her had been humming all night, the way sisters' wires hum, the way wires attached to truth always hum before the truth arrives.

"Tomas." Her face went through three expressions in a single breath — relief, alarm, and then the terrible fourth thing, the reading of his bloodied shirt and empty hands and burning eyes. "Where is he?"

"Gone." The word cost more than he expected. He steadied himself against the ambulance's flank. "They came at midnight. He burned everything — every original, all of it, so that what's moving now can't be disputed, can't be matched, can't be buried in some staff office. He bought the evidence its freedom with the only currency they couldn't confiscate." His breath hitched. "Then he gave himself up to buy me the stairwell. A boy with a rifle didn't wait for orders."

Rafael did not cry. Tomas had imagined, in the running hours, what her grief might look like, and it looked nothing like tears. It looked like a door closing very gently, and behind the door, a furnace taking light.

"The page," she said.

He fumbled it out from against his heart. His fingers had gone clumsy with cold and blood-loss, and she had to help him, peeling the folded paper free with the same sure hands he had watched bind wounds in the dressing station weeks ago. In the grey light she unfolded it, and he watched her read the steady hand she must have known years before, the hand of a man the army had erased with the same bureaucratic indifference he had spent his death documenting.

"There's a message," Tomas said. "At the bottom. He made me memorize it in case the page was lost. He said—" His voice failed, and he hated it, and he pushed through anyway, because a courier's duty is to deliver not merely paper but meaning. "He said: *You were the record all along.*"

For a long moment Rafael Montrose stood absolutely still, the paper trembling in her hands, the ambulance idling beside her, the frost coming down around them like a slow white confession. Then she folded the page with surgical precision, tucked it inside her coat where the ledger lived — he saw it flash briefly into view, that impossible hidden book of struck names — and turned to the driver.

"Piotr. The customs house. Now."

"Matron Calloway's convoy doesn't leave till six," the driver said.

"Then we have twenty minutes to be early." She turned back to Tomas, and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible, grief burned down to its purest form: fuel. "Can you ride?"

"I can die riding," Tomas said, and hauled himself into the ambulance.

She climbed in beside him, and as the engine coughed and gathered and the wheels began to turn through the frozen mud, she took out the page once more and read it again — not the evidence this time, Tomas suspected, but the sentence at the bottom, the seven words a dead man had sent across the divide for a living woman.

"The third checkpoint is the customs house," she said quietly, to him, to the page, to the furnace behind her closed door. "And Aldous will be watching it. Good. I want him to see my face when I finish what Elias started."

The ambulance lurched forward into the grey morning, toward the canal, toward the correspondent, toward the last checkpoint between the dead and their names.
