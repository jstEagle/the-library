## 9. The Customs House

The customs house had been a granary once, before the war taught it to weigh men instead of wheat. Rafael could see the old iron hooks in the ceiling beams where sacks had hung by the dozen, and now there was only one sack that mattered — a wounded man named Private Ellery, bandaged and feverish, sewn into his blankets with the evidence of eight months of erasures pressed flat against his ribs.

She walked beside the stretcher as the convoy lurched across the cobbles toward the checkpoint, and she counted her heartbeats the way she counted names in the ledger: steadily, without flinching, because to flinch was to lose them.

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the world was rinsed grey. The canal ran alongside the road, black and patient, carrying on its surface the reflection of a sky just beginning to consider lightening. Somewhere behind her, in the direction of the burning canal house, smoke still stood up against the clouds like a pillar marking a path through a wilderness. Voss's pillar. She would not let it mark nothing.

Tomas was not with her. They had taken him at the first checkpoint, four hours ago — she had heard the shout, the scuffle, the wet sound of a man going down against a sandbag — and she had kept walking, because stopping was what they wanted, stopping was how evidence died in a guardroom drawer. She carried him now the way she carried everyone: as a name, held.

The customs house doors stood open, lit from within by hurricane lamps that turned everything the colour of old brass. Two military police flanked the entrance with rifles slung backwards, bored in the manner of men who had searched ten thousand blankets and found nothing worth remembering. And beyond them, at a trestle table stacked with manifests, sat a figure Rafael knew better than she knew her own handwriting.

Major Aldous did not look up when the convoy halted. He let the silence do its work first. That was always his method — she had watched him use it on orderlies, on chaplains, on frightened lieutenants — the pause before recognition, the courtesy that came after, like the click of a well-oiled latch.

"Montrose." He looked up then, and his face did exactly what she had known it would do: softened, warmed, became the face of the man who had found her extra rations during the spring offensive, who had written to her mother when Rafael herself was too hollowed out to write. "They told me you'd be coming through. I confess I hoped they were wrong."

"Major." Her voice held. She was surprised by that, and not surprised; her voice had been holding things all war.

He rose from the table and came around it, hands clasped behind his back, and he studied the convoy the way a farmer studies weather. "A bad night for transport. A very bad night, in fact. There was a fire down the canal. A man killed. Papers moving about like rats in a wall." His eyes came back to her, kind and terrible. "You'll want to hand over whatever you're carrying, and I'll want to pretend this conversation never happened. For your sake."

Behind Aldous, near the far wall of the customs house, Rafael saw the thing that decided everything.

A man in civilian clothes, too clean for Flanders, sat on a crate with a notebook open on his knee. A correspondent's armband, hastily tied, rode his sleeve. Beside him stood two more military police, but their attention was loose, divided; they were watching Aldous, not the crowd of stretcher-bearers. The correspondent was watching everything. His pencil moved whenever Aldous spoke. It had moved when Aldous said *a man killed*.

*You were the record all along.* Tomas had pressed the final page into her hands an hour ago, breath torn, blood on his collar that was not all his own, and the words were still folded against her breast like a coal that would not cool. She had thought, walking here, that she would need to smuggle them past this place. Now she understood that smuggling had never been the plan. The plan had been written for her, in a dead man's hand, months before she knew how to read it.

"Major," she said, "may I ask you a question? In front of these gentlemen?"

Aldous's gaze flicked to the correspondent, and something crossed his face — not fear, precisely. Calculation meeting its limit. "Miss Montrose—"

"In April of this year," she said, and her voice carried in the high roofed dark the way her brother's laugh had once carried across a churchyard, "did you sign an order striking Private James Halloran Montrose from the casualty rolls of the Third Battalion?"

The name landed. She watched it land. She had said it aloud perhaps three times in three years, always in whispers, always into pillows or into the empty dark above her bunk, and saying it now in a room full of lamps and soldiers felt like setting a bird free from a cage she had built herself.

Aldous went very still. "That is a matter between officers."

"It is a matter between me and my dead brother," Rafael said. "And you made it a matter of paperwork."

She unbuttoned her coat with fingers that did not shake — later, she would not be able to explain why they didn't; the whole night had been spent shaking and now, at the bottom of it, there was this terrible calm, like the still eye of the storm she'd been walking through since spring. From inside her coat she drew not the packet — the packet stayed sewn beneath Ellery's blankets, warm against a living man's heartbeat — but her ledger. The ledger itself. Three years of struck names, every erasure, every ghost, in her small dutiful hand.

"This book records one hundred and forty-six men," she said. "Every name removed from official rolls while their families waited for letters that would never come. Every erased soldier whose pay stopped, whose mother stopped hoping, whose grave went unmapped — because someone decided the truth was administratively inconvenient." She laid it on the trestle table, open to no page in particular, open to all of them. "And Major Aldous signed the orders."

The correspondent's pencil had stopped. He was looking at the ledger now the way men look at wells in a desert.

"Gag her," Aldous said quietly, to the nearest military policeman. Then, catching himself, softer: "Miss Montrose, you are ill. You've been through a great deal. Sergeant, escort her—"

"I'll go willingly," Rafael said, and she raised her voice so that it reached the rafters and the hooks and the ghosts of grain, "willingly, tonight, before God and the gentleman from the press — if these documents reach the correspondent. All of them. The man on the stretcher carries papers that will tell you what my little book can only suggest. Names, dates, signatures — yours among them, Major, in your own tidy hand. Let them pass this door, and I am yours. Turn them back, and arrest me anyway — and then explain to the inquiry why a nurse's confession mattered more to you than a wounded private's blanket."

The customs house held its breath. Rain began again outside, soft on the cobbles, indifferent.

Aldous looked at her for a long moment. And Rafael saw, in that moment, the exact architecture of the man: not a monster, which would have been simpler, but an administrator, exhausted and afraid, who had signed away lives the way she recorded them — one line at a time, telling himself it was arithmetic, that history happened elsewhere, to other people. She had loved him. She let the love go the way she had finally learned to let the dead go: acknowledged, grieved, released.

"You were the finest nurse I ever posted," Aldous said at last, and it sounded like an epitaph. Perhaps it was.

"That," said Rafael, "is exactly the problem."

He gestured. Not to the guards. To the stretcher-bearers. "Pass the convoy," he said, and his voice was ash. "Through to the rail side."

The correspondent was already on his feet, crossing the floor, reaching for the seam of Ellery's blankets where her stitches showed white against army grey. Rafael walked with him, and together — his penknife, her guidance — they opened the seam and drew out the oilcloth packet, dry despite the night, intact, eight months of Elias Voss's stubborn living testimony. She put it in the correspondent's hands and closed his fingers over it.

"There's a page missing from the front," she said. "It says who gathered this. When you write it — and you will write it — say that too."

"And you?" the correspondent asked. "What do I call you?"

"Rafael Montrose. Sister, Queen Alexandra's." She smiled, and it hurt her face, and she did it anyway. "Formerly."

The handcuffs, when they came, were almost gentle. The sergeant who fitted them avoided her eyes, and she thought: *he has a mother too; somewhere there is a ledger with his name waiting in it.* Everything was connected now; she could see the whole terrible web, and seeing it did not make it lighter, but it made it hers.

Aldous walked her to the door himself. At the threshold he paused, and for one moment the major fell away and there stood only a tired man in a wet coat, watching the evidence of his war walk out of it on a correspondent's arm.

"They'll publish," he said. "And it will change nothing. It never changes anything."

"It changed me," Rafael said.

Then the door took her, and the grey morning received her, and the convoy rolled on toward the rails without her, carrying its cargo of names into the waking world. She did not look back. Looking back was for people who believed history happened elsewhere, and she knew better now; she had always known; she had simply been keeping the proof in a hidden book instead of her own chest.

The cell they gave her was small and dry and quiet, and after three years of mud and moaning and the endless arithmetic of the dying, quiet felt like an extravagance. She sat on the cot with her cuffed hands in her lap and counted her heartbeats, steadily, without flinching.

One hundred and forty-six names. One brother. One captain, dead by now — she felt it the way you feel a clock stop, in some inner chamber of the ear — and one book in a stranger's notebook, already growing legs.

Outside, far off, a train whistle sounded, long and clear, heading west.

Rafael closed her eyes and began, from memory, to write.

* * *

They came for her at dusk, but not with the questions she expected. The officer who unlocked the cuffs wore the red tabs of GHQ, and his face had the careful blankness of a man carrying news too large for his pockets.

"Sister Montrose," he said. "There is a hearing. Tomorrow. And there is something you should read before it."

He handed her a single sheet, typed, still warm from the machine. She scanned it once, twice, and her breath left her in a sound that was half laugh, half wound.

At the top of the page, under the seal of the inquiry, a list had begun — names being restored to the rolls, one by one, in official type. And beside each name, in the margin, a clerk's small notation:

*Source: ledger, R. Montrose.*
