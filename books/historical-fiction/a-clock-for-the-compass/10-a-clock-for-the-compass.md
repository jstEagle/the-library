## 10. A Clock for the Compass

The armistice came in November, and Rafael came home in February, when the snow on the platform at Ashford was grey as old dishwater and the porters moved with the shuffling unhurried grief of men who had spent four years loading other men's trunks toward other men's funerals. She stood a long while on the platform without moving, her kit bag hanging from one shoulder, and watched the steam drift along the rails the way fog had drifted along the communication trenches, and understood that she was waiting — though for what, she could not have said. No one met her. Her mother's letters had stopped in October, not from death but from exhaustion; the last one had said only, *The house is cold and I am tired of praying, come home and we will be quiet together.* So she waited until the steam thinned and the platform emptied, and then she walked out through the barrier alone, and no one asked her name.

They had taken everything from her at the customs house that night in March — the ledger's twin, Voss's pages sewn into Corporal Whitcombe's blanket, her own confession written out three times because her hand shook so badly the provost sergeant had made her begin again. They had taken her freedom too, eleven months of it, first in a cell in Rouen and then a room in a hospital in London where the windows were painted over so the Zeppelins would have nothing to aim at, and where the doctors were kind in the way of men who have decided already what is wrong with you. *Nervous disorder consequent upon overstrain,* the discharge paper read. She had signed it with the same pen they'd let her use to sign away her complaint, and she had thought, pressing the nib down hard enough to bite the paper: *You may keep the ink. The record is out of my hands now.*

Which was precisely where she had wanted it.

* * *

She found the inquiry report in March, at the circulating library on Wexford Street, in the third week of her habit of going there every morning as soon as it opened, because a room with no clocks ticking audibly was the only room in which she could sit still. The report was bound in blue cloth, dull as a school primer: *Report of the Board of Inquiry into Irregularities in Casualty Recording, Ypres Sector, 1916–1917.* Printed by His Majesty's Stationery Office. Four hundred and twelve pages, most of them tables. It sat on the shelf between a treatise on crop rotation and someone's memoirs of salmon fishing, and Rafael took it down with hands that did not shake at all, and carried it to the window seat, and did not open it for several minutes because she was afraid — genuinely afraid, the way she had been afraid crossing the rail yard — that it would be nothing. That the words would have gone soft in the machinery of committees. That the names would be tables again, and the tables would be appendices, and the appendices would be filed, and history would go on happening elsewhere.

Then she opened it, and there he was.

Not Voss by name — the inquiry had been scrupulous about the living and generous only to the dead — but his evidence, page after page of it, reproduced in facsimile with the handwriting she knew better than her own mother's: the tall ascenders, the impatient crossbar on the *t*, the way his sevens were always crossed twice as if once were insufficiently emphatic. *Annex C: Documents recovered from a courier intercepted near Poperinghe, authenticated by comparison with correspondence held at No. 7 Casualty Clearing Station.* Her hospital. Her correspondence. Herself, unnamed but present on every page, the way she had been present at every bed where a man died with his name still in his mouth.

And beneath the annexes, in the findings themselves, the names. Not struck. Restored. Private Daniel Okafor-Lund, previously recorded as deceased 14 October 1916, reinstated to the roll of the missing pending confirmation. Sergeant Amos Byrne. Gunner Pell. Corporal Tomas Ferreira — *granted immunity under provisions of testimony*, which meant he had lived, which meant the letter she had received from Lisbon the previous autumn was true, all of it, even the line at the bottom: *He would have liked the sea here. It is loud in a way that does not threaten.*

She read until the librarian touched her shoulder and told her, gently, that the library would be closing. She copied three passages into a notebook before she left. It did not occur to her until she was out on the pavement, breath clouding in the dark, that she had done it automatically, in the old shorthand of her ledger, and that her hand had remembered everything her captors had spent eleven months trying to make her forget.

* * *

Major Aldous — no longer Major, the newspapers said; *reduced to the ranks and dismissed the service in disgrace*, four words that contained none of the men, none of the ledgers, none of her brother — had shot himself in January. Rafael learned this from a paragraph on an inside page, wedged between shipping news and a scandal involving a bishop, and she stood in the kitchen of her mother's house with the newspaper still unfolded and felt — nothing. Or rather she felt the absence of the thing she had expected to feel, the triumphant burning she had imagined in the cell at Rouen, and what remained instead was only a tired arithmetic: a man who had believed erasure was a kind of mercy, or efficiency, or order, and who had discovered too late that some accounts cannot be closed by their keepers. She folded the newspaper neatly and gave it back to the neighbour from whom she had borrowed it, and she did not attend the funeral, and she did not forgive him, and both of those refusals felt, in time, like the same act of housekeeping.

Her brother's name appeared in the corrected rolls in April. *Lieutenant James Montrose, 2nd Battalion, previously reported killed in action 9 May 1916: record amended to reflect posthumous commendation for gallantry, location of grave identified.* Identified. After three years of a stone with no name under it, her mother walked out to the churchyard with the letter in her glove and read it standing up, and then sat down on the wet grass beside the plot she had tended blindly, and Rafael knelt with her and did not say *I did this* or *I know why it took so long* or any of the ten thousand things crowding her throat. Her mother reached over and took her hand, and pressed it against the earth, and said only: "You're thin. We'll feed you." And that, Rafael thought later, was also a kind of restoration — being returned to the ordinary claims of the living, meat and bread and someone noticing the state of you.

But there was one place left to go, and she delayed going there for six weeks, because she was a coward about exactly one thing.

* * *

She went in June, when the cemetery outside Poperinghe had turned gold at its edges with buttercups and the poplars along the canal road had put out new leaves over the stumps of old ones. The war was two years gone from the landscape and nowhere gone from it at all: the fields grew wheat now between the shallow dimples of shell craters, and farmers worked with long-handled probes in the ditches, and the whole country had the air of a patient who has learned to move carefully around the wound.

Voss lay in row three, plot forty-one, beneath a stone that gave his name and rank and the false date of his death — the spring date, the official one, cut into granite eight months before he actually bled out in a burning canal house covering a stranger's escape. Rafael crouched down and laid her fingers on the carved error and almost laughed. Even his gravestone was a casualty of bad bookkeeping. Even here, the record needed correcting.

"I brought you something," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the open air; she had not spoken aloud outdoors in months. "It isn't much."

She took the compass from her coat pocket. It had been his — the one Tomas had carried out of the canal house in his breast pocket, dented, the crystal cracked, the needle still swinging faithfully north whenever anyone bothered to check. Tomas had posted it to her from Lisbon wrapped in a page of Voss's final message, the words she had read so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth: *You were the record all along.* For a year the compass had lived in her drawer, and she had taken it out some nights and watched the needle settle, and understood at last what he had meant — that a compass does not tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. That had been the whole of it, all those muddy nights at the dressing station, telling herself that recording names was merely a habit, not defiance. She had never been watching history happen to other people. She had been standing inside it, taking dictation.

She dug a small hole in the turf at the head of the stone, set the compass in it face-up with the needle free, and packed the earth around it so the glass caught the light. A clock for a compass, a compass for a grave. Let the gardeners wonder. Let the grass grow over it slowly, and let the needle go on pointing, season after season, at a north that no longer required anything of her except that she remember where she stood.

"James's name is right," she told the stone. "All of them are. It took your pages and my ledger and Tomas's legs and Calloway's silence and God knows what else, and it took two years, and it's right." She paused. The wind moved through the buttercups. "I'm not going to say thank you, because you'd only correct my grammar."

She stayed until the shadows lengthened across row three, and then she walked back along the canal, past the warehouse that had been a safe house and was now a grain store, and she did not look away from it, and it looked back with nothing to say.

* * *

In July she bought a ledger. A plain one, cloth-bound, from the stationer on Wexford Street, and she chose it deliberately — good paper, sewn spine, a book that could survive mud and rain and eleven months in an evidence vault — because she had learned what books must be built for. She ruled it herself at her mother's kitchen table, columns drawn in careful pencil, and at the top of the first page she wrote in her own hand, plainly, no code:

*Register of Names Restored.*

Private Daniel Okafor-Lund. Sergeant Amos Byrne. Gunner Pell. Lieutenant James Montrose. Page after page, the inquiry's findings transcribed in full, each name entered with its date of restoration as the notices arrived — and they kept arriving, weeks behind the bureaucracy, letters from the Record Office correcting what had been wrongly ended, and every envelope she opened was a small resurrection. Word spread, as word does among people who have lost someone twice. Widows wrote to her. Mothers wrote. A man in Canada wrote asking after his entire platoon. Some mornings the hall table disappeared under envelopes, and her mother, who had never asked what the ledger was for, began carrying them in on a tray like correspondence of state.

Rafael answered every letter. She verified every claim against the blue-bound report, cross-checked the facsimiles, wrote to the Record Office in her careful clerk's hand demanding corrections with citations attached — and discovered, with a satisfaction that startled her, that she had become very difficult to refuse. Eleven months in a cell had taught her patience; four years of war had taught her the price of patience wasted.

On the last evening of September she sat at the kitchen lamp entering the day's names — nine of them, men from a Kentish regiment, restored after eighteen months of her correspondence — and her mother looked up from her mending and said, "There's another letter come today. I left it on the hall table. Foreign stamp."

Rafael wiped her pen and went out to the hall. The envelope was worn soft at the corners, as if it had travelled a long way and been carried in a pocket a long time after arriving. Lisbon postmark. Her own name in a hand she recognised instantly — the tall ascenders, the double-crossed sevens — writing that could not exist, dated three days ago, alive and recent and addressed to her.

She stood a moment with her thumb under the flap, the kitchen lamp warm at her back and the cold hall air on her face, and then she opened it, and unfolded the single sheet, and read the first line.
