## 1. The Weight of Water

The harbor woke before I did, the way it always did, and I resented it a little for that — for being first, for never needing sleep, for keeping its own hours the way a rich man keeps his. By the time I came down the Meridian stairs with my diving shoes over one shoulder and my slate in a canvas bag, the fog had already begun its slow argument with the water, and the gulls had started their ledger of grievances, and the first light came off the bay the color of old pewter.

I knew every hour of this place the way other people knew prayers.

Four o'clock: the tide turns, and the chains beneath Pier Three groan like an old man getting out of bed. Half past: the fishwives arrive with their barrows, their voices carrying across the water sharper than any bell. Five: the first company men in their good coats, checking manifests by lamplight, their boots on the planking above me sounding like a slow drum. Six, in summer, the sun finds the gap between the headlands and lays a stripe of gold across the water so precise you could set a clock to it — people did, once, before the harbor clock stopped, but that's a story nobody tells anymore because nobody remembers it.

I was seventeen years old and I could tell you the weight of this harbor at every hour of the day and night, and not one line of that knowledge would ever appear in a book.

That's what I believed, then. That history was something that happened to other people, in other places — to kings and parliaments, to capitals whose names we learned by rote in the charity schoolroom before my father drowned and schooling ended for me altogether. History lived in London, in Madrid, in whatever glittering elsewhere the newspapers described while the ink dried. Port Calloway was not history. Port Calloway was weather. It was tide tables and cargo manifests and the smell of brine and tar. History passed over us the way storms did, dropping a little wreckage here and there, and then went on to matter somewhere else.

I believed that the way you believe things when believing them is easier than the alternative. Which is to say: completely, and without knowing I was doing it.

* * *

The pilings under Pier Three were fouled green-black, as I'd known they'd be, because Old Maren had come by our door two evenings prior and told me so, leaning on his cane, his face folded into the particular scowl he reserved for neglected ironwork. "Your chains are wearing wool down there, girl," he said. "Wool grows heavy. Heavy sinks. You want to be explaining to the company why their coal barge sat low at the mooring?"

So at dawn, with the tide slack and gray, I went down.

There is a moment — every diver knows it, and if you don't dive, no description will give it to you — when the water closes over your head and the world above simply stops existing. The sounds go strange and rounded. The light comes down in shafts instead of sheets. Your body, which on land is a small and much-regulated thing, subject to whistles and foremen and the whole apparatus of the company's attention, becomes suddenly your own again, weightless and answerable only to the pressure in your ears and the pull of the current.

My father taught me that. Not the diving itself — that I'd learned from him too, but slowly, over years, the way you learn a language. What he taught me, really, was the *moment*. "The harbor takes everything else away, Juniper," he told me once, treading water beside me off the rocks below the customs house, his gray-streaked hair plastered flat. "Down here you're not the company's girl or the poorhouse girl or anybody's anything. Down here you're just a creature doing what creatures do." He smiled. My father had a smile like a crack in stone — rare, and worth waiting for. "The rest of it," he said, gesturing vaguely upward, toward the surface, toward the piers, toward the whole apparatus of ledgers and contracts and men in good coats, "the rest of it is just what they tell you the water means."

Then he went down on a bad log one March morning with three other men, and the harbor took him entirely, and the rest of it turned out to mean exactly what the company said it meant: four shillings for the funeral, a condolence letter with a printed signature, and an indenture contract placing his daughter, Juniper Vasquez, aged thirteen, in service to the Meridian Trading Company until her majority or her marriage, whichever came first, terms and conditions as appended.

I had read that contract so many times I could recite clauses of it like scripture. In a way, it was the only scripture I had.

I worked the pilings methodically, the way he'd taught me. Hand over hand along the barnacled timber, knife out, cutting the weed from the chain links where it had grown into felted ropes thick as my wrist. The work was simple and endless and honest, and there were mornings — this was one — when the simplicity of it felt almost like peace. Cut. Pull. Rise, breathe, note the position of the sun through the pier's slats. Dive again. The cold reached through me and found nothing to argue with.

Below Pier Three the harbor floor was silt and dropped coins and the small pale crabs that moved like nervous thoughts. I could work it blind. Some mornings I nearly did — let my hands do the knowing while my mind drifted up through the water and out across the roofs of the town, wondering, idly, uselessly, about the places history actually happened. What was it like, I used to wonder, to live in a city where things were decided? Where the streets themselves had been the site of some event worth remembering? Port Calloway had no such events. We had a founding, supposedly, sixty years back, when the first Meridian fleet came in — but the founding was a painting in the company countinghouse, all golden light and honest faces, and paintings aren't memory. Nobody's grandfather had stories. Or if they did, nobody asked after them, and the askers died out before the storytellers did.

Cut. Pull. Breathe. Dive.

By the time I surfaced for the last time, the sun was fully up and the fog was burning off in rags, and the pier above me had filled with the morning's traffic — cart wheels, boot heels, the thump of crates coming down the gangway of the early coaster. I pulled myself onto the ladder rungs and sat a moment on the lowest crossbeam, wringing the water from my braid, watching the harbor do what harbors do.

A collier taking on coal, her holds breathing black dust. Two company clerks crossing the planks with their heads together, one of them laughing at something the other said, both of them young enough that the laughter still came easily. A pearl-buyer's launch heading out toward the fleet anchorage, its brass fittings catching light. The fishwives' barrows rattling up the ramp road. Gulls. Rigging. Water slapping timber in that patient rhythm that goes on whether anyone listens or not.

I catalogued it all, because cataloguing was what I did. It was how I'd survived the year after my father died — by making lists in my head, ordering the unorderable grief into rows and columns, and the habit had never left me. Four o'clock, the chains groan. Six, the gold stripe. Seven, the collier. Eight, the clerks. Nine, the pearl-buyer. Ten, if the wind held southerly, the smell of the rendering works drifting across the flats so strong it made the newcomers gag and the rest of us breathe it like home.

It was a kind of love, I think, what I felt for the harbor then. But it was a resigned love, the love you feel for a thing you have already quietly agreed will never love you back, will never lift you anywhere, will hold you exactly where you are until it doesn't hold you at all. I mistook it for contentment. People usually do.

* * *

At half past eight I reported to the pier office, as required, to log the completed work. Mr. Havelock, my supervisor, sat behind his desk with the morning manifest open before him and his spectacles pushed up into his thinning hair, and he greeted me with the particular warmth he reserved for useful property.

"Vasquez. Chains cleared?"

"Three pilings, both chains, full length. There's a crack in the north piling about six feet down — old damage, growing. Someone should look at it before winter."

"I'll make a note." He did not make a note; he never made notes; the pilings would be looked at when they failed and not one hour sooner. He studied me instead, a moment longer than the transaction required, and something in the study put a faint cold finger against the back of my neck. "You'll take the afternoon run as well. Fouling on the Persephone's mooring lines."

"The Persephone?" She was new to me — a mail steamer, big, white-sided, lately arrived from London with the quarter's correspondence. "She's not ours."

"She's our client. And she sails next week on the morning tide, so whatever grows on her lines between now and then is our reputation. Understood?"

"Understood."

He returned to his manifest. I stood there a moment longer than I should have, because the cold finger hadn't lifted, and I couldn't have told you why. Havelock was always looking at me the way a man looks at inventory. This morning he had looked at me the way a man looks at a problem.

Outside, the harbor went on saying its hourly prayers. Nine o'clock: the rendering smell arriving on schedule, southerly wind, right on time. I stood on the planking with wet shoes in my hand and let the rhythm settle over me, that vast indifferent clockwork of tide and trade, and I remember thinking — with the strange precision memory sometimes grants to the moments just before everything changes — *this is all it will ever be. This is the whole of it. Tide, trade, fog, chains.*

History belonged to capitals. It had never once come to Port Calloway, and it never would, and there was even a comfort in that, a small dark shelter in being unimportant. Nothing that mattered could happen to a girl who cleaned chains. That was the bargain, and I had signed it, or rather it had been signed for me, four shillings' worth of funeral and a printed signature, and I had learned to live inside it the way you learn to live inside cold water: by not fighting, by breathing when allowed, by becoming, in the end, more comfortable under than up.

I climbed the stairs toward the countinghouse to collect my day's wage chit, and the fog was gone now, burned clean off, and the town lay open and ordinary in the light.

That was when I saw Elias Thorn come out the countinghouse door, and stop, and look at me as though I were a figure rising out of a page he had written long ago and hoped never to publish.
