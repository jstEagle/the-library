## 2. A Letter Sealed Twice

The countinghouse door had a bell, and the bell had a crack in it, so every person who entered Meridian Trading came in sounding slightly wrong — a note bent halfway, like a coin bitten by a suspicious teller. Juniper had heard that bell ten thousand times in seven years. She knew its vocabulary: the brisk double-strike of company men arriving with ledgers under their arms, the hesitant single chime of sailors come to settle accounts, the long grinding rattle of the delivery carts. That morning, just past eight, it rang in a way she'd never heard before — once, weakly, as though someone had leaned against it rather than opened it.

She looked up from the delivery manifest she was copying. Elias Thorn was standing in the doorway holding himself upright by the frame.

Everyone at Meridian knew Thorn. He was the oldest thing in the building that wasn't furniture — a narrow man gone narrower with age, clerk of the back office for forty years, keeper of records nobody asked about anymore. His hands had always trembled; now the trembling had climbed into his shoulders and his jaw. His skin had the gray-yellow tint of old paper left near a window. She'd seen that color before, on her father's crew, on men who'd spent too many years breathing compressed air and not enough years telling anyone about it. Thorn was dying. Not soon — now. It was in the room with him like a third person.

"Miss Vasquez." His voice was dry as a pulled cork. "A moment. Outside."

Mr. Pell, her supervisor, glanced up from his desk across the hall. Pell was a warm man the way a stove is warm — useful in winter, dangerous if you leaned too hard, always burning somebody's money. "Thorn," he said pleasantly, "you shouldn't be on the stairs. You'll fall and sue us for the railing."

There was laughter from the other clerks. Thorn did not laugh. He looked at Juniper the way a man looks at the last lit window in a dark street, and something in her chest went tight and strange.

"I'll be back before the nine o'clock boats," she said to Pell, already moving. Permission or no, he nodded, and she noted — filed away without understanding — that he watched her go with his pen still lifted, ink gathering at the nib like a drop about to fall.

The morning fog was lifting off the harbor in rags. Gulls worked the fish stalls along Quay Lane. Thorn steered her by the elbow — his grip astonishingly strong, fingers like mooring line — around the corner of the building, into the alley where the company kept its rain barrels and its refuse, where nobody stood unless they were hiding something or looking for it.

Then he pressed the letter into her hands.

It was sealed twice. That was the first thing she understood about it, in the half-second before she understood anything else: wax over wax, two different seals, one stamped with the Meridian compass-rose and the second, older one cracked down the middle by the pressure of the first. The paper itself was foxed with age at the edges, though the folds were crisp. Someone had kept this a long time and then, recently, refolded it.

"Take it," Thorn said. "Don't open it here."

"What is it?"

"It names your family." His breath whistled on the word *family*, thin and high, like wind through a chainplate. "The Vasquez line. In something old and rotten. Older than you, girl. Older than the company admits it is."

The harbor sounded very loud suddenly — halyards clinking, a steam tender coughing out at the breakwater, the whole wet machinery of Port Callawey turning over into its day. Juniper held the letter the way you hold something that might be alive. Her father had been dead eleven years; her mother six. What line, what family, what rottenness could reach down through all that water and touch her?

"Whose seal is the first one?" she asked. "Underneath?"

"A man who trusted me and shouldn't have." Thorn's eyes moved past her shoulder, up the alley, down it. Up again. "I counted everything in that building for forty years. Debts. Cargo. People. I know what the company weighs, down to the ounce." He swallowed, and she watched the effort it cost him, throat working like a man fighting depth. "It weighs more than it says it does. And the difference — the difference has a name written next to it, and the name is yours."

"You're ill," she said, because it was easier than listening. "You're confused—"

"I am dying," he agreed, with terrible mildness, "which is why I can finally afford to be honest. It's an expensive condition, Miss Vasquez. Honesty. Most people can't pay for it until the end."

Footsteps on Quay Lane. Thorn straightened with a speed that frightened her — a dying man moving like a soldier — and stepped back into the fog-shadow of the rain barrels.

"Keep it under your floorboards or against your skin. Don't let them see you carry it. And when you're ready—" his voice dropped so low she had to lean toward him, close enough to smell camphor and salt and something underneath both, sweetish, like fruit going over, "—when you're ready to know what your mother knew, read the second seal first."

"My mother?" Her heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. "What does my mother have to do with—"

But he was already shuffling out of the alley, small and bent and quick as a crab, and the last she saw of him that morning was his hand rising in something that was either a blessing or an apology.

* * *

Pell intercepted her at the door. Not at her desk — at the door, which meant he'd been waiting for it, which meant he'd seen the alley through the window or sent someone to watch, and she made her face smooth as slate before he got a good look at her.

"There she is." Warmth poured off him like heat off a boiler plate. Pell was forty and handsome in the way of well-kept things, silver coming in at his temples precisely where it flattered, waistcoat cut to suggest prosperity without boasting of it. He'd been kind to her since the day she arrived at fourteen, orphaned onto the indenture rolls, and she had never once questioned the kindness because you don't question the stove in winter. "Old Thorn giving you trouble? He's been peculiar lately. Peculiarer." He smiled to show the joke was affectionate.

"He wanted to know if the fouled chains were cleared," she said. "On the pilings. I dive them Tuesdays."

"Mm," said Pell, who signed off on her work orders every Tuesday and knew perfectly well what she did on Tuesdays. The warmth didn't waver. That was the thing about Pell's warmth — it didn't waver. It sat there being warm while his eyes went through her coat pockets, her sleeves, the set of her hands. "He's been asking after all sorts of things. Age does that. Makes a man want to tidy his accounts." He touched her shoulder, light as ash falling. "Juniper. If he bothers you again — anything odd, anything unpleasant — you come to me first. Before you speak to anyone else. Will you do that?"

"Why would he bother me?" she said. "I've spoken to him maybe six times in my life."

"Of course you haven't." The smile widened by exactly one tooth. "That's my girl. Nine o'clock boats won't wait. Off you go."

She went. She copied manifests all morning with the letter stiff against her ribs, sewn flat inside her bodice where she'd pinned it behind the counter of the washroom while Thorn's footsteps faded — a trick of her mother's, hiding small valuables against the body's heat, because *things carried in bags get set down, mija, but you never put yourself down.* At noon Pell brought her tea. Pell had never once in seven years brought her tea.

"Thought you looked pale," he said, setting it beside her elbow. "Harbor cold this time of year."

She thanked him. He stood there a moment longer than standing-there required, watching the steam rise off her cup as if reading it, and then he went back to his desk and began, softly, to whistle.

That was the afternoon she started noticing.

Briggs, the junior clerk, taking the same corner she took on her walk home, then peeling off with elaborate unconcern. The lamplighter on Cordwainer Street pausing opposite her boarding house door, doing nothing, lighting nothing, then remembering himself and hurrying on. Mrs. Hetch at the boarding house asking, over salt cod, whether she'd had *an interesting morning*, in the tone of someone who already knew the answer and wanted to hear how she'd tell it.

Port Calloway had always watched Juniper — that was what a harbor did, it watched, it logged arrivals and departures, it was one vast ledger with water in it. But watching had a different weight now. Before today she had been a fact in the harbor's record, unremarkable, a line item: *one indentured diver, minor, father drowned, no account of interest.* Today she had become an entry someone was amending.

* * *

That night she sat on her bed with a candle turned low and the letter in her lap, and did not open it.

She thought about opening it the way you think about diving a wreck you've only seen from the surface. She knew the mechanics — thumb under the fold, the wax cracking, paper unfolding like something surfacing. She'd done it a thousand times to shipping orders. This was different. This envelope had a pressure gradient to it. Open it wrong, fast, greedy, and it would do to her what the sea does to a lung that comes up still full of breath.

*It names your family.*

Her family was four lines long and all of them short. Father: drowned 1887, pearl diver, debt inherited by daughter per contract. Mother: dead 1892, laundress and oyster-shucker, said little, sang less. No siblings. No property except a diver's slate she hadn't looked at since the funeral, and a brass weighting belt that fit Juniper now better than it had ever fit him. What could a piece of paper name in that? What could it possibly accuse?

And yet Thorn had said *your mother knew* — no. He hadn't said that. She replayed it, lying in the dark with the gulls quarreling on the pilings below the window. He'd said, *when you're ready to know what your mother knew.* As if knowing were a place her mother had been. As if there were territory involved.

She got up and knelt at the floorboard seam she knew — every child in the harbor district knew their floorboards — and reached into the gap where the joist met the wall, where her fingers had learned the shape of dust and mouse bones, and found that the space was empty. Of course it was empty. She had never put anything there. But her hand stayed, curled in the cold dark between the floors, and she realized she was waiting for it to tell her something, and she made herself pull it out, and blow out the candle, and lie down.

Sleep came slow. When it came, she dreamed of her mother at eight fathoms, hair drifting like kelp, writing on the slate with steady strokes while little Juniper hung in the blue watching the white letters bloom: *breathe out slow, breathe out slower, the water takes what panics and leaves what waits.* Then her mother turned and looked at her through the mask, eyes enormous and calm, and wrote one word more — and Juniper woke before she could read it, heart going hard, the letter a small rigid weight beneath her pillow like a second skull.

* * *

Tuesday she dove the pilings, because Tuesday was Tuesday and because if Pell wanted her predictable, she would give him predictability until she decided otherwise. The chains were foul with weed and she cleared them methodically, blade in hand, working the cold brown green light, and all the while the letter waited in the company locker above her head like a held breath, and all the while she turned Thorn's words over in the pressure and the dark.

*Something old and rotten.* Every port town had its founding story, polished smooth as beach glass: the brave fleet of '41, the founders' foresight, the clock raised over the customs house that had run true for a year and stopped the day the first charter was signed — stopped, the old divers said, and never been right since, though the harbormaster had it wound every Sunday. History, as far as Juniper had ever been taught it, happened in capitals, to ministers and fleets and men whose portraits hung in frames. It did not happen in the alley behind the countinghouse to a girl scraping weed off chain.

Except apparently it had. Apparently it had happened to her family specifically, by name, twice-sealed.

She surfaced beside the piling, spat her mouthpiece, hung on the chain with the harbor slopping cold around her chin, and looked back at the shore: the countinghouse with its granite face, the customs house clock reading twenty past nine as it had read twenty past nine in some legend or other for fifty years, the forest of masts, the smoke, the gulls wheeling their arguments overhead. A city built on water over mud over whatever else was down there. She had spent seventeen years believing she stood at the very bottom of that city's attention.

Now someone was climbing down to look at her, and they were bringing a lamp.

She climbed the ladder, wrung out her hair, and unlocked the company locker. The letter was where she'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it, weighed it in her palm — heavier than paper had any right to be, or maybe that was suggestion — and read again the faint ghost of the second seal beneath the first, the one her mother had known, the one she was supposed to open *first*, whatever that instruction meant, because how could a seal be second and first at once—

"Careful with company property, Miss Vasquez."

She nearly dropped it. Briggs stood at the top of the pier ladder, grinning, holding two mugs of tea, and she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water in her ears that the tea was not for drinking and the grin was not friendly, and that from this moment forward every kindness in Port Calloway would need to be inspected for seals.

She wrapped the letter back in oilcloth, sewed it into the lining of her coat that evening with her mother's sailmaker's needle, and lay awake listening to the tide turn, and did not sleep at all.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: Thorn had chosen *her*. Out of everyone in that building, everyone in that city, a dying man who had counted everything had walked past forty years of colleagues to press a secret into the hands of a diving girl. He hadn't chosen her because she was safe. He'd chosen her because she was *in it* — because the name on the rotten page wasn't just any name, it was hers, and somewhere under her floorboards or in her mother's grave or sunk in the harbor silt with her father, the rest of the proof was already waiting.

The dead in her family had been quiet for eleven years.

They weren't quiet anymore.
