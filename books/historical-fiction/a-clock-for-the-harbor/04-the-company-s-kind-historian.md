## 4. The Company's Kind Historian

He came on a Tuesday, when the fog lifted just enough to show the masts like stitches in a wound.

Juniper knew him before he knocked. She'd seen his photograph in the *Gazette* — Mr. Aldous Crane, Historian to the Meridian Trading Company, author of *The Founding Fleet: An Account in Good Faith*, a book she had once used to prop open the countinghouse window and been scolded for. He was younger than the photograph. That was the first dishonest thing about him. The second was the basket of gifts balanced on one arm like it weighed nothing at all.

"Miss Vasquez." He removed his hat in the doorway of her rented room, which meant he had to duck, which meant he had chosen to stand in the doorway rather than be invited in, and that choice was itself a kind of message. "I hope you'll forgive the intrusion. I've been wanting to make your acquaintance for some time."

"Have you."

"Since your father's accident." The word *accident* arrived smooth as river stone, worn of every edge. "I write the company's histories, you see. And your father is part of that history — a small part, but no less valued for its size. May I?"

He held up the basket as though asking permission to enter a church. Juniper stepped aside because refusing would have required an explanation, and explanations were currency she didn't have. Crane set the basket on her table with the care of a man arranging museum pieces: a tin of Ceylon tea, a paper of candied ginger, a bolt of blue ribbon, and a book. His book. New edition, gilt letters.

"The tea is from Mr. Halloran's own stock," he said. "He asks after you, you know. The whole countinghouse does. You've made something of a name among them, clearing those chains the way you do. Diving like your father taught you."

"My father didn't teach me."

"No?" Crane smiled, and the smile was warm, genuinely warm, and that frightened her more than coldness would have. "Forgive me. I invent sometimes. It's an occupational failing. A historian who cannot imagine is only a clerk, and a historian who imagines too much is a liar, and the trick of my whole profession lies in knowing where the line runs." He glanced around her room — the narrow bed, the slate hanging by the door, the floorboards she had not yet put back quite straight. "You keep the slate. Most divers give them up when they come ashore."

"It was my mother's."

"Then it's an heirloom. How rare, how fine, to have objects that remember." He touched the brim of his hat again. "I'll take only a quarter hour of your time, Miss Vasquez. I'm writing a new chapter, you see. On the harbor families. The founding families of Port Calloway — the ones who built the piers your chains hold fast. And there are rumors going about, ugly little rumors, and I should hate for your name to be caught in them."

Juniper stood very still. Behind her ribs, the sealed letter sat where she had hidden it three days ago, sewn into the lining of her mother's old coat, and she felt it the way you feel a splinter gone deep — not always, but whenever the weather changed.

"What rumors?" she asked, and hated how young her voice sounded.

* * *

*Aldous Crane, working notes for* The Meridian Century, *chapter the third, "Beneath the Generous Sea":*

There is a temptation, in this trade, to believe the past is a country one visits. It is not. The past is a tenant; it pays no rent; and if you do not furnish its rooms yourself, someone hungrier will. I learned this at my father's knee, or rather at his desk, for he kept accounts for a shipping concern in Bristol and taught me that a ledger is merely a story told in columns, and that whoever chooses the columns chooses the story. He was ruined by men who understood this better than he did. I resolved never to be ruined twice.

Port Calloway is a gift to any honest historian, by which I mean any historian who knows what honesty is *for*. Here is a city built on pearl, salt, and salvage, and here is a company — Meridian — that has fed it for seventy years, and here is the beautiful difficulty: the founding fleet of 1829 carried certain cargoes the manifests do not name, and certain names the manifests do not carry, and somewhere between what happened and what was written down, a legend must be constructed, load-bearing, seaworthy, and kind to everyone still living who can afford to read.

The girl is the difficulty.

Vasquez. The name appears twice in the old correspondence, both times in Thorn's cramped hand, both times circled. Elias Thorn, poor dying moth, kept papers he should have burned and burned papers he should have kept, and now he is coughing his way toward God with the whole architecture of Meridian folded in his coat. If the letter reaches her — and I am assured it already has — then the story I am building has a crack in its keel shaped exactly like a seventeen-year-old girl who dives harbor pilings before dawn and believes, bless her, that history is something that happens to other people.

The remedy is not violence. Violence is what amateurs reach for when they've run out of imagination. The remedy is a better story, told sooner, told kindly, with her own face in it. Every witness wants two things: to matter and to be safe. Offer the first sincerely and the second conditionally, and they will hand you their memories like laundry off a line.

I brought her tea from Halloran's stock. Small kindnesses are ballast. They keep a narrative upright in rough water.

* * *

"You'll understand," Crane said, folding himself onto the one chair she owned without being asked, so that refusal was no longer possible, "that I mean this as protection. There are men in this city — I won't flatter them with titles — who hear a word like *fraud* attached to Meridian and begin asking questions of anyone connected to it. Dockworkers. Divers. Orphans." He said *orphans* gently, an apology in advance for the word. "An indentured girl with a dead diver's name would make a very convenient person for such men to talk to. Do you follow me?"

"I think you're telling me to keep quiet."

"I'm telling you to be *accurate*." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register of a man sharing a confidence across a card table. "Slander against benefactors, Miss Vasquez — that's the legal term, and it's an ugly one. The company fed you after your father drowned. Paid your indenture fees, found you lodging, kept you off the mudflats where girls with your ledger debts end up." He let that sit. Then, softer: "And none of that need ever be spoken of as debt. That's what I'm offering. Not silence — *proportion*. A history where everything is given its right weight. Your father a hero of the harbor. Your mother—" he paused, delicately, "—a woman of her time. And you, Juniper Vasquez, in the acknowledgments of the definitive account. Imagine it. Your name printed in a book that will outlive us all."

For one shameful moment she did imagine it. Her name, in gold, in a room she'd never enter, belonging to a world she'd only scrubbed the windows of.

Then she thought of the letter's last line, the one she'd read eleven times by candlelight: *the Vasquez line was party to the founding arrangement, and the arrangement was theft.*

"Why does my family's name frighten the company?" she asked.

Crane's expression did not change. That was the skill of him. A lesser man would have blinked, laughed, reached for his hat. Crane simply went on smiling with the patience of a portrait.

"Frighten?" He rose, brushed invisible dust from his coat. "My dear girl, nothing frightens Meridian. Companies cannot be frightened; they can only be *corrected*. I correct them for a living." At the door he stopped, half-turned, hat in hand, and delivered the rest as lightly as a weather report. "Your contract came up in review this morning, incidentally. Routine. But reviews can go slowly, and slow contracts go to the review office on Harbor Island, and Harbor Island is where we send the ones whose paperwork has grown... complicated. I do hope yours stays simple. Simple paperwork, simple life. They rhyme, don't you find?"

Then he was gone down the stairs, his footsteps light, almost cheerful, and Juniper stood in her small room with the gift basket gleaming on the table like bait on a hook that hadn't even needed water.

She picked up his book. *The Founding Fleet: An Account in Good Faith.* She turned to the flyleaf, where he had written while she wasn't looking — she hadn't even seen him take out the pen — in a hand so elegant it looked typeset:

*To Miss Juniper Vasquez, whose family's part in our harbor's story deserves to be told truly. — A.C.*

It sounded like a promise. It was a warning. Somewhere in that man's leather satchel, or in the drawers of the countinghouse, or already set in type at the *Gazette*, her father and her mother were being measured, trimmed, and fitted for a story with no room in it for the truth. The past was not a ruin lying quiet under the harbor. It was alive, and it was being edited, and she was standing on the page.

She pulled her mother's coat from under the bed and pressed her thumb against the seam where the letter hid.

If Crane was writing the harbor's history with gifts and smiles, then someone had to write it back. And there was one woman left alive who had dived beside her father, who had hands that remembered what the founding fleet carried, and whose tongue the company hadn't yet bought or buried.

Old Maren lived up past the tar sheds, and Juniper had promised herself she'd visit someday, when it was safe.

Crane had just explained that someday would never come.
