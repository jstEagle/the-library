## 5. Divers' Tongues

Old Maren lived at the wrong end of Gull Row, where the houses leaned together like drunkards holding each other up, and the smell of the harbor at low tide was honest about what it was. Juniper had been there twice in her life: once when her father died, when Maren had come to *her* instead, standing in the doorway of their rooms above the chandlery with his hat in his hands and nothing useful to say; and once more, years later, when her mother sent her with a basket of oranges and instructions not to ask questions.

She carried no oranges now. She carried a letter sealed twice and a diver's slate wrapped in oilcloth, and she took the long way through the net-menders' yard so that anyone watching from the countinghouse would have to work for it.

Maren's door was open to the morning because his stove was lit and the chimney drew badly. He sat just inside it, in a chair made from a barrel and half a deck plate, mending a mask he would never again have use for, his hands moving over the rubber with the tenderness of an old man touching the face of someone long dead.

He looked up. His eyes were the pale gray of water over sand, and they found the oilcloth bundle before they found her face.

"Vasquez girl," he said. "You've got your father's walk. Like the ground owes you money."

"Maren."

"You've got your mother's way of saying a name, too. Like it's a coin she's deciding whether to spend." He set the mask down. "Sit, if you're sitting. The kettle's on if the kettle's on."

The room held one bed, one stove, one wall of hooks from which hung diving things in various stages of retirement — masks, weight belts, a knife with a handle worn smooth as bone. And above them all, framed in driftwood, a single photograph gone sepia: eleven men in canvas suits and brass helmets, arranged on the deck of a tender barge like a crew portrait from some navy that never existed. Her father stood third from the left, young, mustached, one hand resting on the shoulder of the man beside him. That man was Maren, forty years younger, grinning like the whole enterprise was a joke only divers were in on.

Juniper sat on the stool by the door. She unwrapped the slate and laid it on the table between them.

Maren did not touch it. But something moved behind his face, slow as a tide turning.

"Where'd you get that," he said, and it was not quite a question.

"The floor of my mother's house."

"Your mother's house." He said it the way other men said *the wreck site*. Then he poured two cups of tea he had not offered, pushed one across, and drank from his own without looking at it. For a long while the only sound was the stove ticking and, far off, the harbor bell working its one idea.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Maren said finally. "And I want you to understand something first. I have told this story to no one. Not to the priest, who asked. Not to the widow Okonkwo, who guessed. Not to your father's grave, which deserved it more than anybody." He turned the cup in his hands. "Because a story you tell is a story that can be taken away from you, and everything else I had was already taken."

"I need to know," Juniper said.

"I know you need to know. Your mother needed to know too. Look where needing got her." But he was already leaning forward, elbows on knees, and Juniper understood that the refusal was ceremony, that the story had been waiting forty years for a door to knock on.

* * *

"In 1852," Maren said, "I was seventeen, which made me the youngest helmet-man on the Meridian founding fleet, and proud of it in the way that only a boy with empty pockets can be proud. Eleven of us under contract, plus your father, who was nineteen and free-diving then — no brass, no air hose, just lungs and hate for the surface. We came out of Port Calloway with six ships. Six. You've seen the painting in the countinghouse lobby, the one where the fleet looks like a flock of white birds going out to gather pearls for the benefit of mankind."

"I've seen it."

"It's a lie with a frame on it." He drank. "Here is what the painting leaves out. We loaded at night, second night out, past the point where the customs cutter patrols. Two longboats came alongside the *Meridian Star* — that was the flagship — and we took aboard crates. Forty-some of them. Heavy, sealed, marked as pearl cases, and heavy in the wrong way. A case of shell weighs what a case of shell weighs; every diver knows it in his arms the way a mother knows her own child's weight. These cases weighed double. And they didn't clack. Shell clacks. These went *thunk*, like they were packed in metal."

Juniper's hands had gone still around her cup. "What was in them?"

"At the time, we weren't told. Later, when it stopped being possible to not-know, I worked it out same as everyone who could add: arms. Muskets and powder, most likely, run to the islands against every treaty your history books say we signed. Meridian didn't found itself on pearls, girl. Pearls don't pay for a fleet. Contraband pays for a fleet. Pearls are what you tell the customs house."

"But the letter—" She stopped. The letter named fraud, names, sums. "There was more."

"There was more." Maren's voice dropped, not dramatically, but the way a man lowers his voice near open water. "We dove the Verano shoals that season, and the shells were good, but here's the part Crane's books will never hold: the manifests came back wrong. Every port, every year, the tonnage we shipped out never matched the tonnage recorded. The difference wasn't error. The difference was skimmed — pearls and shell sold off the books in San Francisco and Valparaíso, the proceeds banked under company names that existed for exactly as long as it took to move the money. Your countinghouse, girl, was built on a second set of numbers, and the second set of numbers was built on our backs. Divers died that season. Three of ours, and the company logged them as lost to cramp. Cramp doesn't come from being sent down on fouled lines with worn hoses because safe gear costs money and dead divers cost less than safe gear."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below Gull Row a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Your father found the second ledger," Maren said. "I don't know how. He was free-dive, no helmet, and he used to say the water told him things it kept from brass-heads. What I know is this: in '61 he came to me on the dock, and he was gray, and he said, *Maren, they keep two books, and in one of them we are all thieves.* And I told him to shut his mouth, because a man I knew had said the same thing at a card game in '58 and was drowned inside the month — officially cramp, in eight fathoms, wearing a belt of the wrong weight." Maren looked at the photograph on the wall. "I told my friend to be quiet. And he tried, for your mother's sake. He dove and kept his head down and raised you, and he thought silence was a country he could live in. Then DeWitt started altering the dive logs — I saw the pages afterward, girl, I mended nets in that office for three winters and I watched that man's hand write depths your father never went — and one day the water gave your family back its silence, in a box, at low tide."

Juniper did not remember standing, but she was standing. The room had gotten small.

"They killed him," she said. "For the ledger."

"For what he could have done with it." Maren rose, joints cracking like line under strain, and took down the photograph. He touched the glass over his own grinning face with one crooked finger. "Eleven of us in this picture. You know how many are alive? One." He tapped his own chest. "Every year there's fewer witnesses and more chapters in Crane's beautiful book. He doesn't have to kill us anymore. He just has to wait. Time is the company's oldest employee and it has never once asked for wages."

* * *

He fed the stove while Juniper sat back down, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — quieter, wearier, the voice of a man setting down something heavy after carrying it up a long stair.

"Your mother knew. That's the hardest thing I'll ever say to you, and I'll say it once. After your father drowned, she came to me. Asked me what I'd seen, whether I'd testify, whether there was paper. And I said yes to the first two and no to the third, because I hadn't seen paper, I'd only seen crates. And she thought on it a long time by the window, and then she said, *Then we keep it in the water where they can't burn it.* And I understood she meant memory. Hers, mine, the water's. She wrote things, I expect — your mother was slate-tongued from a diving family older than the company — but she never showed me and I never asked, because asking is how people get followed home." He hung the picture back on its hook. "She chose you over the war, Vasquez girl. Don't you dare decide in one week that she chose wrong."

"She didn't choose anything," Juniper said. "She was afraid."

"Afraid is a kind of choosing. So is brave. Most days they use the same muscles." Maren sat again, heavily, and looked at her the way he must have looked at depth gauges — reading the needle, not the glass. "Now. The real question. You came here with a dead woman's slate and a dying clerk's letter, and you're sitting in the one house in Port Calloway the company doesn't watch, because watching me costs money and I'm worth more quiet than watched. So ask it."

"Ask what?"

"The question you climbed my stairs carrying. Not *should I*. That one's already answered or you'd be at the pilings right now, scraping chain and thanking Meridian for your bread." He leaned forward. "Ask me *how*."

And there it was, laid open between them like a deck plate lifted off its screws. Juniper found that her hands had stopped shaking somewhere during the story, and she examined this the way she examined the harbor bottom: factually, without flinching. She had spent seventeen years believing history was a weather that happened elsewhere, to capitals, to men in rooms. But the room had come to her — had been coming to her all along, in crates and falsified logs and a slate under floorboards — and the only question left was the practical one, the diver's question: what is between me and the surface, and in what order do I deal with it?

"How many are left," she said. "Who saw. Who knows. Who would sign their names."

Maren smiled, and it was not a warm thing, but it was a true one.

"Count with me," he said. "Me. Sam Okonkwo's mother kept the tally-books for the longshoremen's fund and read the manifests herself — boy's got her arithmetic in his blood, even if he doesn't know what it adds to yet. There's a watchman at the customs house who was bribed twice and remembers both prices. There's DeWitt, who's dying of conscience slower than Thorn is dying of everything else, and a guilty man can be made to write things down. And there's Elias Thorn, who counted everything, and if he copied what I think he copied, then the second ledger isn't a story anymore, girl. It's evidence." He held up one finger. "But hear the terms. Every one of those people is frightened, and fright is the company's favorite currency — it spends better than gold and it never inflates. If you go collecting testimony, you are not gathering facts. You're gathering frightened people and asking them to stop being frightened in writing. Some will say no. Some will say yes and mean no. And one or two might say yes and die before the ink dries, and you'll carry that."

"I already carry it," Juniper said. "My father's been dead fifteen years and I've been carrying him since."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then he reached to the wall of hooks, took down the old knife with the bone-smooth handle, and pressed it into her hands — hilt first, the way you hand off anything in deep water.

"Your father's," he said. "He left it in my kit the last dive and I was too much of a coward to return it to a drowned man's daughter. Consider the debt paid." He closed her fingers over it. "You want to know how? You do it the way we do everything down there. Slow. On a line. With somebody topside watching your air. You find the paper first — paper before people, because paper can't be threatened and people can. Thorn copied something, and half of it's hidden somewhere in that customs house like ballast in a hull. You get the paper, girl. Then you get the people. In that order, or not at all."

"Why are you helping me?" It came out rougher than she meant. "You kept silent forty years."

Maren looked at the photograph, at the eleven young men grinning on the deck of a lie, and his voice went almost gentle.

"Because silence was a country," he said, "and I've lived in it long enough. And because a witness who won't testify is just an audience." He nodded at the slate, at the letter somewhere under her coat. "Six ships went out. One truth comes back. Go find the rest of it — and mind the tide tables, girl. Whatever you're planning, the company's planning faster."

She was halfway to the door when his voice caught her, one last cast of the line from topside.

"Vasquez girl. The Persephone takes on her mail cargo Thursday next. If I were paper that wanted to leave this harbor forever, that's the ship I'd sail on."

The fog outside had thinned to lace. Juniper walked down Gull Row with her father's knife against her ribs and the beginnings of a timetable assembling itself in her head, tide by tide, name by name — and behind her, in the doorway of the last house on the row, Old Maren stood watching until she was out of sight, then went inside and took down the photograph, and began, for the first time in forty years, to pack.
