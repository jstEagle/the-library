## 6. Indenture Is a Long Word for Chain

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded inside her pay envelope.

*Miss Vasquez — Mr. Crane requests the pleasure of your company at the countinghouse, seven o'clock Thursday evening. A matter concerning your contract.*

Seven o'clock. After the day shift ended, after the clerks went home, after the building emptied of everyone whose presence might be called a witness. Juniper read it three times on the dock with the wind pulling at her collar, and each time the word *pleasure* sat worse in her stomach.

Sam Okonkwo, coiling rope beside her, glanced at the paper. "Crane himself?"

"Crane himself."

"He doesn't summon divers."

"No," Juniper said. "He summons problems."

She thought about not going. She turned it over all Wednesday — the letter hidden in the flour tin where her mother's slate had been, because she had learned that much already: nothing you want to keep should live where hands can reach it. She could stay home. She could claim sickness. She could walk out the harbor gate and keep walking until Port Calloway was a rumor behind her.

But indenture did not permit rumor. Indenture permitted exactly what was written in it, and what was written in it was that she belonged to Meridian Trading Company until her twenty-first year or until her debt was discharged, whichever came later, and the company decided which words meant what. She had signed it at eleven years old with a mark she barely remembered making, her father's hand guiding hers, both of them believing the company that fed them could not also be the thing they needed feeding from.

Thursday came down gray and cold. At six-fifty she stood before the countinghouse, its windows lit gold against the fog, and told herself the fear in her chest was only the cold.

The door was unlocked. The hall was empty. Somewhere above, a lamp burned.

* * *

The office was not Crane's. She understood that immediately — it was too small, too far from the front of the building, a room for work rather than display. A desk, two chairs, a green-shaded lamp throwing light in a hard circle and leaving the corners dark. A file lay squared on the desk like something laid out for surgery.

Crane rose when she entered, and his warmth was so practiced it took her a moment to see it was warmth at all, the way a painted face takes a moment to read as paint.

"Miss Vasquez. Thank you for coming." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit, please. You're wet through. The harbor's been cruel this week."

"I dive chains," she said. "It's always cruel."

"Yes." He smiled as if she'd said something charming. "Your father was the same way, I'm told. A man who made peace with the water. It's a rare quality. I've written about men like him."

"I know," Juniper said. "I've read your history."

Something moved behind his eyes — pleasure, quickly banked. "Have you? Then you know I take care with people. With their stories. That's all I am, really. A keeper of stories." He opened the file without looking down at it, the gesture smooth as dealing cards. "Which brings me to why we're here. There's been a question raised about your contract. A routine review. I want to assure you at the outset that no one is accusing you of anything."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because reviews are conversations, Miss Vasquez. Accusations are trials. I much prefer conversations." He laced his fingers. "Tell me about Elias Thorn."

The name landed in the quiet room like a dropped coin. Juniper kept her face still — years of holding your breath underwater teaches you what a face can hide — but her heart began counting.

"Mr. Thorn held the door for me once," she said. "He seemed ill."

"He is ill. Dying, in fact, though he's fought it longer than any man has a right to." Crane tilted his head. "He spoke to you at the door. I'm told at some length, for a dying man."

"Told me to mind the step."

"Mm." Crane looked at the file. He didn't open it further; he simply rested his hand on it, the way a man rests a hand on a dog's head to keep it still. "Juniper. May I call you Juniper? Your father worked for this company nineteen years. Your mother ran a mending stall on Pier Four under our awning, rent-free, because your father asked and Mr. Harrow granted it. You have eaten from this company since you were old enough to chew. I say none of this as leverage. I say it because it's true, and truth is my trade, and I find that when two people start from true things, the conversation goes better."

"You're saying I owe you."

"I'm saying gratitude is a kind of ledger too." He leaned back. "Now. A sealed letter has gone missing from Mr. Thorn's effects. Papers belonging to the company. If such a letter found its way into your hands — if someone pressed it on you, perhaps, out of sentiment, an old man confused by his illness — then returning it would be a small kindness, easily forgotten. But if that letter were instead used in some fashion — shown about, quoted, offered to newspapers or to persons unfriendly to the company — well." He sighed, genuinely, she thought, or nearly genuinely. "Then it becomes theft of corporate property by an indentured ward. And theft changes the arithmetic entirely."

The lamp hissed. Out in the fog, a ship's bell counted the half hour, and Juniper heard it the way she heard everything now, as a mark against a clock she couldn't see.

"What's in the letter?" she asked.

Crane's smile widened by a degree. "You see, that question is exactly the problem. An innocent girl wouldn't ask it."

"An innocent girl wouldn't be summoned to a dark office after hours, either."

For the first time, the polish cracked — just a hairline, just long enough for her to see the thing underneath, which was not anger exactly but appetite, patient and cold. Then it sealed over again, smoother than before.

"Fair," he said. "Fair. Let me be plainer, then, since plainness seems to be your language. Your contract is under review. Reviews can conclude in several ways. They can conclude that the ward's conduct has been exemplary and her remaining term commuted — freedom, Juniper, real papers, your own name signed in your own hand, no one's permission required ever again. Or they can conclude that the ward has been involved in irregularities, in which case the matter leaves my office and enters the courts, and the courts in these matters have one destination." He let the pause stretch. "You know the island."

Everyone knew the island. Half a mile off the breakwater, low and treeless, where debtors were housed in a long shed and set to breaking ballast stone until their debts were worked off at rates no honest arithmetic could survive. Her father had once pointed at it and said, *That's where they send people who count wrong.* She had been nine. She had laughed, because her father's debts were paid and her own were only beginning.

"The letter names my family," she said quietly. "Doesn't it. That's what Thorn meant. *The Vasquez line.*"

Crane's expression did not change, and the fact that it did not change told her more than any change would have.

"Old men say many things," he said gently. "Dying men especially. They confuse the past with the present, debts with sins, other families with their own. If I told you how many deathbed confessions I've run to ground in thirty years of this work, you'd be astonished. Nine in ten dissolve under a lamp like this one." He closed the file. "But here is what dissolves and here is what doesn't. What doesn't dissolve is paper. Contracts. Records. Your signature, your father's mark, the terms you agreed to. Those are real, Juniper. Those are *history*. And history is very protective of itself."

"That's not what history is," she said, and heard her own voice come out lower and harder than she intended. "History is what happened. To my father at the bottom of the channel. To my mother under the floorboards of a house this company owned. History isn't your file. History is the water."

The lamp hissed. Crane regarded her for a long moment, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its warmth entirely, and somehow that was almost a relief — like surfacing, like cold clean air after too long below.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the diver's daughter. Good. I prefer honesty; charm is exhausting on both sides." He stood, buttoned his coat. "Here is my final word, and it is a generous one. You will bring me whatever papers Mr. Thorn gave you by Monday morning. In exchange, the review concludes favorably, your term is commuted at year's end, and no one — including yourself — need ever think about this again. You'll dive chains for eight more months and then you'll be free, and free girls forget things very quickly. It's one of freedom's best features."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Monday afternoon the review concludes otherwise, and you'll spend the week learning what the island does to girls who count wrong." He gathered the file and paused at the door, and there — just once — he looked almost kind, which was the worst thing yet. "Your father trusted this company with his life, Juniper. Literally. Don't make his daughter the one who betrays it."

The door clicked shut behind him. She sat alone in the lamplight for a long time, listening to the fog press against the windows, and discovered that her hands were shaking — not with fear, or not only with fear, but with something hotter underneath it, something that had been waiting years for a name.

They fed her. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, the thing she couldn't argue away. Every meal since she was eleven had come out of Meridian's stores. The awning over her mother's stall. The boots on her feet. The company had fed her the way a farmer feeds a hog, and she had called it kindness because the alternative was hunger, and now she knew — knew in her bones, in the slate-numbers under her floorboards, in a dead man's whisper at a doorway — what the feeding was *for*. What it purchased. What it silenced.

Her father had died keeping their ledgers safe. Had gone down on falsified logs, on rotten lines, because he knew something or suspected something or simply because a company that cheats on paper cheats everywhere, and the water keeps no records except the ones it drowns.

Juniper stood. She blew out the lamp herself — a small defiance, pointless, delicious — and walked out through the empty countinghouse with her spine straight and her fury banked down to a coal she could carry.

Monday. He'd given her until Monday. Which meant he believed she still had the letter, which meant he didn't know about the customs house, which meant somewhere in this city Elias Thorn might still be breathing, and every hour she spent afraid was an hour Crane spent writing her out of the story.

She stepped into the fog and turned, not toward the divers' rows, but toward the charity ward on Marsh Lane — toward the one man alive who knew what the original ledger said, and whether a coward's confession, delivered late, could still weigh enough to tip a scale.

The tide, she thought, would wait for no one. Neither would she.
