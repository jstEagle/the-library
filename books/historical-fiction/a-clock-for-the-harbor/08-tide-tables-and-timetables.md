## 8. Tide Tables and Timetables

The tide table for Port Calloway hung in the harbor office behind a glass case, and every morning of my life I had walked past it without reading it, the way you stop seeing a wall. On the sixth morning after Thorn pressed his letter into my hands — the letter that named the Vasquez line in something old and rotten — I stopped.

I read the whole table twice.

It was a kind of scripture, if you let it be. High water at 4:17, low at 10:32. Springs building toward the new moon. And there, circled in the harbormaster's own red ink like a wedding date: *Persephone departs on the morning tide, Friday.*

Six days.

I stood there with wet rope burns on my palms and my hair still stiff with salt from the dawn dive, and I understood something so simple it embarrassed me. All week I had been asking questions as if the questions were the point. Who burned the ledger. What my mother's slate meant. Whether Old Maren's stories were memory or legend. But a question with no deadline is just a wish. The Persephone was the deadline. Crane was sailing to London with the official archive under his arm, and once that ship cleared the heads, everything I gathered here would become exactly what history had always been to people like me — a story told about us by whoever could afford the paper.

I copied the tide table onto my slate, the way my mother taught me, characters small and deep enough to survive water. Then I went to find Sam Okonkwo.

* * *

Sam kept a corner of the longshoremen's shelter near Berth Nine, a lean-to of salvaged planks where the stevedores drank coffee strong enough to caulk with and complained about Meridian in voices pitched just below audibility. He was sorting manifest tags when I came in, and when he saw my face he set them down.

"You've got your planning face on," he said. "I've seen it on captains. It never means anything good for dockworkers."

"The Persephone," I said. "Friday tide."

"Mail steamer." He nodded slowly. "Big one. They've already doubled the cargo watch. Whatever she's carrying, somebody cares about it."

"It's Crane. And the archive. Every official record this company has, boxed up and shipped out of reach before anyone in this city can read it."

Sam poured coffee into two chipped tin cups and pushed one at me. "Sit down, Juniper."

"I can't sit down. There are six days—"

"There are six days," he said, "and you'll use them better sitting than falling over. Sit."

I sat. The coffee was terrible. I drank it anyway, and by the third swallow I realized how long it had been since anyone made me do something for my own good, and how much I'd needed it.

"Tell me what you have," Sam said. "All of it. I've been hauling crates for Meridian eleven years and I've never once known what was inside them. Start with what you know is true."

So I laid it out, piece by piece, on the scarred plank table like cards. Thorn's sealed letter, naming the Vasquez line as silent partners in the founding fraud. My mother's slate, its columns of figures matching the letter's references figure for figure. Maren's testimony that the founding fleet ran contraband under pearl cargo. The ledger half hidden in the customs house — which I hadn't retrieved yet, which would take a night and a locked door and more luck than I owned. And above it all, Crane, the historian, gentle-voiced and gift-bearing, rewriting the whole crime into heroism one Gazette installment at a time.

Sam listened without interrupting, which was itself a skill I was only beginning to recognize as one. When I finished he turned his cup in a slow circle on the table.

"You know what happens to cargo," he said, "between the warehouse and the hold?"

"It gets loaded."

"It gets counted," he said. "Three times. Once when it leaves the warehouse, once at the quay, once at the gangway. Three men sign each count, and not one of them reads what they're signing, because the paper is always already filled in. That's how a port works, Juniper. Paper moves faster than ships, and whatever's written first is what everyone else signs." He looked up. "You're trying to write truth after the manifests are filled. You need to be the first signature."

"The notarized record," I said. "Sworn testimony, certified, delivered before the ship sails."

"That's your cargo. Now plan its route like you'd plan a load." He pulled the manifest tags toward him and began flipping them face-down, one by one, laying out days. "Monday. You need the customs house pages. Can you get in?"

"I think so. There's a night watchman who owes—"

"Don't tell me who. Not yet. Tell me when."

"Tuesday or Wednesday. After I've read what's there, I'll know what testimonies I still need."

"Wednesday, then, you gather witnesses. Thursday you get everything sworn and sealed. Which leaves Friday—" He set the last tag down alone, face-up, like a stone on a grave. "Friday you put the papers in the right hands before the lines come off. Do you know whose hands?"

"A federal investigator," I said. "There's supposed to be one in the city. Reyes. Customs revenue division. Nobody at the countinghouse will say his name above a whisper."

Sam grunted. "Reyes boarded the Coriolanus last spring over the silk undervaluations. He doesn't drink with company men. That's either very good for us or very bad, depending on whether he's honest or merely careful."

"How do we find out which?"

"We don't gamble on it," Sam said. "We make sure that by the time he learns your name, he's already learned your evidence. Papers first, person second. A man can refuse a girl. It's harder to refuse a dossier with five sworn witnesses and half an original ledger in it." He tapped the table. "And that means every day between now and Friday has to produce something you could carry aboard if it all ended tomorrow. Nothing waits. Nothing stays 'almost done.'"

I looked at the line of face-down tags — Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday — and felt something shift in my chest that I didn't have a better word for than steadiness. My whole life the harbor had happened to me on its schedule: father drowned, mother silenced, indenture signed, chains fouled, dawn dives. For the first time I was writing on the schedule instead of reading it.

"One more thing," Sam said quietly. "Crane knows you know. The moment your contract went 'under review,' that was a message. He'll expect you to run, or to hide, or to get clever alone. What he won't expect is a dockworker."

"Why not?"

"Because men like Crane don't see ports," Sam said. "They see ships. Ships belong to companies; ports belong to whoever carries things on their backs. He thinks the harbor is furniture." A thin smile. "The harbor is us."

* * *

That evening I sat on the breakwater with my slate balanced on my knee and drew the week the way my mother would have drawn a dive plan — every depth marked, every current noted, every point where the air could run out.

*Monday: customs house.* I wrote it and beneath it: *watchman, locks, tides after dark.* Low water Tuesday came at 1:14 in the morning, which meant the culvert under the north quay would be walkable around midnight. I knew that culvert better than any engineer who'd surveyed it; I'd scraped weed off the iron grate at its mouth for three years of indentured dawns.

*Tuesday: read everything.* Whatever Thorn had hidden, I had to understand it before I could ask anyone to swear to it. A lie survives scrutiny; so does truth; but a half-understood truth dies in front of a notary.

*Wednesday: witnesses.* Maren first — her memory held the fleet, the contraband, the names of the drowned. Then DeWitt, though my hands curled into fists just thinking his name. Then the ward records, if Thorn lasted long enough to speak. Each day I delayed, the list of living witnesses got shorter. Crane was counting on that. Time was his weapon as much as the Persephone was.

*Thursday: seal it.* Somewhere in this city there had to be a notary Crane couldn't buy. That thought stopped my chalk. I didn't know such a person existed. I only hoped.

*Friday: the pier.* Dawn tide, 5:41. Gangway up when the last count was signed, likely 6:15, maybe earlier if Crane hurried them. That gave me — at most — half an hour between high water and departure to find Reyes on a crowded pier, past Crane's men, past the company's dock agents, past every polite obstacle money could arrange, and put the record into his hands.

Half an hour. Six days to earn it.

I sat until the light went copper on the water and the harbor clock across the square clicked through the hour — still stopped, still stuck at ten minutes to four like it had been my whole life, like the whole city was holding its breath since the year of the founding. Below me the tide turned, right on schedule, indifferent and exact. The tide table never lied. Men lied. Companies lied. But water kept its promises, and water was the one thing in Port Calloway I could plan around.

A gull screamed. Lamplighters began their rounds. And far out past the heads, right on time, the pilot boat put out to meet a incoming freighter — small proof that the world still ran on schedules, and schedules could be beaten only by knowing them better than the men who wrote them.

I wiped the slate clean except for one line, written large and deep the way Mother taught me, characters that could survive drowning:

*Five days to the customs house. Six to the tide.*

Then I heard footsteps on the breakwater stones behind me — quick ones, wrong ones — and turned to see a boy from the countinghouse running full-out, holding a note with Meridian's seal broken open in his fist, and his face told me before he spoke that whatever the week had planned for me, Crane had gotten there first.
