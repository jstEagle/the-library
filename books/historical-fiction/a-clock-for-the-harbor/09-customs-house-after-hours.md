## 9. Customs House, After Hours

You tell yourself it is only a building.

That's the first lie of the night, and you let it stand because you need it. The customs house at midnight is just stone and iron and paper — three floors of ledgers, a basement of seized cargo, a clock tower that hasn't run since before you were born. Buildings don't know your name. Buildings can't testify against you. You can walk into a building and walk out again and be the same person on both sides of the door.

Except you won't be. You already know that, standing in the alley behind Sam Okonkwo with the fog coming off the water in slow gray ropes and your mother's slate strapped flat against your ribs under your coat.

"Two ways in," Sam says. He doesn't whisper; he has a dockworker's economy, voice pitched just below the fog's murmur so that anything louder would sound wrong. "Front doors are oak and honesty — nobody locks them because nobody's stupid enough to go in. The watchman makes his rounds at the half hour, up one stair, down the other, forty minutes if he's lazy, twenty-five if someone's paying him to be thorough."

"Which is he tonight?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Sam turns his cap in his hands, once, twice. He does that when he's deciding how much truth a moment can carry. "I asked around. Rennie's got the shift. Rennie drinks. Rennie also paid off a debt to the Meridian store last week that he'd been carrying two years, which could mean luck or could mean somebody bought him a clear ledger for exactly this kind of night."

You feel it then, the first cold thread of it — not fear exactly, but its quieter cousin, suspicion. The city tilts a degree. Every kindness anyone ever did you rearranges itself in the dark, looking for a price tag.

"Then we assume he's thorough," you say.

"We assume nothing. We watch him make one full round first. If he's clean, we've lost an hour. If he's bought—" Sam shrugs. "Then we know what we're dealing with before we're inside holding stolen paper."

"It's not stolen," you say, and hear how it sounds, how defensive, how much like Crane's historian voice — reasonable, polished, wrong. "It's evidence. Thorn hid it there. It belongs to—"

"To whoever holds it at dawn." Sam says it without cruelty, the way you'd state the tide table. "That's not cynicism, Juniper. That's just what power is, until someone like Ferrant gets her seal on it. Come on. There's a coal hatch on the harbor side. Old buildings remember when they were young and poor."

* * *

You wait through the watchman's round crouched in the lee of a bonded warehouse, and the waiting is its own kind of drowning. Your father taught you that about diving — that the descent is easy, that breath is easy, that the hard part is hanging in the cold water doing nothing while your body screams at you to surface and move. You count instead. Fog rolls. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's bell marks eleven-thirty, and Rennie's lantern bobs past the customs house windows on schedule: up the east stair, along the gallery, down the west. Twenty-six minutes. Thorough.

Sam watches the lantern vanish toward the rear of the ground floor and exhales slowly through his nose. "Could still be luck."

"Could be." But you both know what a paid-off store debt looks like in the dark.

The coal hatch gives onto the basement — you knew it would, because Maren told you, because Maren was diving cargoes into this harbor back when this building was new and remembers which officials took bribes to look away from which hatches. The stones inside are slick with a century of coal dust and damp, and the dark down here isn't empty the way night is empty outside. It's full. It smells of tar and mildew and old rope, and underneath all of it, faint as a fingerprint, the sea itself, seeping up through the foundations. The whole city stands on soaked ground. You have known that your entire life without once feeling it until now.

"Third floor," Sam breathes. "Records room, west wall. Thorn said the false back of the shelving, behind the seized-cargo manifests from 'sixty-one. He said he watched them shelve it himself the week before they burned the original — clerk's privilege, nobody notices a man carrying paper in a records room."

"And the half we already have?"

"Is why we're here. One half is an accusation." Sam's teeth show briefly white in the dark. "Two halves is a ledger."

You climb the service stair together with the particular care of people who have spent their lives moving heavy things quietly. It occurs to you, somewhere between the second landing and the third, that you have never been inside this building except to stand in line at a counter while someone behind it decided the shape of your week. Indenture filings. Wage disputes. Your father's effects, inventoried and released to you in a canvas bag: one dive knife, one weight belt, one slate with nothing written on it. You had stood at that counter at fourteen years old and thought: this is where history happens to other people. This counter, these clerks, these stairs you are now climbing in the dark to take something back.

The records room door is unlocked. Of course it is. Nobody's stupid enough to go in.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about theft: it feels like reading.

You find the manifests of 1861 — Sam's lamp shuttered down to a coin of light — and the shelf runs fat and undisturbed, decades of seizures logged in hands long dead. Behind them the shelving back swings on hinges some careful carpenter fitted flush, and the cavity beyond is dry, wrapped in oilcloth, and shallow as a grave. Inside: oilcloth again, tied with tarred twine, and within that, pages. Dozens of them. Cargo tallies in Elias Thorn's small upright hand, and beside each tally, in the same hand, a second column — what was written versus what came off the boats. Pearl shell by the ton, and under it, in Thorn's red ink: arms. Powder. Chained freight, human, thirty head, sold north. And margins full of names — founding names, names on bronze plaques, names Crane has spent a career polishing into legends.

Your hands are steady. You notice that the way you'd notice the water going calm before a squall — as a fact about conditions, not about you.

"Read me the dates," Sam whispers, and you do, low, matching page to page, and somewhere in the middle of it the exhilaration arrives. It comes up through your chest like air from a regulator, sudden and sweet: they wrote it down. They wrote it all down, men who believed no diver's daughter would ever hold a lamp over their arithmetic, and here it is, whole, damning, *real*. For six days you have been chasing ghosts through other people's memories, and memory can be called slander, can be dismissed as an old woman's spite — but ink is ink. This is the thing itself. You want to laugh. You want to run out into the street and wake the whole harbor.

That wanting is exactly what nearly kills you.

* * *

The lantern comes back early.

Later you will reconstruct it a dozen times — Rennie cutting his round short, or making it long, or making it drunk, or making it exactly as bought — and never settle it. What you know is this: light moves at the end of the gallery corridor, and boots ring on stone with none of the shuffle of a tired man, and Sam's lamp dies between one heartbeat and the next, and the pages in your hands suddenly weigh as much as anchor chain.

"West window," Sam breathes, so close it's hardly sound at all. "Service stair's the way they'll come."

The window is painted shut with a century of salt and you get it up a hand's width at a time, easing the sash like drawing a splinter, while the boots come on unhurried and the lantern light slides along the gallery rail toward the door. Below you is the drop to the roof of the portico — twelve feet, wet slate, then a drainpipe, then sixty feet of sheer stone to the alley or the harbor side, whichever your nerve chooses.

The door handle turns.

You go through the window feet-first the way your father taught you to enter black water — legs leading, hands protecting the bundle against your chest, no splash worth hearing. The portico roof takes your landing with a crack of frost-brittle slate that sounds, in the fog-muffled night, enormous. You flatten. Above you, lamplight blooms in the records-room window, and a voice — not Rennie's, smoother, a company voice — says something you cannot hear and do not need to. They know. Or they suspect. Either way the arithmetic has changed.

The drainpipe groans under you but holds, and then you are running, pages bound tight under your coat against the slate, through alleys you have known since childhood and which now feel mapped by other eyes. Every step you take is a step someone might have predicted. Every shortcut is a habit, and habits are legible, and Crane is a man who reads.

* * *

You do not go to your rooms. You had planned to, in the vague way plans live before they are tested, and standing three streets from your own door you understand you cannot. If they found Thorn's hiding place tonight — and how else did a smooth-voiced man come to the records room at midnight? — then they know what Thorn hid and roughly where, and the next place they will look is the bed of the girl whose mother's slate carries matching numbers.

So you stand in the fog with half a ledger against your heart and take inventory of your friends.

Sam walked out the front — his idea, brazen enough to work, a dockworker with a legitimate grievance about wages who'd swear he'd come about a filed claim if they stopped him. Did they stop him? You don't know. He is not at the corner where he said he'd be, and every minute he doesn't appear, the suspicion grows teeth: *paid to report on you*, a voice offers, *that's what fixers buy, watchers, and you handed him the whole plan*. You silence it. Then it comes back. You think of Old Maren, safe in her house above the diver quarter — unless she isn't safe, unless following you is the fastest way to find every remaining witness. You think of Ferrant's card in your pocket and wonder who watches the notary's door. You think of Rennie, drinking his bought sobriety somewhere, and of Thorn dying in a charity ward, and of DeWitt signing logs with a steady hand, and the fog closes in around all of them equally, guilty and innocent alike, indistinguishable in the dark.

This, you understand suddenly, is what Crane sells. Not lies exactly — he barely needs them. He sells the fog itself. Once a man knows the record can be managed, he can never again tell a friend from an informant, and a woman who trusts no one delivers testimony alone, and alone is how these things die.

Well then.

You turn toward the waterfront, toward the divers' quarter, toward Maren's kitchen where the lamp always burns in the window, and you make the choice with your eyes open: you would rather be betrayed by people than fortified by solitude. Crane can have the fog. You'll keep the faces.

But you go the long way, by the waterline, and you do not use the front path, and halfway along the pilings, where the tide sucks soft against the barnacled posts, you stop dead — because there, fresh in the mud of the landing steps, is a boot print pointing toward Maren's door, and it is not hers, and it is not yours, and the fog is very quiet.

The print is still sharp-edged. Whoever made it is minutes ahead of you, walking toward the one witness old enough to remember the founding fleet — or already sitting in her kitchen, being pleasant, being kind, asking gentle questions about what Juniper Vasquez has been asking around town.

You take the slate from under your coat, hold it a moment against the cold, and start to run.
