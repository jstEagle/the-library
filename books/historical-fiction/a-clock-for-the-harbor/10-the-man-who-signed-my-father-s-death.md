## 10. The Man Who Signed My Father's Death

The harbormaster's house stood at the top of Chandler's Hill, the only building in Port Calloway with a view of everything Meridian owned — the piers, the countinghouse, the long gray warehouses, the water itself. Juniper had looked up at it her whole life the way you look at weather. It had never occurred to her that she would one day climb its steps and knock.

She knocked three times. The door opened on the fourth.

Harbormaster Silas DeWitt was not what she expected. She had imagined a fat man, a cruel one, someone built like the company's ledgers — heavy, closed, stamped shut. Instead he was thin as a gaff hook, sixty-odd, with white stubble on his cheeks and a cardigan buttoned wrong, so that the wool pulled crooked across his chest like something listing badly at anchor. He looked at her for a long moment without recognition, and then his face did something terrible. It softened.

"You have her chin," he said. "Your mother's."

"I want to talk to you about my father."

He didn't ask how she'd gotten past his housekeeper or why she'd come at dusk or whether she was alone. He stepped back from the doorway and held it open, and Juniper understood, walking past him into the smell of lamp oil and old paper, that he had been waiting years for exactly this knock. Some men wait for death. He had apparently settled for waiting for her.

* * *

The parlor of Silas DeWitt was a museum of one man's penance, though he probably would have called it something else if he called it anything at all. There were no portraits on the walls. There were charts instead — dozens of them, harbor surveys going back forty years, pinned and layered so that the room's wallpaper was made entirely of Port Calloway's water. And there was a desk, and on the desk a bottle of rum, and beside the bottle a single sheet of paper covered in numbers that Juniper recognized before she was close enough to read them.

Dive logs. Her father's dive logs. The real ones.

"Sit down, Miss Vasquez." He lowered himself into a chair by the cold hearth with the care of a man whose joints had been negotiated with, not defeated. "I'd offer you tea but I haven't the stomach to make it and you haven't the patience to drink it. Am I right?"

"You knew I'd come."

"I hoped." He looked at the bottle and did not touch it. "Feared, too. Hope and fear sit close together in an old man's chest. They share a bunk." He folded his hands. "Ask me."

"Where are we starting?" Juniper said. "The falsified logs? The safety valves your office certified that were never installed? Or should we go back further — to 1861, and a fleet that came in flying pearl flags with something else in the hold?"

Something moved behind DeWitt's eyes — not surprise. Relief, maybe, that she already knew; grief that knowing hadn't spared her the trip.

"All of it," he said quietly. "You've earned all of it. God help me, you've paid for all of it." He turned his chair slightly toward the hearth, toward the cold ashes, as if the story needed somewhere to go once it left him and the chimney was as good a place as any. "I signed my first harbor certification in 1859. Youngest harbormaster on this coast. My father held the post before me, and his before him, and I believed — I genuinely believed — that the office meant something. That a man who kept the charts kept the truth."

"And then?"

"And then the company bought the office out from under me without ever buying the office. You understand? Nobody handed me a purse. Nobody said, *Silas, lie for us.* They did it gentler than that. They made my salary dependent on their tonnage fees. They made my pension dependent on their goodwill. They invited me to dinners where men like Aldous Crane's predecessors told stories about the founding until the founding sounded like scripture. And then one day a diver named Tomás Vasquez came to my office with a slate he'd written underwater and asked me why the air lines on the *Meridian Star* were logged as new when they'd been patched twice, and I discovered that I had never actually been the harbormaster at all. I had been the company's rubber stamp with a family name on it."

Juniper sat very still. Her hands were in her lap and she made sure of it, because she did not trust them yet.

"My father," she said, "went down on the *Star* in '82. On a fouled line. On equipment your office certified as sound."

"Yes."

"He drowned."

"Yes." DeWitt's voice cracked on the word and he let it crack; he didn't try to smooth it over, and somehow that was worse than if he had. "And before he drowned, Miss Vasquez, he was asking questions about the founding cargo. And after he drowned, the questions stopped being asked. I know how that arithmetic looks. I've done that arithmetic every night for sixteen years, and it always comes out the same."

"Say it anyway," Juniper said. "Out loud. In order."

DeWitt closed his eyes.

"Tomás Vasquez found evidence — a manifest fragment, maybe, or a witness — we never knew exactly what, because he was careful. But Crane's people learned he was digging into the original cargo declarations from '61. And rather than kill him — which would raise questions, divers have families, families have mouths — they simply made the water do it. A valve here. A patch logged as new there. A fouling report filed late so the chain he'd be working near wouldn't be cleared. Your father went down trusting the paper in my office, because that paper was the only god in this harbor, and the paper lied."

"Did you sign it before or after?"

The question landed on DeWitt like a dropped anchor. He didn't flinch away from it. That was the one thing Juniper would later grant him: he never flinched away.

"After," he said. "They brought me the log already written. All I had to do was sign. And I told myself it was just ink. Just a signature on a page about a man I'd never met. And then I met his widow at the dock when they brought up the body, and she looked at me — she *thanked* me, Miss Vasquez, for the office's condolences, thanked *me* — and I have not slept a whole night through since." His hands were shaking now, openly. "There is a version of me who refused to sign. I visit him sometimes. He lost his post, his pension, probably his life, and he is a better man than I am, and he changed nothing. They'd have found another stamp. They always find another stamp."

"So you kept the real logs." Juniper nodded at the desk. "Sixteen years of sleeping badly and keeping evidence. Why?"

DeWitt laughed — a small, terrible sound, like a hinge in a house nobody lives in anymore.

"Because I'm a coward with a filing system," he said. "A brave man burns them or uses them. A coward can't destroy them either — destroying felt like killing your father twice. So I kept them. Every corrected depth reading, every patched line logged as new, every signature I put on a lie, with the true figures noted underneath in my own hand, initialed and dated. Forty years of it. Enough to hang me and enough to hang them, all in the same drawer."

* * *

Juniper got up and crossed to the desk and looked at the sheet without touching it. Her father's name appeared four times on the visible page. The handwriting beneath DeWitt's flourishes was cramped and honest, the corrections of a man doing sums in the dark.

Here was the moment, she thought, where rage was supposed to feel like something. She had carried the image of this confrontation for days — through Maren's kitchen, through the mudflats, through Sam's whispered warnings — and in every version of it she had said something devastating and the man had withered. But DeWitt was already withered. Rage poured onto a man who had been drowning slowly for sixteen years just made more water.

"You understand," she said, and her own voice sounded strange to her, level and cold, like her mother's slate-writing hand, "that saying this to me fixes nothing. You understand that confession isn't testimony. You could sit here telling me the truth until you die of old age, and it would change the exact amount of nothing."

"I know."

"The ledger proves the fraud. Thorn proved the cover-up of the fraud. But my father—" her voice caught, once, and she hated it, and pushed through, "my father is just a dead diver to everyone but me. Divers drown. It's written off as weather and bad luck. Unless someone official says otherwise."

"Then someone official will say otherwise." DeWitt rose, painfully, and lifted the entire stack from the drawer — thick, heavy, bound in oilcloth against the damp, decades deep. He held it out to her with both hands, the way you surrender a weapon. "Take it."

"No."

His arms stayed extended. Confusion moved across his face, slow as fog.

"Not like this." Juniper looked at the stack and did not take it. "If I walk out of here with these, you're a coward who hid evidence for sixteen years and handed it to a girl in a parlor, and Crane's people will say exactly that — a grieving daughter and a senile old man trading stories. It dies in a hearing. It has to be sworn. Witness by witness, under oath, in front of the one notary in this city Meridian doesn't own. Judge Ferrant. You'll come to her yourself, tomorrow night, and you'll swear to every signature on every lie, with your name and your office attached. Do you understand what that costs you?"

"Everything I have left," DeWitt said. "Which is to say, finally, something."

"Then you understand." She stepped back. "Keep your logs tonight. Bring them to Ferrant. Because vengeance is yours to want and mine to refuse, and what I need isn't your suffering — it's your signature, on the record, where no historian can rewrite it."

For the first time, Silas DeWitt straightened to his full height, thin and crooked and suddenly bearing weight.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Ferrant. I'll be there." He hesitated at the edge of the lamplight. "Miss Vasquez — your mother came here once. Years ago. Asked me for the same logs. And I sent her away with nothing, because I wasn't ready, because I was still telling myself the stamp wasn't mine." His eyes shone. "Don't carry what I did to her as yours. She chose silence to keep you safe. I chose silence to keep me comfortable. Those aren't the same sin."

Juniper stood in the doorway of the man who had signed her father's death and found, to her fury, that she could not hate him cleanly. Hate wanted a monster and had been given a mirror. She left without another word, down the steps of Chandler's Hill, into streets going blue with evening.

Behind her, in the window, DeWitt watched her go — then blew out the lamp and began, methodically, packing the logs as if for a voyage.

Three streets down, a figure detached itself from a doorway and started after her.
