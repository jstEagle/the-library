## 11. Sam's Bargain

Sam Okonkwo had counted money before. He'd counted it in crates, in bales, in the sweat-wet palms of stevedores who worked a double shift and got paid for one and a half. He knew what money looked like when it was honest and what it looked like when it was bait.

The envelope in his coat pocket looked like bait.

He'd found it Tuesday, tucked inside his pay packet at the Meridian longshore office — forty dollars in crisp notes, more than he made in three weeks of hauling, with no chit attached, no signature. Just a card: *We value reliable men. Continue as you have been.* And beneath that, in smaller type, the initials *A.C.*

A.C. Aldous Crane. The company's historian. Sam had never met the man, but he knew the type. Port Calloway was full of men who paid other men to be their eyes, and Sam had been one of them for eleven days without quite admitting it to himself.

It had started small. Crane's clerk had asked him, casual as weather talk, whether the Vasquez girl came by the docks often. Whether she talked to anyone. Whether she seemed frightened. Sam had answered because answering cost nothing, or so he told himself. Then the questions came again, and again, each time with a coin pressed into his hand like a tip, and each time Sam told himself the same lie: *I'm not telling them anything they don't already know.*

But he was. He'd mentioned the customs house. He'd mentioned Old Maren's name. He'd mentioned, God help him, that Juniper had been asking about her father's dive logs, and two days later DeWitt's office had been swept clean of anything older than five years.

Sam stood on the pier in the gray light before the morning shift, watching gulls fight over fish guts, and understood what he was. Not a traitor — that word was too grand. A leak. A man who let water through a wall one drop at a time and called himself dry.

* * *

She found him at the noon break, sitting on a bollard with bread and salt fish, his feet dangling over black water. She sat down beside him without asking, the way she always did, and for a moment neither spoke. The harbor did its talking — hawsers creaking, winches groaning, the Persephone riding at anchor out past the mole like a promise someone intended to break.

"You've been strange," she said.

"I've been working."

"You've been strange *while* working." She tore a piece off her own loaf and didn't eat it. "You didn't tell me the Meridian clerks were asking after me. Maren told me. Maren, Sam. A woman who hasn't left her kitchen since the king's jubilee."

Sam looked at the water. There was an oil slick out there, rainbow-skinned, spreading thin.

"Juniper."

"Don't make excuses. Just say it."

"I took their money." It came out flat, ugly, finished. "Eleven days. Small stuff at first — coins for answers I told myself were worthless. Then the envelope." He pulled it from his coat and held it up between two fingers like something dead. "Forty dollars to keep watching you. That's what your friendship costs at Meridian rates. Forty dollars."

Juniper went very still. He'd seen her still before — underwater still, the kind of stillness divers learn when the bottom shifts and you don't know yet if it's going to swallow you.

"How much did they know," she said, "because of you?"

"The customs house. Maren. Your father's logs." His voice cracked on the last one and he hated it. "I didn't know what it was for. I swear to you, girl, I thought you were chasing ghosts and they were humoring you, and I thought — " He stopped. "No. That's not true either. I didn't think. Thinking would have stopped me, so I didn't do it."

She stood up. For one terrible second Sam was certain she'd walk away, and he deserved it, and he'd deserve worse, and there wasn't a thing in this world he could say to stop her.

Instead she held out her hand.

"Give me the envelope."

He gave it to her. She turned it over once, reading the card, her jaw tight. Then she handed it back.

"It's yours to tear," she said. "Not mine. If I tear it, that's me forgiving you, and I'm not sure I'm ready. Tear it yourself, and we'll see what happens next."

* * *

He tore it slow. Not for theater — though some part of him knew she needed to see every inch of it done — but because his hands wouldn't go faster. The notes ripped lengthwise first, then crosswise, then into pieces small enough that no clerk could ever tape them whole again. The card he shredded last, the initials A.C. falling into the harbor in confetti-sized fragments, drifting out on the tide toward the anchored ship.

"There," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Now I'm a poor man who can't be bought twice."

"No," Juniper said. "Now you're a man who knows exactly what he's worth to them. That's better than rich. That's *useful*."

She sat back down beside him, closer than before, and told him about Ferrant. About the notary who owed no one. About the depositions that needed taking, and the witnesses who needed walking safely across a city full of Crane's paid eyes, and the morning six days hence when the tide would turn and the Persephone would sail whether Port Calloway liked it or not.

"I need a network," she said. "I need dockworkers who can move people without being seen moving them. I need men at the gangway and men at the customs house steps and men who can count Crane's hired knives faster than he hires them. You said once your crew could move a full cargo manifest off a ship between the pilot boat and the quay without anyone writing it down. I need that. But not bought. Never bought again."

Sam watched the gulls. His throat hurt.

"Why?" he said. "Why give me a second chance? You should spit on me. Half of me wants you to."

"Because loyalty that's never been tested isn't loyalty, it's just habit." She brushed crumbs from her skirt. "My mother taught me that. She wrote it on a slate, actually, under twenty years of floorboards." Something crossed her face then — grief and judgment holding hands — but she pushed it down where she kept such things. "And because you confessed before I caught you. That counts, Sam. In a city where everyone lies for a living, it counts double."

* * *

That evening he called his crew together in the back room of the Anchor & Chain, the ones he trusted, the ones whose fathers had worked these same piers. Eleven men. He laid it out plain — the ledger, the fraud, the letter, the girl whose father the company had sent down to drown. He watched their faces the way he'd learned to watch cargo: weighing, testing, finding the cracks.

Old Tomaso spat in the sawdust. "Meridian fed my family thirty years."

"Meridian drowned her father and stole the harbor," Sam said. "We can eat angry."

Nobody laughed. Nobody left, either.

By midnight the plan had bones. Tomaso would watch the customs house. The Ferreira brothers would walk Old Maren wherever she needed to go. Two men Sam trusted absolutely would stand at the pier come sailing day, counting Crane's men the way they counted bales. And Sam himself — Sam would carry the word along the waterfront faster than Crane could print his pretty lies in the Gazette, mouth to ear, ear to mouth, the oldest telegraph in the world.

Before he slept, he took out the scraps of the torn card he'd fished from the harbor that afternoon, dried them on his stove, and pinned them above his door. A reminder. Not of shame — of price.

Outside, far out past the mole, the lights of the Persephone burned steady against the dark, six days from sailing, carrying a history Sam now meant to help drown in its own ink.

And somewhere across town, he knew, Crane was already looking for a new pair of eyes.

He'd have to look a long time. The waterfront had chosen its side.
