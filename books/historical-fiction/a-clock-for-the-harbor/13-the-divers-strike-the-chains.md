## 13. The Divers Strike the Chains

Maren had not put on a diving suit in eleven years, and she had sworn she never would again — not after the fever, not after the cough that still woke her at three in the morning sounding like the harbor itself trying to speak. But she stood on the pilings at first light anyway, in her good coat, watching the *Meridian Star* swing at anchor with her chains foul and her crew idle, and she felt something old move in her chest. Not the water. Something above it.

The divers were already there. Forty of them, maybe fifty, gathered on the mudflats below the customs house where the tide went out far enough to walk on the harbor's own floor. They had brought their helmets and their breastplates and their air hoses, coiled like sleeping snakes, and they had set them down in a row in the mud where every ship's officer in Port Calloway could see them from his breakfast table.

Nobody had to tell them to do it. That was the thing Maren kept turning over as she picked her way down the shingle. Nobody had told them. Juniper Vasquez had gone from boat to boat for two days, saying only what she'd found and what it proved, and then she'd stopped talking, and the talking had kept going without her.

"Old woman," called Tomas Reyes — no relation to the federal man, though everyone joked about it now — "you've come to watch or to work?"

"To watch," Maren said. "My working days are drowned."

"Your mouth works fine."

"It does." She settled herself on an upturned dory, knees aching, and looked out at the line of helmets glinting in the gray light. "Better than fine, I think. Better than it has in years."

* * *

Juniper had learned by the third day that a strike is mostly waiting, and waiting is mostly listening.

She moved through the crowd on the flats with her collar up and her hood back just enough that people who needed to find her could. Sam had taught her that — how to be visible to friends and invisible to anyone else, how to stand so your face was always half in shadow. The longshoremen had their own signals worked out: a chalk mark on a bollard meant Crane's men had been asking questions; a red rag tied to a crane arm meant stay away from the north pier entirely.

By seven o'clock the *Meridian Star*'s captain had come down to the waterline himself, hatless, shouting across the mud about breach of contract, about men who'd signed articles, about how a diver who refused service was no better than a pirate. The divers answered him the only way they could answer anything: they said nothing. They stood. Fifty men and women standing in mud, saying nothing, was louder than any speech Juniper had ever heard.

"They can't all be arrested," Sam murmured beside her. He'd appeared the way he always did, out of the crowd's blind side. "There aren't cells enough in this city."

"There don't need to be," Juniper said. "They only need to arrest one. One is enough to frighten the rest."

"Then we make sure the one they arrest is worth arresting."

She looked at him. "That sounds like volunteering."

"That sounds like arithmetic." He grinned, quick and crooked. "I'm expensive to arrest. I know judges. I know reporters. I know which constable takes bribes and which one takes offense. If they want to make an example, let them make one that talks back."

Juniper wanted to argue. She wanted, badly, to keep him safe, and she knew — she had known since the mudflats, since the ferry, since the night she'd watched him tear up Crane's money — that wanting him safe was a luxury the week would not allow. So instead she said, "Then don't get caught doing anything. Get caught being present. Presence isn't a crime yet."

"Not yet," Sam said. "Crane's writing fast, though. Have you seen the Gazette?"

She hadn't. She'd been avoiding it the way you avoid a wound you know has gone bad. Now she made herself buy a copy from the newsboy at the head of the pier, and she read it standing up, in the wind, because sitting down with it felt like surrender.

*THE HEROES OF THE FOUNDING FLEET*, ran the headline. Beneath it, Crane's third installment: pearl divers of '61 braving storms to carry the company's treasure through a blockade, loyal clerks burning their own ledgers to protect widows and orphans from creditors, and — she read it twice, feeling her ears go hot — *"certain modern malcontents, trading in forged papers and the vanity of a dead girl's slate."*

Dead girl's slate. Her mother. He'd written her mother into his legend as a prop.

"You're shaking," Sam said quietly.

"I'm deciding whether to shake or burn something."

"Burn the paper. Keep the anger. Anger keeps; paper doesn't." He took the Gazette from her hands, folded it into quarters, and tucked it inside his coat. "And here's what he's done, if you want to see it plain. He hasn't denied the ledger. He's made denial unnecessary. He's given the city a better story. People will choose the better story every time, unless the true one costs them nothing to hold."

"So we make the truth cheap," Juniper said. "We make it public. We make it read aloud."

"That's what the strike's for."

"No," she said, and heard her own voice go flat and certain, the way it did underwater. "The strike is for the ships. The reading is for the city. Two different weapons. We've only fired one."

* * *

Maren saw the girl arrive at the flats on the fourth morning and understood, in the way old divers understand weather, that something had turned.

Juniper came down the shingle with a bundle under her arm — oilcloth, the size of a hymnal — and she didn't go to the fire barrel where the strike committee sat. She went to the waterline and stood looking at the harbor the way her father used to look at it before a deep dive: measuring, respectful, a little afraid.

Maren limped over. "You look like a woman about to ask for something she shouldn't."

"I'm going to ask for something worse than I should." Juniper unwrapped the oilcloth partway, enough that Maren could see the edge of Thorn's copied pages, soft as cloth from handling. "The reading. Saturday. Right here, on the flats, at low tide. The whole ledger, aloud, start to finish. Every witness standing behind whoever reads it. Let Crane print whatever legend he likes — he can't print over fifty voices saying the same words in the same hour."

Maren was quiet a moment. Out past the strike line, the *Persephone* rode at her moorings, black and patient, taking on cargo for London. Three days, now. Maybe four, if the weather held off.

"You know what happens Saturday," Maren said. "He can't touch you while you're one voice among fifty. But afterward? Afterward you're the girl who organized it. There'll be a name on it somewhere, even if you never sign. Names are how he hunts."

"I know."

"And you know some of these people will pay for it. Not you. Them. The ones with contracts, the ones with children, the ones who can't run to a judge named Ferrant when the company comes collecting." Maren's voice was rougher than she intended. Eleven years of silence had left sediment in it. "I've buried divers who died for less than this. I've buried divers who died for nothing at all. I promised myself I'd never again stand at a fire barrel and listen to a young person talk us all into the water."

"But?"

"But." Maren looked out at the helmets in the mud, at the young ones among them — the Okafor twins, barely twenty, their father's old suits let out at the shoulders; little Ines Carvalho who dove the shallows for shell because her lungs couldn't take depth. "But my father helped sink those contraband crates in '61. He never told me straight out. I read it in his hands, in the way he wouldn't dive the east channel, in the way he crossed himself passing the customs house. He carried it down with him and it drowned with him and nobody ever wrote it anywhere, and so it never happened. That's what silence buys. It buys nothing. It doesn't even buy peace. It just buys more silence, deeper, year over year, until somebody's granddaughter goes digging in the mud and finds it all still there, rotting, waiting."

Juniper's eyes had gone bright. She didn't cry. Divers' daughters rarely did where anyone could see.

"Saturday, then," Maren said. "I'll read. My voice is shot but it carries. Old women's voices carry — nobody believes we'll say anything worth stopping."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Feed me. And get off my dory, you've gone and sat on my good coat."

* * *

The committee met that night in the net loft above Okonkwo's uncle's chandlery, and for the first time since the strike began, Juniper did not lead the meeting. She'd asked Maren to chair it, and Maren had refused, and then Maren had nominated the Okafor twins' father, and he'd accepted before anyone could object, and that was how Juniper learned the second lesson of collective courage: it only counts if you can bear to hand it away.

Emmanuel Okafor ran the meeting like a dive — slow descent, check your gauges, no heroics. Twenty-two people around a scarred table lit by two lamps. Dockworkers, divers, a customs clerk who'd come in through the roof hatch and kept his hat pulled low, a washerwoman named Beatriz who surprised everyone by producing a list of forty-seven households along the waterfront who'd pledged bread and fish stew for the duration.

"The company's strategy is hunger," Okafor said. "It's always hunger. They'll wait out the strike and starve us polite. So we eat first. Beatriz has the kitchens. Anyone who wants to break the line gets fed either way — we're not at war with our own people, we're at war with a ledger."

"A ledger we intend to read aloud," Maren said, "Saturday, low tide, on the flats. Public. Witnessed. I'll take the first hour."

"Who takes the rest?" someone asked. "It's near two hundred pages."

Juniper raised her hand, and the room went quiet in a way that made her skin prickle. She realized, suddenly, that most of them had never heard her speak at length. To them she was still the Vasquez girl, the diver's daughter, the one the letter had named. A symbol before she'd ever been a speaker.

"I'll read," she said. "All of it if I have to. And I want it understood — I'm not reading my family's name out of it. When we reach the Vasquez entries, I'll read them exactly as written. No softer, no louder. If my grandfather profited, the harbor hears it in my voice. That's the price of asking anyone else to stand behind this."

Beatriz, from the back: "You'd damn your own blood in front of the whole waterfront?"

"I'd correct it in front of the whole waterfront. Damning is Crane's trade. Correction is ours."

Okafor nodded slowly, and Juniper saw the nod travel around the table, person to person, like a lamp being passed. This was the weight she'd been afraid of and hadn't been able to name: not the danger, not Crane, not the debtors' island. The weight of other people choosing, because of you, to be brave. You couldn't carry it for them. You could only try to be worth it.

Sam spoke last, as usual. "One more thing. Crane won't wait for Saturday. He'll move tomorrow or the day after — warrants, arrests, an accident on the pilings, something. So we post watches. Two on every approach to the flats, day and night. And Juniper—" he looked at her, and there was no grin in it now, "—you sleep somewhere new tonight. And tomorrow. Until Saturday's done, you don't sleep in the same bed twice."

* * *

They came Thursday night, and they came for the nets.

Not for Juniper, not at first. Four men with crowbars and a lantern shuttered dark, into the loft above the chandlery, where the strike fund and the copied pages had both been hidden — pages Juniper had already moved, thank God, two days prior, on Sam's insistence, into a false bottom beneath Odile Ferrant's stove. But the nets were the community's. Thirty years of mending, hung from the rafters, some of them older than the men cutting them down. The strikers' nets, the loaned nets, the nets that meant a diver's family ate in winter.

Maren heard them from her room above the bait shop — floorboards, then the wet rip of canvas — and she was down the stairs and into the street in her nightclothes before her boots were laced, and she was not alone. The waterfront heard everything. By the time the four men got their loot to the alley, there were fifteen people between them and the water, and none of them were holding anything except lanterns, held high, held steady, saying nothing, the way the divers had said nothing to the *Star*'s captain. Light was enough. Witnesses were enough.

The men dropped the nets and ran. One of them dropped something else — a card, heavy stock, embossed — and Beatriz's boy picked it up and brought it to the fire barrel, and by midnight everyone on the flats had seen it: the Meridian Trading Company crest, and beneath it, in small engraved letters, *Authorized Agent*.

"They didn't even hide it," Juniper said. She stood at the barrel with the card in her hand, and her voice was very quiet, and Maren thought: there it is, the cold coming into her, the same cold that comes into water in October, sudden and total.

"They didn't need to hide it," Maren said. "That's the message. *We can afford to be seen.*"

"Then we'll be seen too." Juniper fed the card to the fire and watched it curl. "Saturday. Low tide. Everyone who's ever been told to keep silent — bring them. Bring everyone. If Crane wants an audience, we'll give him the biggest one this harbor's ever drawn."

* * *

Friday dawned fog-thick, and the *Persephone*'s masts appeared over the water like the ribs of something surfacing.

Two days.

Maren spent the morning rehearsing at the waterline, reading Thorn's pages aloud to the gulls because her voice needed the practice and her pride needed the humiliation. Halfway down the second page she stopped, because she'd reached the entry dated the ninth of October, 1861, and it listed the founding fleet's cargo manifest against the customs declarations, and the difference between them was written in Elias Thorn's careful dying hand, and it was enormous. It was the size of a harbor. It was the size of a map redrawn.

A shadow fell across the page. She looked up expecting Juniper.

It wasn't Juniper. It was a young constable, rain-dark cap, standing awkwardly at the tide line with his hands behind his back, and behind him, out in the fog, two figures in good coats who were pretending to examine the moored dinghies.

"Maren Ostergaard?" the constable said.

"Depends who's asking and what for."

"Warrant's being drawn up, ma'am. For Saturday. Unlawful assembly, incitement, defamation of a chartered company." He said it quickly, miserably, the way a boy recites lines he hates. "I'm supposed to give notice. I'm giving notice."

Behind him, the two coats began walking closer.

Maren closed the oilcloth bundle slowly and looked past the constable, out at the fog, where the *Persephone* waited with her holds open and her clocks running. Then she looked at the helmets lined up in the mud behind her — fifty of them now, sixty, more arriving every hour, families carrying them down the shingle like offerings.

"Notice received," she said, and raised her voice so the fog itself would carry it down the waterfront. "Now run along, lad, and tell whoever sent you: Saturday, low tide, we read. And we read *everything*."
