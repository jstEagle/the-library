## 15. Island of Debtors

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded into the pay packet with my wages like it was nothing more than a change of shift.

*By order of the Port Calloway Commission on Indebted Labor, you are required to present yourself at the Meridian countinghouse on Thursday morning for transport to Graymoss Island, there to remain until such time as your account is settled.*

My account. As if I were a column in a ledger. As if the sum of me could be carried and written down.

I read it three times under the lamp in my room above the chandlery, and each time the words rearranged themselves into plainer language: *Crane knows about Thorn's ward records. Crane knows where you've been sleeping. Crane is done being gentle.*

Six days until the *Persephone*. Five now, if Thursday counted. He didn't need to stop me anymore. He only needed to store me somewhere the tide couldn't reach — and Graymoss Island was exactly that. A spit of rock and salt marsh two miles off the harbor mouth, where the company kept its debtors behind a fence no one had ever climbed because the water did the work for them. Mud that swallowed men to the waist. Currents that turned a swimmer's legs to rope. I knew those currents better than anyone alive. My father had mapped them by feel, before the harbor had a name for half its channels.

That was the joke of it, I thought, folding the summons small enough to hide in my boot. They were sending a diver's daughter to an island surrounded by water and calling it a prison.

But I wasn't fool enough to smile about it yet. Prisons don't need walls when they have paperwork, and paperwork was the one thing I couldn't outswim. If I vanished Thursday morning, Crane would have his forgery — the runaway indentured girl, the thief of company documents, the daughter of a fraudster, just as his serialized history was already painting her. Every day I stayed free had to be a day I spent building the record, not running from his.

So I did the arithmetic of a hunted woman. Two nights left. One notary who owed no one. And Sam Okonkwo, who owed me nothing either, which after what he'd told me made him the only man in Port Calloway whose word I trusted completely.

I went to find him while the lamps were still lit along the fish market, moving through the crowd like smoke, taking the long way twice, watching the reflections in shop glass for a face that followed.

There wasn't one. That frightened me more than if there had been. Crane's men had learned to be invisible, which meant they'd learned it from him.

Sam was coiling rope outside the longshoremen's hall, his hands working without his eyes, which were already on me before I reached him.

"You got the paper," he said. Not a question. "Ward clerk told my cousin. Thorn's records went out in a company wagon this afternoon, sealed, signed by Crane himself."

"They took his file." My voice came out flat, which was how I kept it from shaking. "Everything he told us, every page of his deposition notes — if Ferrant's copies are all that's left, then Crane can argue the originals were never—"

"Juniper." He set the rope down. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're drowning standing up." He glanced up the street, unhurried, a man checking the weather. "Walk with me. Slow. Like we're going to argue about wages."

We walked. The fish market smell clung to everything — brine and ice and the sweet rot of the day's unsold catch — and I told him the shape of it in a low voice: the summons, Graymoss, Thursday's ferry, the six o'clock tide.

"Graymoss," Sam said, when I finished. "My uncle did eighteen months there. Said the worst part wasn't the fence or the guards. It was the quiet. You could hear the harbor bells across the water, clear as church, and nobody on the island ever rang back." He stopped at the corner where the lamplighter was making his rounds, flame by flame, pushing the dark back a street at a time. "You're not getting on that ferry."

"I know."

"And you're not swimming out tonight either, so don't give me that look. The flats at low water are a mile of sucking mud, and Crane will have a man on the seawall with a lantern the moment the ward clerk reports your room empty. Which will be tonight, because the ward clerk drinks with the harbormaster's clerk, and DeWitt—" He caught himself on the name, and something moved across his face. "DeWitt signs whatever Crane puts in front of him these days. Guilt makes a man obedient."

"Then how?"

Sam smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who had spent twenty years loading ships and had learned exactly how much weight a plank could bear before it betrayed you.

"The ferry leaves Thursday at six," he said. "And ferries, Miss Vasquez, need pilots. And pilots need tenders. And tenders get crewed by longshoremen who owe me favors older than you are."

* * *

Wednesday passed like held breath.

I did not go to the countinghouse. I did not go to Odile Ferrant's chambers, though every hour away from her felt like a page torn out of the record. Instead I sat in the back room of the Sailors' Rest, a dockside boarding house where the tea was terrible and the landlady asked no questions because she'd once been a smuggler's wife and considered questions a form of bad manners, and I worked.

Ferrant had drilled me on it Monday night, tapping the table with her ring finger like a metronome. "Paper is nothing," she'd said. "Sworn paper is something. Sworn paper delivered into the proper hands before the wrong ship sails — that is everything. So we do it in order, girl, and we do not skip steps because we are frightened. Frightened people skip steps. Frightened people die of skipped steps."

So I copied. Maren's testimony, in her own crooked diver's hand, corrected against my transcription. Sam's sworn statement, witnessed by two stevedores who'd signed with an X apiece. The watchman's confession, extracted at cost of his job and given anyway. DeWitt's pages, still smelling faintly of the brandy he'd drunk before he could make his hand move. And beneath them all, wrapped in oilcloth, the ledger halves — Thorn's copy and the customs house original — matched page against page until the fraud lay open like a dissected animal, every artery named.

The founding fleet had carried contraband in place of pearl. The company had sold shares on cargo that never existed. And when old Manolo Vasquez — my grandfather, a name I hadn't known I had until three weeks ago — threatened to speak, they had bought his silence with a partnership on paper and buried the paper itself. My family hadn't been silent partners in the crime. We'd been silent partners in the *lie*, holding worthless shares while the real profits flowed to London, generation after generation, until the debt came due in the shape of my father's lungs filling with harbor water on a dive log DeWitt had falsified to keep him down deep and quiet.

I wrote it all out clean. Three copies. One for Ferrant's safe. One for Reyes, the federal investigator, whose name Sam had pried out of a customs clerk over two beers and a shared hatred of Crane. And one that would travel sewn into the lining of my coat, because I had learned from Thorn that a man who keeps only one copy of the truth is a man waiting to be erased.

By nightfall my eyes burned and my hand ached and the fear had settled into something quieter and harder, like ballast low in a hull. I ate the landlady's stew without tasting it and lay down on a pallet that smelled of other people's voyages, and I slept, because tomorrow would want everything I had.

* * *

They came for the room at midnight. I know because the landlady woke me with two fingers pressed to my lips, her face a pale smudge in the dark, and pointed toward the street.

Through the gap in the shutter I saw the lantern swing. Two men in company coats, and behind them a third figure I recognized even at that distance, even in that light: tall, unhurried, gloved. Crane stood in the middle of the empty street like a man admiring architecture, and when the landlady told them — I heard her voice, pitched high with practiced ignorance — that she let the back room to no one, that she'd never heard of any Vasquez girl, Crane thanked her so courteously I could have screamed.

Then the lanterns moved on toward the chandlery, toward my real room, toward the floorboards where my mother's slate had lain for eleven years, and I understood that Crane had known about the slate all along. He'd simply wanted to see whether I'd come back for it.

I hadn't. It was under the pallet, wrapped in my spare shirt, and it was going with me.

"Back stairs," the landlady breathed. "Through the yard. There's a boy waiting at the fish gate with a barrow. Fish guts on top. Don't be proud."

I wasn't proud. I lay under mackerel offal in the bottom of a barrow while a fourteen-year-old wheeled me through streets I knew blindfolded, and I catalogued the smells the way my mother taught me to catalogue the harbor — by layers, by depth, by what lived at each level — because cataloguing was how I kept terror from becoming panic. Brine. Tar. Horse. Smoke. The river of the world flowing over me, and me holding still beneath it, the way you hold still in current, the way you hold still when the thing above you has teeth.

The barrow stopped. The boy whistled three notes, low. Hands lifted the offal away, and Sam's face was above mine against the stars, and behind him the masts of the harbor rose up black against blacker sky, a forest of them, rigging humming in the offshore breeze like the whole port was whispering.

"Change of plan," Sam said, hauling me upright. "Ferry's been moved up."

"What?"

"Moves at five now, not six. Company order, signed last night. Crane wants you on Graymoss before the harbor wakes up and starts asking where you've gone." He was already walking, fast, toward the north flats where the pilings gave way to mud and eelgrass. "Which means we go now, in the dark, across the flats, and we meet the pilot tender behind Gull Rock instead of at the quay. You can walk the flats at this tide if you know the channels. Can you?"

It was such a strange question to ask me that I almost laughed. "Sam. I could walk these flats asleep. My father drew the first chart of them."

"Good." He handed me a bundle: dry coat, bread, a hooded lantern with a shutter slide. "Because I can't. I sink past my knees in that mud and I stay sunk. So this is where I leave you, and this is the part where you tell me you'll be at Ferrant's chambers by dawn with the rest of the witnesses, because the depositions finish tomorrow whether you're above ground or at the bottom of it."

I looked at him. Twenty years of loading ships in his shoulders, betrayal confessed and forgiven sitting between us like a third person, and the whole harbor at our backs holding its breath.

"Thank you," I said, which was too small a thing and we both knew it.

"Don't thank me. Outswim Crane." He pushed the bundle into my arms. "Go."

* * *

The flats at night are another country.

I went out with the ebb, boots in hand, barefoot in the cold silt, and the mud took my feet the way it takes everything — slowly, patiently, with the indifference of something far older than any company or crown. The moon was a thin nail-paring behind cloud. Somewhere ahead, Gull Rock stood up against the sky like a broken tooth, and I steered for it by feel, reading the ground under my soles the way my mother taught me to read a slate underwater: firm here, hollow there, channel cutting left where the ebb ran hardest.

Halfway across, the lantern behind me found me.

It came from the seawall — a pinprick first, then a swinging bloom of yellow light sweeping the flats in long arcs, and beneath it, faint across the water, voices. Not many. Two, maybe three. Calling to each other in the flat tones of men doing a job.

I dropped flat into a runnel, cold water to my chin, mud closing over my shoulders, and went still the way you go still when the thing above you has teeth.

The light crawled toward me. I watched it come, and my heart did not race; it slowed, which I knew from my father's stories was the diver's trick, the body spending itself carefully because panic spends everything at once. The light swept ten yards short of me. Swept back. Held on a patch of eelgrass where a heron had been standing, and the bird exploded upward with a sound like a sail flogging, and the men laughed — I heard it, thin and human across the water — and decided the noise was their answer.

The light moved on toward the point, hunting the shore path, the roads, everywhere but the one place a sensible fugitive would never go: a mile of drowning mud in the dark.

Sensible. I lay in the runnel with the tide tugging at my hair and understood that I had stopped being sensible some weeks ago, around the time a dying clerk put a letter in my hands, and that there was no road back to sensible, no version of my life where I surfaced from this water and went quietly back to scraping fouling off the company's chains. The girl who did that was gone. She'd drowned somewhere between chapter and chapter, and what stood up out of the harbor in her place was someone who carried sworn paper in an oilcloth wrap and knew the names of dead men and intended, with a calm that felt almost frightening, to make those names matter.

I waded on. The mud tried to keep me — it always does; the flats take a toll from everyone — and twice I went down to the hip in a soft pocket and hauled myself out by eelgrass roots, the ledger wrap held high, dry, sacred. Cold worked into my bones. My feet went numb past feeling, which was fine; feeling was a luxury for later.

Gull Rock rose out of the dark, streaming with kelp, loud with nesting birds even now, even in the cold. Behind it, sheltered in the channel, rode a black hull with no lights: the pilot tender *Kestrel*, riding easy, a single figure at her rail.

"That you, Vasquez?" a voice called softly. Old Maren's voice. Of course it was. Sam had said favors older than you are, and there was no favor older than the divers owed each other.

"It's me."

"Then quit admiring the view and get aboard. Tide turns in forty minutes, and this old tub doesn't fight current for anybody."

Hands pulled me over the rail. Someone threw a blanket around me that smelled of tar and pipe smoke. Maren crouched in front of me, her face a knot of wrinkles and iron in the dark, and gripped my chin in a hand hard as a cleat.

"Ferry docks at Graymoss at six," she said. "You'll be long gone. But hear me, girl. Whatever happens next — whatever Crane puts in the Gazette, whatever lies they print about your mother — you hold to the record. Paper remembers what people won't. That's the whole trade. That's the whole war."

"I know," I said. My teeth were chattering. "Maren — the others. Tomorrow. All of them, at Ferrant's chambers. Make sure—"

"Every living witness will be in that room if I have to carry DeWitt on my back." She squeezed my chin once, hard, almost tenderly, and stood. "*Kestrel!* Take her to the customs house stair. Quiet as prayer."

The tender's engine turned over with a cough and a mutter. Gull Rock slid away behind us. Ahead, across the black water, the lamps of Port Calloway burned in a ragged line, and among them, high on the customs house, the harbor clock — stopped these fifty years, frozen at the hour of the fraud, the whole city's shame ticking nowhere — hung like a wound in the sky.

Dawn was maybe two hours off. The *Persephone* sailed on the morning tide in four days. Everything I had left was sewn into my coat and beating in my chest, and the city coming toward us out of the dark didn't know yet what was arriving.

I stood at the rail with the wind tearing my wet hair and watched the customs house grow larger, and I made myself a promise with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own tide table:

Four days. One gangway. One chance.

I would be there when the lines cast off — and this time, the harbor would carry *me*.
