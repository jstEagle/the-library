## 16. The Notary Who Owes No One

There are three kinds of people who come up my stairs, and I can tell them apart by the sound of their feet.

The guilty climb fast, as though speed might outrun whatever they've done. The desperate climb slow, because they have already imagined the door being shut in their faces and they are rehearsing the disappointment. And the ones sent by Meridian Trading Company — those don't climb at all. They send letters on cream stock, or they send a boy with money who won't meet my eye, or in one memorable year they sent a carriage that waited outside my window for six hours like a hearse with nowhere to be.

I had been a judge of the admiralty court for nineteen years before they took it from me, and I will tell you what I learned in those nineteen years, since no one else seems to want it: the sea keeps better records than men do, but only men can be bribed, so the sea always loses.

They took my robes over a ruling. I had found against Meridian in a salvage dispute worth more than the harbor clock, more than the customs house, possibly more than the harbor itself if you counted what was under it. My ruling was correct. I know it was correct because eleven years later a clerk named Elias Thorn sent me an unsigned note saying *your numbers were right, Judge* — and then, three years after that, nothing, because Elias Thorn went into a charity ward to die quietly where the Company's historians wouldn't think to look for him.

So now I notarize documents in a room above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, with a view of the harbor that would be beautiful if beauty were the sort of thing that could still reach me. I certify wills, bills of lading, affidavits from sailors swearing they didn't start the fire. The work is honest and small, which is more than can be said for anything else in Port Calloway, including its history.

I knew someone was coming before she knocked. You develop a sense, in this trade, for footsteps that belong to trouble. These were wet boots on dry stairs, climbing neither fast nor slow — climbing the way water rises, which is to say steadily, and without any belief at all that it might be stopped.

She knocked twice. Then, before I could answer, a third time, softer, almost apologetic.

"Enter," I said, "before you talk yourself out of it."

* * *

The girl who came in was soaked to the collar and trying to hide it, which told me she'd come across the mudflats rather than risk the ferry landing. Mudflat walkers leave salt lines on their hems. Hers ran up past her knees.

"Are you Odile Ferrant?" she asked.

"I am whoever the Gazette says I am this decade," I said. "Sit down. You're dripping on my floor, and unlike the harbor, I care."

She sat. She was young — seventeen, perhaps eighteen, with the particular posture of a diver, shoulders rolled forward like someone perpetually about to go down instead of up. Her hands were working-class hands, scarred at the knuckles in the pattern rope leaves. Her eyes went around my office once, cataloguing it the way a careful person catalogues any room they might need to find again in the dark, and then came back to me and stayed there.

"I need something certified," she said.

"Most people do. Wills are Tuesday. Affidavits are whenever you can pay."

"Notarized testimony," she said. "Sworn statements from living witnesses, sealed and dated, regarding the founding fraud of the Meridian Trading Company."

I will admit — because I am too old to waste time pretending I am made of marble — that I laughed. It wasn't a kind laugh. There is no kind laugh available to a woman whose career ended because she took Meridian seriously once already.

"Young woman," I said, "do you know why I sit above a chandler's shop instead of in a courtroom?"

"I heard it was a ruling against the Company."

"Heard from whom?"

"A retired diver named Maren. And a dockworker called Sam Okonkwo. And—" she hesitated, and the hesitation had a shape I recognized; it was the shape of a name she wasn't sure she was allowed to say yet— "a dying man in the charity ward."

The laugh went out of me the way tide goes out of a channel: all at once, leaving things exposed that had been safely underwater all day.

"Elias Thorn," I said.

Her chin came up. "You knew."

"I knew he existed. I knew he kept ledgers. I did not know he'd grown a spine before he grew a grave." I leaned back. "Sit still, Miss — I'll have your name before we're done, never fear — and let me tell you how this works, since apparently nobody teaches law anymore except by getting people killed.

"A notarization is a seal and a signature. That is all it is. A seal says: this person swore this, before me, on this date, and I believed them competent to swear it. A seal does *not* say the statement is true. Do you understand the difference? Because half the frauds in this harbor are committed by people who don't."

"The difference," she said slowly, "is that a seal makes it admissible. Makes it count somewhere other than here."

"It makes it *weight*, " I said. "Whether it floats or sinks depends on where you drop it. A federal investigator with a writ can weigh a sealed affidavit against a company archive. A newspaperman cannot. A mob cannot. A magistrate bought by Crane — and there are four in this city, I could give you their names but you seem busy — will weigh your affidavit against his lunch and rule for lunch."

"So it has to reach the right hands."

"It has to reach the right hands *and survive the journey*. Which brings us to the part you're not going to like."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and I will set it down plainly because plainly is the only register left in my instrument case.

A dossier of sworn testimony against Meridian, assembled in secret, delivered to a federal investigator, is not a document. It is a declaration of war with a wax stamp on it. The moment it exists, every person who signed it becomes either a witness or a target, and the Company does not maintain much distinction between the two categories. Aldous Crane — and yes, I knew he'd arrived, the way you know weather has changed by the behavior of gulls — will not contest her evidence. He will contest her *witnesses*. He will buy some, frighten others, and if he is the historian I believe him to be, he will simply write the inconvenient ones out of existence: dead, departed, discredited, forgotten. History is a tide, and he holds the tide tables.

Therefore, I told her, I had conditions.

"One," I said. "Every witness comes here in person. Not depositions taken secondhand, not letters, not the word of a friend of a friend. If Sam Okonkwo saw something, Sam Okonkwo swears it under my lamp. If old Maren remembers it, Maren sits in that chair and says it aloud while my pen scratches. I will not build a weapon out of hearsay and hand it to a girl to carry into gunfire."

"That's what I came to ask for."

"Don't interrupt. Two: I keep copies. Sealed, in three places you will not know about. If you drown at the gangway — and given your family trade, drowning is evidently the family business — the truth does not drown with you."

Something moved across her face at that. Grief, old and well-kept, the kind people wear like a coat they've stopped noticing.

"My father drowned in this harbor," she said. "Eight years ago. The Company said it was his own fault. Bad knots. Reckless descent."

"And was it?"

"No," she said, and the flatness of it was worse than shouting. "And I can prove it wasn't, because the harbormaster falsified the dive logs, and last week he told me so himself with tears in his eyes and a bottle in his hand."

"DeWitt," I said.

"You knew that too."

"Miss Vasquez—" and there, I had it, the name arriving exactly when I'd promised— "there is very little in this harbor I don't know. What I lack is not knowledge. What I lack is standing. They took my court, but they could not take my seal, because a notary commission, unlike a judgeship, requires no one's goodwill to renew. That is why I keep it. That is why I sit here certifying sailors' wills and pretending I have retired. A disgraced judge is a decoration. A disgraced judge *with a seal* is a loaded thing lying on a shelf, waiting for someone reckless enough to pick it up."

She looked at me for a long moment. I let her look. People think the powerful thing in a negotiation is what you say. It isn't. It's what you're willing to sit through silently.

"You're asking what I'm willing to pay," she finally said.

"I'm telling you the price, and the price is not money. Money is the cheapest thing anyone will offer you this week. The price is this: every living witness, in this room, before the tide turns Thursday morning. All of them. The dockworker who spied on you and tore up the money. The watchman from the customs house, if he'll come — and understand that coming means confessing to taking Crane's bribe, which means prison, possibly, or at least the end of his employment and his reputation. The harbormaster, who will be walking in here to sign away whatever remains of his life. And the clerk, if God is generous enough to keep him breathing until Thursday."

"Thorn is dying."

"Then Thorn signs first," I said, "while he still owns a hand that can hold a pen. Everything else can wait. Death cannot."

* * *

She stood, then, and walked to my window — uninvited, which I noted with more approval than offense — and looked out at the harbor. Dawn was coming up gray behind the masts. Somewhere out there the *Persephone* sat at her berth, loading crates of official memory for London, and somewhere below us the chandler's boy was sweeping yesterday off the step.

"You should know what you're risking," she said, without turning around. "If I bring these people here and you seal their names, Crane doesn't come for me anymore. He comes for you. Your shop. Your seal. Whatever's left of your name."

"My name," I said, "was spent twenty years ago on a ruling that was correct. Everything since has been interest. Let them collect it — I'd rather it went toward something."

That got me the first real expression she'd shown me. Not a smile. Something earlier than a smile, the thing smiles grow from — the look of a person who has been carrying weight alone for so long that the offer of shared weight is almost frightening.

"Why?" she asked. "Why help? You lost everything once already."

I considered giving her the noble answer. I have a noble answer prepared; I keep it oiled for occasions like funerals. But she had climbed my stairs through the mudflats at night, carrying stolen ledger pages sewn into her coat, and she deserved the true one.

"Because I ruled against them once," I said, "on paper, in a courtroom, with every procedural protection around me — and it cost me everything, and it changed nothing. The paper went into a file. The file went into a drawer. The drawer went into a building they owned." I tapped the seal box on my desk, brass worn dull by twenty years of thumbs. "This is different. This is not a ruling. Rulings get appealed. Sworn testimony from living mouths — that gets *remembered*. You cannot appeal what five people said under oath while a notary watched. You can only kill the witnesses, and Crane may yet try, but killing five people in four days in a harbor city full of longshoremen who've decided they like you — that is a harder arithmetic even for a man with his pen."

She turned from the window.

"Thursday," she said. "Before dawn. Everyone."

"Everyone," I agreed. "And Miss Vasquez — bring your mother's slate."

She went very still. "How did you—"

"I read the Gazette," I said. "Crane's serialized history. He mentions a diver's widow who 'kept curious accounts,' in a paragraph designed to make her sound mad. When a historian insults the dead, the dead usually had something worth insulting them for. Bring the slate. Numbers witnessed under oath become evidence. Numbers locked in chalk stay chalk."

* * *

After she left — down the stairs, quiet as tide going out — I sat for a while with my seal box open in front of me, the way a man sits with a gun he hasn't decided about.

I want it recorded, since recording things is apparently what I do now, that I understood perfectly what I was agreeing to. In nineteen years on the bench I sentenced smugglers and wreckers and one memorable forger who cried so beautifully I nearly reduced his term just to make him stop. I thought I knew what corruption looked like. I did not. Corruption, in Port Calloway, looks like a fine building with the company name in gold letters, and a historian who writes the crimes as virtues, and a whole city that walks past both every day without seeing, because seeing would require admitting that the ground under their feet was paid for with somebody's drowned father.

The girl had seen it. Seventeen years old, indentured since childhood, and she had seen it — not because she was cleverer than the rest of the city, though she may be, but because history had finally happened *to her*, personally, with paperwork, the way it happens to everyone eventually if they live long enough near money.

I closed the seal box.

Four days. Five witnesses, if the watchman's nerve held and the harbormaster's bottle didn't finish him and God extended credit to dying clerks. One night of lamplight and scratching pens. Then whatever happened at the gangway would happen without me, because I was too old for piers and too conspicuous for heroics.

But the record — the record would exist. Inked, sworn, sealed, and hidden in three places Crane's men couldn't burn fast enough.

I lit the lamp against the gray morning, pulled a fresh sheet toward me, and began, in my small precise hand, to draft the oath.

Outside, the harbor bell rang the hour, and I noticed — for the first time in years — that I was listening to it hopefully.
