## 17. Testimony by Lamplight

The house on Chandler's Row had once belonged to a shipping magnate who died owing more than the harbor could count. Now it belonged to Odile Ferrant, and the chandeliers had been sold to pay her debts, which meant that when Juniper arrived at half past nine that night, the depositions would be taken by lamplight — six oil lamps, two candles, and a hurricane lamp set apart like a witness of its own.

Ferrant had arranged the parlor like a courtroom stripped for parts. A long table, a stenographer's bench, a chair facing the room so that whoever sat in it would face everyone else at once. She wore black, not mourning-black but working-black, and her hands were already ink-stained when Juniper came through the door.

"Count," she said.

Juniper counted. Maren, wrapped in a fisherman's coat too heavy for the mild night, her hands knotted with arthritis but steady on her knees. Sam Okonkwo, clean-shaven, wearing a shirt Juniper had never seen, pressed and white as sailcloth. The watchman, Corliss, hat in his lap, smelling of cheap gin he hadn't drunk — or had drunk and refused to smell of anymore. Harbormaster DeWitt, gray as fog, seated last, farthest from the door. And in Juniper's satchel: her mother's slate, Thorn's letter, the ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth, and a copy of everything, because Ferrant had insisted.

"Seven witnesses if we count the paper," Ferrant said. "Good. Paper testifies better than people. People die. Paper has to be burned, and burning takes longer." She looked around the room. "Mr. Crane's men have been passing this block since sundown. Two on the corner of Pell Street, one loitering by the lamplighter's pole, one I can't find, which means he's the dangerous one. So we work fast, and we work sworn, and we don't stop until it's done. When you give your testimony, you give it all. The shameful parts especially. Shame is what makes a record true."

She dipped her pen. "Begin with the oldest. Mrs. Maren Oduya."

* * *

Maren did not sit back. She leaned forward, into the lamplight, the way she must once have leaned off a diving ladder into dark water.

"My name is Maren Oduya. I was born free in Freetown, and I was sold into a diver's contract that looked so much like slavery the difference wasn't worth measuring. I dove for Meridian before Meridian had its name — when it was seven ships and a warehouse and men called themselves founders like it was a holy word.

"I testify to the cargo. Not the pearls. Any fool testifies to pearls. I testify to what was *under* the pearls, in the false bottoms of the hold crates, sealed in pitch. Crates that never appeared on any manifest I ever saw, and I saw them loaded with my own eyes because they made us load them at night and told us the weights were a company secret. Sixty-one crates across three voyages. I carried number forty-four myself, down the ladder, and my shoulder has ached for fifty years as a receipt."

Her voice didn't rise. It descended, like something settling onto the seabed.

"The contraband was arms and untaxed silk, and the profit from it is the profit Meridian built its countinghouse on. And when the customs inquiry came in 'sixty-two, the founders didn't fight it. They *bought* it. I know because my own husband was paid twenty pounds to sign a statement saying he'd seen nothing, and he took the money, and it ate him alive for the rest of his life, and I want that in your record too. We were not innocent. We were poor. Those are different things."

Ferrant wrote without looking up. "Do you swear this?"

"I swore worse oaths in worse water. Yes. I swear it."

* * *

Sam Okonkwo went second, because he asked to go before his courage changed its mind.

He told the room about the longshoremen's network — the tally slips passed hand to hand, the way information moved along the docks faster than any ship moved on water. He told about the money. His voice stayed level right up until it couldn't.

"Crane's man approached me in March. I was behind on my mother's rent. They gave me thirty dollars a month to report where Juniper went, who she spoke with, what she carried." He turned and found Juniper in the lamplight, and she let him look, because looking away would have been a mercy neither of them wanted. "I reported on her four times. I tore the money up eleven days ago. I'm telling this room, and this record, that I did it, because a record with only the clean parts isn't a record. It's a portrait."

"Will the witness state why he now opposes the company?" Ferrant asked.

Sam thought about it honestly, which Juniper would remember forever about him.

"Because they paid me to watch a girl drown slowly and call it employment. And because when I stopped, nobody punished me. That frightened me worse than any threat. Men like that only leave you alone when they've decided you don't matter — and I have decided I matter."

Sworn and signed. His hand shook writing his own name, and Ferrant steadied the page with two fingers until he finished.

* * *

Corliss the watchman talked like a man emptying his pockets after being caught, which he essentially was. He named names — the fixer Voss, the night clerk at the Pelican Hotel, the amount he'd been paid to leave the customs house's east stair unwatched on the fourth of the month. He described Juniper's escape in the flat tones of a confession, and then, at the end, he said the thing that mattered:

"They bought me for eleven dollars. Eleven. I've watched that gate for nineteen years for less than I'd get for selling a horse. You want to know how a fraud survives sixty years? It doesn't buy loyalty. It buys *cheap*, and it buys often, and it figures no one will ever add up the column."

Ferrant added it up. Eleven dollars, monthly, since January. Her pen scratched through the arithmetic like a knife going through canvas.

"Sworn?"

Corliss swallowed. "Sworn. And I'll say it to Crane's face if his face comes near enough."

* * *

DeWitt took longest to reach the chair, and longest in it.

The harbormaster sat with his hands flat on his knees, and when he finally spoke, his voice was the voice of a man reading his own autopsy.

"In the winter of 1871, Elias Vasquez filed a complaint about dive depths at the eastern pilings. The safety tables had been altered — shortened bottom times, extended descents — to speed the servicing of Meridian's fleet during a contract crunch. Your father—" he looked at Juniper and could not hold the look "—your father had measured the tables himself, against his own body, and knew they'd been falsified, and said he would take it to the federal maritime office.

"I was instructed to amend his log instead. To record him as having dived within safe limits, so that when he failed to surface, the record would show pilot error. A man's own logbook used to bury him." DeWitt's hands pressed harder against his knees. "I signed the amendment. Three weeks later he went down at the eastern pilings and did not come up, and the harbor called it an accident because the paper said it was, and I have signed that same paper in my memory every night for twenty-seven years."

"Why?" Ferrant asked, gently for her.

"Because they owned my debts too. Because it was one signature. Because it was always just one signature, right up until it was a drowned man and a fatherless child." He turned to Juniper then, fully, an old man offering his head. "There is nothing I can give you that returns him. I know that. I am giving you the signature instead. Use it however it serves."

Juniper had rehearsed something for this moment — cold words, exact ones, forged over the week like iron. But sitting in the lamplight, watching the old man's hands flatten against his knees as though bracing for a blow, she found she only had one thing to say, and it surprised her by being true:

"It doesn't serve me. It serves the record. That's enough."

DeWitt nodded as though she'd absolved him, which she hadn't, and signed as though she had, which maybe he needed more than she did. Ferrant blotted the page. Somewhere outside, footsteps crossed the corner of Pell Street and paused — and moved on.

* * *

They broke Thorn's pages open at midnight.

The copied ledger — half the original, the other half still hidden in the customs house — was laid out sheet by sheet under the hurricane lamp, and Ferrant read each figure aloud while Juniper matched it against her mother's slate. Column by column, year by year, the numbers agreed. The sixty-one crates. The payments to the customs inquiry. The amendment to the dive tables, initialed by order of the founding partners. And at the bottom of the oldest surviving sheet, in ink faded to the color of dried blood: the disbursement record of the *Vasquez line* — shares held silently, dividends paid to a name that existed only as an entry, the family bought and buried in the same transaction.

Her mother had known. Had written the numbers herself, underwater, on slate, because slate was honest and paper was not — and had chosen silence, and paid for it, and left the truth under the floorboards like a debt she hoped her daughter would forgive her for collecting.

Juniper set the slate down beside the ledger pages where the lamp could reach them both, and did not trust herself to speak, and found she didn't need to. Ferrant simply wrote: *Supporting documents authenticated by matching entries, witness: J. Vasquez,* and had her sign beneath it.

It was nearly three in the morning when Ferrant melted the first seal.

She worked with a candle and a spoon, wax from her own stock, pressing her disgraced judge's seal — a scale with one pan broken — into each certified dossier while the witnesses watched. Seven copies. One for Reyes, one for the customs house cache, one for Maren, one for Sam, one hidden in the floorboards where the slate had lived, one deposited with the Gazette's compositor to be printed if anything happened to the rest, and one she kept on her own person, sewn into her coat lining.

"Now hear me," Ferrant said, straightening at last, and her wryness was gone; what remained was older and harder. "This paper is true and this paper is powerless. Do not confuse the two. A record does nothing. It sits. It waits. Its whole power is that someone carries it into a room where power is forced to look at it — a courtroom, a pier, a ship's gangway. Until that happens, this is just expensive kindling, and Crane's men outside are proof that other people understand that better than you do."

"How long do we have?" Sam asked.

Ferrant glanced at the dead clock above the mantel — the harbor clock's twin, stopped at some old wrong hour. "The Persephone sails on the morning tide. Which gives you"—she calculated—"'a little over two days, and Mr. Crane knows it down to the minute. He won't come here tonight. Tonight he'll write. Tomorrow he'll offer. And the day after, he'll do whatever a man does when both offers and writing have failed." She gathered the lamps' light with a look around the room, at each of them in turn — the diver, the dockworker, the watchman, the harbormaster, the girl with the satchel.

"You've done the impossible part. You've made it true on paper. Now the only thing left is the hard part."

Juniper shouldered the satchel, and through the window, down on the black water, she could see the riding lights of the Persephone at anchor — small, patient, and already waiting.
