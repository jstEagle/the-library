## 19. Morning Tide

The clock said four, and for once it was right.

Juniper had not slept. She had copied the ledger's final page three times by candlelight, checked the seals twice, and then sat with the dossier wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child, listening to the harbor breathe. At half past four she gave up pretending and dressed in the dark — wool stockings, her father's old diving coat cut down to fit her, the slate strapped flat against her ribs where it had lived all week.

The certified packet was not large. That was what frightened her. Six days of testimony, half a burned ledger resurrected, a harbormaster's confession, a watchman's sworn statement, Maren's voice pressed into ink — and it all fit into a wrapper no bigger than a Bible. Paper was so light. It should have weighed more. It should have taken two men to lift.

She kissed her palm and pressed it to the floorboards over the hidden fourth copy, the way she'd once touched her mother's cheek to check for breath.

Then she went out into the last dark of Port Calloway.

* * *

Sam was waiting at the corner of Rope Walk as agreed, and beside him stood more people than she had asked for.

Maren, upright in her good coat, hands folded on her cane like a woman attending her own funeral and determined to enjoy it. The watchman Hobbes, hat in hand, eyes red-rimmed from a sleepless night of deciding whose side he was officially on. Two of DeWitt's clerks, carrying the harbormaster's signed depositions because DeWitt himself could barely walk. And behind them, half a dozen divers she knew by face if not by name, standing in the grey drizzle with their arms crossed, saying nothing, asking to be counted.

"You said witnesses," Sam said quietly.

"I said witnesses."

"I interpreted generously."

Juniper looked down the line of them — the whole strange, improbable record made flesh — and felt something in her chest go tight and hot. There was no time for it. She swallowed it down.

"The pier," she said. "Stay together. Don't let anyone separate you. Crane will try that first — he'll try to peel us apart like pages."

"And you?" Maren said. "Where will you be?"

Juniper put her hand flat on the oilcloth packet.

"Wherever Reyes is."

* * *

The Persephone came up out of the fog in pieces: mast, then funnel, then the long black flank of her, taking on cargo under a canopy of hissing gaslight. Longshoremen moved along the quay with crates on their shoulders, and every crate marked MERIDIAN — LONDON went aboard under the eye of Crane's men, who stood at intervals along the gangway like fence posts wearing coats.

The official archive went aboard at five o'clock sharp. Juniper watched from the mouth of Fish Street, behind a fishmonger's awning, as four men carried the iron-strapped chest up the gangway. Crane walked behind it. He wore a travelling coat the color of good sherry, and he paused at the top of the plank to look back at the harbor — actually look, hat brim tilted, taking it in the way a man takes in a view he owns.

He thinks it's finished, Juniper thought. He thinks the book is closed.

Good. Let him think so. A man who believes the ending is written stops checking the margins.

Reyes was already on the pier. She spotted him by his stillness — a compact, grey-bearded man in an unfashionable coat, standing where the customs officers clustered, holding papers nobody had asked him to hold. Federal Investigator Reyes had been in Port Calloway nine days and had made himself about as visible as driftwood. Sam's longshoremen confirmed it: he'd booked passage on the Persephone herself, intending to inspect the company archive at sea, where Meridian's lawyers said no land court had jurisdiction.

At sea. Of course. Crane hadn't just packed his evidence; he'd packed the courtroom too.

"He boards at half past six," Sam murmured, appearing at her shoulder. "Tide turns at seven-ten. They cast off the moment the pilot's aboard. So your window is everything between the gangway lifting and the lines going slack."

"An hour and forty minutes."

"Less, if the fog lifts early." Sam looked at her. "You know they'll be watching for you."

"They've been watching for me all week. I'm getting used to the attention."

"Juniper." His voice dropped. "If they take that packet—"

"They won't."

"If they take *you*—"

"Then you're my second copy," she said. "Everything's sworn, everything's sealed, Ferrant holds duplicates. If I vanish this morning, the story doesn't. That's the whole point. That's what I finally understand."

Sam studied her for a long moment in the grey light, and then something moved across his face that was almost a smile.

"When did you get dangerous?" he said.

"About six days ago. Keep up."

* * *

They moved at ten past six, in a body, openly — because the plan's first trick was that there was no trick. No sneaking. Crane expected a smuggled girl darting through alleys; instead he got a procession of witnesses walking down the center of the quay in full view of half the harbor's dawn shift, and the dawn shift stopped working to watch.

Juniper felt the eyes like weather. Dockworkers leaning on bollards. Divers sitting on upturned dories, not pretending to mend nets. A cluster of washerwomen by the fish stalls. Word had gone through the harbor overnight the way word always did here — not printed, not announced, just passed hand to hand like warmth: *the divers' girl has papers. The divers' girl is going to serve them today.*

By the time she reached the foot of the gangway, there were two hundred people on the pier and more coming down Fish Street.

Crane's men saw it too. Three of them detached from the cargo hatch and converged on her, moving fast but not running — running would draw the wrong kind of attention even for them.

"Miss Vasquez." The tallest one, a smooth-faced man she recognized from the debtors' island ferry, planted himself at the base of the plank. "There's a matter of your contract under review. You're required to—"

"She's required at the customs office," said a new voice, dry as paper. Odile Ferrant had come down the pier behind them at her own pace, leaning on no one, carrying her notary's satchel like a weapon she'd merely chosen not to draw yet. "I countersigned her release pending federal review at nine o'clock last night, witnessed by four persons. Would you like to read it? It's very short. Most true documents are."

The smooth-faced man hesitated, and in that hesitation Juniper stepped around him and put one foot on the gangway.

"Stop her," someone said, low, from above — and it wasn't Crane's voice, but it was Crane's voice by inheritance.

Hands closed on the oilcloth wrapper.

What happened next happened in the space of three heartbeats, and Juniper would remember it forever in slow motion: the tug at the packet, her own grip tightening, Maren's cane coming down across a wrist with the precision of sixty years' practice, and the crowd — the crowd simply *arriving*, the way water arrives, filling every gap, until Crane's men weren't holding a girl, they were holding two fists full of coat while two hundred people looked at them.

The wrapper tore.

Juniper held half of it. The other half stayed in the tall man's fist — the empty half, the wax-sealed duplicate wrapper Ferrant had insisted on layering, and Juniper heard the old judge's voice in her head, wry as ever: *Wrap anything worth stealing twice, child. Thieves believe in packaging.*

The real dossier rode flat against her ribs under the diving coat, strapped where the slate rode, where it had ridden since before dawn.

She climbed.

* * *

The gangway was thirty feet of wet planking, and it was the longest thirty feet of her life.

Halfway up she saw Crane come out of the deckhouse. He saw her, and she watched — actually watched — the composure leave his face in stages, like tide going out: first surprise, then calculation, then something older and uglier that he probably believed he didn't have.

"Miss Vasquez," he called down, and his voice was silk again, repaired mid-tear. "Whatever you imagine you're carrying, I promise you there is a version of this morning in which you are celebrated instead of prosecuted. Come below. One conversation. Ten minutes."

"I've read your version," Juniper called back, not stopping. "It was in the Gazette. Very moving. My mother comes off poorly in it."

"My ship leaves in fifty minutes, child, with or without—"

"Without," Juniper said. "Definitely without."

She reached the top. Reyes was waiting there, exactly where Sam's longshoremen had steered him, and up close he had tired, unglamorous eyes and ink stains on his fingers, and when she pulled the oilcloth packet from inside her coat he received it the way she imagined a priest receives something at an altar — carefully, with both hands, looking at it rather than through it.

"Certified and sealed," she said. Her voice, absurdly, was steady. "Depositions from eleven witnesses, including the harbormaster DeWitt and the clerk Elias Thorn, who is dying and knows it. Copies of the original ledger, authenticated by Notary Ferrant, whose own statement of chain-of-custody is the third document. There is a fourth copy hidden elsewhere in this city which will surface if anything happens to me or to these. Federal Investigator Reyes, do you accept service?"

She had rehearsed the words for two days. They came out anyway sounding like someone else's.

Reyes broke the first seal before he answered.

"Miss Vasquez," he said, reading as he spoke, "I accepted service the moment you started climbing. But yes. Formally. Witnessed—" he glanced up, and Juniper turned to see the entire pier behind her, two hundred faces and the fog thinning over the water like a curtain being drawn by a hand. "—extensively."

Somewhere below, the smooth-faced man was shouting to no effect. Somewhere aft, the Persephone's captain was bellowing about schedules and tides and being paid by the hour. And somewhere amidships, Aldous Crane stood by his iron-strapped archive with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the impound writ go up to his chest like a man watching his own obituary set in type.

Juniper had imagined this moment all week as triumph. Standing in it, she found mostly cold, and mostly weight — the weight of the packet leaving her hands, of the week leaving her hands, of the whole buried thing rising at last into daylight where it would have to be looked at.

Her father had drowned under this harbor carrying pearls for these people. Her mother had kept numbers on a slate because numbers were the only safe language left to her. None of it was fixed. Papers could be contested; courts could be bought; a trial could take years. But the record existed now. It existed outside of Crane's chapters, outside of anyone's permission. Whatever happened next, no one would ever again be able to say it never happened.

That was the whole point.

"Cast off in twenty minutes," Reyes said quietly, folding the deposition list. "I'd suggest you get off my crime scene, Miss Vasquez. Or stay. Your choice — you appear to be the only person in Port Calloway who does whatever she chooses."

Juniper looked down the gangway at the wet planking, at Maren and Sam and the crowd parting to let her through, and beyond them the harbor opening grey and enormous toward the sea, the same water that had taken her father and taught her mother to write.

"No," she said. "I'm staying to watch the lines cast off."

And that was when the fog lifted all at once, the sun came through hard on the water, and from the quay behind her rose a sound she had never heard in Port Calloway before — the harbor clock, dead for forty years, giving one cracked, rusted, impossible stroke.

Someone down there was already fixing things.

* * *

The Persephone cast off at seven-ten precisely, minus one federal investigator, one iron-strapped archive, and one historian who stood at her stern rail until the fog took him — and Juniper stood on the pier with the crowd roaring around her like weather, holding Sam's arm, feeling the deck tremble under her feet through the planks.

Then Sam grabbed her sleeve.

"Juniper." His face had gone white. "The ferry from the debtors' island — it's just docked."

She turned. At the far end of the pier, a small knot of passengers was coming ashore through the thinning crowd, and at its center walked a tall man in a company coat whom she had last seen alive in a lamplit office, telling her that indenture was a kindness.

In his hand he carried a document with a fresh seal, and he was smiling.

"There's someone here says he's got a warrant," Sam said, "and someone else told him you'd be at the pier."
