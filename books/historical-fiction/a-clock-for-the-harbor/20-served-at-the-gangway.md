## 20. Served at the Gangway

The Persephone came up out of the fog in pieces: first the topmasts, then the yards, then the long black flank of her, gangway down like a tongue lolling for cargo. Juniper stood at the foot of Pier Six with the oilskin packet strapped tight against her ribs and counted the men between her and the federal investigator the way her father taught her to count seconds on a dive.

Seven. Maybe eight. Big shoulders, company coats buttoned over no company badges. Crane had dressed his fixers in wool this morning instead of shadows, because this was the hour he'd bought and paid for, and he wanted them visible.

Reyes stood at the head of the gangway with a leather case under one arm, talking to the second mate. A hundred feet away. Forty feet of planking and seven men, and behind her, she knew without turning, two more had peeled off from the fish market to close the street.

"Miss Vasquez." The nearest fixer didn't touch her. They never touched her; that was the trick of it, the politeness of men who'd been paid to make sure she simply failed to arrive. "Mr. Crane asks you to reconsider. There's a boat alongside. Dry clothes. A cabin."

"Tell him I've seen his handwriting," Juniper said. "It's very fine."

She turned away from the pier as if beaten — let her shoulders drop, let them see a seventeen-year-old girl doing arithmetic and losing. Sam was somewhere in the crowd with Maren's crew, holding the dockworkers' line, buying her minutes with their strike. She would not waste them looking back.

There was another way onto that ship. There had only ever been one way, if she was honest, since the night she decoded her mother's slate by candlelight and understood what the water had been trying to tell her all along.

* * *

She walked north along the seawall, quick and unhurried, past the net racks, past the tar vats, until the pilings of Pier Five gave her cover. The tide was half-flooded, running east, cold as a confession. Her mother's voice was already in her ear — *check your weights, check your heart, the water doesn't care which one fails* — and Juniper stripped off her coat and boots and stood in the gray dawn in her diving shift, the dossier wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string, tied high on her chest where it wouldn't drag.

Old Maren had helped her rig it last night, knotting the harness herself with hands bent like coral. "Your father carried papers once," she'd said, not looking up from the knots. "Different kind of run, same water. You breathe like him. Short in, longer out. Don't fight the cold; argue with it."

Juniper argued with it.

The first stroke took her breath and gave back ice. The second took more. By the tenth the harbor had folded around her like a hand she'd known all her life, and she stopped being a girl fleeing seven men and became what she had always been underneath the indenture papers: a diver's daughter, in her element, descending.

Below the surface Port Calloway was a different city. Fog didn't reach here. Light came down in slanted columns, green-gold, moving like slow banners over the pilings. Chains hung from the pier foundations furred with weed, and she swam through them the way she'd swum them at dawn for nine years, clearing fouling, reading the water, cataloging the harbor's rhythms like scripture. This was the work they'd bound her to. This was the work that would unbind her.

A hundred yards. She could do a hundred yards on one breath at this depth — she'd done two hundred for less money than Crane spent on ink. But she wasn't swimming for distance now. She was swimming for the space beneath the Persephone's hull, and the ship was alive above her.

She felt it before she heard it: the deep thrum of the engine room lighting fires, the shiver of a vessel waking up. Bubbles streamed past her face from the bilge outlets, hot and foul-tasting even through the salt. Above her, forty feet of keel moved shadow across the light like a cloud crossing a field.

Her lungs began their argument. *Short in, longer out.* She kicked for the great dark curve of the hull, found the anchor chain — new-painted, heavy links vanishing upward into brightness — and followed it, hand over hand, letting the chain do her climbing.

Halfway up, the tide shoved her sideways into the hull. The knock of her shoulder against barnacles was nothing; she'd taken worse clearing chains. But the jolt cost air, and when she pressed her cheek to the cold iron and looked up, the surface was a ceiling of hammered silver, impossibly far, and the whole weight of the harbor seemed to sit on her chest like a patient hand.

*This is where he died,* she thought. Not here, not now — but here, in this water, in this dark, sent down unsafe by DeWitt's falsified logs, silenced by men who kept ledgers. Her father had come up from dives like this one into a world that had already decided what his life was worth. She was going down — or rather across — into a world that hadn't decided yet. That was the difference. That was everything. History wasn't something that happened to other people. It was happening right now, in the green dark, to her, and she was carrying it on her own ribs.

Her lungs burned. She let them. Pain was just the body keeping count.

She kicked free of the hull and rose.

* * *

The surface broke over her an inch from the Persephone's waterline, and for one long moment she hung there, treading, just breathing — dragging the morning into herself in great ragged pulls while the ship's side rose beside her like a cliff. Voices carried down from the deck, thinned and made strange by the height: orders, laughter, the creak of cargo falls.

Then a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: the groan of mooring lines coming off their bitts.

They were casting off early. Of course they were. Crane had watched her walk away from Pier Six and done the arithmetic himself, and the answer was *now.*

Juniper got her hands on the sea ladder — brass rungs bolted down the hull for painters and pilots, rusted at the waterline exactly where her fingers found them — and climbed. The dossier rode her chest. Her arms were lead and fire. Halfway up, the ship breathed beneath her, the hull leaning as the tug *Cormorant* nosed against her flank to walk her off the wall, and Juniper flattened against the plates and rode the motion like a wave, the way her mother had taught her in some other lifetime, slate in hand, twenty feet down in clear tropical water: *the sea moves, you move with it, fighting is drowning.*

The tug pushed. The gap between hull and pier widened — three feet, five — and Juniper went over the rail in the widening dark of the ship's shadow, landing hard on her knees on wet deck planking.

A sailor saw her first. Then another. Shouts, boots. She stood up slowly, dripping, hair plastered flat, the oilskin packet held out before her in both hands like an offering or a verdict, and walked toward the gangway as the crew parted around her the way water parts around a piling.

* * *

Crane was already there.

He stood at the head of the gangway with the harbor falling away behind him — the whole waking city spread out below in the lifting fog, cranes swinging, gulls wheeling, the strike line of dockworkers a thin dark seam along the quay — and for the first time since she'd known him, Mr. Aldous Crane looked genuinely startled. His coat was immaculate. His valise sat at his feet. Behind him, Reyes had turned from the rail, and the federal investigator's eyes went from Juniper's dripping figure to the packet in her hands with the sharp, total attention of a man whose profession was paper.

"You can't be served here," Crane said, and it came out almost gently, almost sadly, the historian reaching for the right word and finding it missing. "We're underway."

"We're not," Juniper said. "And even if we were—" her breath came hard, her voice raw with salt, but it carried, and she made sure it carried, "—there's a right older than this company. Harbor law, from before Meridian owned the map. *Any diver may serve papers on a vessel in port.* In port until her lines are fully cast and her pilot is aboard. Ask your pilot. Ask him."

The pilot, a weathered woman at the rail with a brass whistle, didn't say anything. She just looked at the mooring lines — two still on, the aft spring still fast to the bollard on the quay — and then at Reyes, and shrugged the smallest shrug in maritime history.

Crane's men were coming up the gangway from the pier, too late, boots hammering. The aft spring line hung slack in the water, half-cast. And Juniper Vasquez, seventeen years old, indentured no longer by any measure that mattered, held out the oilskin packet with both hands to Investigator Reyes.

"C certified copies," she said. "Testimony sworn last night before Judge Ferrant. The original ledger pages. Thorn's confession. DeWitt's. Mine." Her voice cracked and she let it. "It names my family. It names your archive, Mr. Crane. Every page of it. Read it before you read yours."

Reyes took the packet. Broke the wax with her thumb. Looked at the first page, and the second, and Juniper watched a federal investigator's face change the way weather changes over open water.

"Investigator," Crane said smoothly, "that document is the product of coercion and forgery, and I can demonstrate—"

"I'm sure you can," Reyes said, not looking up. "You're evidently very good with documents. So am I." She raised her voice. "By authority of the United States district court, this vessel and her cargo are impounded pending inspection. No lines cast. Pilot, hold station."

The pilot blew her whistle once, short. Down on the quay, Sam Okonkwo's voice rose in something that might have been a cheer, taken up by fifty throats.

Crane looked at Juniper. Water pooled off her shift onto the deck between them, spreading dark. He opened his mouth — and whatever he found there, whatever chapter he might have written about this moment, he did not say it. He looked instead at the sea, at the harbor he had spent a career making into legend, and his face was the face of a man watching the tide go out on something he'd mistaken for permanent.

"You swim well," he said finally. "Your father would—"

"Don't," Juniper said. "You don't get his name in your mouth. You don't get anyone's name. That's over. You wrote history like it was yours to write." She nodded at the packet in Reyes's hands. "Now it's ours. All of it. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts."

Reyes tucked the dossier inside her coat. "Get below and dry off," she told Juniper, almost kindly. "There's going to be a great deal of paperwork."

For the first time in a week, Juniper laughed.

* * *

She stood at the rail while Reyes's men came aboard and the impound seals went up, watching the fog burn off the harbor her family had drowned in and been bound to and finally, this morning, claimed. Somewhere down the quay, Maren's divers were standing on the pier in a silent line, and Sam was grinning up at her like the sun itself, and the aft spring line swung loose in the brightening water, going nowhere.

The Persephone wasn't going anywhere either. Not today. Not with her lies aboard.

But as Juniper watched the customs house clock across the water — the dead clock, the stopped clock, frozen at the hour of the founding fraud — she saw movement on its tower: men with ropes and tools, hoisting scaffolds, and beside them on the parapet, small in the morning light, the unmistakable figure of Odile Ferrant, pointing up at the clock face like a general ordering a charge.

The harbor clock, stopped since the fraud. The city said it would never ring again.

Ferrant had apparently decided otherwise — and Juniper understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones heavier than any ledger, that the next chapter wasn't hers to write alone. The clock was going to move again. And when it struck the true hour for the first time in seventy years, every soul in Port Calloway was going to look up and ask what time it had really been all along.

She gripped the rail, soaked and shivering and freer than she had ever been, and waited to hear it ring.
