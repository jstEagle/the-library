## 1. The Summons

The letter had cost her three weeks of careful work and one sleepless night, and Ansel Quintana read it again as the mail coach jolted up the last mile of mountain road, because reading it steadied her the way a plumb line steadies a wall.

*To the Guild of Horology, Bellhaven: The master clock of the Vell Observatory has ceased its motion. We require an apprentice of proven skill, dispatched under seal, to restore it before the spring ceremony. Wages and lodging to be provided by the Office of the Magistrate.*

Beneath it, the guild's stamp — copied from a wax impression she had bribed off a drunk clerk two winters ago — and beneath that, a name written in a hand she had practiced until her wrist ached: *A. Quintana, journeyman horologist.* Not Anselma. Never Anselma. A. Quintana, who did not exist except on paper, whose father had been nobody's anything but a hanged man, and whose face was only her own face if nobody thought to look for a dead man's daughter inside it.

She folded the letter away into the inner pocket of her coat, where it sat against her breastbone like a second heartbeat, and looked out at Vell.

The town filled the cup of its valley the way water fills a bowl — gray stone roofs, slate and shingle, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. Above it all, on a shoulder of bare rock, the observatory: a white dome and a square tower, and rising from the tower a spire with no clock face in it yet, only the dark rectangle where the dial had been removed. She could see the scaffold poles leaning against it even from here. They had stripped the clock down to its bones and then, apparently, discovered they could not make the bones live again.

That was why she was here. That was why a girl of twenty-three with calluses shaped like gear teeth and a dead man's name worn like a coat was rattling toward a town she had never seen, in a coat too big for her, with her hair bound flat under her cap and her voice pitched low in her throat these six years so that it came out rough and certain and male.

The coach rounded the last switchback and the bridge appeared below them — a high iron span across a gorge where a river ran green and furious, throwing spray that had already begun to feather the rocks with ice. A tollhouse stood at the near end, lamplit, and a man in a garrison greatcoat sat outside it on a stool, smoking, watching the road with the particular boredom of someone paid to watch and hoping nothing comes.

Ansel looked at him for exactly as long as it took to memorize him — young, mustache untrimmed, rifle propped against the tollhouse wall rather than held — and then looked at the mountains instead, which told her nothing about herself and could not betray her.

The coach slowed onto the bridge. Iron sang under the wheels, a long ringing note that rose through the floor and up through her boots and settled somewhere behind her teeth. She put her gloved hand flat against the window frame and felt the vibration there, counted without meaning to — the bridge rang true, no crack in the tone, no dead spot. Her father would have said a structure that rings clean is a structure that can be trusted. Her father had said a great many things, and then he had swung from a gallows in Bellhaven when she was seventeen, and every one of those things had become both gospel and wound.

*A. Quintana,* she reminded herself. *Journeyman. Quiet hands. A man who talks about gears and not much else.*

The coach stopped. The door opened onto cold that smelled of pine resin and river stone and woodsmoke, and Ansel stepped down into Vell carrying her tool chest herself, declining the driver's offer of help with a shake of the head and a grunt, because A. Quintana carried his own tools and did not waste breath on gratitude.

* * *

The town square was smaller than she had expected and older than she had been prepared for. Buildings leaned together over the cobbles like conspirators; a fountain stood dry and lidded for winter; and at the square's center rose the thing she had come four hundred miles to see — the lantern post. It was no ordinary lamp. It was a pillar of black iron twice the height of a man, crowned with a glass case, and inside the case hung a mechanism she recognized at once and had never seen before: a small turret clock, brass and steel, its escapement visible through the glass, its single hand pointing at ten minutes past eleven in the morning.

A clock mounted on a lamp. She stood in the cold staring at it like a pilgrim, and hated herself a little for it, and kept staring anyway. Somebody in this town, decades ago, had decided that time itself should be lit for the people walking home in the dark. There was an engraving around the base, half worn: FOR THE LANTERN HOURS.

"You'll be the guild man."

She turned. The speaker was a woman built like a keel — broad, weathered, sixty perhaps, with gray-streaked hair pinned back and eyes the color of wet slate that went through Ansel's cap, her jaw, the set of her shoulders, and filed everything away somewhere. She wore an apron over a wool dress and stood in the doorway of an inn whose sign creaked above her: a painted lantern swinging on chains, THE LANTERN INN in gold letters gone brown with age.

"A. Quintana," Ansel said. Her voice held. It always held now. "Guild dispatch. I'm told the observatory clock is down."

"Told, or sent?" The woman's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Marta Sable. This is my house. You'll lodge here — the magistrate's office pays for it, which means I feed you worse than my paying guests and charge them the difference." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in out of the frost. You look like your own ghost."

*If only you knew how carefully I've made that true,* Ansel thought, and picked up her chest, and followed her in.

The inn's common room was warm and dim and smelled of tallow, tobacco, and something with onions in it that made her stomach clench with an ache she refused to name in front of strangers. Three men sat by the fire playing cards, and one of them lifted his head — old, white-bearded, with hands like knotted roots and a scar running through one eyebrow — and looked at her longer than courtesy required. She catalogued him the way she catalogued everyone: *miner, retired, hands too wrecked for the shafts; knows everyone; remembers everything.* Then she looked at the fire and became a tired tradesman warming himself, which was not entirely a performance.

"Room at the top of the stairs," Marta Sable said, setting a key and a bowl of stew in front of her without asking whether she wanted either. "Observatory warden expects you at first light. Brannock, his name is. He'll want your papers."

"They're in order," Ansel said.

"I didn't ask if they were in order." Marta wiped the table with a cloth, unhurried. "I said he'd want them. Different thing." Her slate eyes rested on Ansel one moment more — weighing, sorting, filing — and then she turned away toward the kitchen, calling something to the cook, and Ansel sat with her stew going cold in front of her and her heart going too fast and understood that she had just been read by a stranger the way she read movements in an escapement: tooth by tooth.

She ate the stew anyway. It was good. That frightened her too, a little — how easily the body forgives, how quickly it calls a place *warm* and wants to stay.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself she would — she had slept badly for six years and was practiced at functioning inside the exhaustion like a diver working underwater — but the inn's roof beams were close and low and the bed was soft, and softness had always meant danger to her, softness meant the mind wandering where it would, and tonight it wandered up the hill.

To the tower. To the clock.

She had read everything the guild library held on the Vell Observatory movement, which was one paragraph in a monograph on regional turret clocks: *eight-day going train, gravity escapement of unusual design, chime barrel of fourteen pins, reputedly the finest piece of horological engineering between the capital and the coast.* Fourteen pins. Most striking trains used twelve, one pin per hour. Fourteen meant the maker had built in slack for a carillon, or for something stranger, and no document she could find said what.

Her hands itched to have it open in front of her. That was the truth of her, underneath all the other truths: the fear, the forged letter, the buried name — under all of it ran this, the oldest current, the one her father had put there when he set her small fingers on a balance wheel and said, *Listen, mija. Everything that keeps time is also keeping a promise.* She had been nine. The wheel had ticked against her fingertips like a captive heart. She had never recovered.

And under the wanting, threaded through it like a wire through wax, ran the other thing. *Vell.* She knew the word from her father's letters — real ones, kept in oilcloth under a floorboard until the constables took the floorboards apart. *There's money moving out of the miners' fund in Vell, and one day I mean to follow it.* He had never gotten the chance. Or he had gotten the chance, and it had killed him. Six years, and she still did not know which, and she had crossed a country partly to put distance between herself and the question, and now the question had a shape on the horizon: a white dome and a blind tower, waiting for her.

Coincidence, she told herself. Towns had clocks. Clocks broke. Guilds sent menders. The world was full of ordinary machinery and she was an ordinary mender.

She did not believe herself. But she dressed before dawn anyway, bound her hair, checked her papers, and went out into the blue-black cold with her chest in her arms, and climbed.

* * *

The observatory at dawn was a place made of waiting. Frost furred the iron railings; the dome above her sat silent as a closed eye; snow squeaked under her boots in the yard. A single lamp burned in the tower's base, and beneath it, blocking the door with the ease of a man who had blocked doors his whole life, stood Head Warden Brannock.

He was fifty and thick through the shoulders, with a face like a fist that had learned manners, and he held out one hand before she had finished climbing the steps. "Papers."

She gave him the letter. He read it slowly, lips moving, and she stood in the cold and watched him read and let none of what she felt reach her face. This was the discipline, the whole of it: to be looked at and remain opaque. Somewhere behind his eyes her father existed — Brannock would be forty-some, but the name of Tomás Quintana had traveled; it had been in pamphlets; men had worn his initials carved into their tool handles after the end. If Brannock made the connection between a hanged organizer and the surname on a guild letter —

But people rarely did. People saw what the paper told them to see. A man's name on a man's papers, and above it a tired face under a cap, and that was the whole story. Six years of practice said so. Six years of practice had never once been tested in the town where her father's ghost might still walk.

"Quintana," Brannock said, testing the syllables. His eyes came up. They were pale, unhurried, and they stayed on her face a moment longer than inspection required. "Bellhaven guild sends apprentices far afield these days."

"Sends whoever will go," Ansel said. Her voice came out flat and bored, and she was grateful to it. "It's winter, Warden. Nobody ambitious volunteers for a mountain in winter."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile — an assessment. He handed the papers back. "Third floor. Mind the stairs; the ice gets in. The movement's been stripped for cleaning by the last man, who then drank himself out of a job, and the magistrate wants it running by the thaw for the restoration ceremony. You'll report to me daily. You'll speak to no one about the works — no one. And you'll keep the tower locked when you're not in it." He stepped aside, finally, and the door opened on darkness and the smell of machine oil and cold stone.

"And Warden," Ansel said, pausing on the threshold, because A. Quintana was a tradesman and tradesmen asked, "the last man — did he leave notes? Drawings? A movement like this one, I'd rather know what I'm inheriting."

Brannock looked at her for a long moment. The lamp between them guttered in a draft off the gorge, and in the shifting light his face was difficult to read, which she suspected was its permanent condition.

"He left it stripped," Brannock said. "That's all he left." And he pulled the door wider, and gestured her in, and followed her up into the dark of the tower.

The stairs wound clockwise — she noted it absently, the way she noted everything, *clockwise, so defenders coming down have the sword arm free; some old builder thinking of sieges* — and at the third landing Brannock unlocked a door and stood back, and Ansel Quintana walked into the presence of the great clock and stopped breathing.

It filled the chamber the way a cathedral fills a nave. Even stripped — the dial removed, the hands crated, the weights let down — the frame stood taller than two men, iron and brass and oak, and every part of it was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when someone loved them while making them. She could see the maker in the file marks, in the chamfers cut where no one would ever see them, in the little brass plaques engraved with part numbers in a fine old hand. She walked forward without deciding to, set down her chest, and laid her bare hand — she had pulled the glove off without noticing — flat against the going-train's great wheel.

Cold. Dead. A promise paused, not broken.

Then she saw it, and her blood changed temperature.

The escapement — the gravity escapement, the famous one, the heart — had been tampered with. Not damaged. *Tampered.* The pallets had been re-cut with a graver finer than any warden's toolkit, the impulse faces altered by degrees so small that a lesser eye would have called it wear and a lesser touch would have missed it entirely. Someone had done this deliberately, patiently, with skill approaching her own — had reached into the clock's heart and changed what it would do and when, and then reassembled everything and walked away.

Not sabotage to stop the clock. The clock had been stopped openly, for cleaning. This was sabotage to change it. To make it do something. To make it say something, when it next ran.

Behind her, Brannock's boots scraped the stone floor. "Well, mender? Can you fix it?"

Ansel kept her hand on the cold brass and her eyes on the altered pallets, and heard her own voice answer from very far away, steady as a pendulum.

"Oh, I can run it," she said. "The question is — what exactly am I running?"
