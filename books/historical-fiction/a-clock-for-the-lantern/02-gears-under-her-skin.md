## 2. Gears Under Her Skin

The tower smelled of cold iron and pigeon droppings and something older beneath both — the mineral breath of stone that had stood a century. Ansel Quintana climbed the last of the winding stairs with her tool roll over her shoulder and her cap pulled low, and let herself stop, for one unguarded moment, at the door to the clock chamber.

She had seen great clocks before. She had been raised inside them, in a sense — her father's workshop in Aldensport had backed onto the church tower whose bell she had heard every hour of her childhood, and she had spent more nights than she could count lying awake counting its strokes the way other children counted sheep. But the church clock had been a parish instrument, modest and honest. This was something else.

The Vell observatory master clock occupied the whole of the tower's third chamber: a case of black walnut rising twelve feet to a molded cornice, its dial-work connected by rod and cable to the great exterior faces far above, its weight lines descending through shafts cut into the very bones of the mountain. The pendulum — she could hear it even through the case — swung with the slow patience of something that believed itself immortal. Nine feet of steel rod and a bob of polished lead, beating two full seconds to each swing, dividing the mountain night into equal portions as it had done for sixty years.

*You are an apprentice,* she reminded herself. *Apprentices gawk.*

She arranged her face into the appropriate expression — competent, incurious, slightly resentful of the cold — and pushed open the door.

Head Warden Brannock was waiting for her inside, which she had not expected. He was a slab of a man gone soft at the edges, gray-whiskered, with the red-rimmed eyes of someone who slept badly, and he stood directly in front of the clock case with his arms folded, as if she had come to steal it rather than repair it.

"You're the boy from Corvane & Sons," he said.

"Yes, sir." Her voice sat where she needed it, low and flat, a young man's voice with a cold in it. "Ansel Quint—" She caught it, swallowed the surname whole. "Quin. Ansel Quin. Journeyman apprentice."

"Quin." He said it like he was testing a coin between his teeth. "Your letter said you'd be older."

"Your letter said the clock had stopped." She let a little of the professional irritation through, because a real apprentice with real skills would have some pride, and pride was the best armor she owned. "It didn't say anything about an audience. I'll need the room to myself, and I'll need lamps, and I'll need no one touching anything until I've mapped the train. If someone's been fiddling with it—"

"No one has been fiddling with it." Brannock's jaw tightened. "It stopped on its own. Things wear out."

"Things do," she agreed mildly, and set down her roll, and knelt, and opened the case.

* * *

The lock on the case had been oiled recently. That was the first thing she noticed, and she noticed it with the small cold part of her mind that never stopped noticing, the part that had kept her alive through four years of passing as a boy in workshops where the men's hands were twice her size. Oil on a lock meant a key used often. A key used often on a master clock's case meant someone went inside who shouldn't, or went inside often enough that they'd grown tired of scraping their knuckles.

She said nothing about it. She took out her lamp, trimmed the wick, and began.

The proper way to read a sick clock was the way a physician reads a patient: history first, then symptoms, then — only then — the knife. History first. She traced the going train with a fingertip held a hair's breadth above the wheels, following the great brass chain of gearing from the barrel up through the intermediate pinions to the escapement itself, the anchor and escape wheel that were the heart of the whole affair. Dust lay everywhere in a fine gray felt, undisturbed except along one clean channel — recent fingers, reaching for the escapement, nothing else touched.

Symptoms next. She turned the fly by hand, gently, and felt the train resist, and felt it *grind* — a tiny catching, high up, like a pebble in a boot. There. She raised the lamp.

The escape wheel's teeth should have been a matched set, thirty teeth cut identical, each pallet of the anchor meeting them with the same delicate kiss. These did not. One tooth — the ninth, she counted — had been dressed down with a file. Not broken. Not worn. *Dressed*, with the patience of a jeweler, its face altered by perhaps the width of a hair, so that every ninth beat the anchor hesitated a fraction longer than it should, and the error accumulated, and accumulated, and somewhere past the thousandth swing the mechanism simply gave up and stopped, politely, as if tired.

And below it, wired to the striking train where no wire had any business being, a thin brass spring had been bent so that it would catch the gathering pallet once the going train faltered — a second fault, hidden behind the first, waiting to confuse whoever came looking.

Ansel sat back on her heels and forgot, for a long moment, to be an apprentice.

This was not wear. Wear forgave you gradually; wear was honest; wear said *I am old, help me* in a language any clockmaker could read. This was surgery. Whoever had filed that ninth tooth had understood the escapement better than most masters she'd worked under — had understood exactly how much to take and exactly how the error would propagate, had built a failure that would look, to a careless eye, exactly like old age. And the spring behind it — the spring was a signature. It said *I wanted this to be found, but not too soon.* It said *let them chase the first fault while the second waits.*

Her hands, she realized, were shaking, and not from the cold. Professional pride curdled in her chest like milk left out. She had taken this commission for coin and cover — a forged letter, a borrowed name, three weeks' wages and a town far from anyone who might know her father's face. She had expected a tired old clock. She had not expected to find, inside its case, evidence of a mind.

Someone had murdered this clock on purpose. And the question that mattered — the question she was already, helplessly, professionally compelled to ask — was *why this clock, why now, and why so beautifully?*

* * *

She worked through the morning to give herself time to think. That was the discipline: hands busy, mind free. She stripped and cleaned the affected section properly — a good workman would, and she must be a good workman or Brannock would have cause to send her away — and she made careful notes in her ledger in a deliberately cramped, schoolboyish hand, recording the faults she had found and omitting entirely the conclusions those faults pointed toward.

By midday she had also found the third thing.

It was small. A hole in the dust, really, no more than that: a clean absence in the gray felt at the base of the case, behind the weight chutes, where the shape of something box-like had rested and then been removed. The dust around the outline had been smoothed after — someone had thought of it — but smoothing leaves its own texture, and Ansel had spent four years reading the textures of things people tried to hide. Something had lived inside her father's trade's most sacred space, inside the very body of the clock, and had been carried away. Recently. Within days, perhaps within hours of the clock's failure.

She closed the case, locked it, and sat for a while on her heels in the lamplight with her heart going hard against her ribs.

Her father had taught her that a clock was a moral machine. *Every part answers for every other part,* Tomás Quintana used to say, holding a small anchor escapement up to the window light for his daughter to see, his hands scarred from mine work before he ever touched a lathe, his voice the voice he later used on strike platforms in front of ten thousand men. *A gear that lies about its position poisons every gear downstream. That's why we keep them honest. That's why a clockmaker makes a dangerous friend and a worse enemy, mija — we are trained to find the lie in the machine.*

She had buried that voice for eight years. Buried it under her brother's name and her brother's clothes and a deliberate, disciplined forgetfulness. And here it was, rising up out of a stranger's sabotage, speaking to her across a mountain and a decade.

*Find the lie in the machine.*

She stood, brushed off her knees, and went to find Brannock.

* * *

She found him in the warden's room at the tower's base, a low stone chamber that smelled of tobacco and lamp oil, where a ledger of observatory business lay open on the desk beneath a window too dirty to admit useful light. Two assistants were present, which was unfortunate; she would have preferred this conversation private. But there was no help for it, and perhaps witnesses would serve her better than privacy. A lone apprentice making accusations was a problem to be dismissed. An apprentice making accusations in front of subordinates had to be answered, at least a little.

"Mr. Quint—Quin," Brannock said, looking up. His eyes did that thing again, that slow measuring thing, top to bottom, cap to boots. "Done already?"

"Begun." She kept her tone exactly where an apprentice's belonged — respectful, eager, just short of presumptuous. "I've identified the primary failure. The escape wheel, ninth tooth, dressed down with a file to retard the beat. Plus a sprung wire on the striking train set to mask the fault. I can repair all of it. I'll need two days for the tooth alone; I'll have to cut and fit a replacement blank and re-cut the profile by hand."

The younger assistant whistled. Brannock did not move.

"Dressed down with a file," he repeated.

"Yes, sir."

"You're saying someone damaged my clock. Deliberately."

"I'm saying the wear pattern is inconsistent with service fatigue and consistent with hand-tooling." She had rehearsed the sentence on the stairs and it came out smooth and technical and deniable. "It's not unheard of. Sabotage of tower clocks happens — during labor disputes especially. It's why the guild keeps records of every clock's internal geometry. If you have the original specification drawings, I can compare—"

"We don't have them."

The answer came too fast. That was the second thing she noted, in the same cold ledger at the back of her mind. Nobody answered a question about sixty-year-old drawings *too fast* unless they had already considered the question and prepared the refusal.

"They'd be in the observatory archives," she pressed. "Every installation this old would have deposited—"

"The archives are being reorganized." Brannock closed the ledger on his desk with a soft, final sound, like a door shutting. "Listen to me, boy. This clock stopped because it is old. Old clocks stop. The magistrate has engaged your firm to make it run again before the restoration ceremony at winter's end, and that is the whole of your employment. You will not find files. You will not find wires. You will find wear, and you will fix it, and when you're finished you'll sign the guild book and go back down the mountain. Are we understood?"

The silence stretched. In it, Ansel heard the pendulum overhead, faint through floors of stone, keeping its indifferent count. *Two seconds. Two seconds. Two seconds.* Her father's voice again, absurdly, at a strike rally: *they count on us never counting back.*

"I understand that the clock is old, sir," she said quietly, "and I understand what I was hired to do. I'll send my firm a full report when the work's complete. They'll want the failure analysis for the guild records. It's standard."

Something crossed Brannock's face — not quite fear, but the cousin of it. He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh her: a slight young apprentice, sharp-tongued, inconvenient. Weighing what to do about her. Weighing, perhaps, whether there was anything that needed doing at all.

"The archives are closed," he said finally. "Reorganization. Magistrate Corven's order. Nothing in or out until thaw." He picked up his pen. "Two days for the tooth, you said. Get on with it, then."

She bowed her head, said "Yes, sir," and climbed back up the stairs with her blood drumming.

*Closed until thaw. Nothing in or out until thaw.*

She had not yet been told the bridge was shut. She suspected, suddenly and unpleasantly, that no one had hurried to tell her.

* * *

That evening the light through the observation slit windows went copper and then violet over the snowfields, and Ansel worked on, because working was safer than thinking and thinking was necessary anyway, so she did both at once, solder smoke curling under her nose.

Here was what she knew, laid out like parts on a bench:

Someone with genuine skill had sabotaged the clock. Not broken — *sabotaged*, with artistry, in a way designed to fail slowly and look innocent. Someone else had removed an object from a hidden space at the base of the case, and had done it carefully, and had then let the clock die on schedule. The head warden knew more than he said, feared more than he showed, and answered to a magistrate whose name had already been spoken to her twice in two days with a particular flattening of the voice that mountain people used for powerful men they did not love.

And here was what she did not know, and could not stop turning over: what had been in that hidden space? What could a clock's case hold that a man would kill the clock to empty it?

Her trade gave her only guesses, and the guesses frightened her. Tower clocks had hidden compartments — every clockmaker knew the legends, the Reformation-era hiding places for banned books, the smugglers' cavities in weight chutes, the pockets cut behind dial-works for papers and plate and once, in Prague, so the story went, an entire confession. A compartment in a town's master clock was the safest strongbox in that town: guarded by the town's own reverence, opened by almost no one, understood by fewer.

Papers, then. Almost certainly papers. Hidden by someone who had trusted the clock more than the archive vaults — and retrieved, lately, by someone else entirely.

She set down her soldering iron and looked at the great dark case across the chamber, at the pendulum's gleam moving behind the glass like the slow blink of a patient animal.

*I should finish the tooth,* she told herself, *collect my wages at ceremony's end, and walk away down the bridge without asking a single further question.* That was the strategy. Four years of strategy: be dull, be skilled, be invisible, survive.

But the base of the case. The smoothed dust. The box-shaped absence.

She waited until the tower was quiet, until Brannock's footsteps had faded down the stair and the assistants' voices had gone, until only the pendulum kept the hours. Then she took her lamp and knelt again at the base of the case, and this time she did not look at what had been taken out.

She looked at what remained: at the joinery, at the faint ghost-lines of saw kerfs beneath a veneer of age-stained walnut, at the clever concealed finger-hold shaped into the molding where only a clockmaker's eye would ever find it. The compartment was empty. But it had a twin.

Because the man who built one hiding place — her father's voice again, warm and certain, across eight frozen years — *always builds a second. You never put everything in the same pocket, mija. Any tailor knows that. Any smuggler. Any clockmaker.*

The second compartment was behind the striking-weight chute, fastened with a wooden latch she had to trip with a fine blade, and it opened under her fingers with a sigh like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked and bound and dense as bricks, were ledgers.

She did not open them. Not that night — her lamp was failing and her hands were not steady and some instinct older than strategy told her that this was a threshold one crossed only once, deliberately, with light to spare. She rewrapped them, rebolted the latch, restored the dust as best she could, and sat back in the dark with her heart hammering like a striking train gone mad.

Ledgers, hidden in the belly of the town's own clock, by someone afraid of vaults and wardens and magistrates. Ledgers worth killing a clock over. Ledgers someone had already come for — and gotten only part of, or gotten a decoy, or been interrupted—

Or ledgers that had been replaced here, recently, by someone who wanted them exactly where no one would think to look.

Below her, far below, a door slammed in the warden's room. Voices rose briefly and fell. Through the observation slit she could see the town of Vell spilling down its white mountainside, lantern-lit, one bridge arcing over the gorge toward the world — and even as she watched, she saw figures on the span hauling planks, and heard, thin with distance, the ring of hammers driving the seasonal closure bolts.

The bridge was closing tonight. Tonight, and she was standing in a locked tower over a murdered clock and a nest of secrets, wearing a dead man's son's name.

Somewhere down in the town, a dog barked at the hammers. The pendulum swung. Two seconds. Two seconds.

Ansel Quintana — Ansel Quin, journeyman apprentice, invisible, safe — put out her lamp and began, quietly, to plan how to ask questions that would not get her killed.
