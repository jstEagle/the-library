## 3. The Bridge Closes

The snow came in the night, the way mountain snow does — not falling so much as arriving, a white decision made above the town while everyone slept beneath it.

Ansel woke in her narrow room at the Lantern Inn to a silence with weight to it. She had grown up in mining country where silence meant the pumps had stopped and men were dead; she lay still for a full minute, heart counting, before her mind caught up with where she was. Vell. The observatory town. The clock.

She dressed quickly, fingers stiff despite the iron stove that had gone cold somewhere around the hour before dawn, and pulled back the curtain.

The world had been remade. Snow stood knee-deep in the square, unmarked, blue-white in the early light, as if no one in Vell had yet dared to touch it. The observatory tower rose at the square's north end, its dome wearing a cap of white like a scholar who had fallen asleep over his books, and beyond it — past the last of the rooflines — the mountain bridge crossed the gorge on its stone legs, a grey thread stitched between two walls of rock.

Or what remained of a thread. Ansel leaned closer to the glass, breath fogging it, and wiped it with her sleeve.

The bridge was gone.

Not gone, precisely. It was there, arched and patient under its new shroud of snow, but it had become something other than a crossing. The far side was a country seen through a window: the road descending in switchbacks toward the valley towns, the dark line of pines, all of it sealed away behind a curtain of weather. And along the near parapet, where the tollhouse stood with its chimney sending up a thin obedient thread of smoke, she could make out figures — two of them, moving deliberately, dragging something long and pale across the span.

A barrier rope. Or worse, a gate.

Her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, like a gear finding a tooth it did not want to mesh with.

"No," she said aloud, and the word made a small cloud in the cold room, absurd and final.

She had known. That was the bitter thing — she had *known*. The letter of apprenticeship had come in late autumn, and every sensible voice in her head had said what sensible voices say: *you will be wintered in before the work is done, no one repairs a master clock in six weeks.* She had told herself the thaw came early to Vell. She had told herself a great many things, standing in her borrowed coat with her brother's name folded into her pocket like a knife carried flat so it wouldn't cut the lining.

Ansel Quintana — no. Not that name. Ansel had buried that name eight years ago under another man's headstone of a name, *Anselm Ferro*, apprentice clockmaker of Marrow's Cross, and Anselm Ferro could not afford to stand at windows looking stricken.

She straightened her spine, bound her hair tighter, checked the flat binding across her chest out of old habit, and went down.

* * *

The common room of the Lantern Inn held that particular morning warmth that only rooms built of dark timber and older secrets can manage. A fire had been laid and woken; the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions and yesterday's ale sat in the low beams like permanent residents. Three miners took their breakfast at the corner table without speaking, their lamps hanging by the door still crusted with the night's shift. A peddler dozed over his coffee.

And behind the bar, arms crossed, watching the door rather than working it, stood Marta Sable.

Ansel had taken her measure carefully the evening she arrived, as she measured everyone: innkeeper, perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty — the kind of face that refused arithmetic — broad hands, a way of standing that suggested she had once carried things heavier than trays across borders less polite than this one. Her eyes were grey and unhurried and missed nothing, which was the trouble. Ansel had been in Vell four days, and every one of those days Marta Sable's gaze had rested on her a half-second longer than hospitality required.

"Snow's closed the bridge." Marta said it flatly, setting down a cup before Ansel reached the bar. Coffee appeared; she hadn't asked for it. "You'll have heard from your window."

"I saw." Ansel kept her voice level, pitched low — Anselm's voice, a young man's voice worn slightly rough. "How long?"

"How long is the ice bridge shut?" Marta almost smiled. "Till it isn't. Some years it opens at thaw, some years they send the first wagons over in March if the frost holds the timbers honest. You're here till then, mender. We all are."

"The work at the observatory—"

"Will keep. Master clocks have waited out winters before." Marta turned to the stove, lifted the pan, split her attention between the onions and her guest with the ease of long practice. "Warden Brannock gave you leave to lodge here? I heard he wasn't keen on outsiders sleeping in the tower quarters."

"He wasn't keen. The magistrate's office overruled him." Ansel sipped the coffee. It was terrible and scalding and she was grateful for both. "The summons came from the magistrate's own seal. They want the clock running for the spring ceremony."

"Mm." Marta slid onions onto a plate, delivered it to the miners, collected coins, came back. All of it done without hurry, and all of it done while some portion of her attention stayed anchored on Ansel like a small boat on a mooring line. "Corven wants his ceremony. Yes. He's spoken of little else since the clock stopped striking true last summer." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Funny thing, that. Clocks in this town don't tend to stop on their own."

Ansel set down the cup. Carefully. The click of ceramic on wood sounded loud in the morning quiet. "What do you mean?"

"Nothing a mender needs to worry over." Marta's tone stayed pleasant, but her eyes didn't. "Only that I've kept this inn twenty-two years, and I remember when the master clock was put in, and I remember the men who paid for it, and most of them are dead or scattered. So when it falters, I wonder." She picked up a cloth and began wiping the counter in slow circles, though the counter was clean. "You came recommended from Marrow's Cross. That's a fair distance. Strange season to take a mountain commission."

"The pay was good."

"It was. I ask questions at the garrison sometimes, when I'm collecting for the coach company." The cloth paused. "They speak well of an apprentice Ferro. Quiet. Clever with escapements. Nobody there could tell me much about him, though. Where he learned. Who his people were." She looked up, and for a moment the pleasant innkeeper mask thinned and something older looked out — something that assessed, weighed, and priced. "It's a hard thing, being far from your people in winter."

Ansel felt the familiar cold slide down her spine, the cold of a trap recognizing its own architecture. *She suspects.* But suspects what? A forged letter? A girl in boy's clothes? Or only that her guest was running from something — which was true, and common enough in mountain towns in autumn, when the roads filled with men one step ahead of debts and constables and worse.

"My people are dead," Ansel said. Flat. True enough to pass. "I learned my trade in the foundling shops of Marrow's Cross. There's nothing further back worth asking after."

Something shifted in Marta's face — not belief exactly, but a decision to shelve the matter, the way a woman shelves a ledger entry marked *unpaid* rather than striking it through. "Then you'll want warm lodging and steady work, and I'll want my regulator clock seen to. Third floor landing. It's run badly since summer — loses ten minutes a day, sulks, catches up when it feels like it. Fix it and your board comes off your account."

"That's generous."

"It's commerce." Marta turned away, then paused, her back to Ansel, and said to the stove: "There's a storm coming behind this one, they say. Two days of it, maybe three. After that, nobody goes anywhere anyway — bridge or no bridge. Eat your breakfast, mender. Winter's long, and it doesn't care what name you're hiding under."

She said it lightly. She said it exactly like a joke.

Ansel's hand was steady when she picked up her fork. It took considerable work to make it so.

* * *

She spent the morning at the observatory, because idleness was a luxury Anselm Ferro had never been able to afford and because the great clock was the only place in Vell where she felt anything adjacent to safe. In the strikin train's chamber, surrounded by brass and iron and the deep animal smell of machine oil, the world simplified into problems that could be solved with files and patience.

But the walk there and back taught her the shape of her imprisonment.

The square was a churn of excavation and recovery: householders digging out doorways, boys hauling sledges of snow toward the gutters, the garrison detail clearing a path from the barracks to the magistrate's house with military thoroughness while everything east of the fountain waited. At the tollhouse on the bridgehead, she stopped — close as she was permitted to stop — and watched.

The gate was real. Timber and iron, hauled across the span and set into brackets that had clearly waited for it all along, bolted sockets dressed with snow-shedding caps. This happened every year, then. The bridge was not merely closed; it was *locked*, formally, administratively, and the man who supervised the bolting wore the magistrate's colors and checked each fitting twice. Beyond him, the gorge wind ran up through the span and shook loose powder from the cables in long white sighs, like the mountain exhaling on whatever it had caught.

"You'll be the clock-mender." The tollkeeper — a red-nosed man wrapped in enough wool to furnish a modest sail — squinted at her from his doorway. "Ferro, is it? Heard you'd be wanting passage out before now. Bad luck."

"When does it open?"

"Thaw." He said it with relish, the way a man relishes a misfortune that isn't his. "Maybe April, maybe May. Depends what the gorge decides. It decides nothing good, most years. Last year the ice took the lower railings clean off. Year before that we lost a mule." He shrugged philosophically. "Town's got everything a body needs till then. Bread, beer, gossip. Mostly gossip."

"And the post? The valley mail?"

"Goes when the gorge allows. Sometimes a sledge runs the riverbed below, if the ice is thick and the driver is young and stupid." He grinned, showing gaps. "Mostly it waits. Everything in Vell waits, mender. That's what we're famous for. The clock, the mountain, the winters. Patience is our whole industry."

Ansel thanked him and walked back into the town proper with her collar up and her thoughts moving faster than her boots.

*Trapped.* The word sat in her chest with a physical pressure, but underneath the pressure, if she was honest — and she tried to be honest with herself at least once a day, in small doses — there was something else. Something that alarmed her more than fear did.

A part of her had *wanted* the bridge closed.

Because the clock was not fixed. Because on her second day inside the case, she had found the first anomaly — a pivot worn in a pattern no legitimate wear produces, a stress line cut at an angle that said *tool* rather than *time* — and she had not reported it, because reporting it would mean questions, and questions were dangerous, and because some instinct older than her caution had whispered: *there is more here. Look further.*

She had four months. Four months inside a mechanism someone had deliberately harmed, in a town whose magistrate had summoned her with suspicious generosity, watched by an innkeeper who asked questions like a customs officer and a warden who hated her on principle. Four months with nowhere to run and nothing to do but work.

It occurred to her, climbing the observatory path with the wind pushing at her back like an impatient hand, that this was either the safest she had ever been or the most endangered, and that she genuinely could not tell which.

* * *

The regulator clock on the third-floor landing of the Lantern Inn was a sorry thing — a cheap movement in a handsome case, bought by someone who valued appearance over substance, which told Ansel something true about the inn's history or its owner's. Its problem was mundane: a warped suspension spring and a pendulum bob that someone had moved and never properly seated. Two hours' honest work. She did it kneeling on the landing with her coat off, sleeves rolled, enjoying the small mercy of a fault she could actually cure.

She was tightening the last screw when the stair below her creaked.

Marta Sable stood at the turn of the staircase with a lamp in one hand and a covered plate in the other, and she was not pretending to have just arrived. She had been there long enough to watch.

"Better?" she asked.

"Better. New suspension spring — I had one in my kit. The bob needed seating. It'll keep honest time now." Ansel rose, wiping her hands, aware of how she must look: sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair escaping its binding, the posture of the work unmasking in small ways the posture of disguise. She reached for her collar. Marta watched her do it, and watched her notice being watched, and said nothing about any of it.

"Hungry?"

"Thank you."

Marta set the plate on the landing rail, uncovered it — bread, cheese, a heel of smoked fish — and then did not leave. Instead she settled herself on the stair below the clock, lamp at her feet, with the air of a woman prepared to stay awhile.

"Do you know what this regulator was?" she said, nodding up at the case. "It was the coach-clock. Ran the timetable for the night coaches, back when there were night coaches. Before the bridge tolls went to Corven's office and the schedules went to ruin." She smiled without much humor. "Everything in this town used to run to this clock, one way or another. Now it just runs badly, like the rest of us."

"You've been here a long time," Ansel said carefully.

"Twenty-two years. Before that—" Marta waved a hand vaguely westward, which covered a great deal of geography and none of it reputable. "Before that I was in trade. Goods moved. Certain goods, certain nights, certain prices. Nothing that would shock you, mender, though possibly it would." She looked up at the regulator's pendulum swinging its recovered arc. "A smuggler learns two trades, really. Moving things, and reading people. The second pays better and kills you slower."

"Is that so."

"It is." Marta's grey eyes came around, unhurried, and settled on Ansel with the gentle totality of a trap closing — except that traps, in Ansel's experience, did not usually offer you bread first. "And I'll tell you what twenty years of reading people has taught me, free of charge, since you're wintered in with me now and we might as well be honest housemates. You sit like a person who learned posture from a book. You listen like a person who's afraid of being asked to speak. When the garrison sergeant called you 'young master Ferro' on Tuesday, you flinched — barely, a hair's width, but I've made my living on hair's widths. And you check that binding under your shirt when you think no one's looking, which no one ever does, except me."

The landing was very quiet. Below them, faintly, the common room murmured its evening murmur; above them, the repaired regulator ticked with its new honest heart, marking out the seconds of Ansel's exposed and collapsing life.

She found, strangely, that she was not afraid. Or she was afraid, but underneath the fear ran a current of something else — exhaustion, maybe, or the strange lightness of a burden that has at last been named by another mouth. Eight years of flinching at hair's widths, and the first person to see it fully was an innkeeper on a snowy staircase holding a lamp.

"I don't know what you mean," Ansel said, because the words were automatic, and even automatic words deserve their moment.

"Of course you don't." Marta rose, brushed off her skirt, and picked up the lamp. At the turn of the stair she paused, her back half-turned, firelight from below catching the silver in her hair. "I'm not the garrison, mender. I'm not the magistrate. I don't trade in what people are — only in what they need moved, and I haven't moved that kind of cargo in a long, lonely time." She descended two steps. Then: "The gorge keeps its prisoners fed and lodged. Whatever brought you up this mountain in the wrong coat, it'll still be waiting in spring. Best use the winter to decide who's coming down when the ice breaks — the person who climbed up, or somebody new."

Her boots went down the stairs, unhurried. The common-room door opened below; laughter and warmth swelled briefly and was shuttered away again.

Ansel stood alone on the landing with her untouched plate and the ticking clock.

*I know,* the staircase seemed to say. *I have known since the night you walked in. I am not going to do anything about it. Yet.*

She ate the bread. It was good. Everything in this inn was good, and everything in it was watching her.

* * *

That night she wrote in her cipher notebook by candlelight — the habit of a lifetime, entries that would read as escapement calculations to any eye but hers — and found her pen stopping at the same line it had stopped at for four days.

*The wear on the third-wheel pivot is not wear.*

Below it, after a long while, she added:

*The bridge is closed. No one leaves. Which means whoever broke this clock is also still here.*

She looked at that sentence for a long time. Outside, the wind had risen again, driving new snow against the glass in soft persistent fists, and somewhere down the square the sentry at the tollhouse called the hour against the gale — a voice made small by distance and weather, calling numbers into the dark for no one.

Four months. A sabotaged clock. An innkeeper who knew too much, a warden who hated her, a magistrate whose generosity had no visible bottom, and a town sealed shut like a case with a hidden compartment.

Ansel blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the regulator downstairs keeping its newly honest time, and thought: *I came here to repair a clock. Perhaps the clock came here to repair me.* And then, more honestly, colder, as sleep finally pulled her under:

*Someone in this town wanted it stopped. And until I know who, every soul between the inn and the tower is either my witness or my enemy — starting with the woman who pours the coffee.*

Down in the common room, well past midnight, Marta Sable turned the sign on the door to CLOSED, banked the fire, and stood a moment in the dark with her head tilted, listening to the stairs, as if counting footsteps that had already gone up them hours ago.
