## 4. Thirteen Strokes

Ivo Havel had not slept well in thirty years, and he had stopped apologizing for it somewhere around the second winter after the hangings. Sleep, for a man who had stood in the square at Vell and watched four of his friends swing from the magistrate's gallows, was a country he had lost his papers to. So when the cold woke him — that particular deep-socketed cold of the small hours, the kind that crept through shutter and blanket and settled in the knuckles like a debt collector — he did what he always did. He rose. He banked the fire. He poured himself two fingers of the plum brandy Marta Sable pretended not to know he kept under his bed at the Lantern Inn, and he sat by the window with his bad knee propped on a stool, watching the mountain hold its breath against the stars.

The observatory tower stood above the town like a black finger pointed at heaven, and Ivo had spent enough years underground to know that anything pointing at heaven was usually pointing away from something else.

He was thinking about this — about towers and fingers and the way men build tall things to distract you from what they bury shallow — when the clock began to strike.

It should not have struck. That was the first wrongness, arriving before any sound did. The master clock had been dead since autumn, its heart seized, its voice gone out of the town the way a tooth goes out of a mouth — you kept touching the gap with your tongue. The apprentice girl, the one with the careful hands and the careful eyes, had been up in the tower for days now, and the whole town had been listening for the moment the bell would come back to them. But it was past midnight, past the hour when testing was done, past the hour when honest work happened. And yet.

The first stroke rolled down the mountain and into Ivo's chest like a fist closing around his sternum.

He knew that bell. You did not forget a bell you had heard toll your friends' last hour. The bronze of it had a grain, a texture, a slight hoarseness on the decay — the sound fraying at its edges like rope under load — and Ivo's body remembered it the way a scar remembers weather. One. Two. Three. He counted without meaning to, the way a miner counts his steps in the dark, the way a man counts when counting is all that stands between him and the abyss.

Four. Five. Six. Seven.

His hand had gone still on the brandy glass. Outside, dogs were waking. A light kindled across the square in the chandler's window. Somewhere a child began to cry and was hushed.

Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Twelve, and then — Ivo's breath stopped in his throat — twelve again.

Thirteen.

* * *

There are sounds a town makes, and there are sounds a town is made to hear, and Ivo Havel had learned the difference in the worst classroom there is. In the strike winter — they called it that now, though no one said it loudly, the way you don't say a dead man's name too near his grave — the organizers had used the church bells and the mine whistle and once, gloriously, the observatory clock itself, to pass messages the garrison could not read. A pattern of strokes meant *meet*. A different pattern meant *run*. Thirteen strokes — thirteen, an impossible hour, an hour that does not exist — meant the thing no one wanted to send and no one wanted to receive.

Thirteen meant: *they know. Move tonight.*

Tomás Quintana had taught the signal men himself. Ivo could still see him doing it, in the back room of the old cooperage, laying out a row of nails on the bench and tapping them with a knuckle. *A clock can lie,* Tomás had said, and his hands had been beautiful even then, quick and certain, a mechanic's hands before they were a hanged man's hands. *A clock can lie better than a priest, because everyone believes it. So we make it tell the truth by telling an impossibility. When you hear the impossible hour, believe it.*

They had believed it. Some of them had run and lived. Most had stayed because running meant leaving others behind, and Tomás had taught them that too — that the union was a chain, and a chain is only strong if every link stays. It was beautiful doctrine. It filled four graves.

Ivo set the glass down. His hand was shaking, and he was angry at it for shaking, sixty-one years old and shaking like a boy at his first rockfall. He went to the window and pressed his forehead to the cold pane and looked up at the tower, where a single lantern burned in the belfry slit — the apprentice's lamp, he supposed, the girl working late, or the girl who had just done something she did not understand.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. That was the splinter going in under his skin. Either the new clockmaker had repaired the striking train so badly that it stumbled into an extra stroke — which Ivo did not believe, because he had watched her work through the open tower door two days ago, watched her handle the escapement like a woman handling something alive, watched her listen to the gears the way a doctor listens to a chest — or she had struck thirteen on purpose.

And if she had struck it on purpose, then either she was playing a joke cruel beyond imagining, or someone had taught her the old signals, or —

Or she was signaling to someone. Or for someone. Or she was one of the old ones' children, come home wearing a stranger's name, and the impossible hour had risen out of its grave and walked back into Vell wearing a borrowed coat.

Ivo had buried too many people to believe in coincidence. Coincidence was a word comfortable men used to avoid arithmetic.

* * *

Morning came gray and hard-edged, snow squeaking underfoot, and Ivo was in the inn's common room before Marta had more than half the fires lit, planted at his usual table by the window with a pot of coffee he was not drinking. He watched the square. He watched the tower door. He watched the apprentice girl come down the hill path with her tool roll over her shoulder and her collar turned up, walking with that deliberate, measured pace of hers, as if each step were a gear tooth engaging the next.

She crossed the square. She came into the inn, because the inn was warm and the inn had food and there was nowhere else in Vell to be warm and fed in winter. She stamped the snow off her boots and unwound her scarf and sat three tables away, and Ivo looked at her sidelong the way he had once looked at timber for signs of rot — not staring, just reading.

Ansel. That was the name on the guild letter; Marta had mentioned it. An odd name, but names were odd everywhere since the strikes, when sons took uncles' names and daughters vanished into cousins' households and whole families rewrote themselves to survive the magistrates' lists. The girl was young, twenty maybe, with ink-stained fingers and burn scars on her forearms that she kept covered when she could. Her eyes did the thing that Ivo had only ever seen in two kinds of people: miners checking a tunnel's shoring, and fugitives checking a room's doors. They went to the exits first. Always the exits first.

"You're staring at her like she owes you money," Marta said, setting down beside him with her own cup and her voice pitched low. Marta Sable missed nothing; it was her profession, formerly smuggling and currently innkeeping, which were the same profession with different cargo.

"Did you hear the clock last night?" Ivo asked.

"I heard it." Marta drank. "So did half the town. Brannock's already told the council it was a fault in the test mechanism, nothing to concern anyone."

"And do you believe him?"

Marta's eyes went to the girl, then back. "I believe Brannock says what Corven pays him to say. That's not belief, that's bookkeeping." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Thirteen strokes, Ivo."

"I counted."

"I know you counted. You've been counting things since '68." She said the year softly, the way you'd handle a blade by the flat. "Here's what I want to know. Who counts along?"

Ivo followed her glance. Across the room, the apprentice girl sat with her bread and her cheese, eating steadily, eyes down — but her lips moved, faintly, silently. Counting. Not the strokes; those were hours old. Counting something else. Steps, perhaps. Or names.

"She's left-handed," Ivo said quietly. "But she eats right-handed. Trains herself out of it. You see that in people hiding something."

"I see it in people who've been beaten for being what they are," Marta said. "Which is most people in this valley, one way or another." She rose, collected the girl's plate with a smile and a word about more coffee, and Ivo watched the exchange — watched the girl flinch almost invisibly at being addressed, watch her answer in a voice pitched carefully neutral, neither high nor low, a voice worn smooth like a river stone, sanded down until it gave nothing away.

When Marta came back, she sat down and said one sentence: "Her hands are Tomás Quintana's hands."

Ivo felt the words go through him like cold water through a seam in the rock.

"You can't know that."

"I watched her re-hang the inn's regulator door last week. The hinge trick — the one where you cut the pin rather than unscrew it, because screws strip and pins don't. Tomás used to do it in the cooperage. I haven't seen it since." Marta's face was perfectly calm, which for Marta meant the opposite. "I'm not saying. I'm saying I'm watching."

* * *

He waited until afternoon, until the girl climbed back toward the tower, and then he did the thing his grief had been arguing with his caution about all day. He followed her. Not close — he was too old and too wise for close — but along the parallel lane, keeping the stone walls between them, letting his cough cover his footfalls on the frozen ruts.

At the tower door she stopped. She had known, then, or suspected; she turned, and for a moment they looked at each other across twenty feet of trampled snow, and Ivo saw her decide something. He saw it happen — saw the calculation move behind her eyes like a shuttle through a loom: *who is this, what does he want, how many steps to the door, can I outwalk him.* Then she said, in that smoothed-over voice:

"Master Havel. Can I help you?"

"You struck thirteen last night," Ivo said.

The pause was very small and very complete. "A fault in the striking train," she said. "The gathering pallet slipped. It won't recur."

"The gathering pallet slipped," Ivo repeated. "Twelve times it held, and on the thirteenth release it slipped, precisely once, precisely at midnight, and will never slip again."

"Clocks are temperamental."

"Aye. And ghosts are restless." He came closer, boots crunching, and saw her weight shift — not away, exactly, but coiled, ready. "Girl. I stood in this square nineteen years ago and heard that same bell strike thirteen while soldiers rode through the outlying villages with lists in their pockets. Thirteen was a warning. Thirteen meant *move tonight*. Four good men and women answered that bell and are dead under the cairns above the tree line, and one of them built half the gearing you've got your hands in right now." He stopped, breathing hard, the cold knifing his lungs. "So you'll forgive an old man for wanting to know whether last night was a fault, or a message, or a mockery."

Something moved in her face then — something quickly suppressed, the way a needle jumps in a compass when iron passes near, and is still. When she spoke, her voice was level as a spirit gauge.

"If it were a message," she said, "who would it be for? Everyone who understood it is dead or scattered."

"Not everyone." Ivo held her gaze. "I'm standing here."

"Yes," she said. "You are." And there — only there, only for a heartbeat — the smoothness cracked, and underneath it he heard something young and exhausted and terribly alone. "It was a fault, Master Havel. I'll correct it today. Good day."

She went inside. The tower door closed. Ivo stood in the snow a long while, looking up at the black tower, at the belfry slit where the lantern would burn again tonight.

She was lying. He was nearly sure she was lying, and nearly sure she was lying *well*, which was worse, because liars who lied well had usually been given reason. And beneath the lie, in the grain of her voice the way the grain showed in the bell's bronze, he had heard something else — heard, of all things, an echo. The cadence of her sentences, the clipped precision, the habit of answering a question with a question to buy time. He had heard that cadence across a cooperage bench, laid out in nails, tapped out with a knuckle.

*A clock can lie better than a priest, because everyone believes it.*

Ivo turned and started down the hill, his bad knee complaining, his mind moving fast and cold as meltwater. If the girl was who the shape of her shadow said she might be, then she was in danger deeper than she knew, because Magistrate Corven kept lists, and Corven's memory was longer than the mountain's shadow. And if she was not who the shadow said — if she was merely a talented stranger whose clock had stumbled — then he had insulted an innocent and frightened her for nothing, and he would owe her an apology and a bottle of the good plum brandy.

Either way, he thought, either way, he would be at the Lantern Inn tonight, and tomorrow, and the night after, listening. Because Vell had gone nineteen years without hearing the impossible hour, and now it had heard it twice in one lifetime, and a town that hears the impossible hour twice is a town being asked a question.

That evening, climbing the inn's stairs past the girl's closed door, Ivo paused. From inside came a faint, rhythmic sound — metal on wood, soft and patient, tap... tap-tap... tap. Not repair work. Practice. Someone drilling their fingers in an old pattern, the way you drill a prayer you swore you'd forgotten.

He stood in the dark hallway with his heart going like a trip-hammer, and he recognized the rhythm, and the floor seemed to tilt under him.

It was the meet-signal. The cooperage knock. Tomás Quintana's own hand, alive in the dark on the other side of the door, knocking on a table in a mountain town that had hanged him for it.

Ivo put his hand against the wall to steady himself, and did not knock back. Not yet. Not yet. But he stood there a long time, an old man in a cold corridor, listening to the dead speak through living hands — and below him, faint through the floorboards, he heard Marta Sable stop mid-step in the common room, and go quiet, and begin, very softly, to count.
