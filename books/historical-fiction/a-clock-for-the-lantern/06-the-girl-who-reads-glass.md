## 6. The Girl Who Reads Glass

Odile Verany had a theory about windows.

Her father believed glass was an instrument — that its whole purpose was to be forgotten, to stand aside and let light through as if nothing stood between the eye and the stars. Odile knew better. Glass was not absence. Glass was presence: it bent what passed through it, delayed it, made it confess things it would never say in open air. Every pane in the observatory was slightly green, slightly wavy, slightly dishonest, and she loved every one of them for it. Through glass, the world came to her already translated.

She had learned to read lips at six years old, when the scarlet fever took her hearing in a single feverish week and gave nothing back but a strange new patience. Her father, gentle Professor Verany, had wept and blamed himself and bought books on finger alphabets that gathered dust on the shelf beside his star charts, because Odile refused them all. Hands talking were private; hands talking drew the eye; hands talking said *here is a girl who must be reached across a gap*. But lips through glass — lips she could read from thirty feet away, from across the square, from the far side of a telescope's long room — lips made the gap irrelevant. She did not need people to come to her. She needed only a clear line of sight and good light, and she had both, always, living in an observatory on the highest ground in Vell.

So it was that Odile Verany knew more about the town of Vell than anyone suspected, and certainly more than anyone wished. She read the tollhouse keeper arguing with his wife over breakfast through the eastern window. She read the garrison boys boasting and lying by turns on the parade ground below. She read Magistrate Corven delivering Sunday pronouncements with the precise, rehearsed mouth of a man who never once said anything he hadn't said before. The town performed for her daily, unguarded, believing itself unheard, and Odile sat in the telescope room with her mending or her star calculations and took it all in like weather.

Which is why she noticed the clockmaker's apprentice before anyone else did.

* * *

The apprentice had been at the observatory nine days when Odile first saw her properly. Nine days of hearing — no, *reading* — the wardens mutter about the small, quick figure in the tower: *the master's new boy, quiet one, keeps to himself, clever hands though*. Nine days of the name Ansel Quintana passing from mouth to mouth without ever once being shaped carefully enough to betray anything. Odile had read enough careless gossip to know that names, like glass, distorted what they carried.

On the ninth day, snow-heavy and blue-bright, Odile was in the telescope room polishing the eyepiece fittings when the apprentice crossed the square below.

Odile saw her stop — that particular stop, the involuntary halt of a person whose body has been caught by something before the mind agrees to it. She was looking up at the clock tower, and even from the window Odile could see the change in her face: the wariness dropping away for one unguarded second, replaced by something raw and hungry. It was the look Odile imagined her own face wore, before she'd learned to guard it, whenever she watched other people laughing together in a warm room.

The apprentice stood in the cold until the cold won. Then she went on toward the tower door, shoulders squared, face shut again like a case closed over its works.

But Odile had seen. And Odile, who spent her life reading what people did not say, understood immediately and completely that the apprentice in Vell was carrying a secret the way other people carried knives — close to the body, checked hourly, drawn only in necessity.

It was none of her business. She told herself so twice, and then went down and put the kettle on anyway.

* * *

Their first conversation happened, appropriately, through two panes of glass and a great tube of brass.

Odile had taken to working in the telescope room in the late afternoons because the light came in low and gold off the western snowfields, and because from there she could see both the tower door and the square. On the twelfth day, she looked up from her ledger of variable-star estimates and saw the apprentice standing at the tower's high gallery window — the narrow one that faced the observatory dome — apparently checking the exterior clock hand against some interior standard.

Except the apprentice wasn't checking the hands. The apprentice was looking at *her*.

Odile did what she always did when caught watching: she didn't pretend otherwise. Pretense was for people who had something to hide about themselves. Instead she lifted her father's slate, which lived permanently on the telescope room sill, and wrote in chalk letters large enough to cross forty feet of winter air:

**YOU ARE LOOKING AT THE WRONG CLOCK.**

She saw the apprentice start. Saw her glance left, right — the instinct of the hunted, confirming exits. Then, slowly, she came closer to her own window and pressed one gloved hand flat against the glass, a gesture that meant either *who are you* or *what do you want*, and Odile couldn't tell which, and found she didn't care.

Odile wrote again: **THE DOME. LOOK AT THE DOME.**

The apprentice's eyes moved obediently to the great copper dome above the observatory, streaked white with snow, its shutter slit dark against the afternoon sky. Then back to Odile. A question in the set of her brows.

Odile held up one finger: *wait.* She went to the wall where her father kept the dome's training crank — the mechanism that rotated the shutter to follow the sky — and gave it three quarters of a turn. Above them both, eleven tons of copper and timber swung slowly around with a sound like a held note, shedding snow in soft slabs. When it stopped, the shutter faced the tower directly, and through the slit, catching the low sun, hung the great objective lens: a disc of flawless glass two feet across, which now, for perhaps the only time in its working life, was pointed not at the heavens but at a girl in a clock tower.

And the girl in the clock tower, forty feet away, appeared — magnified, inverted, luminous in the golden light — inside the lens like a spirit summoned into a lamp. Odile saw her own reflection superimposed over the apprentice's astonished face, ghost upon ghost, and she laughed out loud, the bright unmodulated sound that made strangers flinch and her father smile.

Through the lens, she watched the apprentice laugh too — a short, startled bark of a laugh, quickly suppressed, but real. Then the apprentice raised one hand and traced a slow circle in the air: *do that again.*

Odile cranked the dome back. Forward. Back. The apprentice's face swam in the lens each time, wide-eyed, and Odile realized she herself must look enormous and strange in reverse — a giantess peering down out of a copper skull — and that the apprentice was getting, right now, the closest view anyone in Vell had ever taken of her, and that neither of them minded at all.

When she finally let the dome rest, the apprentice wrote on frost of her own window, in the confident blocky strokes of someone who wrote constantly:

**THAT IS THE FINEST MECHANISM I HAVE SEEN IN THIS TOWN AND I AM PAID TO SAY SO ABOUT THE OTHER ONE.**

Odile chalked back: **MY FATHER SAYS THE CLOCK HAS A SOUL. HE IS ROMANTIC. I SAY IT HAS TEETH. HAVE YOU BEEN BITTEN YET?**

A pause. The apprentice's mouth moved — Odile read it easily through the lens still hanging between them, that beautiful lying disc of glass: *"Not yet."* But her eyes said something longer, and Odile, who did not need sound to hear, heard it perfectly.

* * *

After that, they met every afternoon at the windows, and their friendship assembled itself the way Odile imagined Ansel assembled clocks: part by part, each piece tested before the next was seated, nothing forced.

They invented their method together, without ever discussing it aloud — which was fortunate, since discussing it aloud would have required exactly the thing Odile never had. The slate worked for long messages, but chalk was slow and the cold ate their fingers. So they fell instead into the older rhythm: Odile reading lips, Ansel learning to shape her words deliberately, exaggerating at first like a traveler mimicking a foreign tongue, then smoothing into naturalness. Ansel was a fast study. Of course she was. Her whole life, Odile began to understand, was the study of surfaces — how to make a face say nothing, how to walk like a boy, how to hold her hands so no one saw them tremble or fly. It exhausted Odile to watch it, the constant performance. But watching Ansel perform *for her* — slowly, patiently, turning her face to the light so the words would land — felt like being handed a gift wrapped in the giver's own skin.

Ansel taught her mechanisms in return. She would hold up small parts to her window — a pallet stone, a section of mainspring, once a complete anchor escapement cradled in her palm like a dead moth — and demonstrate with fingers alone how it breathed: catch, release, catch, release. Odile, who had grown up among instruments but never among their innards, found it the most beautiful language she had ever been taught. Here was a grammar with no sound in it at all. Gears did not care who heard them. An escapement's entire purpose was to divide time into pieces small enough for a person to bear, and it did this silently, endlessly, asking nothing of ears.

*Time is fair,* Ansel signed-said one evening, holding up the anchor as the light died. *It falls on everyone equally. Most things in this town are not fair. That is why I love the work.*

Odile wrote back on the slate: **YOU TALK LIKE SOMEONE WHO HAS MET UNFAIRNESS PERSONALLY.**

And she watched the shutters come down behind Ansel's eyes, quick and total as the dome closing before rain, and she did not press. Pressing was for people who hadn't learned to wait. Odile could outwait snowmelt.

* * *

It was Odile's father who finally brought them under one roof.

Professor Verany was a tall, abstracted man whose beard collected chalk dust and biscuit crumbs in roughly equal proportion, and who had communicated with his daughter for eighteen years through a private shorthand of eyebrows, gestures, and written notes with such fluency that visitors often forgot she was deaf until she laughed. He had noticed the window conversations within days — of course he had; his dome pointed at the tower — and had said nothing, because he was a wise man, but on the fourteenth day of the thawless month he appeared in the telescope room holding two cups of tea and a decision.

"Invite her up," he said, facing Odile directly, his lip movements crisp as a printed page. "The tower is cold. Your new friend is pretending it isn't. Nobody does honest work while pretending about cold."

"She isn't my friend," Odile said. "She's a mystery I am taking apart carefully to see what makes her tick."

Her father's eyebrows performed a lengthy commentary. "In my experience," he said, "those are the same thing. Invite her up. If she refuses, she's hiding something worth hiding, and you'll want her closer, not farther."

Odile considered pointing out the flaws in this logic and decided there weren't any.

* * *

Ansel almost didn't come.

Odile watched the whole drama unfold from the observatory door, where she stood in the biting wind holding a lantern, having crossed the square herself rather than issue the invitation through glass. She watched Ansel appear at the tower door, see her, and go absolutely still — a stillness Odile recognized from wild creatures on the ridge, the full-body listening of prey. She watched her look past Odile at the observatory, at the lit windows, at the possible watchers. She watched her weigh it, all of it, in the space of three seconds.

Then Odile did the thing that, later, she would understand was the hinge of everything. She didn't wave. She didn't beckon. She simply turned her profile to the lantern light, so Ansel could see her mouth, and said clearly:

"It's warm inside. And nobody here reads your face but me."

She watched the words arrive. Watched them do their work. Watched Ansel Quintana take one breath, nod once, and step out of the tower's shadow into the open square — exposed, deliberate, trusting — for the first time since she'd come to Vell.

* * *

Inside, the tea was hot and the telescope room glowed, and for a while the three of them existed in the comfortable silence that Odile preferred to any conversation: her father sketching orbital corrections, Odile logging estimates, Ansel seated stiffly on the very edge of a chair as though it might be repossessed. But warmth works on people the way water works on rusted bolts, and gradually the stiffness ran out of Ansel's shoulders.

It was the orrery that undid her.

It stood in the corner — her father's pride, a brass model of the solar system the size of a dinner table, planets on wire arms around a gilt sun, built by Verany's own hands during a long convalescence and improved every winter since. Odile saw Ansel notice it the moment she stopped guarding herself: saw her gaze snag and stay, saw her pupils widen the way they widened at fine gearwork. When Professor Verany followed her eyes and said, "Would you like to wind it?" Odile read the tremor that went through the answer before Ansel gave it:

"If I may. Sir."

"You may not," said Verany gravely, and paused, and his beard twitched. "You may *help*. Winding it is a two-person job, or will be once I explain my latest improvement, which requires a third hand and infinite patience, and my daughter has the patience but refuses the third hand on principle."

"I refuse the *crank*," Odile corrected. "You've rebuilt the governor twice. It bites."

And then — Odile watched it happen — Ansel laughed. Not the suppressed bark from the tower window but a real one, brief and rusty, the laugh of a machine run for the first time in years. She knelt at the orrery's base with Verany while Odile held the lantern, and the three of them disappeared into the works together: the elder astronomer's broad hands and the clockmaker's quick ones chasing the same problem through the gear train, Odile following by sight alone, reading their mouths in the round mirror her father had long ago installed beneath the orrery precisely so she could watch faces while hands worked.

*"There — the third wheel's undercut—"*

*"I see it. If we shim here—"*

*"Brass shim. Never steel against brass, it—"*

*"—cuts a groove, yes, my grandfather's watch died of exactly that—"*

Odile held the light steady and thought: *there she is.* Under the boy's coat and the flat voice and the checked exits, there was a woman kneeling in lamplight with grease to her wrists, entirely present, entirely absorbed, forgetting to hide because the mechanism had taken all her attention hostage. This, Odile understood, was the truest face Ansel owned — not the wary one, not the performed one, but this one, available only when her hands were full of gears.

She resolved, privately and permanently, to keep this face supplied with gears.

* * *

Later, when the orrery ran true and Verany had retired to his charts, Odile poured the last of the tea and wrote on the slate, because some questions were too important to risk the ambiguity of lips:

**WHY DID YOU COME UP TONIGHT? YOU WATCHED THIS DOOR FOR TWO WEEKS WITHOUT CROSSING THE SQUARE.**

Ansel took the slate. Held it. Wrote nothing for a long time. Outside, the wind worried the shutter hinges, and the fire popped, and Odile waited with the patience she was famous for.

Finally Ansel wrote, in small careful letters:

**BECAUSE YOU SAW ME THROUGH GLASS AND DID NOT FLINCH OR STARE OR ASK WHAT I WAS. YOU JUST SHOWED ME THE MOON IN A LENS. NOBODY HAS EVER LOOKED AT ME LIKE I WAS INTERESTING INSTEAD OF SUSPICIOUS.**

She stopped. Then added, scratching fast as though the honesty might escape if she slowed:

**I SHOULD NOT BE TELLING YOU ANYTHING TRUE. IT IS DANGEROUS FOR BOTH OF US.**

Odile took the slate back. Underneath, she wrote:

**EVERYONE IN THIS TOWN HIDES SOMETHING. I READ LIPS ALL DAY. I KNOW. YOUR SECRET IS SAFE WITH THE DEAF GIRL WHO READS GLASS — WHO WOULD I EVEN TELL?**

And beneath that, because it was true and because true things deserve writing:

**BESIDES. FRIENDS DON'T SELL EACH OTHER. EVERYONE ELSE HERE MIGHT. I WON'T. THAT'S THE WHOLE CONTRACT. TAKE IT OR LEAVE IT.**

She held the slate out. Ansel read it twice. Something moved across her face like weather crossing a valley — and then she did the most astonishing thing Odile had ever witnessed her do. She pulled off one glove, extended her bare hand across the space between them, and shook Odile's hand formally, gravely, like a merchant sealing a deal, her grip calloused and warm and utterly steady.

Contract accepted. Signed in skin.

* * *

Odile walked her to the observatory door with the lantern, and they stood a moment in the blue screaming cold of the square, breath rising between them in twin columns.

That was when Odile saw the man.

He had just emerged from the shadow of the garrison wall across the way — heavyset, fur-collared, unmistakable even at distance and dark: Head Warden Brannock, who answered to Corven and no one else. He was not moving. He was standing in the lee of the wall, half-hidden, and he was looking directly at them — at Ansel — with the fixed, patient attention of a man confirming something he had already begun to suspect.

Beside her, Odile felt Ansel register him. Felt her whole body change, the warmth of the last hour folding away like a dropped hem. Ansel stepped forward, hunched her shoulders into a boy's slouch, spat theatrically into the snow, and called out across the square in a voice pitched to carry and crack: "Late night for walking, sir! Mind the ice!" — the perfect insolence of a young workman with nothing to hide, waving cheerfully at the warden as though warden-watching were a game apprentices played.

Brannock looked at her a moment longer. Then he touched his cap brim, turned, and walked away into the dark toward the magistrate's house.

Odile waited until the square was empty. Then she turned her lantern so its light fell on her own mouth and said quietly, deliberately, each word clean:

"He knows something. Or suspects. Which?"

Ansel stared at her — at this deaf girl who had just read the entire encounter like a page — and Odile watched her decide something, watch the decision cost her, watch her make it anyway.

"Come to the tower tomorrow," Ansel said. "Bring the lantern. There's something in the clock I think you should see before anyone else does. And Odile—" she hesitated at the edge of the lamplight, half in shadow already, "—if anything happens to me, remember what's in the clock. Promise me that much."

Before Odile could answer — before she could write it, shout it, carve it in frost — Ansel was gone, quick footsteps fading toward the tower, leaving Odile alone in the square with the lantern, the cold, and the growing certainty that whatever slept inside her friend's clock, the town of Vell was about to wake it.
