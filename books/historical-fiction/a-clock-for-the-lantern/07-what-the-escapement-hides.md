## 7. What the Escapement Hides

The trouble with the great clock of Vell, Ansel had decided by her fourth day inside its case, was not that it was broken. It was that it had been *touched*.

She knelt on the scaffold platform with a lamp hooked to the frame beside her, its flame bent sideways by the draft that breathed down the tower shaft. Around her the movement rose in tiers, brass and steel and iron, wheels within wheels, the whole architecture of time assembled by some long-dead master whose hand she could feel in every file mark and every rivet set just so. She had come to know him these past days — this builder whose name was stamped into the great wheel's rim in letters worn nearly smooth: *J. Fenn, Vell*. He had been proud. He had been careful. He had cut every tooth true.

And then someone else had come after him, and their hands were wrong.

Ansel lifted her loupe from her eye and pressed the heel of her palm against them until the ghost-images faded. Her fingers ached. Three days of crawling through the striking train, three nights of lying awake at the Lantern Inn listening to the wind worry the shutters while her mind turned the same question over like a coin too worn to read: who sabotages a clock so gently?

Because that was what troubled her most. The damage was almost loving. A pivot filed down by a hair's breadth here, so that the fourth wheel would begin to drag after a season of running — not before, not suddenly, but slowly, so gradually that any keeper would blame wear. A pallet stone loosened and re-seated with wax that would soften in summer heat. A counterweight link swapped for one of near-identical size, near enough that only someone who weighed links in her palm the way other people weighed coins would ever feel the difference. Whoever had done it had wanted the clock to fail — but wanted it to fail *believably*, months after they were gone, when no finger could point back at them.

That took patience. That took knowledge. That took access.

She had asked Brannock about it, two evenings ago, keeping her voice flat under its boyish pitch. *Who services this clock? Who has keys?* And Brannock had looked at her with those heavy-lidded eyes and said the tower was sealed between keepers, that the last man had died two winters past, that she should fix what was broke and leave the rest alone. Then he had stood there a moment too long, watching her pack her tools, and said: *You've clever hands, boy. Clever hands find more than they're looking for, sometimes. Best to know when to stop winding.*

She hadn't stopped winding. She couldn't. The clock was the finest mechanism she had ever been permitted to touch, and beneath her fear and her caution ran something hotter — the apprentice's fury at finding good work abused, honest gears made liars. Every hour she spent inside the case she felt more certain that the sabotage was only the surface of something. A clock this fine did not fail on its own. And men did not quietly ruin it unless there was something here worth ruining it for.

Tonight she meant to find out what.

* * *

She had waited until the inn went quiet, then let herself into the tower through the wicket door whose lock she had already learned to open with a bent wire — not because she intended theft, but because a person who moved through doors honestly was a person who left records of her coming. Marta Sable had watched her cross the square from the inn's window; Ansel felt the weight of that gaze between her shoulder blades all the way to the tower steps. The innkeeper watched everyone. Ansel told herself it meant nothing. She told herself a great many things lately that she did not quite believe.

Now, lamplight pooled around her, she sat back on her heels inside the great case and studied the space below the escapement chamber where the mystery lived.

It was a feeling more than a fact, still. The escapement itself sat above her like a caged animal — the anchor and escape wheel, the beating heart of the thing, ticking its slow division of the night. But the chamber beneath it bothered her. The floor of it was paneled in old oak, and the paneling was wrong. Not badly wrong. *Correctly* wrong. The grain matched across the boards the way grain matches when boards are cut from the same stock at the same time — but the nail pattern didn't. Fenn had used nails the way he'd used everything: sparingly, precisely, each one placed with intent. These were placed with a different intent. Too regular. Too even. Like a man nailing down a lid.

Ansel ran her fingertips along the seam of the nearest board. Oak, aged dark, cold as river stone. She thought of her father's hands teaching hers to read wood the way other children were taught to read letters. *Listen to what the material wants to tell you, little star. Wood remembers being a tree. Iron remembers the forge. Nothing forgets what was done to it.*

Her chest tightened at the memory, the way it always did now, sharper since Vell, since this town where her father's name had once been spoken. She pushed the feeling down. Grief was a luxury. Work was a shelter.

She fetched her smallest tools: the thin knife, the wax-spoon, the little pry of hardened brass that would not mar what it lifted. She worked the blade into the seam and felt resistance, then give — and then a small, definite click, deep in the wood, like a knuckle cracking. A spring latch. Under the nails, hidden in the grain, someone had built a lock into the floor itself.

Her mouth had gone dry. She pried the first board up carefully, easing it free without splitting it — good oak deserved that much — and set it aside. Beneath lay another board, thinner, un-nailed, resting on ledges cut into the joists. She lifted that one too, and the lamplight fell into a shallow cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the cavity lay a bundle wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with cord, and the cord was the kind clockmakers used for weighting — braided hemp, waxed against damp.

For a long moment Ansel did not move. The tower ticked above her, patient as a held breath. Somewhere far below, the town of Vell slept under its snow, and up here in the dark the dead builder's careful hands and some later stranger's careful hands had together made a box in which something had been hidden so well that even sabotage had been worth committing to protect it.

She untied the cord with fingers that refused to be steady.

Inside: three books. Not ledgers of the observatory — she knew those, had inventoried them herself. These were smaller, private-sized, bound in scuffed calf. The topmost bore a date stamped in gold on its spine, eleven years gone. Eleven years. Her body knew before her mind did; her skin prickled cold from wrist to shoulder, some animal wisdom rising ahead of thought.

She opened the book.

The hand was clerkly, cramped, disciplined. Columns of dates. Columns of names. Columns of sums. And at the head of each page, written in a different, heavier hand — a hand she did not know but would come, in the following minutes, to hate with her whole soul — the same notation:

*Paid on order of Magistrate Corven, per instruction.*

She read the first entry. Then the second. Then she stopped reading entries and started reading *names*, because the names were doing something terrible to the inside of her skull.

Hollis Renn. Dagny Skeet. Old Piotr Marun, who had limped. Widow Cattel's son, Joss, who would have been barely nineteen. She knew none of them. She knew all of them. They were witness names, deposition names — she had memorized them at fourteen, kneeling in a courthouse gallery with her brother's cap pulled low over her ears, watching men climb into the witness box one by one and swear away her father's life. She had memorized their faces too. She had spent years making herself forget both, and here they were again, ranked in columns, and beside each name a sum, and beside each sum a date, and the dates ran backward and forward from a single black center like spokes around a hub:

The trial of Tomás Quintana.

The words swam. She gripped the edge of the case to steady herself, and the brass was cold under her palms and the clock ticked on above her, indifferent, beautiful, and she thought distantly, absurdly: *I repaired your escapement. I trued your pallets. Do you know what you have been standing guard over all these years?*

There it was, then. Not suspicion anymore. Not craft-curiosity. Fact, in clerk's ink on rag paper, wrapped in oilcloth in a clock she had crossed half the country to mend.

Magistrate Corven had paid witnesses to swear against Tomás Quintana. Had paid them in installments — she could see it now, the structure of it, the way the sums thinned after the hanging and then resumed, smaller, regular, seasonal, like a pension. Hush money. Retainer money. Blood money paid out over years like interest on a principal of grief. Some entries carried annotations: *reminded re: silence*, or *instructed to avoid garrison talk*, or simply, chillingly, *settled*. One entry, dated eight months after the execution, recorded a payment to Hollis Renn annotated *disturbed; increase*. And the next year, an entry recording Renn's death by misadventure at the mine, annotated in that heavy hand: *convenient.*

Ansel closed the book because her hands were shaking too hard to hold it open, and sat very still, and let the vertigo take her.

This was what falling felt like, she realized. Not the drop itself but the moment you understood the floor had never been there. She had built her whole life on a story: that her father had died a fool's death at the hands of fools, that the law was a machine that ran whether it ran justly or not, and that the only sane response was to disappear into it — take a brother's name, take a trade, become a boy with clever hands whom nobody looked at twice. The story had kept her alive. She had fed it everything: her hair, her voice, her grief, her name. She had believed — she had *needed* to believe — that what killed Tomás Quintana was chaos, mob-madness, the times themselves, something vast and faceless that no one person could have stopped.

But here was a face. Here was a hand writing *per instruction* in a ledger, season after season, year after year, patient as a filing pivot, careful as a loosened pallet stone. Here was sabotage of a life done so gently, so believably, that everyone would blame wear.

*Oh,* she thought, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in. *Oh, it's the same hand.*

Not literally. But the same mind. The same signature. Someone who ruins things gradually so the failure looks natural. Someone who plans the alibi before the act. She looked at the clock around her — at the dragging wheel and the softened wax and the swapped link — and understood, all at once and completely, why the clock of Vell had been made to fail, and why its failing had been scheduled months out, and why whoever ordered it had needed the tower empty and its keeper replaced by an outsider with no ties and no questions.

Something in this clock was dangerous to Magistrate Corven. Something he had hidden here himself, perhaps — or something hidden *against* him, by whoever had stashed these books beneath the escapement. She couldn't yet tell which. The compartment might be a cache or a coffin; the books might be Corven's own insurance or someone's quiet revenge, waiting in the dark like a loaded trap. Either way, the sabotage began to make horrible sense: not to hide the compartment — the floor was innocent-looking enough — but to guarantee that when the clock came apart for repair, it would come apart in front of witnesses, on schedule, so that whatever was found here would be found by controlled hands, or destroyed in the confusion of a "failing" movement stripped to its bones.

Or — the thought arrived cold and complete — so that the tower would be sealed afterward "for safety," and the evidence entombed until spring, when the archives were scheduled to burn.

She had heard that rumor her first week in town, gossiping with the stable boys: the magistrate means to clear the old observatory records at thaw, build something modern. Everyone said it casually, the way people say things that are none of their business.

Ansel wrapped her arms around her knees and shook, silently, in the belly of the great clock, while above her the anchor swung and swung, letting out time in equal measures, fair as scripture, blind as justice.

* * *

It took her a long while to move again. When she finally did, it was because the cold had stiffened her joints and the candle had burned down past the point of prudence, and because underneath the shaking, steadily and without hurry, something in her had changed state — the way metal changes under the hammer, not softer but harder, realigned along new lines.

She examined the books properly then, one at a time, with the loupe, with the methodical calm her father had taught her and her brother's name had protected. The first book covered the trial year and its aftermath. The second ran six further years, payments thinning as witnesses died off or drifted — several entries marked simply *deceased*, in a clerk's neat abbreviation, as though the men had been gear-teeth wearing out of service. The third book was the worst. The third book was current.

In the third book, the payments continued to the present year. Two names remained. Joss Cattel, grown now into a hollow-faced man who drank at the Lantern Inn every market night — Ansel had seen him there, had noted him idly the way she noted all drunks, as background noise. And Dagny Skeet, who had left the district entirely, according to a marginal note, and received her money by post.

Current payments meant current authorizations. Current authorizations meant the hand that wrote *per instruction* was still attached to a living man, and that man was Magistrate Corven, and he sat tonight in his warm house across the square, sleeping the sleep of a man whose secrets were nailed under an observatory floor.

Unless he wasn't sleeping. Unless he knew.

The thought turned her blood to snowmelt. She forced herself through it with procedure, with craft, the litany of small tasks that had saved her composure a hundred times at the workbench: inventory, secure, conceal, withdraw. She copied nothing — copying would come later, if she lived that long, and by light better than this guttering lamp. She noted the compartment's exact construction, the latch mechanism, a sprung brass tongue released by pressure on the third board at its northern end — a detail that might matter terribly someday. She re-wrapped the books in their oilcloth and waxed canvas exactly as found, cord tied in the same pattern — she had memorized the knot; her father had taught her knots too, everything he knew, all of it apparently in preparation for this night.

She replaced the false board, the true board, pressed the nails home with a drift punch so the heads sat flush as they had sat for years. She swept the platform with a camel-hair brush. She banked the lamp and stood a long moment in the dark, listening to the clock tick, and laid her palm flat against the great case as though against the flank of a horse.

"You kept them safe," she whispered. "Whatever you were, whoever hid them here — you kept them safe."

The clock ticked on. Above the town the stars turned, and down in the square the Lantern Inn showed a single lit window, and behind that window, though Ansel could not have known it, Marta Sable sat awake with her own ledger of small debts open before her, adding a line.

Ansel slipped out the wicket door into the blue-black cold and crossed the square with her collar up and her heart going like a detached flywheel, wild and unregulated. Her cover story lay behind her in the tower, collapsed inward like a stripped movement: the boy apprentice, the neutral stranger, the girl who fixed clocks and asked nothing and was nobody. She had come to Vell to hide. She understood now that she had been summoned — summoned by a letter she had never fully questioned, signed by a hand she had never examined — and that the summons had known things about her that only one kind of person could have known.

Tomorrow she would examine the letter. Tomorrow she would start asking who had sent for a clockmaker's apprentice with just her particular training, at just this moment, when a clock needed mending that had been mended-to-fail by design.

But tonight, walking the frozen ruts toward firelight and the smell of Marta's stew, one thought rode ahead of all the others, bright and terrible as a struck bell:

Her father had been murdered twice. Once at the rope's end, and once in ink. And the second murder was still going on — every seasonal payment, every settled entry, every year the lie drew breath.

She reached the inn door and paused with her hand on the latch, looking back once at the tower, black against the stars, the dial's faint circle just visible like a ghost-face. Somewhere inside it, under the beating heart of the clock, her family's whole ruined history lay wrapped in oilcloth, three books thick.

She had come to Vell to repair a clock.

She would not leave until it told the truth.
