## 9. Three Checkpoints

Ivo Garn had carried rock out of the mountain for thirty-one years, and he had learned in that time that every load had a weight at which the rope began to sing. Not break — sing. A high thin note in the hemp, felt more than heard, that said: you are close now, closer than is wise, one more stone and I will tell everyone what you have done. The trick was to listen for it before you loaded the last stone, not after.

He thought of that singing rope now as he laid three brass buttons on the table of Marta Sable's back room and pushed them across the scratched wood, one by one, like a man laying out stones on a grave.

"Tollhouse," he said, touching the first. "Garrison gate." He touched the second. "Ferry landing." The third button sat alone in a circle of lamplight, and Ivo found he did not want to touch it again. "That's the whole road down off this mountain. That's everything between us and the world."

Across from him, the apprentice — Quintana's girl, though she didn't know yet that he knew, or didn't know that he'd worked it out months ago from the way her hands moved when they cleaned a pivot, left-turning, always left-turning, the old Tomás habit — leaned forward into the light. She had taken off her coat despite the cold in the room; the stove was small and the wood was rationed, and she sat with her sleeves rolled like a woman who had decided long ago that comfort was a thing other people bought.

"Say the distances," she said.

"Eleven miles to the tollhouse. Another nine to the garrison gate at the switchback. Then six down to the river, if the path hasn't iced through." Ivo folded his hands. They were ruined hands, knuckles like walnut burls, and he folded them the way he did when he wanted to look calm and wasn't. "And then whatever the river's doing."

"And watchers?"

"That's the part you won't like."

She almost smiled. It was a thin, sharp flicker, gone as fast as it came, and Ivo thought: yes, you're his daughter all right. He'd seen that exact smile on Tomás Quintana's face in the winter of the strike, in the union hall above the cooperage, whenever someone told him the cost of a thing and he decided to pay it anyway.

"Tell me anyway," she said.

So he did.

* * *

"The tollhouse sits where the road narrows against the cliff," Ivo said, and turned the first button with one blunt finger, making it walk a slow inch across the table. "One door front, one back, windows barred since before the war. Two men in ordinary times, but Corven doubled it after the records office fire scare last autumn — that's Duvane and the young one, Pell. Duvane's been on the tolls eleven years. He drinks, but steady, like a clock winding down slow. Never misses a shift, never too drunk to count coin. Pell's different. Pell's got the fever for cards, and worse luck than any man I ever met. Owes money in three towns, and one of them's towns where you don't owe money twice."

The girl watched the button walk. "Which shift changes?"

"Dawn and dusk. Dawn change is the dangerous one — Duvane hands over sober and counts everything twice. Dusk, they're both there an hour together, drinking, arguing about cards. If you were going to move anything past that door—"

"I'm not going to move anything past that door," she said quietly.

"No," Ivo agreed. "You're going to make them look somewhere else while it moves. Same thing, harder arithmetic." He touched the second button. "Garrison gate. That's not two men with a ledger and a bottle. That's eight soldiers under a Sergeant Hake, a wall, a portcullis, and a road so narrow at the switchback that a cart can't turn around. Everything going up or down the mountain passes under their eyes, and their eyes are paid for by Corven directly — not the province, mind. Directly. There's a difference, and the difference is that no auditor will ever see those wages."

"Then the gate can't be bribed and can't be dodged," she said. "It has to be *signed through.*"

Ivo looked at her a long moment. Outside, wind came off the mountain and pressed at the shutters, and somewhere down the lane the Lantern's sign creaked on its hook — that low rhythmic groan he'd heard every night for six years, so familiar it was silence.

"You've been thinking about seals," he said.

"I've been thinking about who has one."

And Ivo thought of Elias Corven, pale and careful, walking through the observatory with his father's clipboard and his father's mouth — the same hard line, softened only by the fact that the boy kept biting his own lip instead of talking — and he said nothing, because some stones you don't lay on the table until the person holding them decides to.

"Ferry landing," he said instead, and touched the third button, and hated it. "In summer it's nothing. A flat-bottom boat, a rope, old Sef on the crank, and he'd carry your ledgers across for the price of a pipe of tobacco and thank you for the company. But it's midwinter, girl. The river's already skimming ice at the banks. In a month it'll be locked solid, and then the ferry's done and the crossing is a crossing — which means anyone watching the landing doesn't need a boat to catch you, just legs and a lantern. You'd be walking out on white ground in the dark, carrying paper, visible for a half mile in any moonlight at all."

"Unless it snows."

"Unless it snows. Snow covers the ground and fills the air. A snow heavy enough to hide you is heavy enough to kill you up on that riverbank, with the current running under the ice and the holes where the springs come in. I've pulled two men out of that river in my life." His voice went flat as slate. "Only one of them lived."

The girl was quiet. Her fingers had found each other in her lap and were working at something — Ivo realized after a moment that she was turning an invisible gear, thumb against forefinger, the little unconscious motion of a hand that has spent years inside movements. Left-turning. Always left-turning.

"You're counting something," Ivo said. "I can hear it."

"I'm counting risks against roads." She looked up. "Three checkpoints. Tollhouse — soft, corruptible, predictable hours. Garrison — hard, honest to its paymaster, needs authority to pass. Ferry — not a checkpoint at all really, just weather and distance, which means it's the one nobody controls, including us." She laid her hand flat over the third button. "Every plan dies at the ferry unless we go early, before the ice locks. Which means everything has to be ready weeks sooner than thaw. Which means—"

"Which means the tollhouse first, fast, before the cold does our thinking for us," Ivo finished. "Yes."

"It's bad arithmetic."

"It's the only arithmetic there is." Ivo leaned back, and the chair complained under him the way chairs complain under old miners. "Let me tell you what I told your f—" He stopped. The sentence hung there a moment, swaying on the rope.

She looked at him. She knew. Of course she knew; she'd known since the word *your* came out wrong. But she let him choose, and he loved her a little for that, in the fierce unsentimental way old men love courage they recognize.

"Let me tell you what I told Tomás, the winter of the strike," Ivo said. "I told him: a route is not a plan until you know where you'll leave things behind. Every runner has a weight limit, and the heaviest thing you'll carry is hope. You plan to drop it first, or it drops you last."

"He didn't listen?"

"He listened fine." Ivo looked at the window, at the dark beyond it, where the observatory tower stood against the stars with its blind black face. "He just decided some loads are worth the singing rope."

* * *

They walked it, three nights later, because Ivo would not map a road he hadn't put boots on. Not all of it — eleven miles down the cliff road and back was a young man's errand, and the girl went alone the far stretch, wrapped in a mender's smock with a toolbag full of junk clocks, God protect whoever owned them — but Ivo walked the near ground himself, slow, in the blue hour before dusk, leaning on his stick and looking harmless, which was the one disguise he'd never once had to practice.

The tollhouse first. He stood at the bend above it, hat brim down, pretending to catch his breath, and counted what could be counted. The lamp burned behind the eastern window; the western window was dark, shuttered. One horse in the lean-to, one gone — Pell, then, out on the road collecting, or in Vell losing money. The chimney smoked thin and even. Duvane at home, sober hours yet. The back door's snow was unbroken; nobody used the back door, which meant nobody shovelled the back path, which meant a person leaving by the back path left a print a blind man could follow. Ivo wrote it all in the ledger behind his eyes, the one that never froze and never burned.

At the garrison gate he did not go near at all. He stood a half mile up at the shrine of the road-saints — some old saint of travelers whose face time had worn to a suggestion — and he watched through the falling dusk the way he'd once watched pit heads for signs of trouble: not at the men, but at the rhythm. Lanterns hung at intervals too regular to be casual. A sentry walked the wall top with the unhurried pace of a man who expected nothing and had orders to expect everything. And at the switchback below, a sledge loaded with winter wool waited in the queue, driver stamping, waiting out the inspection — forty minutes, Ivo judged, from arrival to passage. Forty minutes per vehicle. That was useful. That was a number you could hide inside or starve inside, depending.

The ferry landing he saved for last, and he nearly didn't go, because his knee had opinions about the descent and the dark came down early and mean. But he went. He stood on the high bank above the black water and looked down at the landing where the boat sat hauled up on its ways, iced to the skids already, and he saw what he'd feared he'd see: fresh tracks. Boot prints along the bank, a fire-blackened ring of stones, the stub of candles in a tin plate. Somebody was watching the landing, and not casually — they'd built themselves a camp. Corven's men, sitting out the winter within sight of sixty yards of frozen water, being paid, presumably, on the sound principle that a locked door is cheapest guarded from both sides.

Ivo stood in the dark a long time with the wind coming up off the river and taking the heat out of him through every seam of his coat, and he did his sums, and the sums came out ugly.

Because here was the thing he had not yet said aloud at Marta's table, the thing that sat under all the buttons and distances like water under ice: the ledgers were not the only cargo. The cargo was also a girl with a hanged man's face slowly surfacing in her features, in a town where the magistrate had watched that same face at the foot of a scaffold nineteen years ago and might or might not remember it. Every checkpoint they scouted for paper was also a checkpoint for her. Every route out was a route Corven's men might ride *in*. And the arithmetic of risk did not care which loss hurt more. It only weighed.

He turned back up the hill. Behind him the river muttered under its new ice, the sound of something patient.

* * *

Marta was waiting when he came in, and she read him the way she read weather, which is to say completely and without mercy. She set a cup in his hand before he'd got his coat off — not tea, he noted; the good bottle — and poured a second for herself, which she did perhaps four times a year.

"Well?" she said.

"Duvane's soft but steady. Pell's a door with a broken latch, we just need the right wind. The garrison wants signing, no way around it, and the ferry—" He drank. "The ferry's got a camp on it. Two men, maybe three, wintering on the bank."

Marta absorbed that without blinking. Down the hall, faintly, came the sound of the girl and Odile at the parlor table — the particular rhythm of their lessons, Odile tapping out shapes, the girl repeating them, laughter in small bursts like sparks. Ivo had lived alone a long time and had thought he preferred it; lately the sounds of that household had begun to feel like a fire he was warming his hands at without permission.

"So we go early," Marta said. "Before the ice locks proper. First heavy snow, when the road men pull in and the guards get lazy about faces."

"First heavy snow." Ivo turned the cup. "There's another number I haven't given you."

"There usually is."

"The tollhouse keeps a book. Every soul that passes gets written down — name, business, direction. Eleven years of Duvane's handwriting in Corven's keeping." He looked at her over the rim. "Our clock-mender's already passed that tollhouse twice on her scouting. Twice written down. And Corven's clerk audits everything his father's finger touches, that's the boy's whole reputation. Sooner or later, friend Elias reads a name in that book and wonders why a clock-mender from a town that doesn't exist came down the mountain three times in one winter."

Marta was quiet. Then she smiled, slow and crooked, the smile of a woman doing sums of her own.

"Then we'd best learn something about the magistrate's son before he learns something about us," she said. "Lucky for us, he drinks at my taproom every Thursday, tips like guilt, and looks at that girl's clockwork like a man looking at a country he's been exiled from."

"Lucky," Ivo said, and did not smile, because he had carried enough loads in his life to know that the rope had started singing — high and thin, felt more than heard — and that somewhere below them, in the dark of the tower, in the dark of the magistrate's house, other hands were loading stones of their own.

Thursday was three days off. He very much doubted the mountain would wait for it.
