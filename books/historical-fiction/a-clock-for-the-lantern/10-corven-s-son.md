## 10. Corven's Son

The audit papers had been on Elias Corven's desk for eleven days before he could bring himself to touch them.

They sat beneath a paperweight of green glass — his father's gift, heavy as a sin — and every morning Elias came into the clerk's office at the courthouse, hung his coat, sat down, and looked at them the way a man looks at a letter he already knows contains bad news. The observatory audit. His father's own hand had drafted the order: *A full accounting of observatory expenditures, materials, and correspondence, to be conducted by E. Corven, Clerk to the Magistrate, before the spring thaw. No exceptions.*

*No exceptions.* As though Elias were a man given to making them.

He was not, in fairness, given to making anything. That was the trouble with being the magistrate's son in a town the size of Vell: everyone knew what you were before you opened your mouth, and so there seemed little point in opening it. He signed what was put in front of him. He copied what was dictated. He wore the good wool coat and the polished boots and he walked the square at half past nine each morning so that the town could see the machinery of authority running smoothly, oiled and punctual, like the great clock above them all.

Except the great clock hadn't run since autumn.

Elias pulled the audit papers out from under the glass weight and read them again, though he knew them by heart. There was something in the phrasing that had bothered him from the first — a small wrongness, like a tick that comes a half-beat early. His father wanted the observatory audited. Fine. His father wanted *correspondence* inventoried. Also fine, if unusual. But his father had specified — in his own drafting hand, no clerk's intermediary — that the audit include *the clock chamber and its fixtures*, and that the warden Brannock be present throughout.

Why would a magistrate care about fixtures? Why would Brannock need to hover?

Elias had learned long ago that his father's documents were like ice over a river: the surface told you nothing about what moved underneath. You did not go poking holes in the ice to find out. You walked where you were told to walk, and you thanked God — or whatever passed for God in the Corven household, which was chiefly order — when spring came and the whole thing melted away and nobody had drowned.

He gathered his ledger book, his measuring tape, his seal-stamp, and his father's letter of instruction, and he set out through the snow toward the observatory hill.

* * *

The path up was churned to brown slush by cart wheels. Vell sat in its bowl of mountains like a coin at the bottom of a cup, and the observatory stood above it on the shoulder of the ridge, white dome catching the midmorning sun so that it flared and made him squint. Below, the town smoked gently from a hundred chimneys. Somewhere down there was the Lantern Inn, and somewhere beyond it the frozen bridge, sealed under drifts until thaw, holding the whole valley shut like a fist.

He had been up here twice as a boy — once for a ceremony, once because his father had wanted him to see the telescope and understand, in his father's words, *what looking properly at things accomplished*. He remembered the dome room smelling of brass polish and cold. He remembered the astronomer, old Havel, saying very little, and Havel's daughter — a small girl then, quick-eyed, silent — watching him from behind her father's coat with an expression he had never forgotten and never understood until years later, when someone told him she was deaf and read lips the way other children read picture books. She had been reading *him*, even then. Even at eight years old, he must have had the face of a boy who was only pretending to want to be there.

He wondered if she still lived in the observatory house. He found, uncomfortably, that he hoped so.

Brannock met him at the tower door, keys already in hand, breath steaming. "Master Corven." The head warden was a slab of a man gone soft at the edges, with a warden's habit of standing slightly too close. "Your father's letter said you'd come."

"Eventually," Elias said, and immediately wished he hadn't. Brannock's face did something complicated — not quite a smile, but an acknowledgment between men who both served the same difficult master. "I've been… finishing the quarter accounts first."

"Of course." Brannock unlocked the door. "The clock-mender's up in the gear room. Been at it since November."

"Ah. Yes. The apprentice." Elias consulted his father's letter as though he needed to. "Quintan — " he caught himself, checked the paper, "— Quintel. Ansel Quintel."

"That's the one." Something flickered across Brannock's heavy face, too fast to name. "Quiet enough. Good hands, they say. We'll see if the hands are worth the wages."

They climbed. The tower stairs wound upward through the clock's own anatomy — past the weight shafts, where the great cast-iron drives hung silent in their channels like animals asleep; past the intermediate gearing, all furred with dust; up into the gear room itself, where the master clock stood against the east wall like a cathedral built by a god with a taste for brass.

Elias had seen it before, but never like this — opened, gutted, its dial hands removed and lying wrapped in wool cloth on a trestle table, its movement exposed down to the bare plates. And working inside it, sleeves rolled, a slim figure perched on a ladder with a lamp hooked to the frame, so that the light fell upward and turned the apprentice into something carved out of shadow and gold edge.

"Master Quintel." Brannock's voice filled the room. "Clerk Corven is here for the audit."

The figure came down the ladder without hurry, took the rag off the shoulder, wiped hands that were nicked and stained black with clock oil, and turned.

Elias had expected a boy. Everyone said *the apprentice*, everyone said *he*, and so his mind had prepared a boy — gawky perhaps, or sullen, some miner's son with ink-stained fingers who'd fallen into a trade. What stood before him instead was — he searched for the word and kept landing on the wrong ones — *careful*. A young man, or something very near one, slight-built, with dark hair cut short and practical and eyes that went through Elias the way a gauge goes through a measurement: quickly, precisely, recording everything.

"Sir." A nod. Not servile, not stiff. Measured, like everything else about this person. "You'll want the work records, I expect. I've kept them current."

"You've been keeping your own records?" Elias said. "The observatory has a clerk's book."

"The observatory's clerk's book stopped being current in October, sir. I didn't want gaps." A pause, and then, with the smallest tilt of the head: "I assumed an auditor would prefer no gaps."

It was, Elias realized a moment later, a rebuke — delivered so smoothly it took a while to arrive, like thunder after lightning. He felt heat climb his neck. In his father's courthouse, no one rebuked a Corven, however politely. People apologized to him for things that weren't their fault.

"I would," he said. "Prefer no gaps."

"Then we'll get along, sir."

* * *

The morning went to inventory. It should have been tedious, and part of it was — counting files, weighing stock brass, listing tools against the acquisition ledger — but Elias found himself oddly unwilling to let it be tedious, because the apprentice worked the way water runs: without apparent effort and without any wasted motion. When Elias measured the tool cabinet, Quintel had the key out before he asked. When Elias's pencil point snapped, a knife appeared handle-first. When Elias misread his own shorthand and hesitated over a column, the apprentice waited — patiently, expressionlessly — and then said, "Third entry, sir. The '7' reads like a '1' in your hand," which was true, and which no one had ever dared tell him.

"You write well," Elias said finally, because it was easier than saying what he actually thought, which was *you frighten me a little*.

"My father wrote ledgers," Quintel said. To the workbench, not to him. "Before."

"Before what?"

The pause lasted exactly as long as politeness required. "Before he died, sir."

"I'm sorry."

"Thank you, sir." And back to the work, as though a door had closed on a lit room and Elias had seen only the flash of it.

By noon they had reached the correspondence, and this was where the day went strange.

The correspondence was kept in a cedar chest in the corner of the gear room — letters from the university that owned the observatory, supply invoices, the astronomer Havel's occasional reports. All ordinary. Elias copied titles and dates into his audit book, and Quintel handed him bundles in order, and the routine was almost restful. Almost. Because twice — only twice, but twice was once more than never — Elias looked up from his writing and caught the apprentice watching him. Not idly. With an attention that had weight to it, like a hand resting on the back of his neck. And both times, the moment their eyes met, Quintel looked away and became entirely absorbed in the chest, in the bundles, in the careful arrangement of papers that needed no arranging.

The second time it happened, Elias noticed something else: the last bundle in the chest was bound differently. The others lay loose or tied with plain string. This one was tied with wire, twisted tight, and it sat at the bottom of the chest beneath a folded oilcloth, and when Quintel lifted it out to pass it over, the apprentice's hands — those precise, unhurried hands — were not quite steady.

"What's this one?" Elias asked.

"Old maintenance notes. From before my time." Quintel set it on the table between them. "Nothing in it will concern the audit, sir."

"Everything concerns the audit. My father's instructions are explicit."

"Yes, sir." And the apprentice stepped back from the table — just one step, just far enough — and folded those careful hands together, and watched Elias with a face gone smooth as the face of a dial.

Elias untwisted the wire.

Inside were perhaps forty pages, closely written, in at least three different hands. He turned past the first few — figures, dates, abbreviations he didn't recognize — and then a name stopped him. Not a name he knew. A name like a door left open onto a cold room: *Corven*. His father's name, in a column, beside sums of money, beside other names he didn't know, beside dates that fell — he counted back, frowning — in the year of the strike. The year of the hangings. The year every child in Vell was taught to speak of carefully, the way you speak of a grave.

His father's name. Payments. Witnesses.

The gear room was very quiet. He could hear the wind worrying the dome overhead, and the small sounds of the building settling, and his own blood in his ears. He was aware of Quintel standing motionless two steps away, watching him with those measuring eyes, and aware — distantly, absurdly — of the great dead clock behind them both, its pendulum hanging still as a hanged man.

"Elias?"

The voice came from the doorway, and it was a girl's voice, and it broke the silence like a stone through a window.

She stood framed in the stairwell light — small, dark-haired, wrapped in a man's coat too big for her, and he knew her at once, older now but unmistakably the same: Havel's daughter. Odile. She was signing as she spoke, her hands moving in quick shapes, and her eyes went from Elias to the pages in his grip to Quintel's face, and whatever she read there made her go very still.

"It's all right," Quintel said quietly — and the voice was different. Not softer, exactly. Truer. "Odile, this is Clerk Corven. He's doing the audit."

Her gaze settled on Elias again, and he had the vertiginous certainty that she was reading his mouth, his eyes, the set of his shoulders — reading him the way she had read him through her father's coat ten years ago — and finding in his face something she hadn't expected to find.

"He knows," she said. Not a question.

"He's reading," Quintel said. "There's a difference."

Elias looked down at the page in his hands. His father's signature pattern — he knew it anywhere, that peculiar heavy crossbar on the *r*, the way the final stroke of the name always hooked backward like a claw — appeared in the margin of the third page. Beside it, a sum. Beside that, initials he suddenly, horribly understood: witnesses. Men paid to swear away another man's life. Paid by his father.

"How long have you had these?" he heard himself ask. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"Long enough," Quintel said.

"Why are they still here?" And then, before the answer could come, the real question, the one underneath: "Why did you show me?"

Silence. The wind. The pendulum's stillness.

"Because you're going to write it in your audit book or you're not," Quintel said. "And either way, I needed to see which man you are before the thaw comes. That's all, sir. That's the whole of it."

* * *

Later — much later, hours later, after the light had gone long and blue across the snow — Elias would try to reconstruct what he had done next, and he would find that memory refused to arrange itself honestly. What he remembered was the weight of the pages. What he remembered was his father's study at home, the green glass paperweight, the drafting hand, *no exceptions*. What he remembered was the miners' names — he had grown up among those names, they had been the background noise of his childhood, men who had marched and struck and lost and scattered, men whose sons now drove the carts and swept the courthouse steps and touched their caps to him with eyes that held nothing at all.

What he remembered most clearly, though, was Odile.

While he stood there drowning, she had crossed the room — unhurried, unafraid — and taken the bundle from his hands with a gentleness that undid him more than any accusation could have. She retied the wire. She wrapped the oilcloth. She put the bundle back at the bottom of the cedar chest and folded the other papers over it, and all the while she watched his face, and when she finished she looked at him directly and shaped words slowly, deliberately, so that he could not mistake them:

"You can put it in the book."

And then, after a breath:

"Or you can be useful."

Brannock's boots were on the stairs. Elias heard them coming up the spiral, slow and deliberate, and there was no time — no time to think, no time to weigh, no time to be the careful son who walked only where the ice was tested. The pages were hidden. The chest was closed. Brannock filled the doorway with his bulk and his lantern and his warden's flat stare.

"Well, Master Corven? How does the correspondence look?"

Elias looked at his audit book, lying open on the table. He looked at the cedar chest. He looked — briefly, terribly — at the apprentice, who stood with the lamplight gilding one side of that smooth, waiting face.

"Routine," Elias heard himself say. "Maintenance notes and university letters. Nothing that concerns my father's inquiry." He closed his book. "I'll need to return Thursday to finish the fixtures inventory. The clock chamber itself."

Something moved behind Quintel's eyes — relief, or alarm, or both braided together.

"Thursday," Brannock said. "I'll have the keys ready."

Elias gathered his things. At the stairwell door he paused, and made himself turn around, and made himself say the thing that could not be unsaid:

"Master Quintel. Your records are the best-kept I've audited in this office. I'll note that in my report." A beat. "All of my report."

Then he went down the stairs and out into the cold, and the snow took his footsteps, and the observatory dome flared white and silent above him all the way down the hill.

He did not go straight home. He walked the long way, along the river, past the frozen ferry landing, past the garrison gate where the sentries stamped and blew on their fingers, and he tried to understand what he had done and why.

He had lied to Brannock. He had lied, effectively, to his father. For a stranger with careful hands and a dead ledger-writer for a father, and a deaf girl who spoke in signs, and forty pages bearing his own family name like a brand.

*Because you're going to write it in your book or you're not,* Quintel had said. *Either way, I needed to see which man you are.*

But that wasn't the truth of it, Elias admitted, stopping at the river's edge where the ice groaned and shifted in the dark. The truth was smaller and worse. The truth was that he had spent twenty-six years walking on his father's ice, testing every step, thanking God each spring when it melted and nobody drowned — and today, for the first time in his life, he had heard the water moving underneath, and instead of stepping back,

he had wanted to break through.

Thursday, he had promised. The clock chamber itself.

He turned up his collar and started home through the falling snow, carrying his sealed audit book like a coal he was afraid to set down.
