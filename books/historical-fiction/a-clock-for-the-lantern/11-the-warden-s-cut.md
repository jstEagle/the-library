## 11. The Warden's Cut

The hammering started before dawn.

Ansel heard it through two floors of the Lantern Inn — a dull, rhythmic pounding that came up through the bones of the building rather than the air, the way heavy work always sounded in Vell in winter, as if the mountain itself had taken up carpentry. She lay in her narrow bed beneath the eaves and counted the intervals out of habit, the way she counted everything: pendulum swings, gear teeth, the seconds between a knock at the door and the decision to answer it.

Nine strokes, pause. Nine strokes, pause.

Nails. Long ones, driven by someone with a strong arm and no reason to hurry.

She dressed in the dark, wound her chest bindings tight, tucked every tell of herself away where it had lived for eleven years, and went down the stairs into the smell of woodsmoke and fried onions. Marta Sable stood at the taproom window with a mug of something steaming held in both hands, not drinking it, watching the square.

"Tell me," Ansel said.

Marta didn't turn. "Brannock's men are boarding the observatory tower doors. Planking them over, from what I can see. There's a notice going up on the post." She squinted. "*By order of the Head Warden, the clock tower is closed until such time as the structure may be deemed safe for public assembly.*" She read it flatly, the way she read everything, and then she did turn, and her eyes were sharp as chisels. "Safe for public assembly. That's an odd worry for a building nobody's allowed inside."

Ansel's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, like a weight dropping through grease. "The ledgers are still in the escapement chamber."

"I know where they are. You told me where they are." Marta set the mug down untouched. "Which means half of Vell could know where they are, if you talk in your sleep, and you've been sleeping badly."

* * *

The snow had stopped sometime in the night and left the town scrubbed and bright, every rooftop edged in white fire, the observatory above it all gleaming like something poured from silver. It was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. Ansel crossed the square with her tool bag over her shoulder and her collar up, one apprentice among none — there was no other trade worth walking abroad for in this cold — and stopped at the edge of the crowd that had gathered at the tower's base.

They'd done more than board the doors. Two men were at work on the ground-floor windows with planks cut fresh and pale as peeled flesh, nailing them across the frames in crisscross patterns, and a third was up on a ladder fitting an iron hasp to the little door at the tower's root that gave onto the stair — the door Ansel had walked through every morning for five weeks, whose lock she had long since learned to open with a bent wire and three seconds of patience. The new hasp was a serious piece of iron. It would take more than a wire.

Head Warden Brannock stood in the doorway of the annex, hands clasped behind his back, supervising without appearing to supervise, which was his gift. He was a big man gone quietly soft at the edges, with a gray beard trimmed close and a face built for disapproval but capable, Ansel had learned over the autumn, of other expressions when he thought no one was reading it. She watched him now and read nothing at all. He had shut his face like a strongbox.

"Warden." She stepped forward before her caution could catch her arm. "Those boards are across the ventilation louvers. If you seal the tower completely, the temperature swings will crack the great wheel's rim come a hard freeze. I've only just trued it."

Brannock looked at her the way he always did — a beat too long, a degree too directly, as though checking her against some list only he carried. "The clock's run fine for forty years without you, boy."

"The clock's been dead for six months, Warden. That's why I was sent for."

"And it'll keep till spring." He said it easily, and something in how easily he said it rang false in Ansel's inner ear, the way a cracked bell tooth announces itself to a trained hand. "Structural concerns take precedence. Corven's orders — my judgment under his authority. There's ice in the masonry. A chunk fell off the parapet yesterday and near brained one of my men."

"A chunk fell," Ansel repeated. "In still weather."

"Wind does things at altitude you wouldn't understand." Brannock's gaze slid past her to the crowd, gauging it, and then back. "Go home, apprentice. Take the day. You'll be paid through the winter either way."

There it was. Paid through the winter. Money offered where argument wasn't needed, which meant argument was expected. Ansel bowed her head the correct amount for a journeyman to a warden and said, "Yes, Warden," and made herself walk away at the pace of a man who had simply lost the morning's employment, while behind her the hammers went on — nine strokes, pause, nine strokes — sealing the chamber with the ledgers inside it like a heart sealed in a chest.

* * *

She did not go home. She went around.

The observatory hill had a back, and the back had trees, and the trees had grown up against the retaining wall since the last mason had been paid to mind them. Ansel had noted all of this in her first week, not out of suspicion but out of the clockmaker's oldest instinct: know every way into and out of the mechanism, because the mechanism will fail and you will need to reach it. She had never imagined the failure would be human.

The wall was shoulder-high and furred with snow. Beyond it the tower's north flank rose sheer, pierced only by the pendulum shaft's vent — a slot barely wider than her hand — and by the high louvered openings she had just failed to save from the carpenters' nails. From her place among the pines she could see the workmen's progress by sound alone: nails going in, one after another, each one another tooth added to a cage.

Her breath smoked and hung about her head like a thought she couldn't finish. She made herself think instead, coldly, the way her father had taught her to think about a stuck movement. *Don't force it. Find out why it's stuck.*

Why seal a tower? Not safety — Brannock didn't lie clumsily, so the fact that he had lied clumsily was itself information; he hadn't bothered to prepare the lie because he hadn't expected to need it, because the order had come sudden and hot. From Corven, then, or from circumstance. What circumstance? A warden loyal to the magistrate seals the archive tower in midwinter, weeks before the thaw, weeks before the magistrate plans — so Ivo said, so everyone who mattered said — to burn the archives clean.

Unless the burning had moved up its schedule. Unless somewhere in Vell a match had already been struck, or nearly, and Corven wanted the tower full of kindling and locked before anyone noticed the smoke.

Or unless someone had talked.

The thought arrived with a sensation like a gear tooth shearing off. Elias. The audit. *Thursday,* he had promised. *The clock chamber itself.* Had his father read the promise in his son's face the way Odile read lips through glass — not the words but the shape of them, the intention underneath? Had Corven, that patient spider, felt the first tremor in his own web and moved to bind it?

No. She pressed her palm flat against the pine bark, breathing until the spiral slowed. If Corven knew what the ledgers proved, he wouldn't seal them — he'd burn them, tower and all, and call it the ice. Sealing was the act of a man who suspected a mouse in his walls but hadn't yet found the hole. Which meant she still had time. Which meant the ledgers were still alive in there, breathing through the last unboarded louvers, waiting.

And which meant, she realized, with her hand still against the tree, that she had just reasoned her way into caring about a stack of paper the way she'd care about a living thing. When had that happened? Somewhere between finding her father's name in the payment columns and standing in this snow, apparently.

She turned toward town. There were people she needed to see, and for once the seeing couldn't look like seeing.

* * *

Odile was in the telescope room, which meant Ansel could speak to her without speaking. She took her place at the far side of the glass partition where the girl sat wrapped in a shawl the color of garnets, her notebook open, her father's instruments gleaming around her like a congregation of brass saints. Through the pane, Odile saw her at once — she always did, some tide in the air announcing arrivals before footsteps could — and lifted her chin, and waited.

"You're early today," Ansel said, shaping each word with care, and Odile's quick fingers answered on the slate she kept for the occasions when lip-reading across glass grew tiresome: **I AM ALWAYS EARLY. YOU ARE LATE. THE TOWER HAS BEEN NAILED SHUT. EVERYONE IS SAYING SO.**

"They have." Ansel glanced at the corridor beyond, empty, then back. "Can you get a message to your father? Ask him whether the warden consulted the observatory staff about structural safety, and whether any survey was done?"

**PAPA WAS NOT CONSULTED. PAPA IS FURIOUS. HE SAYS THE ONLY ICE IN THAT MASONRY IS IN BRANNOCK'S HEAD.** Odile wrote it with a flourish that was almost a smile, then the smile faded and her slate moved slower. **WHY DID HE SEAL IT, ANSEL? WHAT IS IN THERE?**

Ansel hesitated. Across the glass, Odile's eyes — pale gray, unblinking, the eyes of someone who had spent her life reading faces because she could not read rooms — held hers without mercy.

"A clock," Ansel finally said aloud, and mouthed it clearly. "A very old clock. With a very long memory."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then she wrote, in small letters: **YOU LIE BETTER THAN MOST PEOPLE BUT WORSE THAN YOU THINK. WHEN YOU ARE READY, I READ FAST.**

She wiped the slate and turned to the eyepiece as if the matter were settled, and Ansel stood a moment longer in the cold corridor, feeling seen straight through, before she went down the stairs and out into the white afternoon.

* * *

Ivo received the news in the inn's back parlor with the unhurried gravity of a man who had expected worse and was recalibrating for merely bad. He sat by the stove with his ruined lung wheezing gently, a mug of Marta's black tea going cold beside him, and listened while Ansel laid it all out: the boards, the hasp, the lie about the ice, the money offered without being asked for.

"Nailed shut and locked." He nodded slowly, as though confirming a diagnosis. "That's not Brannock's idea. Brannock's a hammer; he doesn't design anything. Someone handed him the nail and pointed at the wall."

"Corven."

"Corven, or Corven's fear wearing Corven's coat." Ivo rubbed his knee where the old break ached in the cold. "Here's what your sealing tells me, lad. A man doesn't lock a thing up unless he thinks somebody wants it. Either he's heard whispers — and this town leaks whispers like a dry-stone wall leaks wind — or he's begun his own count of what's in that archive and found a page missing from his memory of it. Either way, the hunt's opened. Quietly. But opened."

"Then we move the ledgers now. Tonight. Through the vent—"

"Through a slot wide as your hand, in a tower with new iron on the door and men nailing planks over every opening, with the whole garrison town watching?" Ivo shook his head. "You don't rush a stuck escapement, girl. You'd heard that somewhere, I think."

The endearment slipped out plain, unarmored — *girl* — and both of them froze. It was the first time since Vell that anyone had said it aloud to her face. Ivo looked at the teapot. Ansel looked at the stove. Neither corrected it. Some words, once loose in a warm room, could not be caught again, and neither of them tried.

"What then?" she said quietly.

"Then you let the trap do its work. Ledgers stay put. Locked towers make curious townsfolk, and curious townsfolk ask questions, and questions are a currency Corven can't tax." Ivo's eyes were steady. "You've friends in this town now, Ansel. More than you know. Use the winter. Learn its shape."

* * *

But winter changed shape faster than anyone could chart it, and the change came that same evening, in the person of Head Warden Brannock himself, standing in the Lantern Inn's doorway with snow on his shoulders and his strongbox face firmly shut.

The taproom went quiet the way water goes quiet over a deep place. Brannock removed his hat, scanned the room with one slow turn of his head — Ansel watched his gaze pass over her, pause a fraction, pass on — and crossed to the bar, where Marta stood drying a glass that was already dry.

"Sable. A word in your parlor, if you please."

"My parlor rents by the hour like any other room in my house, Warden. The rate's higher for officials."

"Marta." Just the name, low, and something under it that Ansel had never heard from him before. Not command. Appeal.

Marta set down the glass. "Ivo, pull us three ales and mind the tap." And to Brannock: "Bring your own chair."

They gathered in the back parlor with the stove ticking and the shutter drawn, and Brannock sat heavily, hat between his knees like a man in a chapel, and looked at Ansel for a long moment before he spoke.

"You asked me this morning about the ventilation louvers. Cracking rim. Temperature swing." He said it slowly, testing each word's weight. "You were right. Every word of it right. And I nailed the boards over anyway, because I was ordered to, and because the order said *all* openings, and because when I asked what the hurry was I was told the hurry was mine to stop asking about." His hands tightened on the hat brim. "I've been warden of this town twenty-two years. I've done things in that time I don't put in my ledger, if you take my meaning. But I have never yet sealed a building with a soul inside it, and I want it understood that no soul is inside this one. The tower is empty. You hear me? Empty."

"Perfectly empty," Ansel said.

"Perfectly." He held her eye a moment longer than the word required, and Ansel felt the ground shift under her assumptions the way the river ice shifted at night — groaning, moving, direction unknown. "Now. The reason I've come. Tomorrow at first light, Magistrate Corven arrives at the tower with two clerks and a document case, and whatever happens in there will happen behind those boards with my men outside and my name on the order. I can't stop it. I'm telling you because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because a warden's job is to keep the town safe, and I find lately I'm unclear on what that word means, and I'd rather be unclear on the right side of a door than clear on the wrong one."

He stood, put on his hat, and paused at the threshold without turning around.

"Thursday," he said. "Someone was to meet someone in the clock chamber Thursday. If that someone still intends to keep the appointment, they should know the door has changed." A beat. "And so should know that iron hasps can be bought open, in this town, for the price of a question asked quietly of the right man. Good night."

The door closed behind him. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came down off the mountain and moved through Vell like a slow hand over the keys of some enormous instrument, trying every note.

Marta looked at Ansel. Ivo looked at Ansel. And Ansel looked at the shutter, through it, up through the dark and the snow to the black bulk of the tower on its hill, where her father's name lay folded in the dark behind new-pale planks and a hasp of good honest iron — one night's grace ahead of the magistrate's clerks, and no way in but the price of a question.

"Well," said Marta, reaching for the lamp. "Let's go buy one."
