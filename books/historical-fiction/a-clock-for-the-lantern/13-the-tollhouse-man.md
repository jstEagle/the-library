## 13. The Tollhouse Man

The disguise began with her hands.

Ansel sat at the workbench in the Lantern Inn's back room at dawn, before the fire had properly woken, and made herself into someone else the way she had learned to make a broken movement run again: one small adjustment at a time, nothing so crude that it would draw the eye. She rubbed tallow and lamp-black into the creases of her knuckles until they looked like the hands of a man who had spent thirty years inside clock cases — not clean, not delicate, but worked. She bound her chest tighter than usual, padded her shoulders with wool cut from an old coat of Ivo's, and let her hair vanish beneath a cap that smelled of another man's sweat and pipe smoke.

Marta Sable watched from the doorway with her arms folded, saying nothing for a long time. When Ansel finally looked up, the innkeeper's eyes went over her the way she might appraise a horse offered too cheaply.

"You walk wrong," Marta said.

"I walk fine."

"You walk like a girl pretending to be a boy. A mender walks like his back hurts." Marta crossed the room, took Ansel by the shoulder, and turned her. "Shoulders down. Weight on your heels. You've been hunched over benches since you were six — use it. Nobody suspects a man who moves like his trade."

It was strange advice coming from her, and stranger still how well it worked. Ansel let her spine curve, let her knees soften, and felt something in the room shift — some animal attention that had been fixed on her all winter sliding off like water off waxed canvas. Marta nodded once, satisfied, and went to fetch bread.

Ivo was waiting outside with the cart, wrapped to the ears in scarves, his breath smoking. He had insisted on driving her the first stretch, close enough to see the tollhouse but not so close as to be remembered there. The old miner talked the whole way, low and steady, the way he did everything now — as if loudness might crack something loose in him that he could not afford to have loose.

"Corven's man is called Dree," he said. "Hollis Dree. Was a garrison soldier until he took a rifle butt to the knee in some border nonsense nobody remembers. Corven gave him the tollhouse because a lame man can't desert and a grateful man doesn't ask questions. He's got a woman in Vell — or had; she left him last summer — and a habit that eats everything else."

"What habit?"

Ivo smiled without any warmth in it. "You'll see. Look at his window when you go past. And look at his ledger book, if you can get near it. A man who owes money keeps it written somewhere, even if only in his face."

The road climbed out of town through pines furred white with rime, and the cold came down off the mountain like a hand pressing them into their seats. Ansel sat with the tool roll across her knees and rehearsed. That was the thing about fear — she had learned this young, watching her father's world come apart — fear was energy, and energy unspent would eat you from inside. Spent properly, poured into craft, into rehearsal, into the thousand small preparations of a job done right, it became something else. Not courage exactly. Something more useful than courage: competence. Fear transmuted, the way base metal was supposed to transmute, except she knew the trick of it and alchemists didn't.

*You are Wendel Foss,* she told herself, using the name stitched into the secondhand coat. *Clock-mender, out of Lowmark, called up the mountain because the tollhouse regulator has stopped and the magistrate wants his roads running on time.* The forged call-slip in her pocket bore Elias Corven's clerk-hand — obtained through channels Ansel had decided not to ask about, which meant Odile's ciphered notes and Marta's old network, which meant she owed more people than she liked. *You are slow-moving, half-deaf from years beside striking trains, incurious. You charge too much and apologize for it.*

"Here." Ivo reined in where the trees broke open. Below, in a fold of the valley, sat the tollhouse: a squat stone building astride the bridge road, lantern light in its windows, smoke standing straight up from its chimney in the windless morning. A barrier arm lay across the road. Two figures — no, one figure and a dog. "I'll be at the quarry track till dusk. If you're not back by then, I come looking, and I'm too old to look subtle."

"I'll be back by then."

"Aye." He caught her sleeve as she stepped down. His grip was surprisingly strong for a man his age, or perhaps not surprising at all; men who had swung picks for forty years kept their strength the way rivers kept their cold. "Girl. Ansel." He almost never used the name aloud — her working name, the safe one — and hearing it now steadied her more than any rehearsal. "Dree is not clever, but he is frightened, and frightened men are dangerous in ways clever men aren't. Don't give him a reason to think. Give him a reason to want you gone."

She nodded, pulled her cap low, and started down the road, letting her heels take her weight, letting her back ache.

* * *

The dog found her first.

It came around the side of the tollhouse at a trot, a heavy-jawed brute with frost on its whiskers, and planted itself in the road ten feet ahead of her with the confidence of an animal that had never once been contradicted. Ansel stopped. Her heart slammed once, hard, against her bound ribs — and then she did what she always did with the slam: she took it apart, examined its mechanism, and put it to work. Fear into craft. She stood still, made herself small and boring, and waited.

"Hold, Biter!" A voice from the doorway, rough with morning. "Hold!"

The dog held. A man limped out after it, favoring his left leg heavily, a blanket over his shoulders like a cape. Hollis Dree was younger than Ivo's description had led her to expect — forty, maybe, with a soldier's thick neck going soft and a face that had been handsome before worry got into it. Worry lived around his eyes now the way damp lived in stone: permanent, structural.

"Who're you, then?"

"Wendel Foss." She pitched her voice low and gravelly, the way she'd practiced against the inn's cellar walls. "Clock-mender, out of Lowmark. Got a call-slip for the regulator here." She produced the paper with fingers that trembled slightly — good, she thought, let them tremble, a cold old man's fingers always tremble — and watched him read it.

He read slowly. She noted that. Note everything, she told herself. The way he shifted weight off the bad leg every eleven or twelve seconds. The way his eyes flicked, twice, toward the window on the east side of the house. The way the blanket hung — over one shoulder, hiding something at his hip, probably a pistol, probably unloaded because powder was dear. The way the dog looked at her hands, not her face. Dogs remembered hands.

"Magistrate's office sent for me," she added, when the silence stretched. "Said the regulator's been losing time since the freeze. I'll want to see it, and I'll want four hours, and it'll be two crowns."

"Two crowns!" Dree's outrage was genuine and, she suspected, welcome to him — a familiar grievance in an unfamiliar morning. "For sitting at a little clock?"

"For sitting at a little clock that tells the magistrate whether his roads are honest." She said it flatly, wearily, the complaint of a tradesman who had made it a hundred times. "Or I turn round and go home, and you explain to the magistrate why his tolls are still being logged late."

That landed. She saw it land — saw the flinch behind his eyes that had nothing to do with clocks and everything to do with whatever it was he logged late, or early, or not at all. Another note for the ledger in her head.

"Two crowns," he muttered. "Robbery. Come in, then. Mind the dog."

Inside, the tollhouse was one room and a sleeping loft, warm from a stove that had been fed generously — generous fuel was itself a clue, she thought; a man burning wood he hadn't paid for either stole it or expected company. The regulator clock hung on the wall between a rack of toll tickets and a framed print of the magistrate, Aldous Corven, looking statesmanlike and faintly bored. Ansel gave the portrait one respectful glance and no more, and turned to the clock.

It was a decent piece, thirty years old, a commercial regulator of the kind installed along coach roads. Its problem announced itself the moment she opened the case: the suspension spring had fatigued and cracked, a common failure in hard cold, and someone had tried to mend it with wire and a prayer. She set her roll on the table, chose her tools with the unhurried deliberateness of a man who charged by the hour, and settled onto the stool Dree dragged over.

And then, for four hours, she did two things at once.

With her hands, she repaired the clock — truly repaired it, because a botched job would bring questions and a good one brought none. She fitted a new spring from her stock, trued the crutch, cleaned the pivots, oiled sparingly. It was pleasant work, honest work, and some quiet part of her mind that never entirely slept noted the irony: here she was, daughter of Tomás Quintana, keeping time for the machinery of the man who had killed him. Let the irony keep. Everything kept. That was what clocks were for.

With the rest of her mind — the part above and behind her hands — she watched, and counted, and memorized.

Dree sat at his desk by the window and pretended to work on his toll ledger. He was bad at pretending. Every quarter hour or so he got up, limped to the east window, and looked out at the road for a long minute before returning. On the third such circuit, Ansel followed the line of his gaze without turning her head: the window faced down-valley, toward the ferry landing, invisible from here beyond a shoulder of rock — but a signal could be seen. A lantern, a flag, a mirror-flash. The tollhouse wasn't just a checkpoint. It was a watch-post, and Dree was watching for something coming up the river road.

On the fourth circuit, she spoke without lifting her eyes from the escapement. "Road's quiet."

"Frozen season. Nothing moves but fools and clock-menders." But he said it too quickly, and his hand had gone to the desk drawer before he caught himself and turned the motion into scratching his knee.

"Quiet roads are good for my trade," she said mildly. "Bad for yours, I'd think. Tolls don't collect themselves."

Something moved through Dree's face then, quick as a fish under ice — and she understood, with the sudden clarity that sometimes came to her in the middle of delicate work, that she had found the seam in the man. Not the pistol. Not the dog. The money. A tollhouse on a frozen road collected nothing, and a man whose woman had left him, whose habits needed feeding, whose pride needed salving, sat all winter in a stone box watching an empty road while his debts grew teeth.

She finished the spring, closed the case, and let the regulator tick — a clean, even tick, the sound of a promise kept. Then she packed her tools with great care and named her price again, and when Dree reached for his purse she watched his face do the arithmetic and come up short.

"Two crowns," he said, and opened the purse, and she saw — because she had positioned herself, casually, in the light from the window, and because she had spent twenty years reading the faces of mechanisms and men — that the purse held perhaps eight shillings.

He counted out one crown and twelve shillings and pushed it across, and his ears went red, and he looked at the wall rather than at her.

"That's short," Ansel said.

"It's what there is." The words came out raw. "You'll get the rest when the thaw comes and the road opens. Everyone waits for the thaw. Write it in your book."

And there it was — the weakness, laid open like a cracked pivot, and Dree too tired and too ashamed to hide it anymore. Ansel thought of Ivo's voice: *look at his ledger book.* She glanced at the desk as she rose, an old man's idle glance, and saw what she saw: the official toll ledger, neat and mostly empty — and beneath it, half-tucked out of sight, a smaller book, cheap paper, swollen with damp, its cover marked with a chalked figure she recognized from Marta's stories of the gambling dens below the quarry: a circle with a cross through it. The mark of the Farthing Wheel. Dree owed money to the Wheel, and the Wheel did not wait for thaws.

"Keep your crown and twelve," Ansel heard herself say.

Dree's head came up.

"I'm not a charity," she went on, gruffly, before gratitude could bloom into suspicion. "But I've been short myself, and the magistrate's office pays me either way. You'll owe me eight shillings and a favor, and the favor is this: when I come through again — and I will, the magistrate keeps calling me out to these clocks — you'll wave me past quick and not make me stand in the cold answering questions. That's all. That's the whole price."

A long silence. The regulator ticked. The dog sighed by the stove.

"You'd trust me for eight shillings?" Dree said at last, and there was something underneath the surprise — something almost hungry, the sound of a man who had not been trusted in a long time and had forgotten what it weighed.

"I trust the magistrate's office," Ansel lied comfortably. "They know where I lodge."

* * *

She walked away from the tollhouse at a mender's pace, back aching, heels weighted, and did not let herself hurry until the road bent behind the shoulder of rock and the tollhouse was gone from view. Then she stood in the snow among the pines and shook — not with cold, though the cold helped — and let the shaking run through her and out, the way a striking train ran down after the hour. Four hours inside the first checkpoint. Four hours counting a guard's movements, mapping his sightlines, learning the rhythm of his rounds the way she'd learned the rhythm of a striking train: when it wound, when it released, where the play was in the system.

Play. Every mechanism had it. Every lock, every guard, every plan. The art was never in fighting the play — it was in finding it and using it.

By the time Ivo's cart creaked out of the quarry track to meet her, she had it all written in her mind's ledger, in columns as neat as Odile's braille:

*Dree. Left leg, favors it, cannot pursue uphill. Dog obeys voice only, name Biter, food-motivated. East window watches the river road — signals expected from below, meaning the ferry landing reports to him, or he to it. Rounds irregular, keyed to his drinking; sober at dawn, worse by evening. Pistol likely present, likely unloaded. And the debt — the Farthing Wheel, eight shillings short on a two-crown bill, trusting and ashamed and lonely enough to shake a stranger's hand at the door when she left.*

"He took the credit," she told Ivo as the cart rolled toward Vell and the light went amber behind the western peaks. "Eight shillings against spring. And a standing agreement: I pass quick whenever I'm called."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Snow squeaked under the wheels. "You bought him," he said finally, and there was something in his voice she couldn't name — admiration braided with unease. "Your father would have hated it. Buying a man instead of waking him."

"My father's dead," Ansel said, "because the men around him were bought cheaper." She heard the hardness in her own voice and did not soften it. "When the thaw comes and the ledgers move, Dree won't be persuaded. He'll be paid, or he'll be blind, and either way the tollhouse falls without a sound. That's not corruption, Ivo. That's engineering."

The old miner looked at her sidelong, and whatever he saw made him rein the horse slower, as if the road deserved respect. "You've changed since midwinter," he said. "Or maybe you've stopped changing back."

She didn't answer. Ahead, Vell's roofs were lighting up one window at a time, and above them the observatory tower stood black against the last of the sky, and inside the tower, sealed behind Brannock's locks and the pendulum shaft's darkness, the evidence of her father's murder waited for a route out. One checkpoint mapped. Two remained. And the thaw — Corven's thaw, the burning thaw — was closer every day.

She was still tallying it, checkpoint by checkpoint, when the Lantern Inn's door opened ahead of them and a figure came down the steps into the lamplight — tall, well-dressed, unmistakably out of place on this street at this hour.

Elias Corven stood in the snow outside the inn, hat in his hands, waiting for her.
