## 14. Elias Asks Questions

Elias Corven stood in the snow outside the inn, hat in his hands, waiting for her.

Ivo's breath stopped beside her — Ansel felt it more than heard it, a small hitch in the dark like a gear tooth catching. The old miner had gone very still, the way men did when they remembered which side of a magistrate's attention they'd rather not be on.

"Go on inside," Ivo murmured. "I'll see to the lamp."

"You'll do no such thing." But it was too late; he was already moving off down the street with the unhurried gait of a man who had spent fifty years walking away from trouble at exactly the speed that made it lose interest. His boots left two neat lines in the snow, and then he turned the corner by the chandler's, and Ansel was alone in the lamplight with the magistrate's son.

She considered running. It was an honest consideration, and she gave it its due weight — the alley behind the cooperage was ten steps to her left, unlit, and Elias Corven did not look like a man built for chasing anyone through snowdrifts. But running confirmed guilt, and guilt confirmed suspicion, and suspicion, once lodged in a Corven, would grow roots. Better to stand. Better to be a clockmaker's apprentice who had every right to be walking home from a long day at the observatory, tired and cold and mildly annoyed to find a gentleman blocking his door.

So she stood, and pulled her cap lower against the cold, and pitched her voice into the flat, bored register of Ansel Quintana, journeyman. "Master Corven. It's late for calls."

"It is." He didn't put his hat on. He held it the way a man holds something he has forgotten why he picked up. Snow gathered on his shoulders — good wool, city cut, the kind of coat that cost what Ivo earned in a season — and he seemed not to notice or not to care. "May I have a word? Not here. Inside, if your landlady permits."

"Marta Sable doesn't permit much of anything after ten."

"Then here." He glanced up and down the street. Empty. The lamplight pooled between them, yellow and unsteady, and beyond it the snow came down in that silent, businesslike way of mountain snow, as though it had been commissioned. "It won't take long. I only have one question, and I suspect you'll know whether to answer it before I've finished asking."

Ansel folded her arms. Her hands were cold; folding them kept them from shaking visibly. "Ask it, then."

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him look — watched the clerk behind the eyes sorting, filing, cross-referencing — and understood suddenly that this was a man who had spent his whole life being handed answers he hadn't asked for, and that tonight, for perhaps the first time, he wanted one he had earned.

"The observatory audit," he said. "My father asked me to conduct it. A formality — the tower's accounts, the warden's requisitions, the repair invoices. I've been over them three times." He paused. "The ledgers are clean. That's the problem."

"The problem," Ansel repeated, keeping her voice as level as a spirit vial.

"When a ledger is dirty, you find smudges, erasures, columns that don't sum. When a ledger is *too* clean—" He stopped himself, and something moved across his face that was not quite a smile. "Forgive me. I'm doing what I always do. Explaining my reasoning to people who haven't asked for it."

"You said one question."

"I did." He drew a breath, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone quiet, gone somewhere private. "Six years ago, there were payments made from the miners' relief fund. Disbursements marked as widow's pensions. My father signed the authorizations — I've seen the signatures a hundred times, on a hundred documents, all my life. And these signatures are *perfect*, Master Quintana. Every loop, every cross-stroke. Too perfect." He looked down at his hat. "A man signs his own name ten thousand times and it wears down, like a stair. Like a coin. These signatures don't wear. They're drawn, not written. The way a forger draws them who has studied the original but never once had to write the name under pressure, in haste, in grief."

The snow filled the silence. Somewhere up the hill, the dead clock stood in its tower, and Ansel thought of the papers hidden beneath the escapement chamber floor — thought of the margins, where Magistrate Corven's hand appeared again and again beside the payments, beside the dates, beside the names of witnesses bought to swear away Tomás Quintana's life. She thought: *he has seen the signatures but not the ledgers. He is circling the truth the way I circle a broken train, tapping the case, listening.*

"And your question?" she said.

"My question is—" He stopped. Started again. His hands tightened on the hat brim until the felt creased. "My question is whether a man can be a good magistrate and a bad one at once. Whether the town can be well-ordered and rotten at the root. Because I have spent twenty-six years believing the first, and lately I keep finding evidence of the second in documents that are supposed to be boring." He laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "That wasn't my question. That was the speech I rehearsed on the walk down. My actual question is simpler."

"Then ask it."

"Do you know anything about the observatory ledgers that you are not telling me?"

* * *

Later — much later, lying awake in the narrow room above the Lantern Inn's kitchen, listening to the banked fire tick and settle — Ansel would turn the moment over and over, examining it the way she examined a suspicious wheel, looking for the flaw in it. But standing in the snow with the question still hanging between them, she had only instinct, and her instinct said two contradictory things at once: *lie*, and *don't lie badly*. A bad lie would be worse than none. This man audited things for a living. He would hear a false note the way she heard a false beat in a ticking escapement — not consciously at first, just a wrongness that would not stop itching.

So she gave him the truth, carefully measured, the way you feed a regulator clock — just enough weight to keep the mechanism honest.

"I know the clock," she said. "The clock is what I was sent for. Sabotage in the striking train — someone cut the lifting pins with a jeweler's file, which takes skill and takes time and takes access. Someone wanted this town's clock dead, quietly, without anyone asking why. That's what I know. If there's something rotten in the ledgers, Master Corven, that's above my station. I fix mechanisms. I don't read magistrates' correspondence."

"Everyone reads everything," Elias said. "That's the first thing you learn in an audit. Everyone reads everything, and no one admits to it, and the truth lives in what people pretend not to have noticed." He put his hat on at last, and the gesture closed something — the conversation, or the evening, or some door in himself that he might not be able to reopen. "Thank you for your honesty, such as it was."

"It was complete."

"No," he said mildly, "it wasn't. But it was more than I expected, and less evasive than I feared, and both of those things tell me something." He took a step back into the falling snow, and paused, and looked at her one last time — a long, level look that made the skin between her shoulder blades prickle, because there was something in it that was not suspicion, exactly. Something more like recognition. Not of her face. Of something else. Of the particular quality of a person carrying a secret the size of a coffin.

"For what it's worth," he said, "when I report tomorrow, I'll report that the apprentice's work is competent and the accounts require further review. Further review takes weeks. Possibly months, if the reviewer is thorough." A beat. "I am capable of being extremely thorough."

"Why?" The word was out before she could weigh it. "Why would you do that?"

He was already turning away, and he answered over his shoulder, so that she almost lost it to the wind — and yet she caught every word, the way Odile caught words through glass, whole and unmistakable.

"Because my father taught me that a signature is a sacred thing. That a man's word, once given in ink, binds his soul. He said it so often I memorized it as a child." The snow was thickening around him, erasing his outline even as he stood there. "And now I've found forty-one signatures in his hand, and not one of them worn down. You spend six years learning watchmaking, Master Quintana. Tell me — what does it mean when the mechanism works perfectly and the thing it's driving is a lie?"

He didn't wait for an answer. He walked up the street toward the magistrate's house on the hill, and the snow took him the way it takes everything in Vell eventually — gently, completely, without malice.

* * *

Inside, Marta was waiting. Of course Marta was waiting. She sat at the kitchen table with a single candle and two cups, and she poured tea into the second cup without asking, because asking would have acknowledged that she'd been watching the street through the curtain for twenty minutes, and neither of them was interested in acknowledging anything so obvious.

"Well?" Marta said.

"He knows something about the signatures. Not the ledgers — the *signatures*. Forgeries in the relief fund payments. He's found the thread but not where it leads."

"And what did you give him?"

"A measured truth. The sabotage, nothing else."

Marta turned her cup slowly on its saucer, once around, the way she did when she was thinking in smuggler rather than innkeeper. "Measured truths," she said, "are like measured doses. Right amount, they cure. Wrong amount, they kill." She looked up. "Which was it?"

Ansel wrapped her hands around her own cup. The tea was too hot to drink and she drank it anyway, needing something to do with her mouth that wasn't answering. "I don't know yet. That's what frightens me. He covered for me — or he will, tomorrow, when he reports. Weeks of 'further review.' Why would a man burn time for a stranger?"

Marta snorted. "Boy's twenty-six years old and just figured out his father might be the villain of the story he grew up in. That's not generosity, girl, that's vertigo. He needs time the same as you do — time to decide which side of the signature he stands on." She rose, took the candle, and paused at the doorway, her shadow long across the kitchen wall. "One thing, though. Free of charge, since I like you."

"What?"

"When a Corven starts asking questions, the questions don't stop. They go home, they sit at dinner, they get asked across the roast." Marta's voice was quiet and absolutely certain. "Whatever he decides by spring — he'll decide it soon. Men like that crack fast or not at all. Be ready for either."

* * *

Upstairs, sleep refused her. She lay in the dark and did what she always did when she couldn't sleep: she built the clock in her mind, wheel by wheel, from the great barrel up through the going train to the escapement, that small tireless heart that divided time into livable pieces. And tonight the mechanism kept resolving itself into faces. The escape wheel turning, and it was Ivo mapping checkpoints in spilled sugar on the inn table. The pallets engaging, releasing, and it was Odile's quick fingers shaping lantern signals against the dark. The mainspring coiled tight with stored force, and it was Marta saying *men like that crack fast or not at all*.

And the pendulum — the heavy swinging mass at the center of it all, the thing whose weight governed everything — was Elias Corven, walking uphill through the snow toward the house he'd been born in, carrying a question like a lit fuse.

Because here was the thing Ansel understood about pendulums, the thing her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see over his bench: a pendulum doesn't choose where it swings. Gravity chooses. Amplitude chooses. But a pendulum does carry the whole train with it — every wheel, every hand, every strike of every bell depends on that one weighted arc, faithful or not.

Forty-one unworn signatures. He'd counted them. He'd sat in his father's office with the fund disbursements spread out before him and counted them, and instead of filing the anomaly away the way a loyal son files away a hundred small compromises, he had walked down the hill in the snow to ask a stranger the only question that mattered to him anymore.

*What does it mean when the mechanism works perfectly and the thing it's driving is a lie?*

She knew the answer. She'd known it since she was eleven years old, standing in a square with her mother's hand crushing hers, watching the state's perfect machinery do its perfect work. The mechanism working perfectly was precisely the horror. Justice that ran smooth as a Swiss movement while grinding up a family in its gears — she had crossed half a country wearing a dead boy's name to get away from machines like that.

But Elias didn't know it yet. He knew it only in his hands, the way she'd known the clock's sabotage in her fingertips before her mind admitted it: something filed wrong, something cut too clean. He was at the stage she'd been at in chapter one of her own life — professional pride curdling into dread.

Let him come back, she thought, drowsing at last, the fire ticking down below. Let him bring the thread and follow it to the spool. There were worse allies than the magistrate's own son. There were also worse enemies. And either way, the snow was falling on Vell, sealing the passes, closing the roads, locking all of them — the liar, the daughter, the doubter, the whole frozen town — inside the same long night together, with a dead clock at the top of the hill waiting to be taught how to speak.

In the morning, there was chalk on the observatory door.

Not graffiti — a mark, small and precise, drawn at eye height where anyone entering would pass within a foot of it. Three strokes and a circle. Ansel stood before it in the grey light with her breath steaming, reading it the way Odile read lips, and her blood went cold, because she knew this mark. Her father had used it. Every organizer in the valley camps had used it, scratched on fence posts and door lintels in the year before the hangings.

*A meeting. Trusted hands only.*

Someone in Vell still remembered the sign. And whoever it was had left it for her.
