## 15. Midwinter Fair

Marta Sable had run contraband through three borders and two wars, and she had learned long ago that the best disguise in the world was a crowd that wanted to look at something else. The Midwinter Fair gave Vell exactly that: a square full of people so busy staring at sugar horses and fire-jugglers that they would not notice a satchel changing hands three feet from their elbows.

She stood at the window of the Lantern Inn's back room with a cup of mulled wine going cold in her hand and watched the town assemble itself below. Snow had been swept from the square into gray banks along the well. Booths of pine poles and sailcloth lined the perimeter in a rough ring, and strings of lanterns hung between the observatory tower and the chandler's eaves like a necklace someone had strung carelessly but lovingly. Children ran underfoot with candied apples. Somewhere near the fountain a fiddle was tuning, the notes wandering up through the cold air half-drunk, finding themselves one by one.

Behind her, the room smelled of ink and lamp oil. Ivo sat with his bad leg propped on a stool, drawing the square on the back of an old delivery manifest — the same map he had drawn for them twice already, but Marta had learned that men who had spent their lives underground liked to draw things out. It steadied the hand. Odile knelt beside him, her dark hair pinned up with two pencil stubs, copying the route onto a slate in her quick, certain strokes. Ansel stood apart, as she always did, arms folded, watching the map rather than the window, and Marta watched her watch it and thought, not for the first time, that the girl moved through rooms like someone who had practiced being unnoticed until the practice had grown into the bone.

"Say it again," Ansel said. "All three."

Ivo didn't sigh, though Marta saw the breath gather in his chest and get put away unused. He tapped the map with a stub of charcoal instead. "Tollhouse on the east road, one man, day shift changes at noon and dusk. Garrison gate, two men, and they change when they please because nobody checks. Ferry landing, which is nothing but ice and a shed now, but Corven keeps a watcher there all the same, because a man who guards a door that won't open is a man telling you what's behind it." He looked up at Ansel. "The ledgers go out at thaw if they go out at all. But we rehearse now, while the fair gives us cover, so that when the night comes our hands already know the shape of it."

"And the decoy?"

"The decoy goes tonight," Marta said, turning from the window. "A little taste. A satchel of nothing, passed from hand to hand across the square, and every eye that follows it is an eye learning to look in the wrong direction. That's the whole art, girl. You don't hide a thing by putting it in the dark. You hide it by giving them something brighter to stare at."

Ansel's eyes came up — gray eyes, steady, older than her forged papers said she was. "And if tonight goes wrong? If someone follows the satchel to its end?"

"Then we lose a satchel of nothing," Marta said, "and learn which of us can't lie yet." She set down the cooling wine. "Which is why we rehearse."

Odile tapped the slate. She had drawn the square from above, the way a gull would see it, with small marks for the booths and the lantern lines, and beside each checkpoint a tiny sketch: a lantern held high, a hand flat and sliding, a fist closing. Her signs were becoming their private language faster than any of them had agreed to it. *Lantern means stop. Flat hand means move along, nothing here. Fist means drop it and scatter.* She looked at Marta expectantly.

"That's right," Marta said aloud, and then made the signs back at her — clumsy, thumb at the wrong angle, and Odile laughed, that soundless laugh of hers that was mostly shoulders and eyes, and corrected her without cruelty. The girl read lips through glass, across a room, in poor light; reading Marta's fumbling hands was nothing to her. But she corrected her anyway, gently, thoroughly, the way she corrected everyone, as if precision were a form of affection. Marta had smuggled with hard men and desperate ones for twenty years and had never once been taught anything kindly. She found, to her private astonishment, that she liked it.

"Booths open at four," Ivo said, folding the manifest away. "The square will be thickest between six and eight, when the fires are lit. We pass the decoy at seven. Ansel carries first, Marta takes second, I take third and lose it in the crowd by the fountain." He glanced at Odile. "You're our eyes. From the chandler's steps you can see the whole ring. Any uniform, anyone standing too still, anyone watching the watchers — you signal."

Odile nodded once, businesslike, then ruined the severity of it by grinning.

* * *

By six o'clock the fair had swallowed the square whole.

The fires were lit in iron braziers, and the light they threw was the color of cider, warm and unsteady, making the snowbanks glow orange at their edges. The fiddler had found himself and been joined by a piper and a drummer, and the music came off the stone buildings in overlapping waves so that the square seemed to hold several songs at once and choose among them. Sugar smoke and roasting chestnuts and wet wool. A peddler was selling tin whistles to every child within reach of his voice. Near the well, the blacksmith's apprentices had set up the ring of rope where boys swung at a suspended goose greased with tallow, and the crowd roared with every miss.

Marta moved through it all in her good winter coat, gray wool, deliberately forgettable, carrying a market basket with turnips on top and a wrapped satchel beneath. The satchel weighed almost nothing. That was the point of it. In her experience the weight of a thing told a pursuer more than its shape ever could; give them a heavy bundle and they imagined gold, papers, guns. Give them nothing and they imagined everything.

She bought a twist of dried figs from a booth to justify her wandering. She argued amiably about the price of candles. She let an old widow she'd known for fifteen years complain to her about her son-in-law for four unhurried minutes, and all the while her eyes worked the edges of the square the way her fingers had once worked a lock: tollhouse road, clear; garrison lane, one pair of boots planted too still beside the cooper's booth — noted, catalogued, dismissed for now.

At the corner by the bakery she passed close to Ansel, who was pretending to study a tray of brass ornaments with an intensity no honest person had ever brought to brass ornaments, and neither of them looked at the other as the satchel left Ansel's basket and entered Marta's in the space of a single stumble over uneven cobbles. Two strangers nearby never turned their heads. Marta felt a fierce, unreasonable pride, the kind she usually kept locked up where it couldn't be taxed. Three weeks ago the girl had hands like a clockmaker's — precise, yes, but honest, trained to work in the open light of a bench. Now she was learning to steal in plain sight, and learning it well.

*He would have hated this,* Marta thought, and didn't let herself finish the thought about Tomás Quintana, because grief at a fair was a luxury and she was working.

She took her time crossing the square. That was the other lesson nobody believed until they learned it: hurry is the loudest thing a person can wear. She stopped to watch the goose-swinging. She applauded a juggler who dropped both pins and bowed anyway. And at the fountain, in the churn of children chasing one another around the basin, she brushed against Ivo's sleeve, and the satchel went into his coat like a secret being forgiven.

Ivo was good. Marta had suspected it and now confirmed it — the old miner had a stillness about him, a way of being exactly as interesting as a tired man with a sore leg ought to be, no more, no less. He limped toward the far side of the square at the pace of a man heading home, and Marta lost interest in him on purpose, because the moment you couldn't stop watching your own courier was the moment you became the weak link in your own chain.

She turned back toward the inn, and that was when she saw the trouble.

* * *

There was a man by the sugar booth who did not belong to the fair and did not belong to the town.

Marta placed him in half a second, the way she could still place a counterfeit coin by its weight in her palm. Garrison boots polished but not garrison-cut coat — a plainclothes man, then, or wanting to be thought one. Young, maybe thirty, with the particular patience of someone paid to stand still. He wasn't watching the jugglers. He wasn't watching the goose. He was watching the flow of the crowd itself, the way a heron watches water, and though Marta could not yet tell what he hoped to see, she knew the posture. She had worn it herself, in another country, in another life, waiting for a runner who never came because Marta had sold him.

The knowledge arrived in her body before her mind: a tightening across the shoulders, a sourness under the tongue. Twenty years since she'd informed on Wren Halloway to save her own neck, and her body had kept the receipt.

She scanned for Odile and found her on the chandler's steps, small and straight-backed in the lamplight, and Odile was already looking — not at the man, but at Marta, and her hand rose in a small, tight motion. Not the flat slide. Not the lantern. Something new, improvised: two fingers pressed to her own collarbone, then flicked outward. *Watch. Behind.*

Marta didn't turn. Rule of a lifetime: never turn at the first warning. She bought nothing from the next booth, touched nothing, simply stood in the warmth of a brazier and let the crowd move past her while she assembled the picture from reflections — the copper pans at the tinker's stall, the dark bottle-glass of the chandler's window, the shine on a child's tin whistle. There. Second man, north side, near the garrison lane, dressed better than the first and worse than he wished. And moving through the crowd between them with a walk that was trying very hard to be idle: a third.

Three watchers at a Midwinter Fair in a town of four hundred souls. Corven hadn't sent men to watch for smugglers. He had sent men because he had begun, somewhere in that careful magistrate's skull, to smell smoke — and a fair drew every kind of person, and a man expecting fire watches chimneys.

And Ivo, with the satchel in his coat, was walking directly toward the north-side man's patch of ground.

Marta calculated. She had always been able to calculate fast; it was the one inheritance from her father besides his chin, and she had made more money with the calculation than the chin. Ivo was forty feet from trouble and moving. To signal him openly was to signal the watchers too. To let him walk on was to feed a decoy satchel of nothing to Corven's men — survivable, perhaps, for the satchel held only rags and an old hymnbook — but the men would follow Ivo home. To the Lantern Inn. Where Ansel slept, and where a clockmaker's apprentice with forged papers and a magistrate-shaped grudge had been living under Marta's roof for a month.

No. That ledger did not balance.

So Marta Sable did what she had sworn, twenty years ago in a border town with rain coming down, that she would never do again: she stepped forward and made herself the story.

* * *

It began with laughter. That was the trick nobody expected — the loud ones were invisible, because everyone looked away from them. Marta let out a great bark of amusement at some joke the piper had apparently told, staggered two steps sideways into the north-side man's path, and gripped his sleeve with a drunkard's perfect imprecision.

"There he is!" she announced, to the square at large, at a volume that turned eleven heads. "There's the handsome devil himself, come all this way and didn't send word—"

The man's face went through a rapid and fascinating series of failures. He tried to pull free; she held on. He tried to look stern; she beamed up at him with the terrible confidence of a woman who has decided you are someone else and cannot be talked out of it.

"You don't—" he started.

"Not one word," Marta said, and her voice cracked just so, grief and gin in equal measure, "not one word, Anders, I've waited two winters and you'll hear all of it, every word, before you take another step—" and she steered him, stumbling, protesting, utterly immobilized by social catastrophe, directly away from the fountain, away from Ivo's path, into the crowd by the goose-ring, where a boy chose that exact moment to finally fell the greased goose to a roar that shook the brazier flames.

Marta held the man there in the roaring, laughing crush for the length of one song. She apologized to him twice, magnificently, for mistaking him for Anders, whoever Anders might be, and pressed a twist of dried figs into his hand as consolation, and by the time she released him he was less a threat than a man in urgent need of somewhere to hide his embarrassment. Across the square she caught the flicker of Odile's hands: flat palm, sliding. *Move along. Nothing here.*

She found Ivo ten minutes later at the edge of the firelight, sitting on a bench with his leg stretched out and the look of a man who had enjoyed a festival immensely and remembered none of it. The satchel was gone — dropped, per plan, beneath the loose board behind the fountain that generations of Vell children used for lost marbles and love notes. By morning it would be back in her pantry, having traveled there in the bottom of Odile's school satchel between a spelling book and a heel of bread.

"Well," Ivo said quietly, not looking at her. "That was twenty years of practice I just watched."

"Fifteen," Marta said. "Don't flatter me."

"I'm not." His voice stayed low, pitched under the fiddle. "You put yourself in the light. Deliberately. You know what that costs, if it goes wrong."

"It didn't go wrong."

"It didn't." He was quiet a moment. The drum picked up; somewhere a girl shrieked with delight. "Wren Halloway," Ivo said.

Marta's whole body went still, the stillness of a clock with the pendulum pinned. "Who told you that name?"

"Nobody told me anything. I've known you nine years, Marta. I knew there was a name you didn't say. I didn't need to know it." He looked at her then, his seamed old face lit orange from the brazier. "What I'm saying is: tonight I watched you spend that old debt instead of spending the girl. Or me. Or Odile. So." He shrugged, elaborately casual. "Consider the ledger marked."

She should have said something. She knew it even as the silence stretched. But some doors open inward, onto rooms a body isn't ready to reenter, and so she only said, "The third man. Did you see him?"

"The one walking idly? Saw him change course when you sang your song. Went north toward the garrison lane like his supper depended on it." Ivo smiled thinly. "We learned something tonight, whether we meant to or not."

"What's that?"

"He learns fast too." Ivo tapped his knee. "Corven's got fair-watchers out. Which means he's not hunting smugglers. He's hunting *us*. He doesn't know our faces yet — but he knows there are faces to find."

* * *

They regrouped after midnight, when the fires had burned to red eyes and the square belonged to the sweeping crews and a few stubborn couples reluctant to let the evening die. Upstairs in the back room, with the shutter latched and the lamp turned low, Marta poured four cups of small beer and set one in front of each of them, including Odile, who was technically too young by a year and had the sense not to mention it.

Ansel was the last to speak. She had been quiet since they'd come in, quieter even than usual, turning her cup slowly on the table as though calibrating something.

"The man by the sugar booth," she said at last. "The one Marta — accosted. I saw him before you did. From the ornament stall." She looked up. "He wasn't watching the crowd. I thought he was, at first. But I checked him three times in the brass, and every time, he was looking at the tower."

The room changed temperature. Marta felt it on her skin the way she felt weather in her hip.

"The observatory," Ivo said slowly.

"The tower door," Ansel said. "He looked at it the way I look at a movement through the case glass. Not suspicious. *Assessing.* Like a man measuring how long a job would take." She turned the cup another quarter. "Brannock sealed the tower two weeks ago. For safety, he says. And now Corven puts assessors in the fair. He knows the ledgers are inside, or he suspects, and either way he's done watching and begun planning."

"Planning what?" Marta asked, though part of her already knew, the way you know a storm is coming by the way birds go silent.

"Fire," Ansel said simply. "Thaw comes early this year, Ivo says. When the bridge opens, the archives burn, and every trace of what my father died for burns with it. The fair was our rehearsal." She looked around the table — at Ivo with his folded map, at Odile with her slate of signals, at Marta — and Marta saw something settle in the girl's face, some final grain of decision dropping into place like a pallet of stones. "We're out of season for rehearsing. Whatever we mean to do, we had best begin doing."

Odile raised her hand, waited — she always waited, courteous as a judge — and when they looked at her, she signed rapidly, and Ansel translated softly, voice barely above the lamp-hiss: *She says the third man, the idle walker — she saw where he went. He didn't go to the garrison. He went to the tollhouse on the east road, and he spoke to the guard there, and the guard wrote something down.*

"Tollhouse," Marta repeated. Checkpoint one. Their first door.

Ansel nodded slowly. "Then that's where we start."

Outside, the last lanterns in the square guttered and went out one by one, and the wind came down off the mountain carrying the first thin taste of a thaw that would bring them all either freedom or fire.
