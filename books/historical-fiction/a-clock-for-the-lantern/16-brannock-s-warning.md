## 16. Brannock's Warning

Head Warden Tomas Brannock had kept the observatory for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned that the mountain spoke first in small things. A door left unlatched. A lamp burning in the wrong window. Snow on the tower stair disturbed in a pattern no wind could account for. The mountain had spoken to him all winter in this small grammar, and he had listened the way an old man listens — reluctantly, then completely.

He knew who she was by the second week of December.

It was not her face that betrayed her. She bound herself well, walked with her shoulders squared and her chin tucked like any apprentice trying not to be noticed, and she had a clockmaker's hands — careful, ink-stained at the nails, steady even when they should have shaken. No, it was nothing so obvious. It was the way she wound the strike train: three turns counterclockwise, thumb over the crank, the little hitch of the wrist at the end, as if setting the mechanism down gently rather than simply letting go of it. Brannock had seen that gesture once before, years ago, on a scaffold platform outside the Vell garrison wall, performed by a man with rope around his throat who had asked — of all things — for someone to note the time of his death precisely, because *time was the only honest witness left*.

Tomás Quintana. The Lantern Clockmaker. The organizer who had come down from the pits with his arithmetic and his pamphlets and his terrible, gentle certainty that the men who died in Corven's tunnels deserved more than a number chalked on a slate. Brannock had stood warden at that execution because someone had to hold the keys, and holding keys was all Brannock had ever been good for.

The apprentice who wound the strike train like Tomás Quintana had been maybe nine years old when the man hanged. Brannock had done the sum on his fingers one night in the warden's room and come up with the same answer every time: a girl of nine watching her father die, then growing into a woman who dressed as a boy and carried her dead father's hands around like borrowed tools.

He did not report it.

That was the thing that surprised him most, in the long dark weeks of January. Every morning he woke in the warden's cot beneath the east wing and thought, *today I will write to Corven*, and every morning he did not. He told himself it was prudence. A warden did not put his name to a letter that might be wrong; a warden verified. He watched her instead, and the watching became its own occupation, its own quiet treason. He watched her eat bread in the corner of the tower landing with the unconscious economy of someone used to less. He watched her talk to the astronomer's girl through the telescope-room glass, her lips moving in exaggerated shapes, patient as a saint. He watched her stand inside the great clock's open case with her arms buried to the elbow in the escapement works, and he thought: *she looks like a woman reaching into her own chest.*

And he watched, with growing dread, the ledgers.

He had known about the compartment since before she had. That was the shame of it — the real shame, if he was going to be honest with himself, which in the small hours he sometimes forced himself to be. Brannock had found the false back behind the third-season maintenance panel fifteen years ago, when he first took wardenship, and he had looked inside, and he had read enough to understand what it was, and he had closed it again and told no one, including the magistrate who had ordered the tower searched twice in his tenure. Because Brannock was not a brave man, but neither was he entirely a rotten one, and the space between those two conditions was exactly wide enough for a hidden panel and a lifetime of looking away.

Now the Quintana girl had opened it, and the looking-away was coming due like a debt at the tollhouse.

* * *

He chose the evening carefully. Thursday, the twenty-sixth of January, when the astronomer kept the far dome until midnight and Odile Ferre was at the inn helping Marta Sable pickle whatever Marta had bought too much of at the fair stalls. The girl worked late Thursdays; he knew her schedule better than he admitted to himself. Snow had stopped at dusk and the air had gone still and enormous, the kind of cold that made sound carry for a mile — the creak of the tower, the tick of the regulator in the entry hall, his own boots on the stone like small gunshots.

She was at the workbench on the second landing, soldering something delicate, her cap pushed back off her forehead because there was no one to see. When his boot hit the landing she turned, and her hand — quick as anything he'd ever seen — slid the soldering iron aside and dropped a rag over the bench, and only then did she look up with the flat, polite blankness of a boy addressing his superior.

"Warden." Her voice was pitched low. She had it down to an art by now. "Something wrong with the regulator?"

"There's nothing wrong with the regulator, Mr. Quill." He let the false name sit between them on the bench like a third object. "Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit down anyway."

She sat. Not quickly, not obediently — she lowered herself onto the stool the way a cat sits, keeping her feet under her, and Brannock felt a flicker of something that was almost admiration. Tomás would have argued with his last breath, but this one argued sitting down, conserving herself. Mountain people. Coal-country blood.

"You've done good work on the train," he said. "Better than good. The third wheel alone — I haven't seen teeth cut like that since—" He stopped himself, but it was too late; he saw her hear the end of the sentence before he swallowed it.

"Since," she said evenly.

"Since your father."

The lamp between them hissed. Somewhere below them, through two floors of stone, the master clock ticked on, indifferent, the loudest silence in Vell.

Brannock pulled the second stool from against the wall and sat across from her, knees nearly touching, an old man's posture, hands loose between them where she could see they held nothing. "I stood at the execution," he said. "Ninth of October, eighteen and eighty-one. Cold day like this one. Your father asked the time to be noted and I noted it. Ten minutes past six by the garrison clock. I wrote it in the log myself." He heard his own voice go rough and was mildly ashamed of it. "I've written a lot of things in logs. Most of them true. Some of them true in a way that wasn't the whole truth, which is a kind of lying that wears a uniform."

The girl said nothing. But she had gone very still, the stillness of someone listening with everything, and Brannock understood suddenly that she had spent her whole life being listened to wrongly — suspected, dismissed, overlooked — and that his talking at all was already a strange gift she didn't trust.

"Quintana," he said quietly. "Your name is Quintana. Anselma, if I remember the records right. Records I shouldn't have remembered right."

For a moment he thought she would deny it. He watched her assemble the denial — he could see the pieces of it, the confused frown, the offended apprentice, the *I don't know what you mean, Warden* — and then, remarkably, he watched her set the pieces down unfinished. She took off her cap instead. Dark hair came loose, tied back plainly, no ornament, a workingwoman's hair, and her face without the cap was just a young woman's face, tired and sharp-boned and older than its years.

"How long have you known?" she asked, and her voice had shifted registers too — a half-tone higher, unhidden.

"Mid-December. You wind the strike train the way he did. Thumb over the crank. Nobody does that. It's not taught anymore; it's inherited."

She almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile — grief wearing a workman's apron. "He never taught me. I just watched."

"That's how inheritance works in this country," Brannock said. "We watch. Then we do the thing that got our fathers killed, and we're surprised when it goes badly."

"It hasn't gone badly yet."

"It will." He leaned forward. Outside the tall landing window, the valley lay blue-white under the moon, the frozen river a dull silver seam, and beyond it, invisible in the dark, the sealed bridge, the tollhouse with its lantern, the garrison gate with its two guards, all the small iron teeth of Corven's machine. "Listen to me, girl, because I'm going to say this once and I'm not going to be able to say it again. I know about the compartment. I've known about the compartment longer than you've been alive. And I know what you took out of it, or part of what you took out of it, because I have eyes and the tower is my tower. Ledgers. Payments. Names."

Her face did not change, but everything under her face changed. He had seen soldiers do the same thing — take the hit without letting it show — and he hated that he was the cause of it.

"If you're going to arrest me," she said, "you'll want help. I don't come quietly."

"I'm not arresting you." He said it flatly, wearily. "God help me, I'm not arresting you. I'm doing the opposite, and the opposite is worse, so pay attention."

* * *

He laid it out the way he laid out patrol schedules, in the plainest language he owned, because plainness was all he had and he knew it.

Corven was moving. The fire at the records office — officially a chimney fault, officially — had burned certain papers that were supposed to burn and, inconveniently, had drawn attention to papers that were not. The magistrate had spent eleven years believing the ledgers were ash or myth; now he believed neither, and a man like Corven, offered a choice between ash and myth, chose search parties. Within the month — sooner, if the thaw came early — there would be men in this tower with crowbars, and they would find the compartment whether it held the ledgers or not, and they would find the apprentice who had been living inside their discovery all winter like a moth in a coat pocket.

"They'll hang you," Brannock said. "Not for theft. For the name. A Quintana in Vell with evidence in her hands — Corven can't let that breathe. He hanged your father on manufactured testimony and he's spent eleven years paying the witnesses quiet money to stay quiet, and if those pages ever see daylight, everything he is comes down. Men don't let themselves be pulled down. They pull the trigger, or they sign the order, or they hire the man who does. You know this. Your whole life is built on knowing this."

"So this is a warning." Her eyes were narrow. "From the warden who keeps Corven's tower."

"From the warden who has kept his mouth shut for eleven years about a compartment he should have reported and a magistrate he should have defied, and who is tired in places sleep doesn't reach." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he took them away he made himself look at her directly, because she deserved that much and because it cost him, and cost was the only honesty he had left to spend. "Here's the offer. It's the only offer I can make and I'll make it once. Leave the ledgers. All of them. Put them back, or give them to me and I'll see them lost properly this time. Walk out."

"Walk out where? The bridge—"

"The bridge is watched, but it's watched for cargo and strangers, not for one mender going home. I can sign a travel pass. Warden's authority, weather-related dismissal of contract — the clock's restored enough to run, the apprentice's work is done, snow closing the passes, all perfectly ordinary. By Friday you're through the tollhouse on my paper. By Sunday you're over the ridge at Halden, and from Halden there are coaches south, and south is big. South swallows people. It swallowed you once already — you wore a dead boy's name for years and it worked." He spread his hands. "Leave the ledgers, leave Vell, leave the name, and I will look at the ceiling for the rest of my life the way I always have. Nobody follows you. I'll swear the tower was empty when I sealed it. That's the offer, Anselma Quintana. Life. Quietly. Elsewhere."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Below them the clock ticked. Above them, through the dome's ribs, he could hear the faint whistle of wind finding the ventilator slits — the astronomer's music, Odile called it, according to the gossip that reached even the warden's cot.

The girl stood up. She crossed to the window and stood looking down at the square, at the Lantern Inn with its lit windows, and Brannock watched her look and understood, with a sensation like a stair missing in the dark, that he had already lost.

Because he knew what was in that square. He knew about the innkeeper — everyone old enough to remember the border routes knew about Marta Sable, whatever respectable fiction she'd wrapped around herself. He knew the astronomer's daughter climbed the pendulum shaft like a squirrel and read lips through glass. He knew Elias Corven had signed a supply requisition last week with a steadier hand than usual, the steadiness of a man who has decided something. He was warden of this tower; the tower heard everything.

"You're asking me," she said finally, without turning around, "to leave the only proof my father was innocent."

"I'm asking you to live."

"He lived," she said. "Look where living got him. Ten minutes past six, noted in the log." Now she turned, and her face in the lamplight was calm in a way that frightened him more than any fury would have, because calm like that was load-bearing; it was holding up something enormous. "Those ledgers have four hundred names in them, Warden. Miners who paid into the welfare fund and got nothing when the roofs came down. Widows. Witnesses who were paid to lie and have been bleeding quiet money ever since, hating themselves. My father's name — not the name on the charge sheet, the real one, the one that was true. You want me to put that back behind a panel and take a coach south and be swallowed?"

"Yes," Brannock said. "Because the alternative is a shallow grave above the tree line, and I'm too old to dig one and too honest to let someone else dig it while I look at the ceiling."

"And if I stay?"

"Then I can't protect you. Do you understand that? Not won't. *Can't.* If Corven comes to this tower and finds you here, there is nothing a warden with nineteen years of service and no family and no leverage can do but stand in a doorway, and Corven's men move warden-shaped obstacles aside without breaking stride." His voice cracked on the last of it and he let it crack; let her see the old man underneath the uniform, the one who had stood at a scaffold and written *ten past six* in a neat hand and gone home and been sick behind the garrison woodshed. "I watched your father die because I held the keys and holding keys is what I do. I am offering you the only thing I have ever successfully given anyone: a door, unlocked, and my eyes elsewhere."

She looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her face — not softening, exactly. Assessment. Weighing him the way she'd weigh a movement in the escapement, deciding whether he ran true.

"Why?" she asked. "Eleven years of looking at the ceiling. Why spoil the habit now?"

And Brannock, who had rehearsed a dozen answers on the walk up — duty, conscience, the memory of Tomás's face — found that none of them survived the trip out of his mouth, and what came out instead was the truth, small and shabby and all he had:

"Because you wind the strike train the way he did. And every night for two months I've stood under that clock listening to it run, and for the first time since October of eighty-one it sounds like him instead of like the drop of the floor." He got up heavily. His knees had opinions about the cold and about confessions. "Take the pass or don't. It's signed and it's in the left drawer of my desk in the warden's room, and it's good until the thaw. After the thaw I have to file my winter reports, and my reports are honest, Miss Quintana. They've always been honest in exactly the way that isn't the whole truth, and I can't stretch that particular kind of lying much further than February."

At the head of the stairs he paused, one hand on the rail, and did not turn around, because turning around would have meant seeing whatever she looked like now, and he had reached his allowance of other people's faces for one night.

"One more thing," he said. "The old miner. Ivo Brandt. He's been up to the tower twice this month on pretext of admiring the works, and he watches the tollhouse from the chapel yard on Sundays like a man counting sentries. I don't know what you're planning. I don't want to know. But whatever it is — whatever arithmetic you're doing with that old man's maps and Sable's routes and the Corven boy's seal — do it knowing the thaw is eleven weeks out on my calendar and probably eight on Corven's. And know that when it comes, this tower gets searched, and I will not be able to look at the ceiling forever."

He went down the stairs. At the bottom, in the entry hall where the regulator ticked its faithful civilian tick, he stopped once more and spoke to the empty room, or to the tower, or to the man who had died at ten past six with his arithmetic intact.

"Ten past six," he said. "Somebody ought to have said the time out loud to somebody who loved him. That's all I ever owed. That's all anybody owes."

Then Head Warden Tomas Brannock buttoned his coat, stepped out into the enormous cold, and began the long walk down through the snow toward the town, where the lights of the Lantern Inn burned yellow in the square, and where — though he could not yet see it from the observatory road, though it was still folded away in a drawer and a decision and a girl's stubborn heart — the thirteenth stroke was already winding itself into the future, one careful revolution at a time.
