## 17. What Justice Weighs

Ivo Harn had not slept a whole night through in eleven years. He did not expect to sleep this one either, but the cold had a way of making an old man honest, and honesty kept him awake anyway, so he climbed the back stair of the Lantern Inn with two mugs of Marta's black tea and let himself into the little room under the eaves where the clockmaker's apprentice sat hunched over a table strewn with brass.

The room smelled of oil and lamp smoke and something fainter beneath it — camphor, maybe, or the memory of a woman's soap worn by someone who had sworn off being a woman in public. Ansel — *Ansel*, he thought the name carefully now, like a coin he wasn't supposed to spend — looked up when the door opened, and her hand moved instinctively across the papers before she saw who it was.

"Only me," Ivo said. "Old men knock loud enough for the deaf and quiet enough for the magistrate, and still nobody's glad to see us."

She relaxed by degrees. That was how everything happened with her — by degrees, like frost forming, like thaw. "I couldn't sleep."

"Neither could I." He set down the mugs. "But you're not sleeping because you've got ledgers on your table and a dead man's name in your chest. I'm not sleeping because I buried everyone I ever drank with. Between us we ought to manage one whole night."

That earned him half a smile. She took the tea. Outside, the wind came down off the shoulder of Vell Fell and pressed against the shutters the way winter always did here — patient, certain of its victory.

Ivo dragged the second stool to the table and lowered himself onto it with the sound old joints make, which is very like the sound a clock makes when it wants oiling. The ledgers lay between them, wrapped now in oilcloth against damp, tied with cord. Three of them. He had held one only once, briefly, and his hands had shaken so badly Ansel had taken it back with a gentleness that shamed him more than fear would have.

"You've been looking at them all night," he said.

"I've been looking at the arithmetic all night." She turned the top ledger toward him, careful as a nurse turning a patient. "Look at the margins. Corven initialed every disbursement. Every payment to every witness who swore my father bought violence instead of preaching against it. Eleven names. Eleven men paid between two and fourteen crowns apiece to remember things that never happened." Her voice stayed level. That was the frightening thing about her, Ivo thought. The level voice over deep water. "Fourteen crowns, Ivo. My father's life weighed less than a good coat."

"Aye." He drank his tea because his hands wanted occupation. "And now you've got the scales in your lap, and you don't know what to weigh."

She looked up sharply. "You read minds now?"

"I was twenty-two when the strike came," Ivo said. "I've watched a lot of people hold evidence and pretend they were holding a plan. You've got the same look. So let's say what neither of us has said out loud yet." He set the mug down. "There are two roads from this table, girl. One goes through the courts at Greyharbour. The other goes through the dark. And you've been walking both in your head all night without letting either foot touch ground."

* * *

The lamp guttered and steadied. Ansel pulled the oilcloth fully back from the second ledger, and the smell of old paper rose up, dry and sweet, like the inside of a coffin.

"Tell me about the courts," she said. "Not what they're supposed to be. What they are."

Ivo had expected the question and dreaded it, because the answer was long and the telling of it opened doors he kept boarded. But she had asked, and there was a kind of mercy in being asked by someone who already knew the worst of it.

"The assizes sit twice a year at Greyharbour," he said. "Spring and autumn. A crown judge comes up on the packet boat, or did, before the bridge. He lodges at the magistrate's house because there's nowhere else fit, and he eats Corven's mutton and drinks Corven's wine, and then he hears cases in a courtroom Corven's grandfather built." He ticked it off on his fingers, an inventory of futility. "The clerk of the court is appointed by the magistrate. The constables are paid from funds the magistrate administers. The mine owners sit on the bench beside him as justices of the peace. And the jury list is drawn from the rate book — which means men who own property, which in Vell means men who own shares, or men who owe Corven their licenses and their leases." He spread his empty hands. "I testified once. At the first inquiry, after the pit disaster. Told the truth about the timbering, about the props not replaced. Corven thanked me for my testimony, called it valuable, and the inquiry found no fault attributable to any party. No fault. Forty-one men in the ground and no fault anywhere, like the mountain swallowed them out of spite."

Ansel had gone very still. It was the stillness of a person listening with her whole body.

"And my father's trial?"

"I wasn't allowed in. The gallery was cleared — they said there'd been threats." He met her eyes. "There were no threats. But your father filled a hall wherever he spoke, so they invented a danger and emptied the room. Two days, they said the trial lasted. The witnesses — the ones in those ledgers — swore he'd called for the burning of the tollhouse and the beating of the overseers. Men who'd been paid. Men whose receipts are sitting on this table under your hand."

"My mother went to Greyharbour afterward," Ansel said quietly. "To petition. She was told the case was concluded and further agitation would be considered sedition. She died that winter. I always thought it was the coal smoke in her lungs. Now I think part of it was that word — *concluded*." She touched the ledger's edge. "So the road through Greyharbour runs through Corven's own kitchen."

"It does."

"Then why am I hesitating?" Her voice cracked at last, just once, like ice underfoot. "Why have I spent three nights building arguments for a road I already know leads nowhere?"

Ivo leaned back. The shutter rattled; somewhere below, Marta's regulator clock ticked with the particular authority of a well-kept mechanism. He had thought a great deal about this conversation while lying awake, and he had learned something in eleven years of thinking: that the questions you ask yourself at three in the morning are the ones you must eventually ask out loud, or they'll ask you instead.

"Because the other road frightens you more than injustice does," he said. "And it should."

* * *

She stood then and went to the window, pushing the curtain aside a finger's width. Below, the square lay blue under snow-light, the observatory tower rising above the rooftops with its blind face, its stopped clock. Somewhere down there a sentry walked the garrison wall, and beyond the town the frozen river waited, and beyond that the sealed bridge, and beyond that a world that had never heard of Tomás Quintana except as a cautionary tale told to keep miners obedient.

"If we take it to the courts," she said, speaking to the glass, "we need the original ledgers delivered intact to someone outside Corven's reach. A judge, a newspaper, a member of parliament — anyone. Three checkpoints between here and the low country. Brannock watching the tower. Corven burning the archives at thaw — you've heard the same rumors I have. If anything fails, the evidence burns and I hang, and my father's name stays dirty forever." She let the curtain fall. "If we don't go through the courts—"

"Then what?" Ivo asked gently. "Say it."

"A broadsheet. Printed here. Five hundred copies nailed to every door in Vell before dawn, with the ledger pages copied out and the names of the paid witnesses. Let the town try him. Let the miners decide what to do with him." She turned. Her face in the lamplight was young and old at once. "Or worse. I've thought about worse. Don't look at me like that — you've thought about worse too. A man with a grudge and a length of rope doesn't need a printing press."

"No," Ivo agreed. "He doesn't."

"But you're going to tell me why I'm wrong."

He was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind shifted and the inn creaked around them like a ship. He thought about how to say what needed saying, because there were two truths in him and they fought each other, and a man owed it to the young to admit when his truths were at war.

"When your father hung," Ivo began, "I stood in the crowd. Third row. I'd promised myself I'd look away, and I didn't. I watched the whole thing, and do you know what I remember most? Not the drop. Not his face. I remember the silence after — how four hundred people stood in a square exactly like ours and let it happen and said nothing, because each one of them thought the law must know what it was doing even when the law was strangling the best man in the valley. That silence broke something in me, girl. I've been living in the broken place ever since." He rubbed his jaw. "So understand me: I want Corven ruined. I want it the way a starving man wants bread. If you put a pistol in my hand tonight and pointed me at his door, God help me, I might use it."

"Then—"

"Let me finish. Because wanting it isn't the question." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's what eleven years taught me about the broken place. If we nail those broadsheets to doors, some good comes of it — truth spoken, shame dealt. But shame wears off. And what lasts? The men who took Corven's money — most of them took it hungry, scared, some of them grieving sons of the disaster themselves. A broadsheet makes them liars forever in the town's eyes. Some of them deserve it. Their children don't. And the garrison — forty men, most of them miners' sons — a mob in the square, even a righteous one, ends with soldiers facing their own fathers, and somebody fires, and then we've got dead boys in the snow and Corven tried in absentia by nobody and the mines closed for a season and widows again. I've seen it. Not here. Elsewhere, in '48, when I was running goods through the low passes. Towns that took justice into their own streets always buried more of their own than of their tyrants."

Ansel sat down slowly. "And if we do nothing but wait for a court that will never convict him?"

"That's the trap, isn't it." Ivo smiled without humor. "Both roads cost. The court costs certainty. The street costs blood. And there's a third thing neither of us has said." He tapped the ledger. "This paper doesn't convict anyone by itself. Paper needs a forum. A court can be corrupted, yes — but a mob can't deliberate. Somewhere between them there has to be a place where four hundred people stand in a square and *choose*, awake, with their eyes open, the way they stood and chose silence when your father died. We don't need to force justice on Vell. We need to make Vell stop being silent. That's different from a riot, and it's different from a petition."

"How?" she asked. "Practically. How do you make a town choose?"

Ivo looked at her for a long moment — at the clockmaker's apprentice with grease still under her nails and a hanged man's stubbornness in her spine — and felt the strange double vision that had haunted him since the night she'd first spoken to him of escapements: that Tomás Quintana stood before him again, younger, wearier, carrying her father's fire inside a body the world refused to see.

"You're asking the wrong man how," he said. "But I'll tell you what your father knew, because he told me once, the week before they took him. He said: *the powerful don't fear anger, Ivo — anger is private, and private things can be waited out. They fear witness. One witness can be dismissed. A hundred witnesses must be negotiated with. A thousand witnesses are a new law.*" He spread his hands. "He believed in the square. Not in the mob — in the square. In people assembled and seeing. That's why they killed him in one."

* * *

Silence held the little room for a while. The tea went cold. Downstairs, faintly, Marta moved through her closing rituals — bolt, banked fire, glass wiped — the small liturgies of an innkeeper, and Ivo found comfort in them the way he found comfort in ticking clocks: proof that some order held, somewhere, even if only of teacups.

"There's a middle road," Ansel said at last. "Isn't there. There always is, and it's always narrower than either side claims."

"Tell me."

"Evidence first. Before any court or any crowd, the copies go out — past the checkpoints, to Greyharbour, to a lawyer or a paper, safe from fire. That's insurance; whatever happens in Vell, the truth survives us." She counted it on her fingers, and Ivo recognized the gesture — a mechanic enumerating parts. "Second, the town. Not a broadsheet dropped in the night like a thief's work. Something public. Something they gather for willingly, where the reading happens in front of them, where Corven stands in the square with the pages falling around him and cannot call it sedition because he'll be standing in the middle of it. Make the accusation in daylight, in front of four hundred witnesses who can't unsee it. Then — only then — the magistrates at Greyharbour have a crowd behind the petition instead of a corpse behind it."

Ivo turned it over. It was, he had to admit, his old friend's daughter's plan — Tomás's faith in assembly welded to a smuggler's logistics. "It hinges on a gathering Corven can't disperse and can't refuse to attend."

"Yes." Her eyes gleamed suddenly. "And I might know one. But it's not built yet — the clock isn't running, and a ceremony for a dead clock gathers no one."

"A ceremony for a *restored* clock," Ivo said slowly, catching the shape of it, feeling dread and admiration arrive together like weather, "gathers the whole valley."

"That's tomorrow's problem." She gathered the ledgers back into their oilcloth, tying the cord with quick, precise motions. "Tonight I needed to know which road we're walking. Courts, crowds, or the dark."

"And have you decided?"

She looked at him — really looked, the way few people ever looked at Ivo Harn, as though an old smuggler might contain something worth finding — and said, "We walk toward the light with the darkness in our pocket. Evidence away first. Then the square. And if Corven forces the dark on us—" her hands went still on the cord "—then we decide that then. Together. Not me alone in a room at three in the morning."

"That," said Ivo, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, "is the first sensible thing anyone's decided in this town since the bridge closed."

He rose to go, joints protesting, and had his hand on the latch when she spoke again.

"Ivo. When my father stood in the square — did anyone speak? At the end? Did anyone say his name?"

He stood with the door half open, the cold coming in, and told her the truth because she had earned it. "The hangman read the sentence. Nobody else made a sound." He paused. "Which means nobody's ever taken it back. There's a debt outstanding in this valley, girl, eleven years unpaid, and I think you've come to collect it."

Below, in the square, the watch bell rang the half hour — and then, confused, rang once more, thirteen strokes rolling across the snow, and both of them went cold in a way the wind had nothing to do with.

Ansel was already pulling on her coat. "That's not possible," she whispered. "I haven't touched the striking train yet."
