## 18. The Copy

Odile had always understood the world through her hands.

Sound came to her secondhand, filtered through vibration and sight, translated by other people's mouths into shapes she had to guess at. But texture — texture never lied. The grain of paper, the resistance of a stylus, the small irregular geography of a page that had been handled too often: these spoke plainly, without ambiguity, without mercy. It was her mother the astronomer who had first taught her that instruments were honest in a way people were not, and Odile had built her whole life inside that honesty — star charts she could read by touch, lenses she could grade by the weight of light on her palms, and now, spread across the worktable of the observatory's lower library, three ledgers full of lies that smelled of lamp oil and old greed.

She could not read them the way Ansel did. Ansel's eyes moved down the columns of figures with the fluency of a woman reading letters from home; Odile saw only ink, dense and gray, a language locked behind glass. So they had built a bridge between them, the way they built everything: patiently, with the tools at hand.

Ansel read aloud — no, that was wrong, Ansel did not read aloud; Ansel *spelled*, her fingers moving in the alphabet Odile had taught her over weeks of evenings, clumsy at first, then sure. The clockmaker's apprentice had hands made for this work, hands trained to feel the smallest misalignment in a gear train, and it showed in how quickly her fingers learned to speak. *Corven*, she spelled, tapping the ledger's margin. *Paid. Fourteen pounds. To Wray, the foreman.* And then, because there was no sign for what that meant, because the alphabet had no room for the taste of it, Ansel would simply stop, and look up, and let Odile see her face.

Odile did not need the words. She had learned long ago that faces carried more truth than mouths ever managed, that the throat worked overtime trying to bury what the brow announced. Ansel's face said: *my father's name is in these pages*. Her face had been saying it for four days now, in every variation grief allowed.

Odile smoothed the sheet of heavy paper before her and set the slate-frame — Marta had found it in an attic, a schoolroom relic with six rows of cells — and began.

Braille was slow. That was its virtue. Each letter demanded a decision, each cell a small act of will; there was no racing through, no skimming past a name that hurt to write. Six dots at a time, pressed deep enough to read with a fingertip in the dark, or by candlelight, or in a cellar while soldiers' boots crossed the floor above. An original could be seized. A copy pressed into paper's flesh could be torn apart, scattered, memorized, rebuilt. Ansel had explained it in fingerspelling and diagrams: the ledgers themselves must stay where Corven expected to find them, bait in the escapement chamber, so that when the moment came he would reach for them and find the town already watching. But if the bait burned — and Corven burned things, Odile had gathered; it was in his history the way frost was in January — then something true had to survive somewhere he could not put his hands.

Her body was that somewhere. That was the plan, anyway. Odile ran her thumb over the first finished page and thought: *they can search a tower. They cannot search my skin.*

* * *

Marta Sable arrived at dusk with bread, cheese, and the particular silence of a woman who had decided not to ask questions in front of doors with ears.

The innkeeper moved through the observatory's lower rooms as though she owned them, which, Odile suspected, in some informal and historical sense she very nearly did. She set the food down without ceremony, banked the fire, checked the shutter latches with two fingers — a smuggler's habit, checking exits even in a friend's house — and only then turned to the table and all its careful industry.

Her eyebrows rose. One hand came up, and she signed, in the rough pidgin of gestures the three of them had assembled over the winter — half Odile's invented signs, half fingerspelling, half pure theater — *What is this?*

"Insurance," Ansel said aloud, and mouthed it after, because Ansel had taken to voicing her own words for Odile's benefit even when Odile could have caught them from her lips alone. It was a courtesy Odile had not asked for and could not repay and treasured more than she knew how to say.

Marta looked at the frame, the punch, the growing stack of pages, and Odile watched understanding arrive in her like weather — the slight settling of the shoulders, the jaw tightening not in alarm but in arithmetic. Marta Sable was always doing sums. Odile had learned to watch for it: the flicker of calculation behind the warmth, the innkeeper weighing every kindness against its cost in risk. This time the sum took longer than usual. Then Marta nodded once, pulled up the fourth stool — kept there now, though no one commented on it; stools simply migrated toward tables where people gathered — and sat.

*Show me,* she signed.

So Odile showed her. She took Marta's hand — asking first, palm open, because you did not seize an innkeeper's hands any more than you seized a cat's — and laid the woman's fingertips against the raised dots. Marta went very still. Odile guided her touch along the first line, then the second, and felt the exact moment comprehension landed, a small change of pressure in the hand beneath hers, the stillness of a woman realizing that a blind man in a dark cellar, or a deaf girl in a burning archive, or anyone with ten minutes and a fingertip could carry a magistrate's crimes out through a checkpoint in plain view of his guards.

When Odile let go, Marta flexed her fingers, looked at her own hand as if it had grown a new knuckle, and laughed — a short, silent laugh, all shoulders, and Odile caught the shape of it and loved it.

Then Marta's face changed. She leaned forward, tapped the ledgers, tapped her own chest, and pointed out the window, toward the town, toward the tollhouse road, and mimed — with a runner's economy, no wasted motion — a satchel, a search, hands turning out pockets.

*If we are stopped,* the gestures said, *the copies cannot look like copies.*

It was Odile who solved it, later that night, and she would remember afterward that the answer had come not from cleverness but from boredom — from the hour when the fire burned low and her fingers ached and her mind wandered to the cipher games her father played with his star catalogs, encoding observations so that rival observatories couldn't poach them. Numbers. Everything was numbers. The ledgers were numbers; braille was numbers, six dots, sixty-four combinations; her father's charts were numbers arranged to look like nothing at all.

She pulled a fresh sheet and began again, transcribing names into strings of figures that might be debts, might be measurements, might be a dressmaker's accounts — dates recast as temperatures, sums recast as star magnitudes, each name reduced to a two-digit key recorded nowhere but inside her own skull, which was, she reflected, the one vault in Vell no magistrate had ever thought to lock.

Ansel watched her work for a long moment. Then the apprentice took the pencil from her gently, wrote on the margin of a scrap: **You are terrifying**, and underlined it twice.

Odile signed back the only reply that seemed adequate: *My father's daughter.*

* * *

The nights developed a shape, and Odile came to love the shape the way she loved the shape of tides.

Ansel came up from the tower after the day's work on the clock, hands smelling of oil and brass, and they ate whatever Marta had smuggled up in a basket — the basket itself a masterpiece of innocent construction, false-bottomed, Odile had discovered, capable of carrying bread above and pages below — and then they worked. Ansel spelled; Odile punched. Sometimes Ivo came, when his cough allowed and the watch rotations permitted, and sat with his ruined hands folded, listening in his way to the small percussion of the stylus, tapping his knee in rhythm like a man keeping time to music he remembered. Sometimes Elias came, briefly, guiltily, bringing paper of better quality than theirs and leaving before anyone could be seen knowing him. He never stayed long. He stood near the door the way a man stands near a door he may need, and he looked at the ledgers with an expression Odile could not fully parse — hunger and nausea braided together — and once, only once, he reached out and touched the column where his father's signature recurred like a stain, and then he left early, and Ansel spelled to Odile, *He is paying a debt we did not know he owed*, and Odile nodded, because she had seen it too.

But most nights it was the two of them, and the fire, and the work.

It was during those hours that Odile taught her the rest of the alphabet.

Not the finger-spelling — Ansel had that cold by midwinter, could spell faster than some people talked — but the older language, the one Odile and her father had built between themselves across years of dinner tables and telescope rooms: the shorthand of gesture that has no grammar and needs none. *Careful*, made with two fingers walking slowly. *Liar*, a flat hand passing over the mouth. *Father* — that one Odile had never had to invent; every deaf child invents it, the hand shaping itself around a word that means the person who listens differently than the world does. She taught Ansel *brother*, and watched Ansel practice it alone, later, when she thought Odile wasn't looking, the gesture repeated over and over like a prayer or a penance, and Odile pretended blindness, which among all people was a gift only she could give.

In return Ansel taught her the clock.

That was the trade neither of them had negotiated and both of them honored. Nightly, in the hour before sleep, Ansel would take her hand and press it to whatever mechanism lay open on the workbench — the model escapement, the striking-train wheels, the little brass anchor whose teeth caught and released, caught and released, the whole secret of measured time resting in that alternation. Odile felt the pallets, traced the escape wheel's teeth, understood at last — in her wrists, in her palms, where understanding lived — what Ansel could not easily say: that the great clock upstairs was not one machine but several, timekeeping and striking and chiming, each train asleep until its hour, and that Ansel was teaching them to lie. A clock that struck thirteen. A chamber wired to open at the impossible stroke. She felt the modified detent, the added lever, and her fingers asked the question her voice could not frame quickly enough — *is it safe, will it hold, what if it fails* — and Ansel answered by guiding her thumb along the double-checked spring, the redundancy, the place where failure would mean merely twelve strokes, merely silence, merely waiting for another night.

*Everything fails eventually*, Ansel spelled, and then, after a pause, *but you build so it fails toward morning, not night.*

Odile kept that sentence. She kept it the way she kept star coordinates — filed precisely, retrievable forever, in the dark.

* * *

On the ninth night, she made a mistake.

She was tired. The fire had burned to coals; the light was bad; and her stylus slipped, punching a dot one cell wide of its true position, corrupting the line — a payment record, a name, fourteen pounds — so that when she ran her verification pass, fingertip trailing backward through the raised points the way her father trailed a finger along a proof to catch an error, the name dissolved into nonsense.

A copy with a corrupted name was worse than no copy. A copy with a corrupted name was a weapon that fired sideways.

She sat back. Her eyes stung. Around her the library held its usual midnight stillness, the smell of cold stone and lamp oil, and above her, faint through floors of rock and timber, she felt rather than heard the great clock turn over into a new hour — a tremor in the floorboards, the building's slow pulse, the heartbeat Ansel had restored beat by beat.

And then Ansel was there, because Ansel noticed everything, had apparently been watching the verification fail, and did what she always did: reached past the mistake without comment, took the spoiled page, set it aside for the stove, and laid a fresh sheet in the frame. Then she held up her hands and spelled, unhurried, as if reporting a fact about gears:

*Errors cost an hour. Hiding them costs everything.*

Odile looked at her — at this woman who wore a dead boy's name like a coat, who had buried herself alive for seven years, who flinched at raised voices and could dismantle a striking train blindfolded — and felt the full strange weight of what the winter had built. They were alike, she and Ansel, though the world would never file them together. Both of them lived in translation. Both of them knew what it was to have the important things happen in a channel the people around them couldn't hear — Odile's silence, Ansel's name — and both of them had learned to make the unheard channel strong, a private telegraph running under the town's noise.

*Ivo says you were like this as a child*, Odile signed. *Obsessive. Precise.*

Ansel's laugh made no sound Odile needed; she read it whole. *Ivo is a liar and an old gossip. I was worse.*

They worked until the sky beyond the shutters went the color of iron filings. By the last candle, Odile had recovered the corrupted line and completed the page, and the stack of copies had passed the halfway mark — thirty-one leaves, each one readable by firelight or fingertips, each one a fuse waiting for a match.

* * *

It was near dawn, packing away the frame, that Odile felt it: a tremor in the floor different from the clock's pulse. Heavier. Rhythmic. Boots — many boots — but distant, over toward the town square, not the observatory road. She stood at the window a long time, watching, and what she saw made the cold come up through the floor and take her by the ankles.

Down in Vell, orange light bloomed against the black. Not lamplight — lamplight didn't leap. Fire. And she knew the direction the way she knew directions, by months of mapping lantern signals across that same square: the records office. Corven's records office, the town's official memory, burning three weeks ahead of thaw, three weeks ahead of any accident anyone could pretend to believe.

Behind her, she heard Ansel cross the room, and felt the apprentice's hand on her shoulder, and followed her gaze down to the fire.

So it begins, Odile thought, and her hands, steady on the sill, were already rehearsing tomorrow's work — the remaining pages, the cipher keys, the hours remaining measured now in a currency that had just been devalued. Somewhere below, the magistrate was watching his own bonfire, tying off the loose ends he knew about. He did not yet know about the ones he couldn't see: pressed into paper's flesh, folded into a basket's false bottom, and stored in the one vault in Vell he had never thought to lock.

Ansel's hand tightened on her shoulder. When Odile turned, the apprentice was spelling fast, her fingers urgent in the dying light:

*We run out of nights. Tomorrow — Ivo, Marta, everyone. We finish the copies, and then we move.*
