## 21. Sealed Tower, Open Night

The lock on the tower door was new brass, and Ansel hated it the way she hated all beautiful things made for ugly purposes.

She stood in the lee of the observatory's south wall with her collar up and her breath turning to frost on her scarf, and she looked at that lock for a long time. Brannock had ordered the tower sealed "for safety" — his word, delivered flat as a slate — and the locksmith had done honest work: a case-hardened body, a shackle thrown across the hasp, and beneath it all a secondary bar bolted through the oak of the door itself, so that even if the lock surrendered, the door would not. It was not the seal of a building being protected. It was the seal of a throat being held shut.

Behind her, in the blue dark between one streetlamp and the next, Ivo shifted his weight and said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. He had spent thirty years underground learning it.

"He means to keep them in there," Ansel said quietly. "The ledgers. Until thaw."

"I'd say he means to keep *you* out," Ivo answered, "but we both know better. A man doesn't lock a room unless he knows what's in it."

Snow came down without wind, the fat unhurried flakes of deep winter, collecting on their shoulders like a patient hand. Across the square the Lantern Inn showed its windows gold; somewhere behind those windows Odile was watching — Ansel could not see her, but she knew it the way she knew when a mainspring was wound too tight, some tension transmitted through the air itself. And somewhere beyond the inn, up the hill where the lamps burned fewer, Elias Corven sat at his father's table eating his father's food and copying names out of a dead man's ledger by candlelight.

*We are four people against a town,* she thought, not for the first time. *Four, and one of us is the magistrate's son.*

It did not bear thinking about directly. She had learned that much from clocks: you did not stare at the whole mechanism at once or you saw only chaos. You looked at one wheel. One tooth. You asked what that tooth must do, and then you found the next.

The tooth in front of her now was a locked door.

"The pendulum shaft," she said.

Ivo grunted. He'd expected it; she could hear the expectation in the sound. "You've been thinking about that shaft since the fair."

"I've been thinking about it since before the fair." She turned her back to the wall so her voice wouldn't carry even sideways. "Every tower clock hangs its pendulum in a shaft that runs down through the building — sometimes two, three stories — because a pendulum wants length and mass, and it wants to be free of the going train's vibration. The Vell master clock is a regulator of the old school. Her pendulum is eleven feet, maybe twelve, and the shaft descends the full height of the tower into the old weight cellar, which opens—" she paused, closing her eyes, walking the architecture the way she'd walked it a hundred times in her mind since Brannock turned his key— "into the coal chute passage. The one that comes up behind the woodshed on the north side. The warden's men sealed the door and the windows and never thought about the chimney of a building they don't understand."

"And inside the shaft?"

"Darkness. Iron rungs set into the wall, most of them older than the clock. Ice, probably, where condensation freezes near the top. And a pendulum swinging through the middle of everything, nine inches above the rungs at the bottom of its arc, moving fast enough to break an arm." She opened her eyes. "But it swings once every two seconds, Ivo. Two seconds is a very long time, if you know it's coming."

Ivo was quiet long enough that the snow filled the silence. Then he said, "In the mine, when the cage broke, we used to climb the guide ropes. In the dark, after the lamps went out." He flexed his ruined left hand, the one the rock had taken part of. "Some men can do that. Some men freeze halfway and you have to decide whether to knock them loose or let them hang there until morning, because either way they'll die but one way is quicker." He looked at her with his pale mine-light eyes. "Which are you?"

"I don't know," Ansel said honestly. "Nobody's ever dropped me far enough to find out."

That surprised a short laugh out of him, breath-smoke curling away. "Then let's hope tonight isn't the night we learn."

* * *

They gathered in the inn's back room at half past ten, when the last of the fair-week travelers had gone up to bed and Marta had turned the lamp down to its lowest wick. The room smelled of tallow and hops and the particular cold-of-warmth that old buildings have in winter, stone holding the day's heat hostage.

Marta Sable stood at the table with a coil of rope in front of her, and Ansel watched her hands while she talked, because Marta's hands told more truth than her face ever did. They were broad, scarred across the knuckles, quick — smuggler's hands, dock-runner's hands — and they moved over the rope now with an intimacy that needed no explanation.

"Hempen, three-strand, sixty feet, tarred against the damp," Marta said. "It came up the coast in a brandy cask in '81 and it hasn't failed me yet, which is more than I can say for most things." She fed the rope through a bowline with two economical movements. "Odile. Say it back."

Odile sat with her back straight and her eyes on Marta's mouth, reading every word off her lips the way other people read print. When Marta finished she signed her answer — quick, certain — and spoke aloud in her clear, careful voice, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Bowline around my hips. Clove hitch at the anchor point. Signal line for the lantern. If I pull twice, come down. If I pull three times, someone fell."

"And if someone falls," Marta said, "you do not go down after them. You hold. That's the whole religion of rope work, girl — the person on the anchor end matters more than the person on the free end, because the free end is already having a very exciting evening and needs no further help."

Ansel saw something flicker across Odile's face then — not hurt, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone being trusted with the heavy end of something for the first time, and finding it fit her hand.

"Why me?" Odile asked, and her voice went quieter. "Why am I at the bottom?"

Because you're the strongest of us, Ansel thought. Because your father taught you star-charts and your hands are steady as a transit instrument. Because you read glass and lamplight and lips, and darkness is only another kind of surface to read.

Marta said none of that. Marta said, "Because I'm too old to climb down a shaft and catch a falling clockmaker, and Ivo's hand won't close on a rope in this cold, and Elias—" she allowed herself one dry beat— "Elias will be standing at the garrison gate in his good coat making sure no one looks at the observatory, which is its own kind of rope work, though nobody ties him a bowline." She began coiling the line again, over and under, over and under. "And because you asked to be. Don't think I didn't notice."

Odile's chin came up. Under the low lamplight her face was all planes and patience, and Ansel thought, not for the first time, that there were worse fates than being seen by this girl.

* * *

The coal chute passage behind the woodshed smelled of forty years of ash and mice. Ansel went first with the shuttered lantern, counting paces — thirty-one to the bend, nineteen past the bend to the hatch — and when she lifted the hatch on its rusted hinge, the breath of the tower came down to meet them: cold iron, old oil, the mineral smell of stone that had been dark longer than anyone in Vell had been alive.

The shaft mouth opened at their feet like a well.

Even shuttered, the lantern showed almost nothing — the first rungs descending into black, the faint sheen of frost on iron, and below, below, the suggestion of depth the eye refused to finish. Somewhere down there, the pendulum swung in its slow metronome of two seconds, and Ansel realized she could hear it: a whisper of displaced air, *shh... shh...*, steady as breathing, as if the tower itself drew breath in the dark.

"Two seconds," Ivo murmured at her shoulder. "Say it till you believe it."

"I believe it already."

"Believe it harder. Fear makes people forget arithmetic."

Marta anchored the bowline-end to the great timber that framed the hatch — tested it with her whole weight, twice — and then it was Odile who went down first after all, because the plan required it: the anchor point at the bottom had to be set by the steadiest hands, and Odile had insisted, in her level way, that the plan was right and should not be changed for sentiment. She went over the lip with the rope singing through the clove hitch, and Ansel lay flat on the cold stones and watched the faint glow of the girl's hooded hand-lamp descend, rung by rung, swallowed. The whisper of the pendulum kept its rhythm. Once the glow passed through the swing-line of the pendulum rod and Ansel's heart stopped in her chest — but Odile had counted too. Of course she had counted. She hung on the rungs between beats, motionless, then moved on, precise as a second hand.

Then the lamp flared briefly at the bottom — the signal — and the rope twitched twice: *anchor set.*

"My turn," Ansel said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect.

The first ten rungs were easy. The next ten were ice.

She learned it at the eleventh: her boot found the rung and slid, a horrible smooth half-inch, and her whole body understood at once what her mind had refused to calculate — that she hung in a vertical stone throat above a swinging iron rod, that the darkness below was total, that the frost had made the iron treacherous in patches invisible to any lamp carried by a living hand. She froze. Not deliberately; freezing simply happened, the way a seized bearing happens, every faculty at once locking solid. Her fingers cramped around the rung. The pendulum whispered below her, *shh... shh...*, indifferent, eternal, and she thought absurdly, *my father hanged and I will fall, and neither death has any dignity in it,* and the terror was a white noise that ate the world—

Twice, the rope pulled taut and eased. Twice. *Come down.*

Not *someone fell.* Come down. Odile, at the bottom, feeling the tremor travel the whole length of the line, telling her without one word: *I have you. The rope does not lie. Come down.*

Ansel breathed. Once. Twice. Found the arithmetic — two seconds, two seconds, the rod passes, move between beats — and climbed.

She would not be able afterward to say how long the descent took. Ten minutes, perhaps, or an hour, or the rest of her life compressed into rung-rung-pause-rung. What she remembered later, in the body rather than the mind, was this: the moment her boot slipped again at the twentieth rung, and her grip tore loose, and the world became falling —

— and stopped. Not caught. *Caught* implies impact. This was gentler than that, uncanny, as if gravity had been repealed: the rope snapping taut in a perfectly judged arc, the shock distributed along her harness instead of her spine, her body swung in a slow pendulum of its own — of course, she thought, dazed, everything here is a pendulum — and below her, Odile's voice, calm as noon:

"You're all right. Lean back. Put your feet on the wall. I've got the whole weight and it isn't much. Climb."

And Ansel, hanging in the dark with her heart hammering hard enough to shake her ribs, laughed — a cracked, involuntary sound — because it *wasn't* much. Because this deaf girl who read lips through glass had paid out line and taken up line with the timing of a born rigger, had felt the fall begin through sixty feet of tarred hemp faster than any shout could carry it, and had simply *solved* it, the way she solved everything, with attention.

"Odile," Ansel said, when her feet found the floor of the weight cellar and her knees nearly buckled anyway, and Odile's lamp threw their shadows huge against the ancient stone. "That was—"

"Don't," Odile said, but she was smiling. "Tell me in daylight, when I can see your mouth."

Ivo came down last, slow as syrup, swearing steadily under his breath in a language Ansel suspected was mostly invented. Marta remained above, at the hatch, keeping watch and keeping the light, because — as she had put it — *somebody has to be able to tell the story.*

Then there was nothing between them and the escapement chamber but the clock itself, and the clock was *hers*, and the fear drained out of her the way it always did when her hands found familiar metal. She unshuttered the lantern fully for the first time, and the great case rose out of the dark like a cathedral, brass and steel throwing back thin blades of light, the pendulum sweeping past them in its patient two-second arc close enough to feel the wind of it on her cheek.

"Hello, old girl," Ansel murmured. "I'm sorry it took me so long."

The hidden compartment was behind the count-wheel, where she'd found it weeks ago in another life, and the ledgers lay inside exactly as she'd left them — wrapped in oilcloth, Tomás Quintana's enemies sleeping in their own handwriting. She lifted them out and felt their weight, not paper-weight but the other kind, and Ivo came to stand beside her and put his good hand flat on the cover, and said nothing at all, for a long moment, in the way that said everything.

"It's real," he said finally. "All this time I kept thinking there'd be a moment when it stops being real."

"It's real," Ansel said.

Above them, muffled by floors of stone, the tower's small hours ticked on. And it was then — wrapping the ledgers again, cinching the satchel strap, turning toward the shaft and home — that Odile touched her sleeve and pointed up, toward the sealed door, high above.

Through the darkness, faint but unmistakable, came the scrape of a key turning in new brass.
