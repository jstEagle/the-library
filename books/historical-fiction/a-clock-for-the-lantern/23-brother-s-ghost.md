## 23. Brother's Ghost

The dealer came to Vell on a Tuesday, driving a lean gray mule before a cart stacked with clock faces like a load of pale moons.

Ansel saw him first from the observatory steps, where she had gone to breathe air that did not smell of oil and old stone. The snow had stopped two days past and the light came down hard and white off the peaks, so that the whole square seemed cut from new silver. Against it the cart was a dark splinter, and the man atop it was wrapped in a coat that had been good twenty years ago, with a hat pulled low and a scarf wound up to his eyes. Only his hands showed, red-knuckled on the reins, and even from that distance Ansel found herself reading them the way she read everything now — the callus at the base of the thumb, the way the fingers flexed and curled in an idle rhythm, as though counting teeth on some invisible wheel.

A dealer's hands. A mender's hands.

She told herself it was nothing. Strangers came rarely enough to Vell in winter that each one felt like an omen, but this one would sell a few clocks, water his mule, sleep one night at Marta's inn if the Lantern had room, and roll on toward the passes before the next snowfall. She had work to do. The striking train lay half-disassembled upstairs, its hammers laid out on wool like surgical instruments, and she had promised Brannock — who watched her now with an attention she could no longer entirely classify — that the great clock would speak again by month's end.

She went back inside. She did not see the dealer rein in beneath the tower and sit there a long while, looking up at the dial.

* * *

He came to her that evening, and he did not come the way strangers came.

Ansel was working late in the tower by lamplight, perched on her ladder-back stool with a pair of pliers in her teeth and the escape wheel cradled in a cloth on her knee. She heard the door at the tower's base open — no knock, but not forced either; someone who knew how to lift a latch quietly and chose, deliberately, not to — and then footsteps on the spiral stair, unhurried, each tread placed with the flat certainty of a man who had climbed a thousand towers and expected nothing at the top of any of them but clocks.

"You've shimmed the fourth-wheel pivot," the voice said, from the doorway of the escapement chamber. "Punched your own bushings out of brass stock instead of buying them. And you file your click-spring shoulders on a bevel, left-handed."

Ansel turned slowly, pliers still in her hand, and looked at him.

He was smaller than he'd seemed from the steps, weathered down like a stone in a river, with a face full of seams and eyes the color of weak tea. His scarf hung loose around his neck now. He carried his hat in his hands, turning it, and he stood just inside the doorway without coming farther, as if aware that a woman alone at night was owed her distance even when she was wearing trousers and pretending to be somebody else.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't." He stepped closer anyway, drawn despite himself to the spread of tools, to the open heart of the clock, and his gaze moved over the disassembled train with something close to reverence. "But I know a left-handed bevel when I see one, because I spent four months of my life, thirty years ago, filing them alongside a man who invented the trick. Tomás Quintana. He taught it to exactly one other person in this world, and that person was his daughter, who used to hold the parts for him in a machine shop in Harrow Vale while the men laughed at a girl with grease on her cuffs."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft Ansel could not feel. Her own breath had gone somewhere very far away.

"My name is Fenn," the dealer said. "I sold watches in Harrow Vale the year they were organizing the mines. Your father repaired my regulator twice and refused payment once, and I have thought about him more times than I can count since the day they—" He stopped himself. Turned the hat once more in his hands. "I'm sorry. That's a cruel way to open a conversation."

"Then why did you?" Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, that it came out level.

"Because I saw you today," Fenn said, "and I nearly said your father's name out loud in the middle of the square in the town where Corven sits in judgment over everything that moves. Because I am too old and too careful to make that mistake twice, and so I waited until dark and climbed your stairs instead." He met her eyes. "Anselma Quintana. I'd know that filing anywhere in the world. It's like hearing a voice you grew up with through a wall."

The name landed in the room between them and sat there, enormous, in a place where for seven years no one had spoken it.

Ansel set the pliers down on the bench. Very carefully, as if the small precise motion might keep the rest of her from shaking apart. "You're wrong," she said. "My name is Ansel Ferrin. Apprentice to Master Halden of Coldbridge, God rest him, papers and all."

"I've seen forged papers before. I've carried a few myself." Fenn's tone held no mockery, only a kind of tired patience. "Girl. Child. Whoever you are. I watched you walk across that square today with your collar up and your stride shortened, doing a fair job of it, better than most would do — and I watched you look at the tower the way a person looks at a grave they tended with their own hands. Nobody else in this town looks at anything that way."

Silence filled the chamber. Above them the great wheels hung motionless in their frames, the pendulum stilled for the night's work, and the lamplight moved in slow amber tides across the brass. Ansel became aware, distantly, of the ledger lying not ten feet away behind the false panel in the case — the thing she had bled for, the thing that had already cost Elias his father's roof of certainty, the thing whose existence she had told no one in this town about except four people, and every one of those four had become necessary to her survival in ways that frightened her.

She measured this stranger against that arithmetic. Smuggler's math. Marta had taught her some of it without ever meaning to: *count what a man wants before you count what he knows.*

"What do you want, Fenn?"

"To talk." He lowered himself onto the far stool uninvited, with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and set his hat on his knee. "And maybe to trade. I've a stock of mainsprings in my cart that haven't been made since the Harrow works closed, and I'd part with a coil or two cheap for an old friend's daughter. But mostly to talk."

"My father is dead," Ansel said. "Thirty years of talking won't raise him."

"No. But he left something behind besides a daughter and a grave." Fenn leaned forward, elbows on knees, tea-colored eyes catching the lamplight. "I was there, you know. Not at the hanging — I made sure to be elsewhere that morning; I'd seen enough of magistrates' justice by then — but after. In the years after. Men came through Harrow Vale on their way to other valleys, other towns, carrying what he'd started in their shirt pockets like a coal that wouldn't go out. He organized three hundred miners who'd never trusted each other further than they could spit. He got the fund built — the widows' fund, the injury fund — before anyone had ever heard of such a thing in these mountains. Do you know what happened to that fund?"

Ansel's hands had gone cold in her lap. Behind her, faintly, the tower ticked with the settling of cooling metal, the small night-sounds of a body at rest.

"It vanished," she said.

"It vanished," Fenn agreed. "Stolen by the very men sworn to keep it, and paid out again — a shilling here, a crown there — to buy the testimony that put its founder under the ground. And everyone in five valleys knows it in their bones and no one can prove a word of it, because proof has a way of dying or disappearing or leaving town in the night under another name." His gaze rested on her, steady and terribly kind. "Until now, perhaps. Until a left-handed girl with her father's files came to fix a clock in Corven's own town and stayed all winter, and I watched Magistrate Corven's face across the square today when she walked past, and I'll tell you what I saw there, miss. I saw a man who has begun to be afraid."

* * *

They talked until the candle burned down to its collar.

It was not confession, quite, and not strategy either — or rather it was both braided together the way Fenn braided everything, commerce and memory and grief, so that Ansel could never afterward say at which point the talk stopped being about clockwork. He told her about the years after Harrow Vale: how he'd kept a portrait of Tomás Quintana folded inside a watch-case lid for a decade and finally burned it when garrisons began searching carts; how he'd taught himself to smile at magistrates; how he had watched, valley by valley, the organizing die down to embers and then to ash, because dead founders make poor correspondents and frightened men make poor converts.

"He should have had successors," Fenn said. "He wanted them. There was a plan, near the end — a network, runners, a way of moving word and money through the mountains that the tollhouses couldn't touch. He used to say a strike is just a clock stopping, and a clock stops easiest when you understand what drives it." He smiled without humor. "Then Corven bought his witnesses, the rope did its work, and the plan died with him in front of a crowd that mostly wept and did nothing. Mostly."

"Mostly," Ansel echoed. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, as though someone else were speaking through the tower's brass throat.

"There was a woman," Fenn said. "With a child on her hip. In the second row. I remember because the child wasn't crying, and everyone else's children were." He did not look at Ansel when he said it. He looked at the escape wheel, gleaming on its cloth. "I often wondered what became of them."

Ansel thought of her mother's hands, afterward. The terrible competence of grief. She thought of her brother's boots, too large, worn into the eastern country with a new name stitched inside the tongue. She thought of herself at nine years old learning to shorten her stride, lower her voice, file right-handed for the first year until the reversal became second nature and then no nature at all — until the bevel came left-handed only in secret, in private, a habit buried deeper than any ledger.

"I became a clockmender," she said at last. "That's what became of us. One child ran east and one child ran into gears."

"And now?" Fenn asked softly. "Now that the gears have led you here, to the exact center of the web? Now that you're sitting in the one tower in these mountains that may hold the proof of what was done to him?" He straightened, wincing, and his voice took on an edge that was not unkind but was very old and very hard. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer. I have spent thirty years selling time to people who waste it. I have driven my cart past gallows in six towns. I kept my head down and my stock moving and I called it prudence, and prudence is what I'll die with unless—"

"Unless," Ansel said.

"Unless somebody finishes it." Fenn's hands closed on the brim of his hat. "Your father started a thing in Harrow Vale, Anselma Quintana, and it is not finished. The fund is not repaid. The names are not cleared. The men who bought the rope are dead or fat, and Corven is both. You can leave here in spring with your ledgers and your life and your left-handed bevel, and set up shop somewhere far away, and mend clocks until your hands give out, and that will be a life. Some would say a wise one. But you'll always know that you stood inside the mechanism, girl — you, with your hands literally inside the works of the man who killed him — and you chose to walk away with the spring still wound."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, in the sleeping town, a dog barked twice and fell silent.

"You don't know what it costs," Ansel said quietly. "You don't know what they do to—"

"I know exactly what they do." For the first time something cracked in the old man's voice. "I informed once. Early. On a runner named Cade Lassiter, over a debt and a threat, and they flogged him at the crossroads and he lived, which was worse, because he knew it was me, and he forgave me, Anselma, he *forgave* me, and I have carried that forgiveness like a stone in my chest for twenty-six years. So yes. I know the cost. I've been paying it in installments my whole life." He drew a long breath, and when he spoke again the crack had sealed over, and only the hardness remained. "Which is why I'm not asking you to be brave. Brave is for ballads. I'm asking you to finish the work. Whatever shape it takes. Whatever it costs. Because you are the last living soul who carries both halves of it — his blood and his craft — and when you die, wherever you die, it dies with you. Or it doesn't."

* * *

Afterward she could not have said how long they sat in silence. The candle had perhaps an inch left when Fenn rose, groaning, and settled his hat on his head.

"I'll be at the Lantern tonight," he said. "Selling the innkeeper a wall clock, if she'll have one. Leaving at first light for the ferry landing, and thence across the river before it thinks about freezing." He paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the stair's darkness. "I won't tell a soul what I know. My silence isn't worth much, but it's yours, freely. What you do with the rest —" He lifted one shoulder. "That's between you and a dead man."

"Fenn."

He stopped.

"The fund," Ansel said. Her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect. "When it's proven — when the ledgers come out and the payments are shown — the money itself is gone. Spent. There's no recovering it. You understand that whatever I finish, it won't be restitution. It will only be truth."

Fenn considered that for a moment, head tilted, and then he smiled — a real one this time, crooked and tired and strangely young.

"Lassiter forgave me with a split lip and a ruined back," he said, "because I was the only soul in that crowd who couldn't look at him. Truth is restitution, girl. It's the only coin they can't counterfeit." He rapped his knuckles once, gently, against the clock's great case, the way a man greets an old horse. "Keep the bevel left-handed. It's a good signature. It deserves to sign something true."

His footsteps descended the stair and faded, and the tower door closed behind him with a sound like a held breath released.

Ansel sat alone in the amber dark with her father's craft in her hands and her father's name sitting newly upright inside her chest, not buried anymore, not hiding — awake, and asking things of her.

She thought of the plan laid out on Ivo's kitchen table: tollhouse, garrison gate, ferry landing. Three checkpoints, three chances to hang. She thought of Odile's quick fingers and Marta's smuggler's maps and Elias burning his father's world one seal at a time. She thought of the striking train half-built above her head, thirteen strokes waiting inside her like a second heartbeat.

*Finish what your father started.*

Her father had believed a strike was a clock stopping. Well. She was building them a clock that would stop them instead.

She picked up the pliers. She reached for the escape wheel. And outside, far to the north, beyond the black shoulder of the mountain where she could not see it and did not yet know to fear it, the river was already growing its first skin of ice — thin as a watch-glass, quiet as a lie, closing over the water that every plan she had depended upon.
