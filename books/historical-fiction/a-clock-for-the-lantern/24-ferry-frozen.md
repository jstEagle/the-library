## 24. Ferry Frozen

Ivo Hallden had lived his whole life by the river's moods, and he knew before he reached the landing that morning that the river had turned against them.

He felt it in his knees first — an old miner's barometer, set in him by thirty years underground and twenty more above it, joints that ached ahead of frost and sang ahead of storms. They had woken him before dawn with a deep, grinding complaint, and when he pulled back the curtain of his room above the Lantern Inn and saw the breath of the river hanging gray over the water, he knew.

By the time he'd buttoned his coat and taken his stick down from its peg, the light was coming up thin and colorless over the eastern ridge, and the Vell was steaming like a kettle left too long on the stove. That was the sign every boatman on the river feared. Warm water under cold air, giving itself away in vapor, surrendering its heat to the sky. The last act before the lock.

The path down to the landing was iced over, and Ivo took it slow, planting his stick with the deliberation of a man who had once watched a friend slide forty feet down a shaft head and break every promise his body had ever made. At the bottom, where the wooden platform jutted out over the black glass of the shallows, he stopped.

Slush. Ice floes the size of dinner tables rode the current downstream, clinking against each other like glasses at a wake, and along the edges of the channel the water had already gone still and white. Out in the middle, where the current ran hardest, the river still moved — but it moved thickly, sluggishly, a beast slowing to sleep.

"Early," said a voice behind him, and Ivo turned to find Piet the ferryman standing in the doorway of his hut, arms crossed against the cold, wearing the expression of a man reading his own death warrant. "Three weeks early, if it's come to this. Three weeks."

"How long?" Ivo asked.

Piet spat over the rail, an old habit, though there was nothing downstream to hit but ice. "If it locks tonight or tomorrow, it's done until thaw. Done. No pole breaks this, no horse pulls it. And even if a body could cross — which they can't, don't look at me like that, Ivo, I've seen strong men try the spring ice and I've helped fish what was left of them out come April — the garrison won't let anyone near the bank anyway. Corven posted a watch on both sides the moment word came down about the records office fire. We're sealed."

Ivo looked at the river for a long time.

It was strange, he thought, how a thing could be foreseen and still arrive as a blow. They had known winter would close the bridge — that was the whole architecture of the plan, the whole reason Ansel had been trapped in Vell since the first snow. But the bridge was stone; stone held; stone could be argued with, bribed through, walked past with the right papers and the right lies. The river was not stone. The river made no promises and took no bribes. He should have remembered that. He had spent his youth watching men argue with mountains, and the mountain always won, but slowly, over years, so that you could pretend for a while you were winning. The river didn't even give you that pretense.

"The tollhouse is settled," Ivo said, mostly to himself. "The gate is settled, or will be, once the boy commits. And now the third one freezes shut from the outside. Not locked. Frozen." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "We planned around three doors, Piet, and God went and bricked up the wall behind the third."

Piet said nothing. There was nothing to say. The two of them stood there while the light strengthened and the slush thickened, and somewhere upstream, invisible, the great white silence spread.

* * *

Back at the Lantern Inn, the kitchen was warm and smelled of bread and tallow, and nobody in it was talking.

That was how Ivo knew the news had already arrived — Marta Sable's runners were faster than any post rider. She stood at the big table with a map of the valley unrolled across it, weighted at the corners with salt cellars and a candlestick, and she was looking at it the way a surgeon looks at a wound she cannot reach. Odile sat nearby with her hands folded, her eyes moving between faces, reading the room the way she read lips — patiently, completely, missing nothing. Elias Corven stood apart by the fire, and there was a rawness about him these days, a man who had crossed a line and could hear it snapping behind him.

Ansel wasn't there yet. She would come. She was always at the tower by now, keeping the clock alive, keeping their secret ticking inside its case.

Marta looked up as Ivo shook the cold off his shoulders. "Well?"

"You've heard."

"I've heard. I want to hear it from your mouth."

Ivo sat down heavily on the bench, his knees thanking him. "Locked. Tonight, maybe tomorrow. Three weeks early. Piet says the garrison's watching both banks regardless — no crossing, no ferrying, no fool sliding out on the ice. It's not a checkpoint anymore. It's a wall."

The map said what no one wanted to say aloud. Vell sat in its bowl of mountains like a coin at the bottom of a cup. One road out, over the high bridge, closed since the first snows. One road downriver, past the tollhouse — settled, bought quiet through Marta's old debts-and-favors network, a brittle victory none of them trusted farther than they could throw it. The garrison gate between here and everywhere, guarded by men whose sons and brothers Ivo had worked beside in the old days, men who might refuse an order to arrest half the town but who would not refuse an order to hold a door. And then the ferry landing, the final throat of the escape, the place where the ledgers — or rather Odile's copies of the ledgers, the originals now sleeping inside the clock's own case like a heart inside a chest — were meant to pass out of the valley and into the hands of the mining union men down at Ferrin Crossing, who would know what to do with them.

Except there was no ferry anymore. There was a white ribbon of death, and three weeks of winter arriving like a debt collector ahead of schedule.

"We wait for thaw," Elias said from the fireplace. His voice was careful. "That was always the fallback. Spring comes, the river opens—"

"Spring comes," Marta said, without turning, "and Corven burns the archives. That was the other half of the arithmetic, boy. Your father isn't waiting on the weather. Every week we sit in this valley is a week he spends finding matches." Now she did turn, and her eyes were flat and hard as slate. "And every day he gets closer to understanding what's living inside that clock."

"He doesn't know," Odile said. She said it quietly, but everyone turned to her, because Odile did not speak often and never carelessly. Her hands moved as she talked, an old habit, sketching her words in the air. "If he knew, he would have broken the tower open already, ceremony or no ceremony. He suspects a runner. He suspects *someone*. He doesn't suspect the clock. If he suspected the clock, Ansel would be dead by now, and so would we."

"She's right," Ivo said. "But knowing and suspecting are two ends of the same rope, and Corven's pulling on his end. The fire at the records office spooked him. A man like that starts searching harder, not softer. How long before someone remembers the tower was sealed, and asks why? How long before Brannock—" He stopped himself. That was a thread none of them liked to pull.

Silence again. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind had begun to rise off the frozen river, a sound like cloth tearing slowly, endlessly.

Ivo looked at his hands on the table. They were an old man's hands, scarred at the knuckles, one finger shortened by a drill press in '81, the year everything happened, the year Tomás Quintana swung from the scaffold and the town learned what justice weighed in Vell. He had carried that year inside him for twelve years like a splinter too deep to dig out. And now, at last, the splinter had a name and a shape — pages, signatures, sums — and it was sitting in a clock tower a quarter mile away, and the one road that could carry it to daylight had frozen solid overnight.

*God gives us the rope,* his father used to say, *and then sells us the length.*

"It was never going to be clean," Marta said at last. She began rolling the map, briskly, as if putting the problem away would solve it. "No plan survives. You adapt. That's what running is — adapting. We find another way over the river."

"There is no other way," Ivo said. "Not with this cold. Not with the watch on the banks. I've thought on it since the landing, Marta, and I'll spare you the hours I spent — there's the footbridge at Kessel Gorge, forty feet above the rapids, and the garrison burned its planks in November. There's the ford at Miller's Bend, waist-deep in summer, certain death now — a man goes in the water at twenty degrees, he's dead before the far bank, and if he makes the bank he can't walk. There's riding around through the pass at Cold Saddle, nine days' hard travel through country where the snow drifts over the trail markers, and even if a soul survived it, they'd arrive at Ferrin Crossing frost-bit and half-mad, carrying copies no court would take seriously off a stranger babbling about conspiracies." He shook his head. "I'm not saying it to be cruel. I'm saying it because I've run the roads all night in my head and every road ends the same way."

"Then we don't use the roads," Elias said.

They all looked at him. He had come forward from the fire, and there was something new in his face — not hope exactly, but the refusal of despair, which is hope's stubborn cousin.

"And what do we use, lad?" Ivo asked. "Wings?"

"The town." Elias laid his hand flat on the rolled map, as if pressing something into it. "My father's power in this valley has never been the garrison. Twelve soldiers can't hold a town of four hundred souls if the four hundred stop obeying. His power is that everyone believes there's no alternative to him — no proof, no voice, no witness. Take that belief away in front of everyone at once, and the garrison doesn't matter. The river doesn't matter. Nothing frozen matters." He paused, and Ivo watched the cost of the next words move across his face like weather. "There's a reason Ansel keeps saying the clock must run. Maybe the clock doesn't need a road out. Maybe it needs an audience."

"That's a gamble," Marta said.

"So is waiting for thaw while my father hunts," Elias said. "Every gamble we have left is bad. We're choosing which bad one."

Odile tapped the table twice, sharp, and pointed at Ivo. In the months since the circle had formed, they had all learned her shorthand: *you haven't said what you think.*

Ivo looked down at his ruined knuckles for a long moment.

What he thought was this: he had spent twelve years being careful. Careful after the strike was broken, careful when they arrested Tomás, careful at the trial where no miner's testimony survived cross-examination intact, careful at the hanging, careful in the years after, keeping his grief folded small and his anger smaller, surviving. Careful had kept him alive. Careful had also buried Tomás Quintana twice — once in the ground above the tree line, and once in history, where Corven's version of events lay on the grave like a stone slab, smooth and official and heavy.

And here was winter, the oldest argument there is, telling him: *wait.* Wait like you've always waited. Be careful. Survive.

"I'm tired," he said aloud, and the others stilled, because his voice had changed. "Twelve years tired. I've been careful since the day they hanged him, and careful got us nothing but a longer wait. The boy's right, God help us. If the evidence can't leave the valley, then the valley becomes the courtroom. We make the whole town the witness." He exhaled, long and slow, and it felt like setting down a sack he'd carried so many years he'd forgotten the weight of it. "But understand what we're choosing. Once that clock strikes and those pages fall, there's no ferry home for any of us either. We'll be standing in the square with Corven when it happens. Whatever the town does or doesn't do, we're inside it. No walls, no river, no retreat."

"No retreat," Marta agreed, and something in her face — some old ghost, some runner she'd lost and never spoken of — nodded along with her.

* * *

Ansel came at dusk, snow in her dark hair, her cheeks whipped red, and she read the room before she'd fully closed the door — read it the way she read gear trains, tooth by tooth, fault by fault. Her eyes went from the rolled map to Marta's face to Ivo's hands, and Ivo watched the arithmetic complete itself behind her eyes.

"The river," she said.

"Frozen," Ivo said. "Locked till thaw. Early. Three weeks early."

She stood very still in the doorway, snow melting on her shoulders, and Ivo thought — not for the first time — how young she was under all that borrowed gravity, how much of her father lived in the set of her jaw. For a moment the stillness looked like it might crack into something else, grief or rage or simple exhaustion, and he found himself rising off the bench without deciding to, the way you rise toward a person standing too close to a shaft edge.

Then she blinked, and the crack closed, and she hung her coat on the peg and sat down at the table like a woman reporting for a shift.

"Tell me everything," she said. "Then tell me what we have left. Because I've been thinking, all afternoon, about striking trains."

Ivo told her about the landing, the slush, Piet's gray face, the watch on both banks. He told her about the footbridge and the ford and the pass, laying out the failures plainly, because she deserved plainness and because hiding a bad road from this particular young woman had never once worked. He watched her listen the way Odile listened — wholly, with her whole body — and he watched Elias begin, hesitantly, to speak of audiences and squares and the town as courtroom, and he watched Ansel go very quiet at that, quieter and quieter, her fingers moving unconsciously on the tabletop, tracing the shape of some mechanism only she could see.

Outside, the wind came down off the mountain carrying the sound of the frozen river — a vast, low groaning, the ice settling and shifting and testing its own strength, the sound of a door closing that would not open again for months.

And beneath the wind, fainter, steady as a heartbeat, the tower clock ticked on — keeping its false innocent time, holding its dead men's names in its belly, waiting, like all of them, for someone to decide what it was going to be made to say.
