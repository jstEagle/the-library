## 25. Corven's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy who would not meet her eyes.

Ansel was at the inn's workbench, fitting a new pallet stone into Marta's regulator clock, when the knock came — three sharp raps, official in their rhythm. The boy stood on the threshold in a garrison coat two sizes too large for him, holding out an envelope sealed with wax the color of dried blood. He could not have been more than twelve. His boots were caked with the gray slush of the square, and his breath came in quick white puffs.

"From the Magistrate," he said. "You're to come at once. Alone."

She took the envelope because refusing it in front of the boy would have meant nothing and cost her everything. Inside, on paper heavy enough to have its own gravity, a single line in a clerk's careful hand:

*Mr. Quintana will present himself at the magistrate's house this evening, after the six o'clock bell.*

*Mr. Quintana.* She turned the name over like a coin she suspected of being shaved. Six weeks in Vell and the lie had worn smooth as a river stone; she answered to it without flinching now, mostly. But in Corven's mouth — or his clerk's — it sounded like something being tested for weight.

Marta said nothing while she read it over Ansel's shoulder. When Ansel looked up, the innkeeper was wiping down the bar that didn't need wiping, her rag moving in slow circles, her face arranged into the particular blankness she wore when she was thinking hardest.

"You don't have to go," Marta said.

"Yes, I do." Ansel folded the letter along its original creases. "Refusing tells him I'm afraid. Going tells him nothing until he shows what he knows."

"And if he shows everything?"

"Then I've been walking toward this since the bridge closed." She said it lightly, and hated how much it cost to keep her voice level. "He can't prove anything about the ledgers. He doesn't know they exist."

It was the first time she had lied to Marta Sable outright, and by the way the older woman's hands went still on the bar, she knew it landed badly. Marta let it go. That was worse.

* * *

The magistrate's house stood at the top of Church Row, the only building in Vell with glass in every window, and even now, in the deepest cold of winter, ivy clung to its southern wall in brown ropes. A lantern burned over the door. Everything about the house was lit, warm, deliberate — a stage set waiting for its actor.

A servant opened the door before Ansel knocked. She was shown through a hall hung with portraits of men whose faces had been painted to look wiser than their sons, up a staircase worn concave in the center of each tread, into a study that smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil and, beneath it all, faintly, of ink.

Corven sat behind a desk that had never known honest labor. He was smaller than Ansel expected — she had built him tall these past weeks, a giant made of her father's ghost — and older, with a clerk's stoop beginning to curve his shoulders and white threading through the black of his beard. His hands, resting flat on the desk's blotter, were fine and well-kept, the hands of a man who signed things rather than lifted them.

"Sit down, Mr. Quintana," he said. "Or should we dispense with that? We haven't much night, and you strike me as someone who dislikes wasted time."

Ansel did not sit. Her heart was slamming against her ribs so hard she could hear it, but eleven years of practice kept it off her face. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Corven agreed mildly. "You wouldn't. Sit anyway. Standing there makes this feel like a trial, and I am trying very hard not to hold one."

She sat. The chair was upholstered in leather gone soft with use. Across the room, a fire ticked in a grate, and above it hung a landscape of the mountain pass, the bridge a thread of gray across the gorge. Somewhere under that paint, she thought, was the road her father had walked to the gallows.

"Do you know," Corven began, pouring two glasses of brandy without asking whether she wanted one, "what I remember most about your father? Not the speeches. Everyone remembers the speeches — Tomás Quintana standing on the minehead steps with his voice carrying clear across three terraces, no horn needed. What I remember is quieter. It rained that morning. The whole town stood in the rain to watch, because they'd been told it was justice, and rain makes people patient somehow, makes them stand and wait for a thing to be finished." He set the decanter down. "Your father looked at the crowd the entire time. Not at me. At them. As though they were the ones who owed him something, and he'd simply come to collect."

The room tilted slightly. Ansel gripped the arms of the chair. Eleven years, and still the word *rain* could put the rope back around her throat.

"I don't know my father," she said. "I was apprenticed young. My family were clockmakers in the lowlands."

"Yes," Corven said, "that's the story. It's a good story. You've maintained it beautifully — the calluses are right, the vocabulary is right, even the way you squint at small mechanisms is exactly what a lowlands apprenticeship would teach." He leaned forward. "But I knew Tomás Quintana's hands, Mr. Quintana. I watched them for years from a courtroom bench. And when a woman walks into my town wearing those same hands, and the same jaw, and the same habit of looking at powerful men the way your father looked at crowds — as though collecting — then I do not need proof. I need only patience."

Silence filled the study like water filling a hull. Ansel became aware of her own breathing, of the fire's ticking, of a carriage passing far below on the square. She catalogued each sound the way Odile had taught her to catalogue light: something to hold onto while the ground moved.

"How long?" she asked. Her voice came out steady. She would be proud of that later, if there was a later.

"Five weeks. Since the day you arrived and Brannock described the new apprentice to me over dinner — young, slight, keeps the hood up indoors, works without speaking for hours at a stretch." He smiled without warmth. "I have known for five weeks, and I have done nothing. Ask yourself why."

"I'm asking."

"Because I wanted to see what you would do." Corven rose and moved to the window, his back to her, watching the snow fall through the cone of the streetlamp below. "A daughter comes home to hang beside her father's memory. That is grief, and grief is stupid, and I have hanged enough clever men to know that stupidity kills faster than any court. But you didn't come for revenge. Revenge burns hot and fast and leaves evidence everywhere. You came and you fixed my clock. You asked questions about the sabotage — good questions, Brannock told me, too precise for idle curiosity. You made friends carefully. You learned the town." He turned. "That is not grief. That is strategy. And strategy, Mr. Quintana — Anselma — means there is something you want. Something in my observatory worth more to you than escape."

*Anselma.* Her own name, spoken aloud in this room, in this voice. It felt less like being seen than being flayed. She kept her hands loose in her lap and gave him nothing, but inside, coldly, precisely, she recalculated everything: the sealed tower, Brannock's warning, the fire at the records office. He had been circling her for weeks while she circled him, and neither of them had blinked first until tonight.

"There are papers," Corven said, returning to the desk. He did not open a drawer. He simply stood over her, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "In this house. Accounts. Correspondence. Enough to hang a dozen men if it fell into the wrong hands — which is why it lives where it lives, and why in spring, when the thaw opens the roads, it will burn. All of it. Every page of twenty-two years of administration, gone in an afternoon, and Vell will start clean because Vell has no memory except the ones we allow it."

He let that sit. Then:

"I am going to offer you a trade, and I want you to understand that it is sincere. Leave the ledgers where they are — whatever you believe you know, leave it. In exchange: a full pardon, signed and sealed, for the crime of false identity. Passage south on the first ferry of the thaw, paid in advance, with letters of introduction to the guild houses in Torvane that will set you up better than any apprenticeship ever could. Money." He named a figure. It was obscene. It was, she realized with a strange lurch, roughly the sum her father had died organizing miners to claim — the withheld wages of a hundred men, sitting in this man's accounts, offered now to buy the silence of one.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then spring comes, the archives burn, the ledgers become ash, and you are a woman who committed fraud against the guild and harbored stolen documents against a magistrate — and I am a man who tried, sincerely, on the record, to be merciful." He spread his fine hands. "I am not threatening you, child. I am showing you the arithmetic. Your father believed the town could be moved. I watched the town stand in the rain and do nothing. Twenty-two years later, they still do nothing. They'll take the clock back and love it, and they'll take the taxes, and they'll bury their dead, and next winter some other organizer will climb the minehead steps and some other magistrate will hang him, and none of it — none of it — will have been worth your life."

There it was. Not cruelty — she had braced for cruelty and found something far harder to withstand. Corven believed what he said. He had built a whole architecture of certainty around that rainy morning, and in his telling, he had won fairly, and the world had agreed, and mercy was simply the interest paid on a debt already settled.

"You loved him once, didn't you?" Ansel heard herself say. "My father. Before."

Something crossed the magistrate's face — fast, quickly shuttered, but real. He looked old suddenly, and tired, and very far from the giant she had built in her head.

"I respected him," Corven said quietly. "Which in the mountains passes for love. He was the only man I ever prosecuted who frightened me, and I have spent twenty-two years telling myself it was because he was dangerous." He straightened his shoulders, and the tiredness folded away like a letter. "You have until the thaw. Not a day longer. Think about what he would actually want — a dead cause, or a living daughter."

* * *

She walked back down through the falling snow without remembering most of the route. The square was empty, lamplit, blue-white under the new fall. Above her the tower rose black against the sky, and somewhere inside it, behind Brannock's seals and locks, the ledgers slept in their compartment like a heart in a chest, keeping time.

At the Lantern Inn, the windows glowed. Through the glass she could see Marta behind the bar, Odile at her corner table bent over her ciphered notes by candlelight, Ivo nursing a beer and talking with his hands the way deaf-adjacent men learn to when half their conversation partners read lips. Elias was there too — she saw him hesitate at the door, glance at the snow, and stay. Her people. Her impossible, assembled, accidental family, warm behind glass like a mechanism she had built without meaning to.

They were the arithmetic Corven hadn't counted.

Ansel stood in the snow a long moment, letting the cold settle into her bones, sharpening the choice until it gleamed. Pardon and passage and a life in the lowlands with her name still buried. Or the thaw, and the burning, and whatever she and these four could build against it in the weeks between.

Through the window, Odile looked up. Even through two panes and a yard of falling snow, even without the telescope room's clear glass to help her, the girl seemed to feel Ansel's gaze — and she raised one hand, palm out. *I see you. Come in.*

Ansel pushed open the door to warmth and lamplight and the smell of stew, and hung her coat on the peg like a woman who intended to stay through spring.

Inside, Marta poured her a beer without being asked and said, quietly, "Well?"

"He's known for five weeks," Ansel said. "And he's giving us until the thaw."

She set Corven's obscene figure on the table between them — not the money, just the number itself, spoken aloud, the price of her father's silence measured in the wages of a hundred dead men's labor — and watched Ivo's face go the color of the slate above the minehead where Tomás Quintana had made his last speech.

"The thaw," Ivo repeated, and his hands, mid-gesture, slowly closed into fists. "Six weeks. Maybe seven."

Outside, the snow fell on, sealing the pass, sealing the ferry landing, sealing them all in together with a magistrate's patience running out — and Ansel understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that Corven had made one mistake tonight, the same mistake he'd made on a rainy morning twenty-two years ago.

He had told her exactly how much time she had.

Now she only had to build a clock that could beat it.
