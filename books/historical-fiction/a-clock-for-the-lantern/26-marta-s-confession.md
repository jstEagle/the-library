## 26. Marta's Confession

The storm came down off the pass two days after Corven's men searched the tollhouse, and it shut the Lantern Inn so completely inside its own walls that Marta could not hear the town bell for the wind. Snow piled against the north door to the second rung. The fire drew hard. The regulator clock she had once traded a winter of lodging for—the one Ansel Quintana had rebuilt with hands no bigger than a sparrow's—ticked in the corner of the common room with the patient, insolent steadiness of a thing that knew it would outlast them all.

Marta counted her people the way she used to count crates at midnight: by feel, in the dark, without letting her lantern show.

Ivo sat nearest the fire, whittling a gear shape he would never finish into a scrap of pine, his old strike-scarred knuckles working the knife like a rosary. Odile was at the long table with her ciphered notes spread before her, transcribing again by lamplight, her fingers moving over the braille dots the way other women moved over embroidery. Elias had not come that night; his father kept him close now, and every absence of his left a shape in the room like a chair pulled back from a table.

And Ansel—Ansel was up in the loft with the ledgers' copies and a candle stub, doing something to the striking train drawings that Marta did not pretend to understand, her boots hanging over the loft rail like a boy's, because that was how Ansel still sat when she forgot herself. When she remembered who she had decided to be again.

Marta poured herself a finger of plum brandy, thought better of it, and poured a second finger.

She had been putting this off since midwinter fair. She had been putting it off, if she were honest, since the night Ansel first walked through her door with snow in her cropped hair and a forged letter in her pocket, and Marta had read the forgery in four seconds flat and said nothing, because she had recognized something in the girl's face—the particular hunger of someone carrying more than their own weight. She had been putting it off because there are confessions that change nothing and confessions that change everything, and she could not tell which kind this was until it was out of her mouth.

But Corven had smelled smoke now. There were soldiers on the road even in this weather. And tomorrow or the day after, one of them would knock on the Lantern's door, and Marta needed to know—if it came to it—that the girl upstairs knew exactly what sort of woman was asking her to trust her life to this house.

"Odile." Marta spoke plainly, facing her, so the girl could catch the shapes of her lips across the room. "Put the pen down a moment. Ivo. Both of you."

Odile looked up. In the lamplight her face had the open, unhurried attention of someone who spent her life reading light instead of sound, and Marta had learned in these weeks to love that face the way you love a window after years in a cellar. Ivo laid his half-carved gear on the hearthstone.

"Where's Ansel?" Ivo asked.

"Upstairs." Marta set the brandy down untasted. "Which is why I'm saying this now, while she can't hear it, and deciding after whether she ever needs to."

Odile's brow folded. *Saying what?* her whole posture asked.

Marta came around the bar. Her knees complained; they always did when the weather turned, forty winters of mountain roads settled into the joints like gravel. She stood with her back to the great stone fireplace, where the heat pressed between her shoulder blades, and she looked at the two of them—Ivo with his ruined lungs and his dead friends, Odile with her lantern alphabet—and she made herself begin.

"I ran the passes for eleven years," she said. "Before I took this inn. Salt, tea, silk when the tariffs went high, letters when the tariffs went higher. People, twice. Only twice, because people are heavier than anything else you can carry, and I don't mean in weight." She let that sit. "In the eleventh year, the garrison at the ferry landing caught one of my runners. A boy named Teodor Vass. Nineteen years old. He carried leaflets for the union men downriver—this was before your time, Ivo, before the strike, when it was just pamphlets and meetings in barns."

Ivo had gone very still. Old men know when a story is coming for them.

"They held him three days," Marta said. "Then they let him go, which should have told me everything, because garrisons do not give gifts. And a week later they stopped me on the toll road with a full load and waved me through, and I was grateful, and I did not ask myself why the checkpoints had gone blind in exactly the places my loads passed through. I told myself I was good. I told myself the luck of eleven years was holding." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced this part for thirty years without knowing it. "They weren't blind. They were being fed. Someone was telling them my routes, my nights, my names. And the only person alive who knew all three was the boy they'd let go."

The fire popped. Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked under Ansel's shifting weight, and Marta made herself go on.

"I could have warned him. I had two days' warning—a friend in the ferry office sent me a word, one word, just *run*, and I sat with it all one night trying to decide whether I believed it. Because if I believed it, then everything I'd built was built on a boy's betrayal, and if I didn't believe it, then I only had to keep not believing it until the feeling went away." She picked up the brandy now, finally, and drank, and it burned all the way down like a debt coming due. "I chose not to believe it. That is the honest name for what I did. I didn't inform on him. I informed on myself—I lied to myself so thoroughly that by morning I truly didn't know. And Teodor Vass took the mountain route on the fourth of March with a satchel of names in it, and the garrison was waiting at the switchback, and he ran rather than be taken, and the ice on the cornice gave, and they never found all of him."

Odile's hand had risen to her mouth. Not a hearing person's gesture—her own. Her eyes were bright and furious and wet.

"They hanged him posthumously anyway," Marta said. "Tried him in absentia for sedition, convicted him, strung the sentence over his mother's door in the river town so everyone would see what a traitor's house looked like. I went to the funeral. I stood at the back with my hood up, and his mother looked right at me—right through the hood, I have always believed she saw me—and she nodded. Just nodded. Like we had an understanding. And I have carried that nod for—" she calculated, and was appalled at the number— "thirty-one years."

Ivo spoke for the first time. His voice was gravel from the fire's dry air. "You're telling us this why? Now?"

"Because Corven knows about the ledgers. Maybe not where they are, but that they exist, and that they're mine to move or lose. And because when the moment comes—and it is coming, Ivo, this month, before the thaw—he will offer me what the garrison offered me. He will look away from one shipment, open one door, make the road easy. And I need—" and here, at last, Marta Sable's voice cracked, one hairline fracture across sixty years of masonry, and she hated it, and she let it stand— "I need witnesses to what I am about to do, so that I cannot lie to myself the way I lied to myself then. If I fail this girl, I want it to be a failure in front of God and both of you, not a failure I get to call luck."

Ivo put down his knife. He looked into the fire for a long moment, at the coals rearranging themselves like men shifting in their sleep.

"You know what they did with Tomás," he said quietly.

"I know what they did with Tomás Quintana."

"They bought his witnesses. Paid them off, a few silver each, men who owed the company store more than they owed their souls. And every one of those men had a night like yours, Marta. A night with a choice in it. And every one of them chose the version where they didn't really know." He tapped his chest, twice, slow. "The difference is you're choosing different *now*. Thirty years late buys nothing for Teodor Vass. But it buys something for the girl upstairs."

"That's the arithmetic," Marta agreed. "It's ugly arithmetic. But it's the only sum I've got."

Odile had been writing while they talked—fast, her pen scratching. Now she tore the page free and held it up. Marta crossed the room and read it, and read it again, and felt something in her chest turn over like a heavy wheel finding its bearing.

*I heard everything. I read lips through glass, remember? I also read them through grief. Ansel has never heard this story and should not have to. So I will hold it with you instead. All three of us will hold it. That is what it is for.*

"Well," Marta said, rough-voiced, "there's a use for a deaf girl and a half. Reading grief through glass."

Odile smiled, wet-eyed, and wrote beneath it: *Someone has to keep the books.*

* * *

Marta climbed the loft ladder past midnight, when the storm had eased to that deep, ringing silence that snow makes, as if the world had been wrapped in wool. Ansel was asleep sitting up, cheek against the striking-train diagrams, ink smudged along her jaw, one hand still curled loose around a pencil. Twenty-two years old and she slept like a soldier, ready to wake, because twenty-two years old and she had never once been safe enough not to.

Marta stood at the top of the ladder and watched her and did not wake her.

There would be time—or there would not. That was the truth of it, the cold arithmetic Ivo loved and she despised. Corven had offered Ansel pardon and passage already; the girl had come back from that meeting gray-faced and stubborn, and Marta had fed her stew and said nothing wise, because there was nothing wise to say. Next it would be Marta's turn to be offered something. Magistrates always found the lever. Hers was obvious: an old woman's inn, an old woman's record, an old woman's fear of ending like the boy on the cornice.

She reached down and, very gently, drew the blanket up over Ansel's shoulders. The girl stirred but did not wake.

"I lost one already," Marta whispered, too low for any lip in the world to read. "Not this one. This one I carry the whole way or not at all."

Below, the regulator clock ticked on, and somewhere in its small brass heart the escapement let the seconds through one at a time, each one spent and unrecoverable, and Marta Sable stood in the dark of her own inn and resolved, at last and completely, to spend hers well.

She was halfway down the ladder when Odile appeared at the foot of it, lamp in hand, face pale in its circle of light. The girl's hands moved fast, urgent, and Marta had learned enough by now to catch most of it without the slate:

*Lantern in the square. Three short, two long. That's our signal — someone's asking for Elias.*

Marta's blood went cold and clean, the way it had on the worst nights of her running years, when fear sharpened instead of dulled. She took the lamp from Odile's hand and set it down.

"Get Ivo," she said. "Wake Ansel. Whatever's begun tonight—" and she reached for her coat, and the coat was already snowy at the shoulders, though she hadn't been out — "it's begun."
