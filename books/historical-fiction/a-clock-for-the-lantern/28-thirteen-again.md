## 28. Thirteen Again

The tower at midnight was the only place in Vell where Ansel could breathe.

She had learned its rhythms the way she had once learned her father's heartbeat against her ear — the settling groans of the timber as the cold deepened, the tick of the great escapement like a patient pulse, the way the moonlight came through the east lancet and laid a pale blade across the floor that crept, hour by hour, toward the pendulum shaft. She knew when the warden's patrol passed below by the crunch of boots on frozen gravel, and she knew the four minutes between patrols when the tower door's lock — Brannock's new lock, iron and smug — could be worked from inside with a bent file and patience. She had stopped thinking of the tower as a prison weeks ago. It was a workshop. It was a weapon being forged, one quiet night at a time.

Tonight she sat cross-legged on the planking beside the striking train with her coat off, sleeves rolled, a spirit lamp guttering at her elbow and a scatter of brass around her knees like fallen leaves. The chamber with the ledgers was sealed behind her, hidden again in the case below the going train, but its presence filled the room more than the clock itself did. Every gear she touched now, she touched for what it carried.

She was rebuilding the striking train to lie.

It was the finest thing she had ever built, and it was a lie, and both of those truths sat in her chest like counterweights.

* * *

The problem had come to her three nights ago, in that borderland between waking and sleep where her father used to say the good ideas waited for honest workers. A clock that struck thirteen would gather a crowd — Ivo had said it first, his old grief sharpening into strategy: *the town remembers, girl. They'll count. They always counted.* A crowd Corven could not disperse, not on a day of ceremony, not with half the garrison made of miners' sons. But a crowd was only noise unless the clock spoke clearly. Unless the pages fell.

So: the evidence chamber had to open at the thirteenth stroke. Not before — the ledgers were safer inside the clock than anywhere else in Vell, hidden where every searcher had already looked twice and found only gears. And not after, because after meant Elias or Marta or Odile carrying them out through three checkpoints and all the weather and treachery between here and the lowlands. No. The clock would hold them until the whole town stood beneath it, and then it would open like a mouth and give them up, and there would be no satchel to seize, no courier to arrest. You cannot confiscate what two hundred witnesses have already read.

Which meant a second escapement. Which meant the most delicate work of her life, done by lamplight, in secret, on a machine that had to keep perfect time while she gutted part of it.

She had spent last night making the locking plate. It lay before her now on a square of felt she'd cut from an old hat lining: a brass disc the size of her palm, pierced with thirteen notches instead of twelve. Thirteen teeth. When the hour wheel released the strike train at midnight, the gathering pallet would count notches — one, two, three — and where a proper clock's pallet met the twelfth notch and found the stop pin, hers would find one notch more, and the train would lift the hammer one final time into the silence after the twelve, and let it fall alone.

One stroke, unaccompanied, into a held breath. That was how you made a town listen.

But the thirteenth stroke had to do more than sound. As the pallet crossed that extra notch, it would trip a small lever she was fitting to the underside of the plate — a trip lever no longer than her little finger, filed down from a broken hand-spring. The lever would release the detent holding the second escapement, the one she'd built into the wall of the evidence chamber itself, a tiny anchor-and-toothed-wheel affair driving a worm screw, and the worm screw would turn the chamber's false back panel, racking it slowly open along two brass rails. Slowly — that was essential. If the panel swung wide at once, the wind through the louvers might catch the loose ledger pages and take them over the parapet, out over the square, into the snow, gone. Instead the panel would creep, and the pages would slide free one at a time, or in soft sheaves, dropping through the opening onto the bells' frame and then, weighted with the little tin tags she'd crimped onto each corner so they'd flutter rather than cartwheel, down through the sound-holes into the lantern light of the square below. Rain. Her father's handwriting raining onto the stones where he had been condemned.

She picked up the locking plate and held it to the lamplight. The thirteen notches gleamed. Somewhere below, the real clock ticked on, innocent, keeping the true time for a town that did not yet know what its time was worth.

*You'd have loved this,* she told her father silently, the way she did most nights now, the words worn smooth from use. *Or you'd have hated it. You'd have said a clock should tell the truth and nothing else.* But this was the truth, she argued back at him, as she always did; this was the truth in the only grammar the town would hear. Twelve strokes said: all is well, all is ordered, go to your beds. The thirteenth would say: *count again.*

* * *

Dawn found her still working, which was becoming usual, and it also found her hands shaking, which was not.

The trouble was the governor. She had known it would be, from the first night — she'd simply been too tired to face it. The striking train ran on weight and momentum, and if the thirteenth stroke tripped the chamber mechanism mid-fall, the sudden drag of the worm screw would slow the train unevenly, and the hammer would land the thirteenth stroke thin and cracked, a stutter instead of a statement. Worse, a jolted train could bounce the pallet off the plate entirely and leave the chamber half-open — enough to hang her, not enough to save anyone.

She needed the trip to happen clean, at full lock, with the train momentarily at rest between strokes. She needed the mechanism to wait for its own breath.

She sat back on her heels and stared at the gears until her eyes burned, and what finally came to her was not engineering but memory: Odile's hands, moving as she spoke without voice. Odile never signed on the beat of a sentence; she signed in the pause after it, in the space where the air settled, because she had learned — Ansel understood this now, had learned it across a telescope-room window in a winter of small lessons — that meaning lived in pauses. That the space between strokes was where attention went to listen.

*Let it trip in the pause.*

She rebuilt the trip lever that afternoon with the patience of someone braiding rope. Instead of mounting it on the locking plate where the pallet's motion was continuous, she mounted it on the fly fan's arbor — the little brake that spun between each stroke and stilled, just for a moment, before the next. At the twelfth stroke, the fan would spin down into stillness, and in that stillness — that held breath — the trip lever, weighted and poised, would fall into a slot cut in the arbor collar and turn with it as the train gathered itself for the thirteenth blow. By the time the hammer rose, the detent below was already releasing, already beginning its slow work. The stroke itself would be pure, unspoiled, the loudest and clearest note of the thirteen. And by the time its echo died off the mountainside, the first page would already be sliding toward the edge of the dark.

She tested it forty times with the weights disconnected, cranking the train by hand through a spare winding square, watching the lever fall, watching the little tooth of the second escapement click free, watching the worm screw turn the model panel — she'd made a mock-up from scrap pine — open by degrees. Click. Click. Click. Each degree of rotation took eleven seconds. The full opening would take nearly four minutes. Four minutes of pages falling into the square while two hundred people stood in the snow and did nothing but look up.

On the forty-first trial, her hand slipped. The winding square kicked back and split her knuckle against the great wheel's teeth, and she swore — a word her father would have washed her mouth out for, a word that sounded exactly like him when it left her — and sat there in the grey light sucking blood from her hand and laughing until the laughing turned into something else and she had to press her sleeve against her eyes for a long time.

When it passed, she cleaned the wound, wrapped it, and went back to work. There was no other honest response to fear. She had learned that young.

* * *

Odile came at dusk, which had become their arrangement. She could not knock — well, she could, but Ansel would not hear her over the clock — so she had taught Ansel to watch for her shadow crossing the lower lancet, angled and unmistakable, a signature in silhouette. Tonight the shadow came early, and with it a second, broader one that could only be Ivo, and Ansel's stomach tightened even as she worked the door's inner bolt.

They climbed to the clock room carrying a covered basket that smelled of bread and stewed rabbit, because Marta sent food up every second night and pretended it was leftovers, and because none of them had the heart to tell her that pretending was the glue holding all of them together.

Odile looked at the bench first, always the bench first — her eye went straight to the work like a compass finding north — and then she turned to Ansel and signed, quick and bright: *Finished?*

*Almost,* Ansel signed back. Her signing was still clumsy, all elbows, but Odile read it the way she read everything, generously, filling the gaps with context and mercy. *Two nights. Maybe three.*

Ivo set the basket down and lowered himself onto an upturned crate with the care of a man whose joints kept their own calendar. He was seventy if he was a day, and the cold got into him worse each week, but he had not missed a night yet. "Show me the plate," he said, which was his way of saying hello.

She lifted the locking plate and turned it so the firelight caught the notches. Ivo counted them with one cracked fingernail, lips moving, and when he reached thirteen he was quiet for a long moment.

"Last time I heard thirteen strokes," he said finally, "was the night before they took your da. The union men rang the chapel bell wrong on purpose, thirteen times, so everyone in the valley would know something was coming that the magistrate didn't want known." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Corven remembers too. You know that. He was there."

"I'm counting on him remembering."

"That's either very clever or very proud."

"Both run in the family," Ansel said, and Odile snorted, and the tension broke the way it always did among the three of them — quickly, easily, like ice underfoot giving way to water.

Then Odile stepped closer to the bench and tapped Ansel's arm to get her attention, and her expression had changed. *Problem,* she signed, and pointed at the trip lever assembly, and then mimed — with two fingers walking down through the air, slow, deliberate — *the falling. The pages. Wind.*

Ansel frowned. *The louvers?*

Odile shook her head and pointed upward, toward the belfry above, then cupped her hand and blew across it. Then she pointed at herself, at her ears — a habit she had, touching the ears she couldn't use, as if to say *trust me on sound* — and signed: *The bell. Thirteen strokes close together. The air in the tower will shake. Everything loose will move. Your pages are loose.*

Ansel stood very still.

Of course. Of course. She had thought about wind through the louvers and snow load and the sag of the case timbers, and she had not thought about the simplest force in the building: the bells themselves. Twelve strokes in six minutes, each one sending a shudder through the frame, through the floor, through the rails her panels ran on, through the neat stacks of ledger sheets she had fanned ready for their fall. The first stroke would shuffle them. The third would tangle them. By the sixth, they'd be a jammed wad wedged sideways in the chamber mouth, and the mechanism would grind against its own cargo and stall, and the ceremony would end with Corven smiling and the clock struck dumb, holding its secrets forever.

She closed her eyes. *Thank you,* she signed.

Odile shrugged, pleased and embarrassed at once, and went to unpack the basket.

Ansel worked until the middle of the night on the answer, and the answer, when it came, was beautiful in the way that only necessity makes things beautiful: a follower. A light spring-loaded finger of brass, mounted at the chamber's threshold, pressing gently downward on the topmost sheet — just enough pressure to keep the stack coherent, to make the pages leave one at a time, in order, no matter what the bells did to the air. Like a deal of cards dealt by a careful hand. The ledgers would not rain chaotically. They would be *read out*, sheet by sheet, in the order Tomás Quintana had written them, in the order the payments had been made, in the order the witnesses had been bought — and order, Ansel knew, was the difference between a pile of paper and testimony.

At some point she became aware that Ivo had not gone home. He sat by the dying lamp with his cap in his lap, watching her work with an expression she couldn't read until he spoke.

"Your father mended my mother's mantel clock the winter before the strike," he said. "Cost him nothing. Said a house shouldn't have to live with a lie in it." He nodded at the great dark shape of the master clock above them, at the hidden chamber inside it. "That one's been living with a lie in it for nine years. You're evicting it."

Ansel's throat was too tight to answer. She signed *thank you* to Odile, who translated aloud — Odile's voice was rough and unpracticed from disuse, and she spent it rarely and deliberately, like coinage — and the two words filled the tower anyway.

* * *

On the third night, she assembled everything.

The locking plate with its thirteen notches went into the striking train at midnight, when the tower was coldest and the patrol below had passed with a cough she was learning to recognize. The trip lever went onto the fly fan's arbor. The follower spring went to the chamber threshold. The second escapement, already built and waiting in its bracket behind the false panel, received its detent, its connection to the trip rod she had run down through a channel drilled in the case's inner post — a channel disguised as an old woodworm scar, filled with wax tinted to match the timber.

Then there was nothing left but the truth test.

She could not run the full train — not with the weights connected, not with the whole town able to hear any stray stroke — so she did what her father had taught her to do when a finished mechanism could not be safely exercised: she listened to it. She put her ear against the case, closed her eyes, and had Ivo crank the going train slowly by hand while she followed the chain of consequence link by link: hour wheel, lifting piece, pallet, notch — one, two, three — the fan spinning and stilling, the lever poised, falling, the faint far-off click of the detent, the whisper of the worm screw taking up its burden.

Click. Click. Click. Eleven seconds per degree. Four minutes to openness.

"It'll do," she said aloud, and her voice came out strange, hoarse, as if she'd been the one ringing all night.

Below her, Vell slept under a foot of snow, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, the magistrate's house dark on the hill, the garrison lamps burning their bored yellow watch. Somewhere in that sleeping town were two hundred people who did not yet know they had been drafted into a plan, who would walk to the ceremony in their best coats expecting music and speeches and taxes, and would receive instead the arithmetic of their own betrayal, counted out stroke by stroke.

Ansel packed her tools, banked the lamp, and pressed her palm flat against the clock's case — the way she had once, as a child, pressed her palm against her father's chest to feel the ticking there, the joke he made of his own trade, *a clockmaker's heart, see, keeps better time than yours*.

"Midnight," she whispered to it. "Thirteen. Make it clean."

And it was only later — descending the pendulum shaft by feel, rope biting her palms, Odile's knots holding her weight over the dark as they had held it once before — that she heard the footsteps in the observatory below. Heavy ones. Deliberate ones. Stopping at the base of the shaft.

A lantern flared, and Brannock's voice came up out of the blackness, quiet and terrible:

"You'd best come down, apprentice. The magistrate wants a word about clocks — and he knows yours is lying to him."
