## 29. Betrayal at the Inn

Brannock had kept the tower keys on a ring of iron so old it had gone the color of dried blood, and for thirty years he had carried them the way a priest carries a crucifix — not because he loved the weight, but because he could not imagine his hand without it. Tonight, walking down from the observatory in the blue hour before full dark, he felt them differently. He felt them like coins paid out for something he hadn't agreed to sell.

The snow squeaked under his boots. Vell lay quiet beneath its winter hush, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, and from the square below came the smell of the Lantern Inn — woodsmoke and roasting onions and the particular sweetness of Marta Sable's spiced cider that she only brewed when she expected trouble or company, and often could not have told you which.

He stopped at the head of the stairs that led down into the square and stood there longer than a man with no business dawdling ought to stand.

Corven's summons had come at noon. Not a summons — Corven was too careful for that, too careful by half — but an invitation to take supper at the magistrate's house, just the two of them, just old friends, and Brannock had known before he'd finished lacing his boots what the supper would cost. He'd sat across from Corven through three courses of venison and listened to the man talk about the records office fire as though it were weather, as though fire simply happened to buildings that held inconvenient paper, and then Corven had set down his fork and said, with the mildness of a man discussing a fence repair:

"Tomorrow, Brannock. The observatory. A thorough search. I'll want your men, and I'll want you there when they go through the clock."

"And if I ask what we're looking for?"

"You won't." Corven had smiled. It was not an unkind smile; that was the horror of it. "You've been my warden for thirty years. You've never once asked me what we're looking for. That's why you're still my warden."

Brannock had eaten the rest of his supper in silence and drunk the wine he was offered and hated himself for both, and now here he stood above the square, keys in his pocket, doing arithmetic he had spent a lifetime avoiding.

* * *

He had known Ansel Quintana — no. He corrected himself even in the privacy of his own skull, where no one could hear the name. He had known *the apprentice*, the Quintana boy, since the day the forged letter arrived. That was the thing about Brannock that Corven had never understood, though Corven thought he understood everything: Brannock noticed. Thirty years of warden's work in a mountain town taught a man to notice — the way a stranger's boots were broken in wrong for the mountains, the way the apprentice's hands were small and scarred in the pattern of fine work rather than rough, the way the boy — the girl — flinched, only slightly, only once, when old Ivo had said the name Tomás aloud over his beer like a toast to a ghost.

Brannock had said nothing. For months he had said nothing, and he had told himself it was indifference, and then he had told himself it was prudence, and somewhere along the way he had stopped telling himself anything at all and simply watched, the way he watched weather come over the pass, knowing there was nothing to be done about it and watching anyway.

But this was different. Tomorrow soldiers would tear the clock apart plank by plank, and whatever the apprentice had hidden inside it — and Brannock would have bet his pension there was something, because the girl looked at that clock the way a widow looks at a grave — whatever it was would be found, and whoever had helped hide it would be found alongside it.

And the helpers, he thought, descending the stairs now, his decision arriving in his body before it finished arriving in his mind — the helpers drank at the Lantern Inn.

* * *

Marta Sable was behind the bar when he came in, which was unusual enough that half the room noticed. She left the serving to her girl most evenings and held court from the corner table where she could see the door, the windows, and the faces of everyone who entered — habits, Brannock had always assumed, from her running days, the ones she never spoke of and everyone knew about anyway. Tonight she was polishing glasses that were already clean, and when the door shut behind him and the cold fell away, she looked up, and her eyes went over his face the way a farrier goes over a hoof: quick, professional, finding the crack.

"Warden." She set down the glass. "You look like a man who's been eating food he didn't like."

"Venison," Brannock said. "At the magistrate's."

Something moved behind Marta's eyes — not surprise; Brannock realized with a small cold shock that she had been waiting for exactly this, perhaps for weeks — and she jerked her chin toward the back room. "Cider's better back here. Less ears."

The back room smelled of apples and lamp oil and the ghost of every cargo Marta had ever moved through these mountains. She poured two cups without asking whether he wanted one, pushed one across the scarred table, and sat down opposite him with her forearms on the wood, and waited. She was very good at waiting. Brannock understood suddenly that this room had hosted a hundred conversations that began exactly this way, and that he was the first warden ever to sit in the guest's chair.

"I'm going to say a thing," Brannock said, "and then I'm going to drink this cider, and then neither of us is going to remember I said it."

Marta's mouth twitched. "That's how the best conversations start."

"Tomorrow morning. Early. There'll be a search of the observatory — soldiers, Corven's own men, not mine. They'll go through the tower top to bottom, and they'll open the clock." He turned the cup in his hands. His knuckles had gone the color of the keyring. "Whatever's in that clock, it won't be there after tonight."

The lamp between them hissed softly. Somewhere in the front room, someone laughed, ordinary and warm, and the sound seemed to belong to another country.

Marta did not thank him. Brannock noted that, and was grateful for it — gratitude would have made it charity, and this was not charity. She only asked, in the flat voice of a woman confirming a delivery schedule: "What time?"

"First light. Maybe before. Corven doesn't trust mornings; he'll want it done while the town's still asleep."

"Then it'll be done while the town's still asleep." She stood, already moving, pulling a lantern down from its hook, and then she stopped, halfway between the table and the door, and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to.

"Why?" she said. "Thirty years you've been his dog, Brannock. Why unchain yourself now?"

It was a fair question and a cruel one, and he let it sit while he finished the cider, because he owed her more than a shrug and less than a speech. When the cup was empty he set it down carefully, the way he did everything, and said:

"I hung men for Corven. Two of them, over the years, on his word and his evidence, and I checked his evidence both times because that was my work, checking, and both times it was good enough, and I went home and slept." He rose, joints cracking like green wood. "I never checked the first time. Twenty-two years ago. I took the witnesses' word because the magistrate vouched for them, and a man they hanged off that word had children, and one of those children is up in my tower right now fixing my clock better than it has run in forty years, and tomorrow I was going to hand her to him." He pulled his coat straight. "I'm not going to sleep tonight either, Marta Sable. But I mean to sleep tomorrow night."

He walked out through the taproom without looking at anyone, and if his step was slower than usual, no one in Vell was rude enough to say so.

* * *

The inn emptied fast after that, faster than Marta could have managed honestly, and Brannock, watching from the shadowed archway of the grain store across the square, admired the craft of it. She didn't clear the room — that would have raised eyebrows and worse. She simply announced that the cellar needed re-stocking before the deep cold came and that any man who wanted his credit extended another month would carry crates for it, and Vell's miners, who understood debt the way fish understand water, carried crates. By the time the church bell rang nine, the Lantern Inn stood dark except for one lamp in the kitchen window, and carts began to move in the yard with their lamps hooded, going out the back toward the icehouse road, quiet as breath.

They were moving more than cider. Brannock knew it and made himself keep walking, past the inn, past the well, around by the chandlery, giving the operation its distance the way a man gives a wild animal distance — not because he feared it, but because his nearness could kill it.

At the corner by the chandlery, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step beside him, and Brannock's heart slammed once before he recognized the narrow shoulders and the cap pulled low: the apprentice. The Quintana girl. She had her hands in her pockets and her chin tucked against the cold, and she walked beside him for twenty paces before she spoke.

"You told her."

Her voice was low, pitched under the wind. In the dark she might have been any boy of the town, and Brannock understood again how she had survived so long wearing a dead brother's name like a coat.

"I told her," he said.

"Why?"

He considered lying to her, and discarded it — she had a mechanic's ear for false notes; he'd heard her diagnose a cracked gear tooth by sound alone through two floors of tower.

"Because I checked the evidence," he said. "Twenty-two years late. But I checked it."

She stopped walking. In the dark he could not see her face, only the shape of her stillness, and the stillness lasted long enough that he counted his own heartbeats against it — four, five, six.

"The tollhouse guard," she said at last, and her voice had gone strange and careful, "owes money to a man in Lowmark who collects through a woman who drinks at your wife's cousin's tavern. If anyone asks why Dree looks the other way Thursday night, that's the thread they'll pull. Make sure it pulls slow."

Then she was gone, swallowed by the dark toward the icehouse road, and Brannock stood alone in the cold with his mouth open, understanding that he had just been given instructions — by the fugitive, for the protection of the betrayal he himself had arranged — and that he intended to follow them.

* * *

He did not go home. He climbed the tower instead, because it was his tower, and because if this was to be the last night it was wholly his, he wanted to spend it among the gears.

The master clock filled the stairwell with its breathing — that vast slow exhalation of the pendulum, tick and tock stretched over four seconds each, the heartbeat of Vell that the town had stopped hearing generations ago the way a man stops hearing his own heart. Brannock climbed to the movement room and lit the work lamp and stood under the great wheel train with his cap in his hands like a man in church.

The apprentice had done beautiful work. He knew almost nothing of clockmaking and everything of maintenance, and even his ignorant eye could see it: the cleaned pivots bright as new coin, the escapement oiled with a restraint that spoke of real love, the striking train rebuilt with parts that had not existed in this tower a season ago. She had brought the dead thing back. Whatever else she was — fugitive, forger, the daughter of a man whose name the town still lowered its voice to say — she had given Vell back its time.

And tomorrow Corven's soldiers would put their boots in here.

Unless.

Brannock stood a long while, listening to the pendulum. Then he took out his keyring, found the small key that fit the movement-room door, and did not lock it. Instead he locked the outer tower doors — all three, properly, with the full ceremony of his office, chains and bars and the great iron hasps — so that when Corven's men arrived at first light, they would find a warden who had sealed his tower overnight for safety, as regulations required in hard frost, requiring the magistrate's authority to be produced in writing before the seals could be broken. It would buy hours. Perhaps a day. Perhaps only the appearance of obstruction, deniable, procedural, the stalling of a fussy old man in love with his rulebook.

It was not much. He knew it was not much. But it was sideways, and sideways was the only direction Brannock had ever been able to move.

On his way down, he passed the case of the clock and paused, and on an impulse he would never explain to anyone, laid his palm flat against the wooden panel below the escapement chamber, where the apprentice's secret slept if it slept anywhere.

"Hold together," he told it, gruffly, foolishly, to a piece of furniture, in an empty tower. "Hold together till spring."

The pendulum answered, tick, and tock, indifferent and faithful, keeping faith the only way it knew.

* * *

At first light, the soldiers came.

Brannock met them at the tower doors in his full dress coat, with the regulation book under his arm and the frost-seal order signed and dated in his own hand, and he was magnificent, and he was despised, and he bought them six hours. By noon Corven's written authority had arrived by runner, and the seals came off, and the search began — thorough, destructive, methodical, the work of men who enjoyed breaking things and had been given permission. They pulled the clock's panels, sounded the walls, emptied the astronomer's cabinets, and found nothing but dust and a dead mouse and the immaculate, humming machinery of a clock that ran better than any clock in the province.

By evening the report went to Corven: the observatory clean, the clock innocent, the tower empty.

In the taproom of the Lantern Inn, reopened that afternoon as though it had never closed, Marta Sable poured Brannock a cider on the house and said nothing at all, which was the loudest thanks he had ever received. And in the corner, hooded and half-asleep over her accounts, the clerk Elias Corven read his father's copy of the search report twice, folded it small, and burned it in the candle flame — because the report was true, and because he knew, the way one knows a clock is lying by the sound of its tick, that true was exactly what his father would refuse to believe.

Up in the tower, in the dark, the pendulum swung on.

And inside the case, behind a panel no soldier had thought to weigh, the ledgers waited — patient as ore, patient as grief — for midnight, and for thirteen strokes.
