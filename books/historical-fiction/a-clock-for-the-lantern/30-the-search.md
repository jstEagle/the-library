## 30. The Search

Odile felt the soldiers before she heard them — a tremor in the floorboards of the observatory's lower gallery, a shiver running up through the stone the way a struck bell shivers after its note has already gone. Sound was a country she visited through glass and vibration and the careful architecture of other people's mouths, but her feet had always been honest. Feet did not lie. Something heavy was climbing the observatory road.

She was alone in the meridian room, cataloguing star transits by lamplight, when the lantern on the tower's south face began to speak.

It had been Marta's idea to hang it there — an old signal lamp of smuggler make, its shutters worn smooth by twenty years of illicit weather — and it had been Ansel who taught herself the alphabet Odile spelled out across the square at midnight, her fingers clumsy at first, then quicker, then fluent, until the two of them could argue about gear tolerances in light the way other people argued in whispers. Now the lamp stuttered: *soldiers coming. stay away from glass.*

Odile set down her pen. Her heart did not pound so much as go very quiet, the way the observatory went quiet before a storm rolled over the shoulder of the mountain — a hush that was not peace but attention.

*How many,* she signaled back, though she knew the lamp's answer would come from Ansel, watching from the inn's upper window across the square.

*Twelve. Brannock leading them. They have a writ.*

The lamp went dark. Odile closed her ledger, squared its corners out of habit, and considered the room around her. On the long table lay three weeks of Odile's own work: the transcription, forty pages of braille punched into heavy paper, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false bottom in the star-chart drawer, and beside it the ciphered notes in her small, exact hand — sums, dates, names, the whole rotten arithmetic of Magistrate Corven's conscience rendered into dots a sighted man could not read and a deaf girl could recite in the dark. Those were safe. Those were hers to protect.

But the originals — the ledgers themselves, Tomás Quintana's name written and rewritten in payments and signatures like a wound being kept open on purpose — those sat where Ansel had hidden them eleven days ago, inside the great clock's case, behind the panel weighted with the counterpoise, in the one place in Vell no searching soldier would think to look twice at because no one but a clockmaker would ever have reason to open it.

*Let them come to the clock,* Ansel had said, when they had planned for this night without quite believing it would arrive. *A search is a performance. Corven wants the town to see him tearing through the observatory. Let him perform. The clock will sit there being enormous and dull and full of gears, and gears are the one thing in this world that frighten men who cannot tell time from machinery.*

Odile blew out her lamp, and waited for the knocking.

* * *

They knocked the way armies knock — once, formally, and then not at all.

She opened the door to torchlight and snow-melt and the broad gray bulk of Head Warden Brannock, whose face she had learned to read across two years of estate business between her father and the magistrate's office. His lips were moving; she watched them with the flat patience she gave all lip-readers who did not know they were being read.

"—writ signed by Magistrate Corven, authorizing search of the observatory premises. Where is your father?"

Asleep, she mouthed, and let her face do the rest: the slight downward pull of annoyance, the shrug of a daughter inconvenienced. It was true enough. Her father slept like the dead on fair nights and would wake to find his library rearranged and blame the wind. Brannock studied her a moment longer. She saw him decide, with visible effort, that the astronomer's silent daughter was furniture — a piece of the observatory like the orrery, odd, harmless, to be worked around.

Furniture heard everything. Furniture watched twelve soldiers fill her father's hall like water filling a hull, boots shedding snow onto the rugs her mother had woven, lanterns thrust into corners where no lantern belonged. She heard their work the way she heard everything now, with her whole body: the floor's complaint under eight hundred pounds of armed certainty, the wet creak of leather, a shelf being cleared with a forearm's sweep and the crack of a ceramic inkwell she had owned since she was nine. Someone laughed. Someone else said something containing the word *papers*, and another voice answered with *burn what's loose, take what's bound* — no, not burn. She made herself look, made herself catch the syllables off the speaker's turned mouth: *list what's bound.* Corven wanted it legal-looking. Corven wanted theft dressed as inventory.

Brannock stood apart by the cold hearth, not pretending to direct anything, his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral he disapproved of. Twice his eyes found Odile across the room, and twice she looked back at him without lowering her gaze, and both times he was the one who looked away.

Good, she thought, with a coldness that surprised her. Be ashamed. Ashamed men are slow.

* * *

They took the stairs to the meridian room four abreast, and Odile followed at their heels because following was invisible and being left below was not. In the star-chart room a sergeant wrenched open the drawers one by one — false bottom, true bottom, her heart a held bellows — and passed over the oilcloth packet because it sat beneath a decade of her father's lunar observations, dense as gravel, and no man in a hurry reads astronomy. He did pause at the braille. He held a page up to the lantern, frowned at the constellation of raised dots, and turned to her.

"What is this? What language is this?"

*Blind-writing,* she shaped slowly, letting her lips be exaggerated, foreign, hard to follow. *For my eyes. I read with fingers.* She held out her hand, palm up: give it here. And when he hesitated, she crossed the room and ran her fingertips down the page and spoke aloud — her voice came out rusty, flattened, exactly right — "Nineteenth of December. Transit of Regulus, four minutes past two. Cloud from the west." She let her fingers linger, reading, patient as winter. "Star positions. Would you like me to read you all of it?"

The sergeant looked at the page, then at her white, unblinking eyes in the lantern light, and put it back in the drawer as if it had burned him a little. Superstition, she thought, watching him wipe his palm on his coat. Every ignorant man carries a private pagan somewhere behind his ribs, and tonight hers was wearing it like armor.

Marta had told her once, over the inn's regulator clock with its pendulum swinging gold in the lamplight: *You don't hide a thing by making it dark, girl. You hide it by making it boring. Darkness makes men curious. Boredom makes them leave.* Odile had filed the sentence away the way she filed all speech, precisely and forever, and spent from it now.

An hour past midnight they came down the spiral stair to the clock room, and Odile's nerve, which had been stretched taut as a suspension wire since the first knock, sang one low, terrible note.

The master clock filled the chamber end to end, nine feet of walnut and brass, its pendulum breathing in the dark behind the case glass — tick, tock, tick, tock, the heartbeat Ansel had restored stroke by stroke, the heartbeat that was, tonight, also a strongbox. The ledgers lay six inches from the sergeant's lantern, behind a panel whose seam was invisible unless you knew to feel for the counterweight's shift, and Ansel was across the square, and Odile was the only thing standing between twelve men and her best friend's whole life.

She could not follow them in without seeming to care. So she cared enormously, silently, from the doorway, and made her face into a mask of boredom so complete it was almost a work of art.

They opened the case's front — the glass door, the dial works, the winding drums, all the ordinary organs any curious fool might poke. A corporal put his head half into the movement and came out webbed with grease and swearing. The sergeant prodded the weights with a bayonet point, rang a small dull note off the great wheel, grunted. Tick, tock, went the escapement, above their heads, patient as ore, patient as grief. One of them said something Odile was glad to catch only in pieces — *why anyone'd guard a clock* — and the sergeant said, *because the magistrate says,* and that settled it, because that was how Vell had been governed for thirty years.

Then the youngest soldier, a boy with a farm boy's shoulders still, pointed at the back panel and said, "What's behind that?"

The room went very still for Odile. The pendulum did not. Tick. Tock. She watched the sergeant turn, watched his mouth form *nothing, it's the case back* — watched him believe it even as he said it, because the panel was flush and pegged and greased to look thirty years untouched, because Ansel Quintana was an artist and artists are the best liars in the world, they lie in brass and it holds its truth forever.

"Case back," the sergeant repeated, and rapped it once with his knuckles — and the sound that came back was solid, because Ansel had backed the panel with lead sheet against just this question, had stood in the inn kitchen explaining resonant frequency to Marta while Odile spelled questions back across the table, and Odile could have kissed that lead sheet if lead sheets were kissable.

"Onward," said Brannock, from the doorway, in a voice pitched flat and final. His lips barely moved. "Clock's sealed. Nothing in a clock but a clock."

And there it was, Odile thought, as the squad shuffled out under the pendulum's unhurried stare — there was Brannock's soul, decided at last, sideways, in six words. He had tipped Corven off, yes; he had sent his warning to Marta first, an hour ahead of the writ, and Marta had banked her fires and walked the inn's guests out the back and stood in the square now with her arms folded, watching the soldiers' lanterns stream down the observatory steps like a controlled hemorrhage. Brannock had chosen the town over the magistrate and could never say so, and would carry it the way Odile carried sound: invisibly, entirely, in the body.

At the door he paused beside her. His mouth moved, carefully, over-articulated, the way people did when they half remembered she read lips and wanted to be understood without saying anything aloud worth repeating:

"It isn't here. Whatever they think."

She gave him nothing. She gave him the flat stare of the moon, and he nodded as if she had answered, and was gone.

* * *

The soldiers regrouped in the square, and Odile climbed to the meridian room window to watch, because watching was her trade and her weapon and her consolation. Below, the search became a smaller, meaner thing: lanterns bobbing to the records office annex, which had already burned once and had little left but char; lanterns at the tollhouse road; one lantern detached from the rest and crossing the square toward the Lantern Inn, where Marta Sable stood on her own step with a shotgun broken open over her arm like a lady's fan, entirely courteous, entirely immovable.

From the inn's upper gable, a shutter cracked. Light spilled, was shuttered, spilled again — the signal lamp speaking low and fast, aimed up at the observatory, meant for Odile's eyes alone among all the town.

*They took nothing,* Ansel spelled. *Are you hurt.*

*I am furniture,* Odile signaled back, and felt the corner of her mouth lift. *Furniture is uninjured. They broke my inkwell.*

Below, the lantern at the inn's step retreated. Somewhere a sergeant was calling the roll of a failed expedition. The snow had started again, fat slow flakes sifting down through the torchlight, and the soldiers' shadows stretched long and uncertain across the square, pointing every direction at once, pointing at nothing.

Then the last signal came, and Odile's breath went thin.

*Corven knows the search failed. He is done searching quietly. Ansel — he has summoned her. Tomorrow. To his house. Alone.*

Odile stood a long moment with her hand on the shuttered lamp, feeling the message cool in the air between them, feeling the weight of what answering meant. Across the square the inn's gable window glowed, steady, waiting. Inside the tower the pendulum swung on, counting down toward midnight and thirteen strokes, toward a crowd that could not be dispersed and a dead man's name waiting in the dark like a seed. But between this night and that midnight stood one more door, one more room, one more conversation with the man who had bought her father's death in a ledger and signed it in a margin.

Her fingers moved. She spelled it plainly, so there could be no mistaking across sixty yards of falling snow:

*Tell her we will be with her. All of us. Tell her she does not walk into that house alone.*

The shutter across the square stayed dark a moment. Then it opened wide, and the light that came back was not code at all — just one long, unbroken beam, held steady, held home, the way you hold out a hand.

Odile closed the shutter, took up her ciphered pages, and went down through the sleeping observatory to keep the appointment her whole life had been making for her.
