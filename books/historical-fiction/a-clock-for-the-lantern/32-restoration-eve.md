## 32. Restoration Eve

The clock had never sounded so loud as it did in silence.

Ansel stood in the tower's heart with her ear against the great wheel housing, listening to nothing, and the nothing roared. Tomorrow at dusk the whole town would climb the square — miners still gray-faced from shift, shopkeepers in their good coats, children hoisted onto shoulders — and Corven would stand beneath the dial in his fur-trimmed coat and speak of renewal and civic pride and, if the rumors Marta carried home were true, of new taxes dressed in ceremony's clothes. And Ansel would stand among them, one hand in her pocket, her fingers resting on a lever she had filed down herself until it moved like silk.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only this: the smell of oil and cold iron, four days without proper sleep, and the last gear of the striking train seated true in its jewel bearing, waiting for midnight to prove her.

She checked it anyway. Seventh time. The pallets engaged, released, caught. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; everything else in her felt like a spring wound past its limit.

*You are a clockmaker,* she told herself. *Tonight you are allowed to be nothing else.*

She did not believe it. She had not believed anything simple about herself since the ledgers came out of the escapement chamber with her father's name inside them, since Brannock said *I know who you are* in a voice almost kind, since Corven sat across from her in his study with her face reflected in the decanter between them and said, softly, *Quintana.* But the hands knew their work even when the mind frayed, and so she worked, and the hours fell away like filings.

When she finally descended, the square was already strung with lanterns. Men on ladders, women directing, the inn's cart loaded with benches. Vell was decorating itself for a lie, though most of them didn't know it — they thought tomorrow was about a clock. In a way, Ansel thought, climbing the Lantern Inn's back stairs, it was.

Marta Sable was awake. Of course she was. She sat at the kitchen table with tea gone cold in front of her and a map folded into eighths beside her hand, and when Ansel came in she looked up with that flat, assessing gaze that had stopped frightening Ansel sometime around midwinter and become, instead, something like shelter.

"You look like something the river refused," Marta said.

"I feel worse than that."

"Eat." There was bread and hard cheese and the end of a jar of pickled onions, and Ansel ate standing up, too tired to sit, watching Marta fold the map away without comment. Three checkpoints, memorized twice over now. A decoy satchel that had passed the tollhouse clean. A garrison gate signed through with a dead man's seal by a magistrate's son who no longer slept well. And a river frozen solid, which had broken every plan except the one nobody could stop now, because the clock would run tomorrow whether Corven liked it or not.

"Odile's signals are set," Marta said. "Ivo's got six men who'll stand near the tower steps. Elias swears the garrison roster for tomorrow night is mostly names his father can't trust to shoot straight."

"And if he does order them to?"

Marta turned her cup in its saucer. "Then we find out what Vell is made of."

Ansel set down the crust she wasn't eating. "I keep thinking I should feel afraid."

"Do you?"

"No. That's what frightens me."

Marta studied her a long moment. Outside, someone dropped a lantern-glass and swore, and laughter followed. The town, preparing its pageant. "That's not frightlessness," Marta said at last. "That's being finished. You've done all the work your hands can do. Fear's got nowhere left to live." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the dresser, and came back with something in her hands that made Ansel go very still.

A comb. A bottle of sweet oil. A hand mirror, silver-backed, old.

"Sit," Marta said.

"Marta—"

"You've been wearing a boy's cap under a workman's coat for three years, girl, and tomorrow you walk into that square as whoever you decide to be. I'm not telling you which. But you'll choose it looking at yourself, not avoiding yourself. Sit down."

The exhaustion helped. That was the strange truth Ansel would remember afterward — that she was too tired to perform the habit of hiding, that hiding had become labor and her body simply refused the overtime. She sat. The chair creaked. Marta stood behind her and began, without ceremony, to draw the comb through Ansel's hair.

It had not been loose in public since she was nineteen. It was longer than anyone in Vell could have guessed, thick and dark where it escaped the cap each night, and Marta worked through the tangles with a smuggler's patience — no hurry, no flinching, working out knots the way one works out a route past a checkpoint: a little at a time, always from the ends, never forcing.

"My mother used to braid it," Ansel heard herself say. The words came from somewhere below thought. "Before my father—" She stopped.

"I know," Marta said. "Go on or don't. Either way I'm not stopping."

"She'd do it by the cookfire. Two braids for workdays, one for feast days. She said hair was the first ledger a woman keeps — everything you lived shows up in it eventually." Ansel watched in the mirror as Marta's broad hands divided the weight of it, sectioned it, began. "I cut it short when I took my brother's name. Joaquín's name. I thought if I looked like him I'd be safe. He was dead two years by then; nobody in this country would know the difference."

"And did you look like him?"

"No," Ansel said. "I looked like me, pretending. For years. Then somewhere along the way I stopped pretending and just... was. Both things at once. A boy called Ansel Quintana's ghost and a girl called nobody." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, because there was no one in the room she needed to be strong for. "Tomorrow Corven says *Quintana* in front of the whole town and either the name hangs me or it frees me, and I found today that I don't care which anymore. I care that it's mine."

Marta's hands kept moving, sure and slow, crossing strand over strand. In the mirror Ansel watched her own face change — not the features, but the thing behind them. It was like watching a mechanism come into time. All her life the parts had been true; they had simply been running against a wrong reference, beating fast or slow against a world that demanded she read as something she wasn't. Now, with the braid forming dark and heavy down her back, some internal escapement clicked into engagement.

"There," Marta said quietly. "Look at yourself."

Ansel looked. A woman sat in the silver glass — exhausted, pale, twenty-three years old with her father's jaw and her mother's braids — and Ansel did not look away from her. That was the whole miracle. She did not look away.

"One braid," Ansel said. "Feast day."

"That's what I'm doing, girl." A pause, and then, rougher: "Your mother would have done it tighter. But mine does well enough."

*Mine.* Ansel turned her head and looked up at her, and Marta scowled to cover whatever was in her own face, and tightened the braid's end with a strip of red cloth torn from her apron hem.

Footsteps on the stairs — Odile's particular rhythm, light and quick, and then she was in the doorway with her breath fogging and her eyes going straight to the mirror, to the braid, to Ansel's bare head. Her face did something complicated and wonderful. She raised both hands and signed, fast: *Beautiful. Like a queen. No — better. Queens inherit. You chose.*

"I understood all of that," Ansel said, marveling. Odile had been teaching her signs since midwinter, patient as snowfall, and tonight, for the first time, a full sentence had arrived whole.

Odile grinned and signed more slowly: *Tomorrow, stand where I can see your face. Whatever happens, I want to read it.*

"Whatever happens," Ansel agreed, and reached up, and touched the braid as though testing whether it was real.

Ivo came next, stamping snow off his boots, took one look, and put his hand over his mouth. When he lowered it his eyes were wet. "Begging your pardon," he said gruffly, "but you've your mother's look. I saw her at the pit gates once, the year of the strike. She handed out bread to the men coming off shift." He cleared his throat. "Tomás talked about her the way other men talk about saints."

Elias arrived last, from the back door, hood up, and stopped dead just inside it. He looked at Ansel for a long moment and seemed unable to arrange his expression, and finally said, "I was going to report the garrison positions. I—" He stopped again. "He won't recognize you. That's the first good news I've brought in weeks. He thinks the apprentice is still the apprentice."

"He'll know soon enough," Ansel said. She stood, and the braid swung against her spine like a pendulum finding its arc, and around the kitchen table her people gathered without arranging themselves to — Marta, Ivo, Odile, Elias — the family assembled not by blood but by choice and circumstance and a stolen ledger, and Ansel found her exhaustion had transformed into something else entirely, something with edges and a fine bright weight.

"Tomorrow at dusk," she said, "Corven stands under my clock and tells this town the future belongs to order. At midnight, the clock answers." She looked around the table. "One braid. Feast day. Let's dress for it."

Outside, the lanterns of Vell burned steady in the windless dark, and above them, faint through the frost-furred windows, came the sound none of them had heard yet but all of them were waiting for: the great wheel turning over once, in test, deep in the tower — a single low note, like a held breath before speech.

Midnight was coming. This time, it would strike thirteen.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments, and Ansel dreamed of her father's hands — not the gallows, for once, but the hands themselves, filing a pallet stone at the kitchen table while her mother's firelight moved over both of them, and in the dream he was saying something she couldn't hear, over and over, patient, and she leaned closer and closer trying to catch it, and woke at dawn with the word still uncaught and the shape of it familiar as her own pulse.

The day went by in preparations that felt like a held chord. Marta baked until the inn smelled of cardamom and closed coal-smoke, feeding half the square off the kitchen pass. Ivo drilled his six men in gestures alone, standing them along the tower's flank like fence posts. Odile spent the morning on the observatory gallery with three lanterns, flashing silent rehearsals across the rooftops to Elias, who answered from the garrison wall in clumsy, earnest strokes that made her laugh — a sound like struck glass, rare enough that Ivo paused his drilling to listen.

And Ansel climbed the tower one last time before the crowd, and stood in the chamber where her father's name lay folded in oilcloth behind the second escapement, and put her palm flat against the case.

"Not revenge," she said aloud, to the gears, to the man who had taught her gears. "Testimony. You taught me the difference, even if you never said it."

The clock ticked on, indifferent and faithful, counting down the daylight she had left. Below, the square began to fill — a low murmur rising like water behind a weir — and somewhere in it, unmistakable even at distance, the magistrate's voice warming up for ceremony.

Ansel buttoned her coat over the lever, squared the borrowed wool cap on her head — but worn now atop the braid, not to bury it, a detail visible to anyone who cared to look, and she found she wanted it seen — and started down the stairs toward the noise, the lanterns, the crowd, the hour.

The clock would speak at midnight. First, she intended to watch Corven dig his own pulpit.
