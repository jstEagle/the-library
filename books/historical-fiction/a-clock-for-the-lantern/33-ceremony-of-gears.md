## 33. Ceremony of Gears

The morning of the ceremony came down off the mountain like something poured — a thin grey light that thickened by degrees into silver, then into a pale, cold gold that lay along the rooftops of Vell and made the frost on the square glitter as if the town had been dusted with ground glass. Ansel stood at the window of her room above the Lantern Inn and watched it happen, her breath fogging the pane in slow, measured clouds. She had been awake since three. She had not so much slept as lain in the dark listening to the tower, to the great clock's heart ticking above the town she had come to love against every instinct of self-preservation.

It was running. That was the miracle she still could not make herself take for granted. Three days now it had run without falter — the escape wheel biting its teeth into time and letting go, biting and letting go, the pendulum swinging with the deep patient rhythm she had tuned by ear and by lamplight over eleven weeks of winter. She could hear it even from here, faint under the wind: *tick... tick... tick*, like a pulse felt through a wall. Like a heartbeat in the next room.

In her coat, hanging on the chair by the cold hearth, waited the release lever.

She had built it herself, of course. Everything about it was hers — the brass stock cut from an old regulator mainspring, the notch filed to catch the secondary escapement's trip-wire, the leather wrapping stitched so the whole thing looked, at a careless glance, like nothing more than a clock-mender's tool. A winding key, one might say. Something honest in a workman's pocket. Only when her thumb found the recessed stud and pressed, only then did the spring inside flex and the hooked tip swing free, ready to drop the counterweight that would open the hidden chamber above the striking train and let the clock do what she had rebuilt it to do.

Strike thirteen.

Ansel dressed slowly. Shirt, waistcoat, coat. She bound nothing today; there was no need, and Marta would have scolded her. Her hair hung in the braid Marta had made the night before — two nights ago now, on Restoration Eve — plaited openly, unashamedly, the way women in Vell wore theirs to church and market and funerals. It had hurt more than she expected, that small tenderness. Not the pulling of hair but the laying bare of it. For nine years she had tucked herself away behind a boy's name and a boy's flatness of manner, and last night Marta Sable had run a comb through her hair and hummed some old canal song and said, *there, now you look like somebody's daughter,* and Ansel had wept quietly into her sleeve while the innkeeper pretended very hard to be interested in the comb.

*Somebody's daughter.* Tomás Quintana's daughter. Today, if everything held, if the gears held and the crowd held and her own nerve held, the whole town would know whose daughter stood among them.

She took up the coat. The lever sat in the right-hand pocket like a small warm secret, though brass holds no heat and never has. She fancied it did anyway.

Downstairs, the Lantern was already roaring. Marta had opened early, and the common room was full of steam and voices — teamsters in from the valley road before the thaw closed it for good, fair-day bakers who had rented her ovens overnight, half of Vell's respectable poor warming themselves on the way to the square. The smell of scorched flour and tallow and wet wool wrapped around Ansel like a second coat.

Marta herself stood behind the bar, grey-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be two fork tines and a pencil, pouring small beer for children and something stronger for their fathers, and her eyes found Ansel the moment her boots hit the bottom stair. They always did. Eleven weeks of watching this woman watch her, and Ansel had stopped finding it unsettling somewhere around midwinter. Now it felt like being moored.

"You'll eat," Marta said. Not a question.

"I can't—"

"You'll eat." A bowl of porridge slid across the bar, heavy with honey and cream, followed by a heel of black bread with butter melting into its grain. "A clock-mender who faints at her own ceremony is no use to anyone. Sit. Chew. Brood afterward."

"I'm not brooding."

"You've been brooding since you were four feet tall, I'd wager." But Marta's voice gentled, and she leaned across the bar, close enough that her words reached Ansel alone beneath the din. "Ivo's in position. Odile's at the observatory with her lanterns, and Elias..." She paused, weighing how much to say in a room full of ears. "Elias is where he needs to be. Everything moves at midnight. Until then, you're just a girl watching a clock get blessed. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Eat your porridge."

Ansel ate her porridge. It was absurd, how much good it did.

* * *

The square filled through the afternoon the way a basin fills — first a scatter, then a sheet, then a pressing depth of people. Stalls went up along the east side selling roasted chestnuts and twisted sugar ropes and little tin clocks that didn't keep time but delighted children anyway. Someone had strung lanterns between the fountain and the tower steps, hundreds of them, unlit, waiting for dark like fruit waiting to ripen. The garrison turned out in dress uniform, maybe forty men drawn up in ranks along the south edge of the crowd, and Ansel noted — as she had trained herself all winter to note — that they stood loosely, rifles shouldered rather than presented, several of them craning like everyone else to see the tower's newly cleaned face. Miners' sons, most of them. Ivo had counted them once, name by name, over a map drawn in candle smoke.

She moved through the crowd and let it move through her. This was part of the craft too, the same discipline she'd used at the tollhouse: walk like you belong, look at nothing twice, carry your hands easy. A girl in a good coat with braided hair, come out for the festival. Nobody looked at her twice. Nine years of practice saw to that — though the thought arrived, unwelcome and sharp, that after tonight she might have to learn an entirely new way of walking. A way that invited looking.

The tower rose over the square, scrubbed and gleaming. She had climbed it for the last time yesterday evening to set the striking train, and the memory of that hour came back to her now with a mechanic's mixed pride and terror: the great wheels spread out in the lamplight like a sleeping animal's organs, the new trip-wire threaded along the case in a path only she knew, the secondary escapement cocked and holding, holding, holding, waiting for the lever's release to begin its slow arithmetic. At midnight, when the main train lifted the striking rack for twelve, her addition would hold it past the twelfth stroke — one more lift, one more fall, the thirteenth hammer-fall landing on a silence that would crack the square open like a struck bell.

And then the chamber above would open. And the pages would come down like snow.

She checked the pocket. Brass and leather. Still there. Of course it was there. She had checked it eleven times since breakfast and it had not developed legs.

"There you are."

Odile stood beside her at the fountain's edge, materializing out of the crowd the way she always did — soundless, sudden, her dark eyes bright with the particular pleasure she took in startling people. She wore her good shawl and her hands were already talking before Ansel had fully turned.

*You slept badly,* Odile signed. Her signs were quick and private, kept low between their bodies, learned over a hundred evenings of trading words back and forth across Marta's kitchen table. *Your eyes are wrong.*

"My eyes are fine."

*Liar.* Odile's mouth curved. Then, more seriously: *Ivo says the wind is from the north. Good carrying air. My signals will reach the observatory clean.*

"And if Corven posts men at the windows again?"

Odile's smile sharpened into something feral and lovely. She tapped her own temple, then made the sign they had agreed on months ago, in the telescope room, through glass: *I will show them lights that mean nothing and they will chase meanings that don't exist, and by the time they understand, midnight will have them.*

There were times, looking at Odile Ferrant, that Ansel understood exactly why astronomers' daughters grew up strange and magnificent. She squeezed her friend's hand — Odile permitted this, on occasion, as one permits a small dog its habits — and Odile squeezed back hard enough to grind knuckles, which meant *I love you* in the private grammar they'd built, and then she was gone, swallowed by the crowd toward the observatory path.

Ansel checked the pocket. Twelfth time.

* * *

Magistrate Corven took the platform at dusk.

They had built it fresh for the occasion, a scaffold of planed timber facing the tower steps, draped in bunting that the wind kept trying to steal. He ascended it slowly, deliberately, a man who understood theatre as well as any clock-maker understood trains — grey-haired, broad-shouldered still, his magistrate's chain catching the lantern-light as the town's lamplighters went along the strings and the square bloomed orange. Behind him stood Head Warden Brannock, hands clasped at his back, face like a door left shut, and beside Brannock, in clerk's black, stood Elias.

Ansel had not been able to look at Elias properly in six days. Not since the search. Not since the night soldiers tore through the observatory on Corven's order while Odile spun false signals across the dark and the true ledgers sat ten feet above their heads, sewn into the belly of the clock like a swaddled child. She looked at him now, briefly, sideways. He stood with his ledger-book under his arm and his father's shadow falling across him, and his eyes swept the crowd once, found her at the fountain, and passed on without pausing. Perfectly. It was the passing-on-without-pausing that told her he'd seen her.

Corven began to speak. His voice carried beautifully; she had to give him that. The years of judgments and proclamations had honed it to an instrument.

"People of Vell. Neighbors. On this night, after long months of labor, the great clock of our observatory — the beating heart of this town — runs again."

Applause, scattered then general. Ansel clapped along with them, palms stinging, and watched his face.

"For too long we have lived under darkness of one kind or another. The darkness of winter. The darkness of... difficulty." A pause, weighted, aimed. She knew that pause. Every soul in that square who had lived through the strike knew that pause; she saw old miners shift their weight, saw women fold their arms. "The darkness of unrest sown among us by agitators, by foreign notions, by men who taught us to distrust our neighbors and our betters alike. Those days are behind us. And tonight I bring you light of another kind."

Here it comes, she thought, and her hand found the pocket.

"The cost of restoration, of order, of the light we enjoy, must be borne together. By ordinance of my office, effective at thaw—" the paper in his hand rustled as he raised it "—a maintenance levy of two marks per household per quarter..."

The words washed over the crowd and she watched them take it — the flinches, the bitter arithmetic starting behind a hundred faces. Two marks a quarter, from households that had buried fathers in the collapse and been paid nothing. Two marks, gathered by the same office that had taken the miners' fund and used it, page by page, entry by entry, to buy the testimony that hanged Tomás Quintana.

Her hand closed around the lever. Brass and leather. Her thumb found the stud.

*Not yet.* Midnight was hours away. The plan was the plan: wait, endure, let him crown himself, let the town stand assembled shoulder to shoulder until the bell rang out the year's last hour and the clock spoke for the dead. Patience was the whole architecture of the thing. She had built patience into the striking train itself, wheel upon wheel upon wheel, each one waiting its turn.

But God, the waiting.

"...and I know," Corven was saying, warm now, expansive, spreading his hands to the lantern-lit square, "that Vell will bear this burden as it has borne all burdens. With duty. With unity. With—" he smiled, and the lanterns lit the smile from below like something rising out of a fire "—with respect for the instruments of order. Which reminds me."

The warmth dropped out of his voice between one breath and the next.

"Before the clock strikes, there is a small matter. It has come to my attention that certain persons may have entered this town under false pretenses. Papers forged. Names borrowed." His gaze moved along the crowd, unhurried, a man reading a room. "Persons who believe the past can be dug up and shaken at the living. I invite such persons to consider carefully, tonight, before midnight, what the consequences of mischief might be. Vell has hanged men for less."

The square went very quiet. Wind moved the lantern strings; somewhere a horse stamped; the fountain murmured on, indifferent.

Ansel did not move. She had spent nine years learning not to move. Her heart slammed against her ribs and she breathed around it, slow and shallow, a girl watching a ceremony, and her thumb rested on the stud of the release lever, and she thought with perfect clarity: *he doesn't know. If he knew, he wouldn't warn me. He'd simply take me.* It was a bluff, a fisherman's cast across dark water, hoping to feel something bite.

Wasn't it?

Beside Corven, Brannock's eyes found her across the crowd — found her, unmistakably, deliberately — and held for one long second. Then the warden looked away, back to the middle distance, face giving nothing. And Ansel could not tell, could not tell at all, whether that look had been a warning of danger or a promise of silence, and the not-telling sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Corven resumed his speech. The taxes, the unity, the great and glorious clock. Above him the tower face glowed, its hands crawling toward nine, and Ansel Quintana stood in the press of her neighbors with her hand in her pocket and her father's name folded up inside her like a letter eleven winters undelivered, and she counted strokes in her head that hadn't happened yet.

Three hours. One thousand people. One lever.

At the edge of the square, high on the observatory ridge, a single lantern blinked once — twice — and went still. Odile, watching. Waiting.

Ansel waited too.
