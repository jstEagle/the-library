## 34. Thirteen Strokes at Midnight

Ivo had heard the great clock strike midnight ten thousand times in his life, and he had thought himself past trembling at the sound of bells.

He stood at the edge of the square with his cap crushed in one fist, wedged between the baker's cart and a family of quarrymen he half remembered from the old days — Halvorsen's boys, grown thick through the shoulders, their father's knuckles reproduced exactly on their hands. The whole of Vell was there. He had known they would be. A town does not get its heart restarted after three winters of silence and stay home to count it. They came out in their good coats and their patched boots, children perched on fathers' necks, old women on kitchen chairs carried down from doorways, and every face tilted up toward the tower as though the tower were a pulpit and midnight a sermon.

Lanterns hung from the inn's eaves. Marta had lit every one of them; Ivo had watched her do it an hour ago, climbing the ladder herself when the boy she'd hired hesitated, her skirts pinned up, her breath smoking in the cold like a woman half her age. She had not looked at him. She had not needed to.

The magistrate's platform stood at the square's far end, draped in bunting that had faded from red to the color of old meat. Corven sat upon it beneath a canopy, gray and upright, his hands folded on the ledger of new taxes he intended to announce before the night was out. Ivo could see him clearly even at this distance because Ivo had spent forty years looking at that man across mine mouths and courtroom rails and the length of a rope. Some faces you learn the way you learn your own hands.

Beside Corven stood his son, hatless, pale as tallow. And somewhere in that crowd, small and dark-coated and indistinguishable from any other apprentice in Vell, was Ansel Quintana.

*Anselma,* Ivo corrected himself, silently, the way he had been practicing for weeks. *Her name is Anselma.* He had promised himself he would say it properly tonight, if there was anything left of him by the end to make promises to.

The tower's dial glowed faintly against the stars, and below it, the open arches of the belfry showed black. The wind coming off the mountain smelled of iron and snowmelt. Eleven fifty-eight.

Ivo counted his own heartbeats because he could not bear to count the minutes.

* * *

He had been twenty-six years old the last time a clock in Vell struck thirteen, and he remembered the sound of it the way other men remembered the sound of their mothers' voices. It had been the year of the strike, the winter the mines went quiet and the town went hungry, and Tomás Quintana had stood on a beer barrel outside the Lantern Inn and told four hundred men that the company's books were lies and the weighbridge was rigged and that a man who worked in the dark deserved more than darkness for his wages. Thirteen strokes had rung out over the square that night — a thing no honest mechanism does — and every miner within earshot had lifted his lamp and cheered, because thirteen was the signal, thirteen meant *we are awake, we are counting, we see what you have hidden in the extra hour.*

They had all known the trick then. Every man jack of them. The striking train could be set with an extra pin, a thirteenth tooth slipped into the count wheel, and only a clockmaker's hand could do it, and there were two clockmakers in Vell that winter, and one of them drank with miners and the other drank with magistrates.

Ivo had not thought of that sound in thirty years. He had trained himself not to. Grief was a mine shaft; you learned which tunnels to seal.

And now he stood under the tower with his heart going like a stamp mill, waiting to hear whether a dead man's daughter had kept her nerve.

Twelve fifty-nine. A hush moved through the crowd the way wind moves through wheat. On the platform, Corven rose, smoothing his coat, turning to face the town with the expression of a man about to deliver them good news and a bill for it. His voice carried: something about renewal, about the observatory restored, about Vell entering a new century prosperous and orderly —

The first stroke fell.

It went through Ivo like a nail driven into green wood. The whole square flinched and then laughed, a soft collective sound, the laughter of people remembering together. One o'clock. Midnight made official. Children clapped.

Corven smiled the thin smile of a man enduring rustic sentiment.

The second stroke. The third. Ivo knew the interval between them the way he knew the weight of a shovel — four seconds, no more — and he counted each one down into silence, and on the twelfth stroke the crowd began already to murmur and shuffle, coats rustling, someone's baby crying, the ceremony of the hour concluded, the magistrate lifting his arms to speak —

The thirteenth stroke rang out over Vell like a door kicked open.

* * *

For one full second nothing happened anywhere in the world. The sound hung in the frozen air above the square, and every lantern flame seemed to lean toward it, and Ivo felt his throat close around thirty years of sealed tunnels.

Then the tower spoke again — but not with bells.

There came from inside the case a sound that only a handful of people in the crowd understood, a ratchet releasing, a counterweight taking up slack, a long deliberate click of machinery doing precisely what some precise hand had built it to do. Ivo understood it. He had spent six evenings in the Lantern Inn's back room while Ansel chalked the diagram on the floorboards: the second escapement, the wire run through the hollow of the pendulum rod, the panel in the escapement chamber rigged to swing outward when the thirteenth stroke's recoil tripped the detent. *The clock itself will testify,* she had said, chalk dust on her upper lip, her eyes burning like coals in a stove. *Nobody disperses a crowd that came to hear midnight.*

High in the tower's flank, a dark seam opened like an eye.

And the pages came down.

They came in a slow white spiral, dozens of them, hundreds, catching the lantern light as they fell — ledger pages, dense with columns of figures, loose now, unbound, drifting over the square like the strangest snowfall the mountain had ever produced. One settled on the baker's cart. One caught in a woman's upswept hair. One landed face-up at Ivo's boots, and he did not pick it up, because he did not trust his legs yet, but he looked down at it and the lamplight was kind and the figures swam and sharpened, and he saw a name he knew. Halvorsen. Paid. Witnessed. Signed.

Old Halvorsen's boys were standing right beside him. One of them bent, picked up the page, turned it in the light. Ivo watched the young man's brow fold, watch him mouth his own father's name, watch comprehension arrive in his face the way cave-ins arrive — all at once and forever.

"What is this?" someone called out. "What's falling?"

"Ledgers," said Halvorsen's boy, and his voice cracked and carried farther than he can have meant it to. "These are payment ledgers. There's money recorded here. Payments to — " he stopped, swallowed, started again. "Payments to witnesses."

The murmur that went around the square was not loud. It did not need to be. Ivo had heard that murmur once before, in a courtroom, when the foreman read a verdict, and he knew it for what it was: the sound of several hundred people changing their minds in the same direction at the same moment.

On the platform, Corven was on his feet, shouting for order, for the garrison, for someone to climb the tower and stop whatever was happening — and his voice was lost, simply swallowed whole, because the crowd had begun to press forward, and every hand that caught a page held it up to a neighbor's lantern, and every reading became a reading aloud, and every reading aloud became another voice, until the square sounded like a hive opened.

A page drifted onto Ivo's shoulder and stayed there like a hand.

* * *

He found her at the base of the tower, pressed into the shadow of the buttress, still holding the release lever's wooden handle in her fist as though letting go of it might undo everything. Her coat was buttoned wrong. Her eyes found Ivo's and held them, and what was in them was not triumph. It was terror, worn thin by use, like a coin rubbed smooth.

"It works," she said. Just that. As if she had built a shelf and it held.

"It works," Ivo said, and put his hand on the back of her neck the way he had once put it on the backs of younger men going underground, the old blessing, the old benediction. "Now comes the hard part. Now somebody has to say the words."

"That's you," she said. "Ivo. That has to be you. You're the only one left who knew him. Who knew all of it."

"I'm seventy years old and my voice sounds like gravel in a bucket."

"There are four hundred people in this square," Ansel said, "and not one of them under forty remembers why the clock struck thirteen tonight. But they'll remember tomorrow. If you tell them whose trick this was. Whose name goes with those pages." Her grip closed on his sleeve, and her voice dropped low and rough. "You told me once that grief is a tunnel you seal. You don't have to seal this one. Not tonight."

Ivo looked at the pages falling. He looked at his hands, scarred and bent, hands that had shoveled rock for men who paid in scrip and testified against men who asked for wages. He looked up at the tower where a dead clockmaker's daughter had taught a dead man's machine to speak.

"Get me a lantern," he said, "and something to stand on."

* * *

Marta Sable's beer barrel had not survived thirty years, but the Lantern Inn still owned a delivery crate of solid oak, and Marta herself carried it out into the middle of the square on one shoulder and set it down where Tomás Quintana had once stood, and if anyone thought it strange that an innkeeper should know that exact spot, no one said so. She put a lantern in Ivo's free hand — he had not noticed her take the pages from him — and she gripped his elbow briefly, hard, and said in his ear, "Steady as you go, old man. I've run cargo past worse crowds than this," and then she stepped back into the press of bodies and was gone, or seemed gone, though Ivo could feel her there the way you feel a wall at your back in the dark.

He climbed the crate. The square turned its face to him.

He was afraid in the old way, the pit-bottom way, the way a man is afraid when the candle gutters and the air tastes of dust. Then he unfolded the page Ansel pressed into his hand, and the lantern light fell on the figures, and the fear went out of him like heat out of an open forge, because these were numbers, and numbers had never frightened a miner yet.

"On the ninth of October, twenty-two years ago," Ivo read, and his voice did crack, and the crowd went so quiet he could hear the tower ticking behind him, patient as ever, "a payment of eleven crowns was entered in this book to the account of Jonas Weir, wheelwright, for services rendered as witness. Jonas Weir swore before Magistrate Corven that he had seen Tomás Quintana pay men to threaten the strikebreakers. Jonas Weir never saw any such thing. Jonas Weir bought a new cart that spring."

He paused. Let it sit. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began quietly to weep.

"Second page. Fourteen crowns to the widow Brandt, sworn testimony regarding threats received. Third page. Nine crowns to a garrison corporal whose name I will not shame his mother with, for altering a report." His voice steadied as he went, gathering strength like water down a sluice. "Page after page, neighbors. Year after year. Every man who spoke against Tomás Quintana at his trial has an entry in this book, and every entry bears a mark in the margin — a little hook, like a fishhook — and I have seen that mark before. It's on the weighbridge tickets. It's the magistrate's own tally sign, the one he uses when he counts what's owed to him."

He turned the page toward the light so the front rank could see, and a ripple ran backward through the crowd like wind through barley.

"Tomás Quintana was hanged on the twelfth of December," Ivo said, "for a crime that was bought and paid for out of the miners' relief fund, which this same book shows emptied crown by crown into a private account in Lowmark. I was there. I stood in that square where you're standing now, and I watched it happen, and I could not stop it, and I have lived twenty-two years in a town that could not stop it either." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "But somebody stopped it tonight. Somebody opened that chamber and let this book fall. And I want every soul in Vell to know whose daughter she is."

"No—" Ansel's voice, sharp, from the base of the tower.

"—whose *son* he pretended to be," Ivo said, "to survive among us, which shames none of us less than it should. The clock that struck you thirteen tonight was rebuilt by Anselma Quintana. Tomás's girl. The dead man's daughter. Say the name with me, Vell. You owe it. We all owe it."

For a moment, nothing. The lantern hissed. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, sifting down through the light.

Then Halvorsen's boy said it. "*Quintana.*"

And a woman near the bakery said it. And a man at the back. And then it was not voices anymore but one voice, hundreds strong, rolling off the mountain walls and back again — *Quintana, Quintana, Quintana* — and Ivo stood on the crate with tears freezing in the seams of his face and let it wash over him, thirty years of sealed tunnels breaking open all at once, grief and rage and something underneath both that he had no better word for than *finally*.

Ansel had come out of the shadow. She stood in the falling snow with her cap gone — Marta must have taken it, Marta was grinning like a knife beside her — and her hair came down past her shoulders, and she did not hide, and the town said her father's name over her like a bell.

* * *

"Arrest them," Corven shouted from the platform, and his voice had gone thin and high, a whistling through a cracked pipe. "Arrest the thieves, the saboteurs — garrison! To me!"

Boots rang on the garrison road. A file of soldiers came double-time into the square, twelve of them, bayonets fixed, and the crowd parted grudgingly around them, and Ivo's stomach turned over — he had seen what soldiers did to squares full of angry miners, once, twice, more times than a man wants to remember.

But the sergeant halted his men at the edge of the lantern light, and stood looking at the pages lying everywhere in the snow like blossom, and at the crate where Ivo stood, and at the crowd — at the faces in it, which were the faces of his fathers and mothers and cousins, because Vell's garrison had been recruited from Vell's families for two generations, and every one of those soldiers had grown up on stories of the twelfth of December.

"Sergeant," Corven called. "Clear the square. Arrest the ringleaders. That is a lawful order."

The sergeant removed his helmet. He did it slowly, deliberately, the way a man does a thing when he means everyone to see it. He looked up at the magistrate for a long moment, and then at the crowd, and last of all at the page Ivo held up toward him, the fishhook margin mark plain in the lantern light.

"Beg pardon, sir," the sergeant said, and his voice carried to the far corners of the square without effort at all. "But I read. Slow, mind, but I read. And I'd like to hear the rest of the book before anybody gets arrested."

Behind him, twelve soldiers grounded their arms, one after another, the sound ringing off the tower like slow applause.

On the platform, Elias Corven rose from his chair, and took from his coat a leather folder Ivo recognized — the audit copies, the corroborating ledgers, the son's own handwork — and walked to the front of the platform, away from his father, and stood at its very edge facing the town with the folder held out in both hands like an offering or a confession.

And high above them all, the master clock of Vell, repaired and running and patient as the mountain, ticked steadily on toward the next minute, and the next, keeping perfect time for the first time in three years — as if it too intended to stay and see how this ended.
