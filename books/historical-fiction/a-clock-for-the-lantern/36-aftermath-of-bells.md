## 36. Aftermath of Bells

The square still smelled of lantern oil and trampled snow, and Ansel could not stop looking up at the tower.

It had been six days since the clock struck thirteen, and the town of Vell had not yet finished talking about it. She heard it everywhere she went — in the bakery queue, at the well, in the corner of Marta's common room where the miners' widows now held court as though they had always been the most important women in the valley. *Thirteen strokes. Did you count them? Thirteen, plain as your own name.* And then the lower voices, the ones that dropped when they saw her pass: *the clockmaker's apprentice. The one who opened the chamber. Quintana's girl.*

She was not hiding from those voices anymore. That was the strangest part. When a woman at the well had said it aloud last Tuesday — *Quintana's girl* — Ansel had turned and met her eyes and nodded once, and the woman had pressed her hand to her mouth and then, unexpectedly, to Ansel's sleeve, and said, *Your father built my mother's coffin clock. He was a good man.* As if that were news. As if Ansel needed telling.

The ledgers lay in the magistrate's strongroom now, under seal, catalogued by clerks who had arrived on the first coach after the thaw began to whisper through the valley. Trials were promised. The word came down from the provincial capital with all the weight of official wax: *investigations pending, proceedings to follow, restitution considered.* Ivo read the notice twice at the inn and pronounced it the finest collection of careful words he'd ever seen assembled by men who hoped to be somewhere else by summer. But they were words on stamped paper, and stamped paper was more than Tomás Quintana had ever been granted, so the old miner folded the notice into his coat pocket over his heart and let people buy him drinks and did not argue.

Brannock resigned on the fourth day.

Ansel learned of it not from any announcement but from the sight of the head warden himself, coatless, carrying a crate of personal effects down the observatory steps in broad daylight like an ordinary man moving house. He stopped when he saw her at the base of the tower. For a moment neither of them spoke. The mountain wind moved between them, smelling of pine rot and running water.

"They'll want statements," Brannock said finally. "From everyone. Including you."

"I know."

"I'll give mine honestly." He shifted the crate to his other arm. Inside it she could see a brass rule, a tin of calipers, a framed certificate gone brown at the edges. Twenty years of a career, packed into something a man could carry. "I knew who you were before you ever climbed that tower. From the second week. You file your teeth on pinions left-handed — did you know that? Your father did the same. I watched him do it, the year they built the tollhouse clock. I stood in Corven's anteroom two winters ago and told myself the girl coming up the road was nobody." His jaw worked. "I told myself a lot of things."

"Why warn Marta?" Ansel asked quietly. "That night. You tipped the search, but you warned her first."

Brannock looked past her, up at the tower, where the great dial stood pale against a sky the color of slate. "Because I've spent thirty years keeping order for a man who mistook silence for justice," he said. "I suppose I wanted, just once, to know which side of it I was standing on while I chose." He adjusted his grip on the crate, nodded to her — a workman's nod, one craftsman to another — and walked on down toward the square without looking back.

Ansel stood a long while watching him go. She thought of her father's hands, filing pinions. She thought about how many small choices a life was made of, and how few of them ever got made in daylight.

* * *

The bridge reopened early.

That was the thing everyone kept saying, in the tone of people discussing weather that had behaved badly: *early, weeks early, unheard of.* A chinook had come over the passes warm as a held breath and stripped the ice off the river gorge in four days, and by the fifth the bridge crew had tested the chains twice and hung the green lamp at the far end, and Vell was suddenly, abruptly, connected to the rest of the world again.

For half a year that bridge had been a wall. Now it was a door.

Ansel felt the change of it in her body before she understood it in her mind. She woke on the morning after the green lamp was lit and realized her chest was lighter — and then understood why, and sat very still on the edge of her cot at the Lantern Inn, listening to Marta bang pans downstairs, and named the lightness for what it was.

She could leave.

No one would stop her. That was the miracle no one had arranged and no one could take back: the magistrate confined to his shuttered house, the garrison answering to sergeants who were miners' sons, the guild papers waiting in the capital with her true signature already inked on them, witnessed by four people who knew exactly who she was and had signed their own names beside hers anyway. Anselma Quintana could walk out of Vell across that bridge in the morning light, and no checkpoint would open her satchel, and no warden would study her face, and no soul in the world beyond this valley need ever connect the quiet clock-mender with the hanged organizer or his frightened daughter.

Free. Anonymous. The two things she had spent eleven years believing were the same thing.

She went downstairs with her hands cold around nothing.

* * *

Marta Sable was in the common room doing sums she plainly intended to lose, because the ledger lay open and abandoned and she was instead watching the door with the particular stillness of a woman who has run cargo past armed men and can smell a departure being packed. Ansel set her satchel on the table between them. Marta looked at it the way other people look at drawn knives.

"You've oiled the straps," Marta observed.

"Old habit."

"It's not an old habit. It's a *going* habit." Marta poured two glasses of the plum spirit without asking and pushed one across. "Bridge is open."

"So I heard."

"Ferry's running at the landing. Coaches twice a week now, three times by month's end. You could be in the capital by Sunday." Marta drank, watching her over the rim. "You could be anywhere by Sunday. That's rather the point of everything we did, isn't it? So you could go anywhere and be no one again."

"That was the point," Ansel agreed. "Eleven years ago."

"And now?"

Through the window behind Marta's shoulder, Ansel could see the square, and the tower, and — small in the distance, crossing from the observatory with a canvas case under her arm — Odile, late for her morning's work at the telescope as she was late every morning now because she stopped to talk to everyone, because a town that had learned a little of her language kept offering her new words for it. Beyond her, Ivo's slow shape by the war memorial, sweeping snow from the stone with a hand-broom, every morning, though there was hardly anything left to sweep.

"Now," Ansel said, "I don't know what the point is."

Marta turned the ledger around and slid it across the table. Ansel looked down at it: the famous ledger of small debts, the one every guest at the Lantern somehow appeared on and no guest had ever seen. And there, in Marta's cramped hand, a fresh entry. *A. Quintana — lodging, winter, board, repairs to regulator clock, lessons in lantern signals (invaluable), one braiding of hair (priceless) — paid in full.*

"When did you write that?"

"Last night." Marta closed the ledger. "You should understand something about debts, girl. I ran contraband across three borders for fifteen years and I never once carried anything worth what you carried through those checkpoints. And I've kept this inn twenty years and I have never once written *paid in full* and meant it until now." She stood, gathered the glasses, and paused with her back to the room. "The door's open. I put it there myself, along with the others. But doors that only swing one way aren't doors. They're exits. There's a difference, and you're the only one who knows which one this is." She carried the glasses to the bar and added, too casually, "Regulator's losing four minutes a day again. Thought you should know. In case you were still here Thursday. Which is none of my affair."

Ansel laughed — a short, wet sound she hadn't expected — and picked up her satchel, and went out into the morning.

* * *

She walked to the bridge.

She didn't decide to; her feet decided, and she let them, because she had learned long ago that sometimes the body knows the shape of a truth before the mind consents to hold it. The road out of Vell followed the river through the pines, loud now with meltwater, and the air had that raw sweetness of a winter ending, and her boots found the old ruts in the snow going down and did not turn aside.

The bridge itself was magnificent in the way of things built by people who expected them to matter: iron bones, timber ribs, the frost coming off the cables in threads of silver. The green lamp swung at the far anchorage. A tollkeeper's shed stood open and unmanned — checkpoint one, the tollhouse, where a guard with gambling debts had once looked elsewhere for coin, now just a shed with a stove and a chair, its authority dissolved along with the magistrate's. She walked its length alone, boots ringing on the planks, the river roaring white beneath the gaps, and stopped at the exact middle, where the mountain fell away on either side and the whole world opened southward — the capital, the coast, cities where a skilled clock-mender with a forged name could disappear into workshops and boarding houses and never once be asked whose child she was.

She had dreamed of this for eleven years. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Every night in some hired room in some town where nobody knew her, she had lain awake and imagined this exact view: the way out, lit green, unguarded. Freedom meant this. Freedom had always meant this — the vanishing, the clean severance, the daughter erased so thoroughly that even she forgot her.

And now she stood on it, and found it weighed nothing.

She took the satchel off her shoulder and set it on a plank and opened it, and looked at what she'd packed at dawn: tools, yes — the good ones, the ones her father's hands had taught hers — and spare escapements, and the regulator's missing pallet stone wrapped in wax paper, which she had taken from the inn's clock months ago and had told herself was for practice. All of it repair-work. All of it *staying*-work. Somewhere between packing and walking she had filled a bag for a future with no exit in it, and her hands had known before she did.

The wind came up the gorge and lifted her hair off her neck — hair that Marta had braided openly, woman-fashion, five nights ago in front of a mirror, both of them crying and pretending it was the lamplight. Downriver she could hear the ferry bell, and closer, behind her, faint but unmistakable across the melting miles, the tower clock striking ten — her regulation, her escapement, her father's trade running true in the tower above the town that had spoken his name by lantern light with six hundred voices.

Tomás Quintana had died anonymous in everything but infamy. His daughter had lived anonymous in everything but fear. And here was the door out of it, standing open, and the strange discovery at the middle of the bridge was that anonymity was not freedom. It was just a smaller cell with the walls painted like sky.

Anselma picked up the satchel. She stood a while longer, letting the wind have her face.

Then she turned around and started walking back — not slowly, and not sadly, but with the long stride of a woman who has checked the mechanism of her own heart, found the fault, corrected it, and set the train in motion.

There was a regulator clock at the inn losing four minutes a day, and it was none of Marta's affair whether she was still there Thursday. There was a grave above the tree line that she and Ivo and Odile meant to visit as soon as the path cleared, carrying a small brass escapement that had taken her three nights of quiet work to finish. There was a tower dial that would need a lantern-lit face someday, if she stayed long enough to build it — and a town below it that had seen her, all of her, and called her by her name.

Halfway back up the road, she heard hoofbeats and looked up to see Odile riding pillion behind the baker's cart, waving both arms in the extravagant grammar she used when words were too slow. The cart drew level. Odile leaned down, eyes bright, and shaped two words with exaggerated care so Ansel could read them across the distance:

*"Your bench—"*

Then, laughing silently, she pointed back toward Vell, toward the observatory road, and Ansel understood: the workbench in the tower, the one she'd built herself from scaffold planks last autumn, was buried under delivered lumber — someone had ordered timber for the dial housing, timber for a project no one had commissioned yet, paid for by an account Elias refused to explain, and everyone in that conspiring town had simply assumed.

Assumed she was staying. Assumed the clock was hers to keep.

Ansel stood in the road with meltwater singing on every side and the bridge at her back standing open all the way to the horizon, and found that her eyes were stinging and her mouth wouldn't stop smiling.

"Well," she said aloud, to Odile, to the river, to her father wherever his dust kept time. "I suppose I'd better unload it."

And she turned up the road toward the tower, toward the timber and the bench and the four people waiting, as behind her the green lamp swung on its chain over the empty bridge, keeping its light for whoever needed it next.
