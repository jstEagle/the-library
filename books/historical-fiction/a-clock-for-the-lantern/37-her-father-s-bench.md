## 37. Her Father's Bench

The morning of the climb, Ansel woke before the light did.

She lay a while in her room at the Lantern Inn, listening to the building breathe — the tick of the regulator clock she had restored in the common room, the settling groans of old timber, Marta moving below with the particular care of someone trying to be quiet and failing at it, because Marta Sable had never in her life done anything quietly that she could do loudly. A pan clattered. A kettle was set down with exaggerated gentleness. Ansel smiled at the ceiling and did not call out.

Today she would go up. Above the tree line, where Ivo said the graves of the miners' dead were cut into the thin soil like letters pressed into wax. Where Tomás Quintana had been buried — if buried was the word for it — in the winter after the hanging, when the town had wanted the body gone quickly and the mountain had obliged by freezing the ground too hard for ceremony.

Nineteen years. She had not come in nineteen years.

She dressed slowly, and then not slowly at all, because there was no use pretending she wasn't afraid. She bound her chest, and then stood before the small mirror with her hair loose down her back and her hands on the edge of the washstand, and looked at herself for a long moment — at the face that Brannock had known, that Corven had known, that half the town now knew from the square, from the falling pages, from the story told and retold in the days since with the embellishments stories acquire. *The clockmaker who made the clock speak.* They had already given her a legend. Legends were only another kind of disguise, and she had worn disguises long enough.

She left her hair unbound.

Downstairs, the common room smelled of woodsmoke and fried onions, and Marta had laid out bread and hard cheese and a flask of coffee without being asked, which was Marta's way of saying everything without saying anything. She stood at the hearth with her arms folded, a woman built like a door, watching Ansel come down the stairs with an expression that was attempting neutrality and landing somewhere nearer to tenderness.

"You'll want the east path," Marta said. "Not the switchback. The switchback's iced."

"Ivo said."

"Ivo says many things. Some of them are even true." Marta pushed a wrapped parcel across the table toward her — bread, cheese, and, when Ansel lifted the cloth, three squares of honey cake wrapped in waxed paper. Three. "For the walk," Marta said, daring her with a look to comment on the number. "Sugar keeps the cold off. Old smuggler's trick."

"Is that true?"

"No," Marta said. "But it should be." Then, quieter, with her eyes on the fire: "You don't have to bring anything back. You understand that? Whatever you carry up that mountain, you can leave up there. The mountain's good at holding things. It's what mountains are for."

Ansel looked at her. Nineteen years of carrying, and this innkeeper who had known her barely a season had seen straight to the weight of it, the way she saw through locked doors and sealed crates and men's careful lies — because grief was only another kind of contraband, and Marta had spent her whole life moving goods past people who didn't want to see them.

"I'll remember," Ansel said.

"See that you do." Marta turned back to the stove, and her voice came gruff over her shoulder. "And eat the honey cake before the top, girl. Your hands will shake less."

* * *

Ivo was waiting at the tree line with two walking staves and Odile, who wore a coat too large for her — her father's, Ansel guessed, sleeves rolled twice at the cuff — and a knitted cap pulled low, and carried her satchel slung across her body the way she always did, strap tight, as though the world might try to take things from her and she intended to feel them try.

Odile signed to her before Ivo had finished his greeting: *You came.*

"I came."

*Your hair is down.*

Ansel touched it, self-conscious. The wind took a strand and played with it. "It seemed like a day for it."

Odile studied her face with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing, that read mouths and hands and the set of shoulders the way other people read print, and whatever she found there, she did not remark on. Instead she held up one finger — *wait* — and dug in her satchel and produced a small paper packet, which she opened to reveal dried apricots.

*From the fair,* she signed. *I saved them. For today.*

"You knew I'd ask you to come?"

*Ivo told me Monday.* Odile's mouth curved. *You wrote the note and then walked past the observatory four times before you left it under the door.*

"I did not walk past four times."

*Three times,* Odile conceded, *and once you stopped and almost knocked.* She laughed — a bright, unguarded sound, more breath than voice, the laugh of someone who had never heard her own voice enough to be self-conscious about it — and handed Ansel the packet, and Ansel found she could not stop smiling, even here, even going where she was going. That was the thing about Odile she still marveled at: she made the present tense possible. In her company the past loosened its grip, because Odile took no notice of anything that wasn't happening right now, in front of her eyes, in the movement of hands and light.

Ivo cleared his throat. He was seventy if he was a day, weathered down to sinew and opinion, and he had put on what Ansel suspected was his funeral coat — the good black wool, brushed clean, mended at the elbow with thread that didn't quite match. He looked at her hair, and at her, and said nothing about either, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"It's four hours up," he said. "Three if we don't talk. Two and a half if we argue. I vote for talking, because my knees have opinions about gradients." He handed her a staff. "The graves start above the last pines. His is the third row from the cairn, facing east. I helped carry him up. You should know that, since you weren't here to know it. There were eleven of us. Eleven men carrying a coffin in February, and every one of us certain Corven would find a reason to hang us too. We did it anyway."

Ansel's throat closed. She managed, "Thank you," and Ivo nodded once, briskly, the way he did when emotion threatened and needed turning back at the border, and set off up the path.

* * *

The mountain in late winter was a country of white and blue and the hard glitter of ice, and they climbed through it in the loose rhythm of walkers who each needed their own silence. Ansel walked behind Ivo's steady shoulders and let her mind do what minds do on climbs — circle, return, circle again.

She thought about the last time she had seen this mountain: from the window of the diligence heading south, seventeen years old, wearing her dead brother's coat and name, with a forged letter of apprenticeship in her bag and her father's grave somewhere above her, unseen, unvisited, a wound she had decided to treat by refusing to look at it. She had been so proud of that decision. *Strategy*, she had called it. Grief reframed as survival. And it had worked — she had survived; she had become a clockmaker good enough that towns summoned her by letter; she had passed through garrisons and guild halls and a hundred pairs of eyes without one of them seeing Anselma Quintana, daughter of the hanged man.

And it had cost exactly what such things cost, which is everything, paid slowly.

She thought about the ledger pages, still sitting inside the clock — the originals, kept as bait per their plan, though bait for what, now, no one quite knew; the magistrate confined to his house, the garrison mutinous with conscience, the trial promised for the spring assizes. She thought about how strange it was that the proof of her father's innocence had been ticking above the square all these years, hidden in an escapement chamber, keeping time around the evidence of his murder. As if the clock had been holding his place. As if it had been waiting for someone to come who would know how to open it.

She had left her father a message in the striking train, in the only language she had that was older than words: thirteen strokes. Her father had used it in the strike years, a signal among organizers — *we are more than you counted*. And she had wondered, all winter, whether he had known, wherever he was, whatever he was, that the clock had spoken for him. She had decided that it didn't matter. Signals were sent for the sender as much as the receiver. She understood that now.

Halfway up, at a shelf of rock where the wind dropped, they rested and ate. Odile distributed apricots with the solemnity of a sacrament and then, sitting with her back against the stone, began signing as she chewed, unhurried, her hands making shapes in the cold air:

*My mother is buried on the other side. Past the lake, in the churchyard at Lowmill. My father has never gone. He says he will go when the great lens is finished. But the lens is finished now, and he has started grinding a bigger one.*

Ansel watched her hands. "Why are you telling me this?"

Odile considered. Her hands were quiet a moment, resting in her lap, and then they moved again, carefully, choosing.

*Because I want to know if going helps. Before I ask him to go. You are our experiment.*

Ivo snorted into his coffee. Ansel laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something open in her chest, some sealed valve, and to hide it she looked out at the view — Vell small below, the observatory tower with its new-lit dial, the river a white seam, the tollhouse a toy against the slope — and found her eyes were wet anyway.

"Yes," she said to Odile. "I'll tell you honestly. Whether it helps or it doesn't."

Odile nodded once, satisfied, and ate another apricot, and looked out at the view with her, shoulder against shoulder, and asked nothing more. That was the other thing about Odile: she asked for truth and then made room for it, however long it took to arrive.

* * *

Above the tree line the world opened, and the graves began.

They were not what she expected. She had imagined rows, ordered, municipal. Instead the miners' dead lay scattered on a south-facing slope where the snow melted first, each marked by a stone gathered from the ground itself, uncut, chosen — round ones, flat ones, one like a folded hand. Some had initials chiseled crudely. Some had nothing but their shape and their placement, meaning legible only to those who already knew. Here and there a cairn rose where several lay together — the cave-in years, Ivo said quietly, gesturing with his chin. Forty-one men. The stones had been carried up, Ansel realized; every marker hauled on someone's back along this same path, because the mountain offered nothing suitable and love is impractical by nature.

The air was thin and enormous. Below, the world. Above, only sky, the pale hard blue of high altitude, and a silence so complete she could hear her own blood.

Ivo led them to the third row from the cairn, facing east.

The stone was a slab of gray granite, wider than it was tall, chosen Ivo told her because it was "a stubborn rock, like him." No dates. Just letters, cut deep and filled with lead so they showed black against the stone:

**TOMÁS QUINTANA**

and beneath, in smaller characters, added later by a different, steadier hand:

**HE KEPT COUNT**

Ansel stood before it and felt — nothing. That was the first thing. Nothing, a vast white nothing, the numbness of a limb woken wrong, and behind it a rising panic: *after all this, after the clock and the pages and the thirteen strokes, to arrive here and feel nothing—*

Then the wind moved. And the nothing broke like ice underfoot.

She did not kneel so much as fail to remain standing. Her knees found the frozen grass. Her hand found the stone, and the stone was bitterly cold, and the cold was real in a way nothing had been real in nineteen years, and she heard a sound and realized distantly it was coming from her, something between a word and the memory of a word — *Papá* — in a language she hadn't spoken aloud since she was seventeen, since the last person she'd been allowed to speak it to was him.

Grief, it turned out, had been waiting. All this time, all these miles, all these disguises — it had simply waited, patient as a mechanism, storing her energy spring by spring, and now it discharged, all at once, exactly as designed. She shook. Odile knelt beside her — not touching, just near, the way Odile knew better than anyone how to be near — and Ivo stood behind them both facing outward, an old soldier's instinct, standing watch over her grief the way no one had stood watch over her father.

She cried for the hanging. She cried for the boy's clothes she had lived in for nineteen years. She cried for her mother, dead of fever two winters later, and for her brother Mateo, who had died at twelve of the same fever and whose name she had stolen and worn until it fit. She cried for the eleven men who had carried the coffin, whom she had never thanked. She cried for the girl she had been, walking away down the valley with her eyes forward, so proud of not looking back.

And then, as all storms do, it passed. Not cleanly — grief never passes cleanly; it leaves debris, changed contours, watercourses rerouted — but passed. She became aware of the sun on her neck, of Odile's warmth at her side, of the smell of cold granite. Of the fact that she was kneeling at her father's grave with her hair down, in daylight, in front of witnesses, as herself.

Nineteen years, and this was the first ritual anyone had given her: not forgetting, not strategy, not silence. Just this. Kneeling. Weeping. Being seen.

"He kept count," she said at last, her voice wrecked and rough. "Who wrote that? Who cut it?"

"I did," Ivo said. "Cut it the spring after. Took me four trips up here because I kept crying and couldn't see the letters." He came and stood over her, looking down at the stone, and his own eyes were wet and he made no effort to hide it, this time. "It's what he always said at the meetings. When the owners said the men were exaggerating, when the magistrate said the injuries were exaggerated — your father would get up, slow, the way he did, and say: *then we will count. Everything. Every hour, every wage, every man in the ground. We will keep count, and the counting will convict them.*" Ivo's jaw worked. "He was right. Took nineteen years and a clockmaker, but he was right."

Ansel reached into her coat.

The escapement was small — a jeweled pallet fork and escape wheel, brass and steel, no larger than her palm, the first component she had ever fully finished alone. She had made it at sixteen, at her first bench, under a master who never knew whose daughter he was teaching. Her father had been a miner, not a maker; the trade was her brother's inheritance, borrowed. But she had understood, even at sixteen, what an escapement was: the piece that turns ruin into measure. That catches the fall — the unwinding, the weight's endless desire to drop — and divides it, and makes of falling something else entirely. Time. Order. A count.

Her father had kept count. So would she.

She placed it on the stone, centered beneath the name, and the brass caught the sun and threw a small coin of light onto the granite, trembling slightly in the wind, like a heartbeat made visible.

"Papá," she said, in the old tongue, and then in the trade tongue, because she was done dividing herself into languages, one for grief and one for living: "I finished it. The count. It's not done — the trial comes in spring, and justice is slow — but it's finished enough for you to rest under. I brought you a clock. I'm sorry it's so small. I'll build you a bigger one. I'm going to build this whole town a way of keeping time that doesn't lie."

Odile touched her arm and signed, slowly, so she could read through tears: *Tell him about us.*

"There are people here with me," Ansel said. "A smuggler who feeds everyone and pretends it's commerce. An astronomer's daughter who reads the world with her eyes and speaks with her hands, and is the best friend I have ever had or will have. A young man who chose truth over his own father, and is paying for it daily, and does it anyway. An old miner who carried you up this hill and then spent a winter mapping checkpoints for your daughter because he decided you were owed." Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "You worried, I think, dying. About me being alone. I want you to know that I was alone for nineteen years and I am not anymore. I built it myself, the way you taught me to count. One hour, one person, one day at a time."

The wind moved through the grass above the tree line, and the little escapement gleamed, and if the dead answer, they answer in weather and light and the hearts of the living, and Ansel was finally willing to accept those terms.

* * *

They stayed an hour. Odile wandered among the stones reading the names and inventing, for the ones that had none, lives she told back to Ansel and Ivo in quick signs — *this one was a fiddle player; this one cheated at cards; this one is why I think the cairn stones are arranged like a constellation, look* — and Ivo protested that she was making it up and Odile signed back, serenely, *All history is making it up, kindly,* and Ivo grumbled and then, Ansel noticed, did not disagree.

On the way down, at the shelf of rock, Odile sat and waited until Ansel sat beside her.

*Well?* she signed. *The experiment. Does it help?*

Ansel looked back up the mountain, at the slope where a small point of brass light still burned in the sun, invisible at this distance except that she knew exactly where it was, and would always know.

"Yes," she said. "It doesn't fix anything. But it divides it. Into hours you can live inside." She smiled, wiped her eyes with her sleeve. "It's a very clockmaker's answer."

Odile grinned and signed: *Good. Now help me convince my father. Because I've decided—" and she stopped, hands pausing mid-shape, and her face changed, and she turned toward the path below them.

Two figures were climbing. One moved with the stiff, deliberate pace of a man ascending under protest. The other — the other walked like someone used to staircases and ledgers, coat too fine for the scree, hat in his hand, looking up the slope with an expression Ansel could read even at fifty yards because she had learned to read it across a square: Elias Corven, flushed and uncertain, and beside him, leaning on a borrowed stick and complaining audibly even before the wind delivered the words —

"—entirely unreasonable altitude, Odile, I raised you to grind lenses, not to drag your aged parent up—"

Ansel got to her feet, heart hammering strangely, and Odile stood beside her and signed one word, radiant:

*Told him.*
