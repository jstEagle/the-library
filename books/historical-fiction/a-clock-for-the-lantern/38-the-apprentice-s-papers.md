## 38. The Apprentice's Papers

The guild papers arrived on a Tuesday, carried up from the valley by a post rider whose mule had opinions about the switchbacks and expressed them loudly at every turn. Ansel heard the animal from the tower window where she sat cross-legged among her tools, and she knew before the man dismounted that the packet bound in grey twine was for her. Nothing else came to Vell in winter but bad news and tax notices, and the twine was the blue of guild correspondence — she had seen it once, years ago, through a shop window in a city whose name she had let fall out of her memory like a worn gear.

She did not go down at once.

That was the thing about moments like this one, she thought. They did not announce their weight. The paper would be ordinary — heavy rag stock, watermarked, printed in the cramped upright hand of the Guild of Horologists' provincial secretary. It would say what all such papers said: that one *A. Quintana*, apprentice of record under the late master Aldous Renne of Grayharbor, had been examined by provision of the guild's emergency statutes, found competent in the regulation, escapement work, and striking-train restoration of a public turret clock, and was hereby advanced to the rank of journeyman with leave to sign works under her own hand.

Her own hand. She looked at it — ink-stained, scarred across two knuckles from a slip with the polishing wheel when she was fourteen, small for the work it had done — and felt the strangeness of being asked, after all these years, simply to use it.

Below her, the town moved through its morning. Smoke rose from forty chimneys in straight winter columns. The market square lay swept and pale, the scaffold of the clock's dial throwing long early shadows across the cobbles, and here and there a figure paused to look up — at the clock, or perhaps at her silhouette in the tower window, they had gotten used to looking at both. Since the thirteen strokes, since the pages came down like snow over the square and Ivo's voice cracked twice reading the names of dead men aloud, people had begun to greet her. Not deferentially. Not warmly, either, not yet. With the careful nod one gives a person one is still deciding how to know.

She pulled her coat on and went down.

* * *

Marta Sable had laid the common room of the Lantern Inn for an occasion, which for Marta meant she had not changed anything except to polish the table until it shone like a mirror and set out the good cups, the ones without chips, that Ansel had never once seen used. A fire went in the hearth though the morning was mild for February. Odile sat at the table already, spine straight, hands folded, wearing her best grey dress and an expression of grave ceremony that she had borrowed, Ansel suspected, from watching her father preside over observations. Ivo stood by the fire because Ivo had not sat down comfortably in twenty years and saw no reason to start for a piece of paperwork. And Elias —

Elias stood apart, near the door, in a coat that had been good two winters ago and was brushed carefully now. He had resigned his clerkship in everything but name; the name would follow in the spring, he said, when the tribunal came up from the provincial seat and his testimony was done. His father remained in the house on the hill with shutters closed against the whole talking town. Ansel had seen Elias look up at those shutters as he passed, more than once, with an expression she was grateful she had never had cause to wear.

"You'll want to read it first," Marta said, putting the packet in front of Ansel and cutting the twine with a single motion of her belt knife. "Guild men are honest about mechanisms and dishonest about everything else."

"Present company," said Ivo.

"I was never guild."

"You were something worse and better and you know it."

Ansel unfolded the papers. Three sheets. The language was exactly as dry and exact as she had imagined, and yet her eyes snagged on every instance of her own designation — *the said apprentice* — as if each one were a small locked door and behind it something waited that she was not ready to see. On the final sheet, above a line ruled for signature, the space for the maker's name stood open. Blank. Patient.

"The secretary included a note," Marta said, sliding a smaller paper across. "Reads like a man covering himself. Says the examination was irregular, conducted under emergency statute, that objections may be lodged within the year." Her mouth twisted. "Meaning if Corven's friends make noise, they can try to unmake you."

"Let them lodge objections," Ivo said. "I'll lodge them back down the hill."

"There will be objections regardless," Elias said quietly. He had come nearer, drawn by the papers the way he was drawn to any document, that clerk's gravity in him still. "The guild hates irregularity more than it hates crime. But irregularity in your favor, with the province watching this town now — they will ratify it rather than revisit it. Paper flows toward quiet water." He paused. "That's something my father taught me. It's true even of decent institutions. Perhaps especially of them."

Ansel looked up at him. There was no bitterness available in her for Elias Corven anymore, only a kind of wonder that had not dulled — that a man raised inside that house on the hill had chosen, document by document, to walk out of it carrying the truth. "Then we give them quiet water," she said. "And a signature worth keeping."

Odile touched her wrist. When Ansel turned, Odile signed — slow, deliberate, so that even Ansel's half-learned hands could follow: *You are nervous. Why? You have already done the hard part.*

"Why," Ansel said aloud, and heard how unsteady it came out. She tried again. "Because I have signed false names so many times the pen doesn't know the difference. Because the last time my name went into official paper in this province, it went into a coroner's record and a jail ledger, and both times it was spelled wrong." She smoothed the sheet flat. "*Quintana.* Two syllables and people still find ways to break it."

Ivo came to the table then, slowly, and put one knotted hand flat on the wood beside the papers. "They spelled it right in the ledgers," he said. "Every payment. Every witness. Corven wrote it correct eleven times because he liked the way it looked in his own hand." His voice stayed level, but the firelight caught wetness in the seams of his face. "Today you spell it right one more time. For a better reason."

* * *

It should have taken a minute. It took most of an hour, and none of them minded.

First there was the question of ink — Marta produced her accounting bottle, then frowned at it, then sent Odile down the lane to the stationer for the iron-gall, the proper stuff, because, as she said, if a woman was going to commit herself to a name in front of witnesses she would do it in ink that outlived the paper it sat on. Then there was the question of the date, and a small sharp argument about whether the year turned at the equinox or at Lady Day, resolved by consulting the very clock Ansel had restored, whose calendar train clicked over faithfully above them and settled the matter. Then Elias insisted on reading the whole instrument aloud, every clause, because he would not permit anyone in the room to sign anything unread — "I watched my father profit from signatures given blind," he said, simply, and no one argued.

And then it was time, and Ansel discovered that her hands, which could file a pallet stone to a tolerance of a hair's breadth without tremor, shook like a learner's.

She thought of her father's bench. That was the trouble. She had spent the night before, alone in the tower, not working — a rare thing — but sitting at the old man's tools she had inherited secondhand through three pairs of anonymous hands, and she had thought about the way he held a graver, the little roll of his wrist at the end of a cut, a motion she had copied so long ago that she no longer knew whether it was his or hers. He had signed work too. There was a watch somewhere in the world, she supposed, still ticking, with *T. Quintana* engraved inside the case back where no one would ever see it, and the injustice of that — a fine maker's name hidden inside the dark of a case, doing its honest work unseen — had seemed suddenly unbearable, unbearable precisely because she had made herself into the same thing.

"You don't have to perform it," Marta said, watching her. Marta missed nothing; she had never once missed anything, from the first night Ansel walked into the inn with her cap set low and her story rehearsed. "Nobody here needs a speech. We just need your hand and our names beside it, and then we need lunch."

That startled a laugh out of her, and the laugh loosened something, and she dipped the pen.

The nib whispered. She wrote the capital slowly — a fine tall *A*, the same letter that had opened *apprentice* and *anonymous* and *A. Quintana* on a hundred forged waybills, but now it stood for nothing but itself. Then the rest: **n-s-e-l-m-a**. Her mother's choosing, her mother's mother before that, a name for the feast day of a saint who kept lamps burning. She had not spoken it aloud in eleven years and had nearly forgotten it belonged to her; now it flowed out of the pen whole, as if it had been waiting folded in her wrist all along, the way a striking train waits wound.

Then the surname, and here her hand did not shake at all. **Quintana.** Five letters, clean, upright, spelled exactly as a magistrate had written it eleven times in a secret ledger and exactly as it was carved on a grave above the tree line. She finished the final *a* with her father's little roll of the wrist, and it was hers, and it was his, and both things were true at once, and neither thing hurt the way she had braced for.

The scratch of the pen had been loud in the silent room. Now the silence had a different texture. Odile was smiling with her whole face. Ivo had turned away toward the fire, shoulders square, giving himself privacy the old men of the mines gave each other. Marta simply nodded once, the nod of a woman confirming a cargo had arrived intact.

"Witnesses," Ansel said. Her voice held. "Four of you. In order of seniority, I think — Ivo first."

"I'll not argue with a journeymaker." Ivo took the pen like a shovel handle and wrote his name in blunt miner's capitals, then his mark beside it, an X he had never stopped making out of stubborn pride. *Ivo Rask,* and the year.

Marta wrote hers swiftly and well, a smuggler's signature, compact and slanted for speed. Odile took longer, forming each letter with care, and beneath her name added, unprompted, in her precise schoolbook hand: *read and explained to the signatory by me, at her request* — a joke in the family style, since Ansel needed no explaining, and Odile's eyes danced while she wrote it. Elias last. He hesitated over the page — not long, but visibly — and Ansel understood that for him the hesitation was the opposite of hers: he was not afraid of claiming a name but of adding another entry to the long account between himself and his blood. Then he wrote *Elias Corven* firmly, the whole weight of the name given willingly to her side of the scale, and set down the pen as a man sets down a tool he has finished using for the day.

Ansel blew on the ink. Five names drying on rag paper in a mountain inn, in the sight of nobody but themselves, and she thought: this is the mechanism. This is the whole escapement of it. A tick and a tock — a wrong done in ink, and a right done in ink, twenty years apart, and the wheel between them carried forward one tooth at a time by ordinary hands.

"Well," said Marta, and began clearing the good cups toward the stove to fill them after all. "Now we eat, and then, journeywoman Quintana, you have a regulator clock downstairs that loses four minutes a week and has lost them shamelessly since autumn."

* * *

They ate. It became, without anyone deciding, a kind of celebration — Marta produced a bottle she claimed was medicinal, Ivo told the story of the thirteen strokes for the third time and improved it in the telling, giving Ansel a lever she had not actually pulled and giving himself, modestly, fewer tears than he had actually shed. Odile recounted the search of the observatory in her quick expressive signs, Ansel translating haltingly for the others, and when Odile reached the moment she had misdirected the soldiers with false lantern signals — *and the lieutenant saluted the empty bell tower, he saluted it* — the whole table laughed until the cups rang.

But late in the afternoon, when the light had gone long and amber across the snow and the others had drifted to tasks, Ansel climbed the tower again with the ratified papers in her satchel. She had one more piece of business with the clock, and she wanted to do it alone.

In the escapement chamber, where she had once knelt in the dark and found the compartment that broke her life open, she worked by lamplight with a jeweler's screwdriver. The false plate came away easily now; she knew its weight like her own pulse. Inside, the cavity that had held the ledgers stood empty — the originals rested in the strongroom below the provincial courthouse, sealed under tribunal writ — but she had left something else there through the winter, and today she took it out.

A single page, written in her own hand weeks ago and never shown to anyone. It was not evidence. It was not for any court. It read:

*Restored by Anselma Quintana, daughter of Tomás Quintana, clockmaker of the Vell district, hanged 4th of June, 1882, for a crime manufactured by W. Corven. This clock keeps time for him now. Every hour it strikes is his.*

She folded the page small and wedged it into the joint of the frame deep in the case, where no inspector would look and no repair short of a total teardown would find it — a message in a bottle launched not across water but across years, addressed to whoever might someday open this machine with love. Perhaps no one. Perhaps some apprentice not yet born, kneeling in this dark with a lamp. It was enough to imagine them.

Before she closed the plate, she pressed her palm flat against the iron frame. The pendulum swung somewhere above her, patient, immense, and through the metal she felt its beat arrive faintly in her bones — tick, tock, tick, tock — the heartbeat she had listened to all winter, the one that had called her here under a false name and was now, in a sense she could not fully explain and did not need to, keeping her name for her.

"Done," she said aloud, to the clock, to the chamber, to the man who had taught her that a mechanism honestly built will tell the truth eventually. "Both of us."

She closed the plate and screwed it home.

When she came down, dusk had gathered in the square, and lanterns were being lit along the arcade — Odile's doing, probably, or the lamplighter's, but Ansel checked them anyway, out of habit, reading the positions the way Odile had taught her, message by message across the dark. At the inn door Marta waited with a shawl over her arm and an expression that meant news.

"Rider came through before the bridge watch changed," Marta said. "Provincial post. The thaw's come early to the low valleys — river's open at Halden already." She paused, and her eyes were steady and kind and knowing. "Bridge at Vell should be open within the fortnight. First time since autumn anyone could leave this town freely." She handed over the shawl against the evening cold. "So. Anselma Quintana, journeyman horologist, woman of record and name. What will you do with a open road?"

Ansel looked up the street, past the lamplight, to where the mountain rose black against the last green-grey band of sky — and above the tree line, barely distinguishable at this distance but perfectly visible to her anyway, the small stone that bore her father's name, freshly cleaned, with a brass escapement glinting on it still.

"First," she said, "I'm going up to tell him the news."
