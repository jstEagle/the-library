## 39. Spring Thaw

The river gave way on a Tuesday, with a sound like a door unbolting.

Ivo heard it first from the porch of the Lantern Inn, where he had taken to sitting each morning with his tea going cold beside him, watching the ice the way other men watched a sickbed. For five months the Aune had lain under its lid of grey-white silence, and for five months Ivo had carried in his chest the old miner's certainty that sealed things stayed sealed — shafts, ledgers, hearts. Then, just past dawn, came a groan from deep in the river's ribs, then a crack that ran upstream like a rumour, and the whole frozen length of it shrugged and heaved and began to move.

"Bridge'll be passable by tomorrow," he said, when Marta set a fresh cup beside his elbow. "Day after, certain."

"I know." Marta stood in the doorway with her sleeves rolled, looking out at the water with an expression Ivo could not entirely read — relief and loss wearing the same face. "I heard it from the kitchen."

"You'd have heard it in your sleep, more likely."

"I did hear it in my sleep." She said no more about it, but she stayed in the doorway a long while, and Ivo, who had spent sixty years learning when not to talk, drank his tea and let her.

By Thursday the bridge boards had been swept of grit and checked by two men from the quarry who drew lots for the privilege of being first across, and the road down from the pass showed wheel ruts for the first time since autumn. By Friday the first travelers came up the mountain: a peddler with pots strung along his mule like wind chimes, a surveyor with mud to his knees, a family of three from the valley towns who stopped in the square and craned their necks at the tower as if they'd been told to look.

They had been told. Everyone below the pass had been told.

* * *

The story arrived ahead of itself, the way stories do, growing finer in the telling with every mile it traveled. In the low towns they said the clock of Vell had struck thirteen at midnight, and that a dead man had spoken through it. In the valley market at Brell they said pages had rained onto the square like black snow, and that a magistrate had stood among them white as flour while a one-eyed old miner read out names — bribes paid, testimony bought, a miners' fund emptied into a private purse — by the light of a hundred lanterns held aloft by men whose fathers' names were among them. Some versions had the crowd singing. Some had the garrison laying down their arms. Neither was quite true; both were truer than what the town had lived with for twenty years.

The travelers who came up the mountain in that first week of thaw all wanted the same thing, though most of them circled it politely at first. They ordered soup, took rooms, bought postcards of the observatory that Odile's father had begun selling again from the little kiosk by the telescope dome — and then, over the soup or the register or the coins, they asked.

"Is it true about the clock?"

"Did you see it yourself?"

"Thirteen strokes — how does a clock even do thirteen? Wouldn't the works refuse?"

Marta answered the question of lodgings with perfect innkeeper smoothness and let Ansel answer the rest or not, as the mood took her. Most evenings Ansel chose not, keeping to the corner table with her tools laid out on oilcloth, working on whatever small mechanism had come up the mountain in a traveler's pocket in need of help — a music box, a pocket watch gone deaf, once a mechanical bird that had forgotten its own song. The travelers would watch her hands and never guess. That was the strange part, she thought, week after week. The woman who had pulled the truth out of a magistrate's locked chamber in front of four hundred witnesses sat mending watches in a tavern corner, and nobody knew her.

Except that everybody knew her. That was the other strange part, the one that took longer to get used to.

A wool merchant from Brell worked up his courage on his second night and finally asked outright: "The apprentice. The one who fixed the master clock. Is it true she's Quintana's daughter?"

The taproom went quiet in the particular way taprooms do when everyone already knows the answer and wants to hear it said. Ansel set down her tweezers.

"Yes," she said. "She is."

The merchant nodded slowly, as if confirming something he'd suspected all along, which perhaps he had. "My father walked with him, back in the day. The march on the tollhouse. He talked about Tomás Quintana till the year he died." He raised his glass slightly, awkwardly, in her direction, and the gesture was picked up around the room until half the taproom was holding its cup or mug at that same small tilt, and Ansel, who had spent nine years practicing the art of being no one, discovered there was no posture in her repertoire for being someone — so she simply nodded back, and let her hands shake where only Marta could see them.

"That'll be two coppers for the soup and nothing for the sentiment," Marta announced to the room, and the ordinary noise of an evening resumed, and Ansel finished the music box, and the bird sang.

* * *

Odile met the thaw differently. Where the others felt the season as an opening, she felt it as a question arriving on schedule: now that the world could reach Vell, what would Vell choose to be?

She put the question to work instead of to worry. Her father, whose eyesight had continued its slow retreat from the stars but whose delight in the observatory had only grown since the night the clock testified, had signed the whole lower floor over to her "for instruments, or whatever else you fill it with," and Odile filled it. She built a signal board by the east window — a frame of pegs and sliding brass plates, legible from the square — and taught two of the quarry wives its alphabet in evening sessions that kept expanding until she had to move them to the inn. She catalogued the observatory archives herself, shelf by shelf, because if the town's records were going to survive another Corven they would need to live somewhere fire could not find them all at once, and she had ideas about the dry cave above the tree line that Ivo called impractical and helped her measure anyway.

And she kept the ledgers' copies. Not the originals — those had gone, in sealed boxes with Elias's seals upon them, to the provincial assize at Coldharbour, into the hands of an investigating magistrate with a reputation for incorruptibility and a fondness for punctual clocks — but Odile's own transcription, braille and cipher, three copies in three places, made by her fingers through the long weeks when the originals had still been bait inside the clock. She could have destroyed the copies once the assize had theirs. She did not. Some things, she had decided, should exist in more hands than necessary, precisely because powerful men preferred them to exist in none.

Travelers found their way to the observatory too, drawn by the story, and Odile developed a routine for them. She would meet them at the great telescope room window — the same window through which she had first traded words with Ansel, lip to lip through glass — and she would read their questions through the pane, and answer on her slate, and walk them through the orrery and the transit instrument and, last, to the narrow stair that looked down into the clock's chamber, where the great escapement ticked with the patience of something that had outlasted everything meant to break it.

*How does it strike thirteen?* they asked her, always, mouths moving hopefully against the glass.

She wrote the answer on her slate and held it up, and had settled on it early because it was true and because it ended the conversation in the direction she wanted:

*A clock strikes whatever its maker intends. Ask who made the intention.*

They would frown, copy it into their notebooks, and go away pleased, and mostly never understand that the answer was also a signature.

* * *

Elias Corven left the magistrate's house on the first warm morning and did not go back before dark, and noticed, somewhere on the third street, that he had stopped flinching at doors.

It was not that his father's confinement had made the house quieter. If anything the old man was louder behind his windows, shouting instructions at solicitors who came less and less often, drafting petitions of protest that no clerk would file. But the shouts no longer reached Elias the way they used to. He had learned, over the winter, the difference between a wall and a door, and between obedience and habit, and he had burned rather more bridges than the one at the garrison gate.

He worked now out of two rented rooms above the chandler's, at a table buried under paper. Petitions. Testimony transcriptions. A catalogue of every name in the embezzled fund, cross-referenced with payroll rolls and burial registers, so that when the assize finally sat — and it would sit; the investigators from Coldharbour wrote twice a month now, asking careful questions — restitution could find the families it belonged to. It was slow, unglamorous work, and Elias had discovered he loved it with an ardor that embarrassed him. His father had governed Vell for thirty years and could not have named ten miners. Elias could name four hundred and eleven, and knew what each of them was owed.

Twice that spring he went to the house on Chapel Street to see his mother, who had chosen, in the way of pragmatic women, to remain in a falling house and keep its accounts honest. His father did not receive him. Once, passing the parlour door, Elias saw the old man standing at the window with a newspaper folded to the shipping reports, and for a moment their eyes met through the gap, and neither of them moved, and Elias understood that this — the unmoving, the unsaid — was the last thing his father would ever give him, and that he could carry it or set it down, and he set it down on the doorstep on his way out, and the air smelled like thawing earth, and he walked home whistling.

The town's judgment on him was slower than its judgment on his father and more complicated. Some spat. Some nodded. Mrs. Aldery at the bakery, whose brother's name was ninth on the restitution list, began leaving a roll beside his ink bottle every morning without comment, and Elias decided that this, too, was a verdict, and possibly the only kind worth having.

* * *

The Lantern Inn filled and refilled. Marta hired a girl from the valley to help in the kitchen and pretended she had done it out of generosity rather than because her own nights had gotten shorter and her mornings earlier and the stairs steeper, and Ivo let her pretend, and the girl let her pretend, and the pretense suited everyone.

On the third Sunday of thaw, with the taproom loud with road-dust and speculation, a traveling clock-dealer came in — not the one from before, not the man who had recognized Ansel's hand in her father's technique, but another of that wandering guild, a round comfortable man named Pellar with samples rattling in his cases. He ate, he drank, he asked about the clock, and then, paying his reckoning, he said to Marta, in the mild tone of a man making conversation:

"There's word moving along the trade roads. About the guild papers in Vell. Says the clock's been restored and entered on the rolls under an apprentice's name." He paused, delicately. "An unusual name."

Marta dried a glass. "Names are usual enough, in my experience."

"*Quintana,*" said Pellar, "is the name in question. And the guild at Coldharbour is asking whether the entry was properly witnessed, seeing as no journeyman of that name appears in any roll south of the pass these twenty years."

The taproom noise seemed very far away suddenly. Marta set down the glass.

"And who," she said, "wants to know?"

"No one hostile." Pellar spread his hands. "The opposite, if anything. There are masters in the low-country guilds — older men — who remember Tomás Quintana's bench. Who remember that when he died, his papers died with him, unratified, and his daughter vanished into somebody else's name before anyone thought to ask. There's talk of petitioning to restore the family entry retroactively. Recognition of the father's mastery, posthumous. And the daughter's apprenticeship validated under her own name, proper and permanent." He smiled at Marta's face. "You can tell her the guild doesn't want to punish the name. It wants to give it back. With interest."

Marta was quiet a moment. Then, because she was Marta, she poured him a brandy he hadn't paid for and said, "She'll want to know who's behind it. Names. She's had enough of anonymous friends."

"Tell her Master Hollen of Brell. And Master Ferrin. And six others who'll sign if she says yes." Pellar drank. "And tell her — tell her the phrase they used, sitting around my own table, was: *the daughter finished the work. Let the record show whose work it was.*"

Upstairs that night, Marta delivered it all verbatim, sitting on the edge of Ansel's bed while Ansel sat very still with a watch disassembled in front of her and did not touch it.

"Posthumous recognition of the father," Marta repeated. "Validation of the daughter. Permanent, proper, in the rolls forever. Every door in the trade open to you, in your own name, anywhere south of the pass." She let it sit. "Or you stay here, where the guild has no reach and the name on the clock is already yours whether the rolls say so or not."

"That's not the choice," Ansel said slowly.

"No?"

"No. The choice is—" She looked down at the watch parts, at the escape wheel balanced on its jeweled pivot, holding and releasing, holding and releasing. "The choice is whether I let them make me legitimate, or whether I make *them* legitimize me. Whether I say yes and am grateful, or say yes and set terms." A small, dangerous smile that Marta recognized, because she'd seen it on the eve of the ceremony. "Because if the guild wants to honor Tomás Quintana's daughter, they can do it in Vell, at a table of my choosing, with my people in the room. And they can start by recognizing Odile's cipher method as a trade skill. And Ivo's testimony as expert witness, paid. And—"

"—and you've been composing this speech since before he finished talking," Marta said, "haven't you."

"Since the bridge closed," Ansel admitted.

Marta laughed, kissed the top of her head on the way past — an affection she permitted herself perhaps twice a year — and went down to write a reply to Master Pellar in her flat smuggler's hand: *Terms to follow. She says yes. She says yes like her father.*

* * *

Spring came on properly after that, the rude abundant spring of high country, snow retreating upslope in ragged fields, crocuses forcing through the square's cobbles wherever the mortar had failed, the Aune running brown and muscular under the new-repaired bridge. Brannock resigned formally at month's end — a short letter to the council citing his health, which was fine, and his conscience, which was recovering — and took a cottage two valleys over where, rumor said, he repaired farm machinery badly and gave advice about locks for free. The investigating magistrates came and went with their boxes and their seals. The trials were promised for midsummer. The town, which had spent twenty years not talking about certain things, began talking about them everywhere, loudly, in queues and kitchens, the way a limb wakes.

And the clock ran. That was the thing travelers commented on most, those who stayed a night — not the story, which they already knew, but the sound. All through the dark hours the great mechanism ticked in the tower, steady as a heart, and on the hour its voice crossed the square and through the shutters of every house, and people had begun confessing that they slept better for it, that a town with a running clock felt like a town that intended to have a future.

On an evening in late April, with the light going long and gold, the five of them ended up on the inn's porch without arranging to. Ivo with his pipe. Marta with the day's first cider tray. Odile with a book of star tables open on her knee, reading by the last sun. Elias with his sleeves rolled and ink to the second knuckle, carrying a letter from Coldharbour that he had read four times and would read aloud presently. And Ansel on the top step, hat off — she wore the braid openly now, and had stopped noticing strangers noticing — watching the road dust settle where the day's last travelers had passed.

"They asked me again today," she said, to no one in particular. "In the shop. Whether it's true the clock struck thirteen."

"What did you tell them?" Elias asked.

"That a clock strikes what it's built to strike." She turned the little brass escapement over in her fingers — the one that lived in her pocket now, the twin of the one resting on a grave above the tree line. "And that sometimes what it's built to strike is the truth, one hour at a time, forever, whether anyone's listening or not."

Odile tapped the arm of Ivo's chair and signed something quick and laughing, and Ivo, whose sign language remained a scandal of approximation, guessed wrong, and Odile laughed aloud — a sound the whole square had learned to stop and enjoy — and Elias cleared his throat and unfolded the letter from Coldharbour.

"It's the summons," he said. "For the assize. They're setting the trial date. Midsummer, as expected." He paused, and the pause had a shape to it, and everyone on the porch heard the shape. "But there's a line at the end. A request. They want the master clockmaker of Vell to appear as an expert witness — to testify on the sabotage of the striking train. To explain to the court, formally, on the record, exactly how the clock was made to speak."

The porch was quiet. Somewhere below, the river ran loud with meltwater, hurrying toward the sea with the whole winter's news.

"They mean you, Ansel," Ivo said, unnecessarily, because everyone already knew, and because some things deserve saying anyway.

Ansel looked up at the tower, gold-lit, ticking, and slid the little escapement back into her pocket, and smiled.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd best prepare the witness."
