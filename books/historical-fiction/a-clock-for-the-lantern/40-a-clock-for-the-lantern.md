## 40. A Clock for the Lantern

The autumn light came down the mountain in long slanted sheets, and Ansel Quintana stood at the top of the tower with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and brass filings glittering on her forearms like a second skin.

Below her, Vell arranged itself for evening. Smoke rose from the bakery chimneys first, then the houses along the river road, then the Lantern Inn, whose sign swung gently on its bracket and caught the last sun the way it had caught it every evening for years — faithfully, without comment, the way Marta did everything. The square was filling with the small honest traffic of dusk: Ivo crossing from the miners' hall with his cane tapping a rhythm he never varied, Odile coming up the observatory path with a satchel of star charts over one shoulder, children racing the lamplighter to the corners.

Ansel looked at all of it the way she had once looked at a movement laid open on her bench — counting the parts, admiring how they fit, knowing precisely where the friction lived and where the oil went.

Then she turned back to the work.

It had taken her three years to design it, off and on, between commissions and repairs and the endless patient labor of keeping the master clock true. A lantern dial: a face of ground glass backed by a ring of oil lamps, geared so that the flames turned with the hands, so that anyone standing in the square below — anyone walking home late from the ferry landing or the garrison gate or the far end of the mill row — could look up and read the hour in light instead of guessing at shadows. Other towns had illuminated dials, she knew. Geneva claimed one; so did half the capitals of the plain. But those were lit by gas and money, and this one would be lit by hand, wound each night like everything else in Vell that was worth keeping.

She had built the frame herself, walnut from the south slope, and fitted the glass with the same care she gave an escapement, because light through imperfect glass shivered, and shivering light told lies about time.

"You're stalling."

She didn't startle. After all these years, Odile's voice arriving at the top of a tower still surprised most people — she came up the ladder soundless, having learned long ago to feel footsteps through wood rather than hear them — but Ansel always knew when she was near, some register below hearing that had nothing to do with ears.

"I'm checking my work," Ansel said, facing her so Odile could read her lips. The habit was so old now that Ansel spoke more clearly than she had as a girl, shaping consonants like a woman setting type.

Odile set down her satchel and signed as she answered aloud, her two languages braided together the way they always were, hands and mouth saying the same thing twice because she liked the way it felt. *You checked your work eleven times. I counted.* Then, grinning: "Marta wants to know if we're lighting it tonight or if she should sell tickets."

"We're lighting it tonight." Ansel wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the dial one more time — the great pale face of it, the ring of twelve lamp housings behind the glass, each one fed by its own little wick-gear, the whole assembly driven off the striking train by a shaft she had bored through timber that had not been disturbed since before her father died. "If the wind holds. If the draft doesn't gutter them. If the governor—"

"If the governor fails you'll rebuild it," Odile said. "You always do. Come down. Elias brought wine and Ivo brought opinions and Marta brought both of them to heel."

* * *

They ate in the inn's back room, the one with the low ceiling and the long table that Marta had bought, broken, and repaired in the first spring after the thaw, when it became clear that certain people would be staying in Vell whether or not the town quite knew what to do with them.

Ansel sat in her usual place — left of Marta, across from Odile, with the corner of the room at her back. She had needed years to learn to sit facing outward, to let a room hold her instead of bracing against it. Some evenings she still caught herself calculating exits, mapping the doorways out of pure old instinct, and when she noticed she would look around the table — at Ivo arguing cheerfully with Elias about the wording of the compensation petitions, at Odile signing something outrageous to Marta across the table while Marta pretended not to understand — and the calculation would simply dissolve, unneeded, like frost in sun.

"To the clock," Elias said, raising his glass when the argument burned down. He was grayer at the temples than his father had ever allowed him to be in memory; the magistrate's house on the hill still stood, shuttered, waiting for a trial that had been promised and postponed through three autumns now. Elias had moved into rooms above the records office — the rebuilt records office — and spent his days turning other men's ledgers right side up. "To the clock, which has kept better time than any of us."

"To the clock," they said, and Ivo added, "and to the woman who makes it lie beautifully when lying is called for," and Ansel threw her napkin at him.

Later, when the plates were cleared and Marta had chased the last of the town councilmen out of the front room, the five of them climbed the tower together, slowly, Ivo resting on each landing, Odile carrying the lamp, Marta carrying a bottle she declared was ceremonial and refused to explain further.

At the top, in the cold clean wind, Ansel showed them the lantern dial.

She had expected to give a speech. She had composed one, actually, in the nights of fitting the wick-gears — something about time belonging to everyone, about how a clock you couldn't read in the dark was a clock for people who could afford candles. But standing there with her family crowded around her under the great dark face of the tower, the speech fell away, and what she said instead was simpler.

"My father used to say that a clock is a promise the town makes to itself." Her voice carried oddly in the stone throat of the belfry. "That when it strikes, everyone inside the sound is bound to everyone else. I used to think that was just something clockmakers said." She touched the walnut frame, warm even in the cold from her day of handling it. "I've made a lot of clocks. This is the first one I've made as a promise."

Odile stepped forward and signed, slowly, deliberately, for all of them though only Ansel and Marta could follow easily: *Light it.*

Ansel opened the housing and began lighting the lamps, one by one, working clockwise from midnight, and the others watched the faces come alive behind the glass — soft golden circles blooming in the dark ring, the hour-hand shadow thrown huge and gentle across the ground-glass face. When she reached the last lamp and closed the housing, she stepped back beside Marta, and they all looked up together.

Above them, the clock glowed. Below them, the square began to notice.

It happened the way such things happen — one child shouting first, then doors opening, then people coming out into the street with their collars turned up, pointing, laughing, calling to their neighbors to come and see. The lamplighter stood directly beneath it with his pole forgotten in his hand. Somewhere near the fountain someone started clapping, and then it was not one person clapping but the whole square, a sound like rain on a tin roof, rising up the tower stones to where the five of them stood.

"They can read it from the bridge," Odile said wonderingly, looking toward the river. And she was right — the new bridge, wide enough for carts, open nine months of the year now that the town had voted the funds, glowed faintly in the reflected light of the dial, and travelers on it had stopped mid-crossing to stare up at the tower.

Marta uncorked the ceremonial bottle without ceremony and poured. "To the clockmaker," she said. "Who came here under a false name and stayed under a true one."

"To Anselma Quintana," said Ivo.

"To Ans," said Odile, and grinned, because she had earned the short name and knew it.

Ansel drank. The wine was local and too sweet and perfect. Around her the square went on celebrating a thing she had made, and she thought — not for the first time, but with a completeness that felt new — of the girl who had come up this mountain seven years ago with forged papers and a dead man's name folded flat inside her chest like a document hidden in a case.

She had come to repair a clock. She had found, in its escapement chamber, the proof that had hung her father; she had found, in its gears, a language precise enough to say what no court in the province would hear. She had found Marta, who had sized her up in one glance across the Lantern Inn's common room and decided — correctly, as it turned out — that she was a fellow runner in need of a route. She had found Odile reading lips through telescope glass and given her a tower full of mechanisms and eventually a life measured in shared shifts and signal-lanterns and the particular intimacy of two women who could sit together in silence for hours and call it conversation. She had found Elias, who had traded his name for his conscience and never once asked for it back. She had found Ivo, keeper of griefs, reader of ledgers aloud by lantern light, who still came to the tower every Sunday to wind the case clock in the entry with his own hands because he said a man ought to put muscle into timekeeping at least once a week.

And she had found, hardest of all, herself — not in some grand moment of revelation but in the slow accumulated work of being seen: Marta braiding her hair on ceremony eve, Odile signing her name into the guild witness line, the whole town chanting *Quintana* in the square the night thirteen strokes rang out and the pages rained down like snow that finally, finally told the truth.

"Come down before you freeze," Marta said now, touching her arm. "The clock won't stop if you blink."

But Ansel lingered one moment longer at the rail, looking down at the lit square, at the people milling beneath the glow, at the boy from the mill row standing with his head back and his mouth open the way she had once stood in front of shop windows as a child, wanting things that belonged to other lives.

The dial would need winding every night. Someone would have to climb in all weather, trim the wicks, fill the reservoirs, replace the glass when hail came off the mountain. It was, by any rational accounting, a gift that cost more to keep than to give.

That was the point of it. That was what she had finally understood about promises, and clocks, and towns, and family: the keeping was the gift. The daily climbing, the trimming, the showing up in wind and thaw and first snow — none of it was maintenance. All of it was love wearing work clothes.

She followed the others down the ladder, Odile ahead of her, Marta's lamp swaying below, Ivo's laugh echoing up the stairwell, and behind them the lantern dial burned steadily over Vell, telling the hour to anyone who looked up.

At the bottom of the tower, in the doorway where the cold met the lamplight of the square, Ansel paused and took out the old letter — she kept it still, softened now to cloth by handling, the forged summons that had started everything, *the observatory's master clock requires the attention of a qualified apprentice, urgently* — and she read it once more by the light of her own clock burning above her.

Urgently. All those years ago, and the summons had been urgent indeed — just not in the way its writer meant.

She folded it away, turned up her collar, and walked out into the square to be seen.

The clock struck the hour above her — twelve clear strokes, neither more nor fewer, a promise kept exactly — and Ansel Quintana answered it the only way she ever had, the only way she ever would:

She came.
