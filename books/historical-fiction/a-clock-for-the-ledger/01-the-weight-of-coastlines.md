## 1. The Weight of Coastlines

My master died on a Tuesday, which I have always considered uncharacteristically careless of him. Master Oren Vail was a man who checked his knots twice and his measurements four times, who would not commit a river to vellum until he had heard it named by three separate sources, and who once made me redraw the entire eastern shore of the Gulf of Ansel because I had rounded a headland by a degree and a half. A degree and a half. He died falling down his own stairs, in his own house, on a Tuesday evening, as if geography itself had finally decided to punish him for a life of precision.

But that is getting ahead of things. This morning there was ink to mix.

I sat at the long workbench under the north window of the workshop — the good window, the one with glass that cost more than my mother's coffin — and I ground gall-nuts until my shoulder ached, and I told myself that this was because the atlas required a fresh batch, iron-gall being temperamental stuff that will not sit overnight without turning the color of an old bruise. I told myself that the ache in my shoulders was from grinding, not from carrying anything heavier.

The atlas lay open before me at its last plate: the coastline of Vessamare itself, drawn in pencil by the master's hand and waiting eleven days now for my pen. It was the only unfinished plate in the whole book. Forty-one coastlines, three hundred islands, two hundred rivers rendered with the patience of a man who believed God lived in the details and was checking His proofs — all done, and then the last one, our own town's shore, left bare. Pencil lines like something remembered rather than committed.

Perhaps he had been saving it. Perhaps he had simply run out of Tuesdays.

I dipped the pen. My hand did not shake, which surprised me, and which I decided not to think about, because thinking about steadiness is the fastest way I know to lose it.

Here is a thing they do not teach apprentices: coastlines are lies. Every map ever made has lied about them, and every mapmaker knows it, and we all agree not to mention it at dinner parties. A coast at true scale is a fractal nightmare, an infinite argument between water and stone, and no pen in the world can hold it. So we choose a scale, and the choosing is where the lying begins. Draw a coast small enough and the coves vanish. Draw it fine enough and your map becomes a country unto itself, useless to anyone who wants to actually sail past it.

Master Vail used to say you could measure distance in miles or in days, but never both at once, because they disagree. A mile is fixed. A day depends entirely on whose feet are doing the walking, what they're carrying, and what they're afraid of.

I drew the harbor wall first, because it was easiest. Stone on stone, the way the masons built it, each block accounted for. Then the fish market behind it, which on a map of this scale was nothing but a rectangle with a label, though I knew that at dawn it smelled of brine and lamp-oil and old men arguing about prices their grandfathers had argued about. Then the salt warehouses, the customhouse, the crooked lane called Tallow Row where the chandlers worked and the air was always faintly greasy. Then the town proper, climbing the hill in terraces like a stubborn plant growing toward light it didn't fully trust.

I measured none of it in miles. I measured it in days of walking, the way the master taught me, and I found that Vessamare, end to end, was a very short place indeed. You could walk from the harbor wall to the hill gate in half a morning, slow, stopping to buy bread. You could walk out of it entirely — out of the atlas, off the plate, into the blank margin beyond the map's edge — in a single day if you were determined, or in an afternoon if someone were following you.

I had never walked out of it. Twenty-two years, nineteen of them in this workshop, and I had never once needed to leave. That is not humility; that is engineering. I had made myself small the way you make a joint invisible in fine cabinetry: not by hiding, but by fitting so exactly into the space provided that no one thinks to look for a seam. The apprentice. Ishikawa's boy, though Ishikawa was my father's name and dead these twelve years, and Vail took me in when the town decided an orphaned foreigner's son was better as an apprentice than as a charity case, charities being thin in Vessamare and suspicion being plentiful. *The quiet one,* people called me, approvingly, the way you'd approve of furniture. *Good hands, that boy. Says nothing.*

It is remarkable how much a person can see while saying nothing. I had watched the whole town pass through this workshop at one time or another — sea captains wanting charts, merchants wanting their holdings flattered, the harbormaster wanting the sandbar at the harbor mouth moved six yards to the east because it embarrassed him where it was. I drew what they paid for and kept what I saw. It seemed a fair trade.

The pen moved. Headland by headland, the coast of home assembled itself under my hand, and I confess that I drew it finer than any other plate in the atlas. Not because it deserved more care — well, yes, because it deserved more care — but because I could not help measuring it honestly. Here was the rock called the Nun, where Marisol and I had nearly drowned ourselves at fourteen trying to prove we wouldn't. Here was the shingle beach below her father's house, where old Cortez Marquez had taught us both to read the stars by pointing with a boat-hook, shouting that a cartographer who trusted only instruments deserved the rocks he'd get. Here was the little estuary where the river Sennet gave up and became the sea, drawn — I checked three times — to the degree and a half.

He would have noticed if I hadn't. That was the trouble with grief: it kept setting exams for the dead to grade.

* * *

Toward midday, Tam Rennick knocked the way he does everything, which is too hard and without apology. Tam is the clerk's under-clerk, a round, damp young man whose function in the civic organism is to carry paper uphill and complaints downhill.

"Isandro." He stood in the doorway wiping his boots with excessive thoroughness, which meant he'd already tracked mud in and knew it. "Clerk Ambrose's compliments. He wants the atlas inventoried. Officially."

"Inventoried," I said.

"Registered civic property, now the master's gone. Estate matters." Tam looked everywhere except at the atlas, which is Tam's way of apologizing for things he has no power over. "There's forms."

Of course there were forms. Vessamare could not bury a man without paperwork; I suspected that when the plague finally came, it would arrive bearing documents requiring counter-signature.

"When?"

"By week's end. Also—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide whether the second part of his errand was official or personal, and saw him land, characteristically, straddling the line, "—also, they're asking questions. About the master. About how he fell."

"Stairs fall down," I said. "It's the traditional direction."

"Isandro." Tam lowered his voice as though the stairs might be listening. There were, in fact, stairs in the room, leading up to the loft where the master had slept, and I confess I had not looked at them since Tuesday. "People are saying he was agitated lately. That he'd been up to the customhouse twice asking after cargo manifests. That he sent a letter to the capital and burned the draft in his own grate, and Marta Ashdown saw the paper through the window and says it wasn't a letter at all, it was a list."

"A list," I repeated, in the flat tone I keep for such occasions. It is a useful tone. It makes people feel foolish for having spoken.

Tam shrugged the shrug of a man who delivers messages and disclaims them in equal measure, and left, taking most of the air with him.

I sat with the atlas for a while after that. The harbor wall gleamed wetly, fresh-inked. Somewhere outside, gulls conducted their eternal negotiation over nothing, and the tide came in the way tides do, which is to say regardless.

A list. The master had been a careful man, and careful men do not burn drafts unless the draft is dangerous, and drafts are only dangerous when what they contain cannot be unsaid. I thought of the locked drawer in his writing desk — the bottom one, iron-bound, the key absent from his ring the night they brought him up from the foot of the stairs — and then I thought of something else instead, because that was how I managed things. One drawer at a time. One grief at a time. Small steps, small self.

* * *

In the afternoon I finished the plate.

It took four hours longer than it should have, because I kept stopping — not from trembling hands but from the opposite problem, from catching myself drawing too slowly, too lovingly, giving the Nun its lichen and the estuary its third sandbar and the fish market its crooked roofline, when the scale permitted none of it. A coastline should be a summary. I was writing a eulogy in ink and calling it cartography.

Marisol came by at the gray hour, when the light goes soft and honest and makes liars of everyone's complexion. She let herself in the way she always has, without knocking, and stood looking over my shoulder at the plate for a long moment before she said anything.

"You've drawn the Nun wrong," she said. "Too small."

"The scale—"

"I know what the scale permits." Her voice was level, but her father's daughter looks at coastlines the way priests look at scripture, and she was not fooled for a second. "You've given it a soul anyway. He'd have approved."

She said *he*, and we both let it stand there in the room with us, unburied. Marisol had come back from the capital a month ago, before the master died, and I still did not know everything she'd brought back with her besides her trunk and a new stillness around her eyes. Her father's letters from the capital had stopped in spring. His maps — the beautiful coastal surveys that every sailor in Vessamare swore by — still hung in the workshop's back room, because nobody had told me to take them down, and I had not volunteered.

"He never finished this plate," she said, touching the margin above the pencil ghost of the town. "Eleven days I've been back and it was still blank when he died. Why do you suppose?"

"Saving the best for last."

"Or couldn't bear to." She said it lightly, but Marisol says heavy things lightly; it is a family trait, possibly hereditary, possibly contagious, since I caught myself doing it too. "Will you sign it? Apprentice plates get the master's name and the shop mark. But this one's yours, really. First coastline you've owned."

"Not owned. Finished." There is a difference, and every apprentice learns it eventually: you finish what another man began, and the work passes through you like light through glass, and if you're honest, the glass leaves no color of its own. "Vail's atlas. Vail's name."

"And yours underneath, small."

"Small is where I live," I said, and she laughed, briefly, and the laugh did what laughs do, which is less than hoped but not nothing.

She stayed while I washed the pens and capped the ink and laid the plate under weights to dry. At the door she paused — and here I must record what happened accurately, because accuracy is the whole of my trade and I have begun to suspect it is also going to be the death of me — she paused and said:

"If it gets worse, Isandro. What you promised. When we were children on the Nun, and again last month. You remember the terms?"

"I remember the terms."

"Good." She looked at me a moment longer, reading my face the way her father read weather, and whatever she found there, she did not say. Then she was gone down Tallow Row, and the workshop was mine again, and the atlas lay finished except for signing, forty-two plates, a town reduced to ink, a coast weighed in days.

I blew out the lamp. And in the dark, before climbing to the loft, I stood a moment at the foot of the stairs my master had fallen down, and looked up into the black where the loft was, and beyond it, in the corner of my mind where I keep the things I am not yet ready to look at directly, sat a desk with an iron-bound drawer, and a missing key, and a list that a careful man had thought dangerous enough to burn twice.

The atlas was done. Which meant that tomorrow, for the first time in nineteen years, there was no work standing between me and everything else.

I went up the stairs carefully. I counted them as I climbed — fourteen — and told myself it was habit, and knew I was lying, and slept anyway, badly, the way the diligent sleep when diligence runs out of excuses.
