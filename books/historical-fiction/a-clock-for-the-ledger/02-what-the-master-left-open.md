## 2. What the Master Left Open

My master died on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult to the rest of the week. Tuesdays in the workshop were ink-mixing days, and there is something obscene about a man being struck down on a day that smelled of oak galls and rainwater, when the morning's first task had already been decided for him. He should have died on a Sunday, at least, when nothing was expected of anyone.

Instead he died at his desk with a pen behind his ear and the last plate of the atlas half-finished under his elbow, and three days later it fell to me—his apprentice, his lodger, and now, apparently, his executor—to sort through everything he'd left open.

Which was nearly all of it. Master Oren Ishikawa was not a tidy man. He was tidy about his work—the man could draw a meridian so fine you'd swear the paper was embarrassed to have a line on it—but the rest of his life existed in a state of cheerful sediment. Shelves of charts layered over shelves of older charts. A chair whose seat was invisible beneath a geology of pamphlets, proofs, and at least one boot. A teacup I had been meaning to throw out since my second year of apprenticeship and had instead developed a kind of respectful distance from, the way one does with a hornet's nest.

Now I had to go through all of it. The town clerk had been very clear about this, in the way clerks are clear about everything: an inventory of the workshop's contents must be made before any inheritance could be formally transferred to the apprentice. Paper before grief. That is Vessamare's whole liturgy in four words.

So I sorted.

I began with the easy things, because I am a coward by inclination and have found the habit serves me well. The pens went into one box. The unused quires of paper into another. I found eleven penknives, each purchased because the previous one had been misplaced, none of them actually lost, and put them all in a jar like rescued drowning men.

The physical act of touching a dead man's belongings does something to you that standing at his funeral does not. At the funeral I had felt properly solemn, which is to say I had felt nothing much at all and dressed the feeling in black. But holding his spare spectacles—the ones he kept for reading in bad light, the ones with the crack he refused to have mended because "the crack divides the page honestly"—that undid me. I sat on the floor among the sediment for some while, holding cracked spectacles, being twenty years old and entirely unqualified for the day.

Then I got up, because the alternative was to sit there until someone found my skeleton wearing a dead man's glasses, and that would be an undignified entry for the civic record.

* * *

By afternoon I reached the drafting table itself, and here the sorting stopped being archaeology and became trespass.

The atlas occupied the center of the table like a patient mid-surgery. *The Coasts and Soundings of the Middle Sea,* the title page read, in Oren's small exact hand. Eleven plates finished and colored. Coastlines from the southern capes to the northern archipelago, every harbor sounded, every shoal charted, every cliff drawn with the affectionate attention he otherwise reserved only for his lunch. It was to be his masterwork. Every mapmaker in the province had known it would be; Oren himself had pretended not to know any such thing, and referred to it as "the side project," usually while stroking it.

Twelve plates were commissioned. Eleven were done.

The twelfth lay beneath the others, covered with a cloth he never used for anything else—a square of blue sailcloth that smelled, when I lifted it, faintly of tar and salt. And I confess my hands hesitated over it. There are objects that carry their own instructions. This one said: *not yet.*

I lifted it anyway. Grief makes obedient people of us in some directions and criminals in all the others.

The final plate was the coast of Vessamare itself. Of course it was. He had saved home for last, the way you save the best bite or the hardest conversation, and he had gotten as far as the harbor mouth before stopping. The headlands west of town were complete—every fold of them rendered with that peculiar love mapmakers develop for terrain, as though rock could be flattered. The town was blocked in lightly, awaiting detail. The harbor wall was a single tentative line. And eastward, where the coast ran up past the salt pans toward the cliffs, the pencil simply stopped, mid-stroke, as though the hand that held it had been called away to something more urgent.

He had died with his home coastline unfinished. There was a sermon in that, if I attended church, which I did not, mostly because the sermons there were worse than the one this plate preached without any words at all.

I set the blue cloth back over it gently, the way you'd draw a sheet over a face.

It was then that I noticed the drawer.

* * *

You should understand something about the workbench in our workshop. It was old—older than Oren, older probably than the town's interest in accurate maps—and it had a dozen drawers, most of them swollen shut with salt air and neglect. Oren used perhaps six of them. Pens in one, tools in another, and so forth in the diminishing fashion of a man who had organized his working life sometime around his thirtieth year and saw no reason to renegotiate terms with furniture.

But there was one drawer, low on the right-hand side, just below where a seated man's knee would knock it, that was different. It had a lock. Small, brass, gone green at the seams, but a lock nonetheless, and in nine years of apprenticeship I had never once seen it open.

Nine years. Consider what that means in the economy of an apprentice's curiosity. I had opened every other drawer in that workshop within my first month, mostly looking for snacks. I had read Oren's letters, his accounts, a bundle of extremely embarrassing poems from his youth that I preserved as leverage and later, when I grew fond enough of him to be ashamed of myself, burned. I knew where he hid his money (in the flour crock, which explained why his bread was always terrible) and where he hid his brandy (behind the atlases marked "for reference," which explained several of his opinions about northern cartographers).

But that drawer stayed shut, and whenever I had asked about it—younger, braver versions of me asked maybe once a year, the way children ask about scars—he would give the same answer. Not angry. Not evasive, even. Just final, in the way of a door that has been painted shut.

"That drawer belongs to the part of the work that isn't finished," he'd say, and go back to his inking, and somehow the sentence would end the conversation without ending it, the way a coastline ends a map.

*What work?* I'd asked, the first time, aged eleven, all elbows and appetite for other people's secrets.

"The kind that gets people hurt if it's done badly." Then, seeing my face: "Not yours, little fish. Eat your lunch."

Little fish. That was his name for me the whole nine years, delivered without affection or irony but with a strange tenderness I never learned to translate. Little fish, bring the burnisher. Little fish, you've smudged the Adriatic again. Little fish, don't grow up to be honest; honesty is a fine quality in eulogies and a terrible one in tradesmen.

I knelt down in front of the drawer now, alone in the lamplight with a dead man's secrets and no permission left to refuse them, because permission requires a living granter.

The lock was brass and old and I am a mapmaker, not a locksmith, but I am also twenty, and I had a penknife, and pride is a kind of key if you're desperate enough to turn it. It took me the better part of an hour and cost me two nicks on my knuckles, and then the lock gave with a click so small I almost missed it, and the drawer slid out on runners that had waited so long for motion they complained the whole way.

Inside: papers. Perhaps thirty sheets, folded twice, stacked with a neatness entirely foreign to the rest of the workshop. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a book. Ledger-sized. Bound in grey.

I did not touch them.

That will sound like a lie by the end of this account, so let me be precise about the shape of the truth: I knelt there for a long moment with the drawer open and the book sitting inside it like a heart in a chest, and I wanted—I want to be honest, since we've begun—so badly that my hands shook. Curiosity is supposed to be a gentle thing, a cat's thing, but mine came up through the floorboards like floodwater, and underneath the curiosity, colder and moving faster, was unease. Because the papers on top were folded *uniformly*. Folded by a careful hand, recently, many times over. Whatever they were, my master had read them often, and hidden them always, and he had died on a Tuesday with a pen behind his ear in a town where nobody dies of anything but age and fish.

And I thought: whatever is in this drawer got him killed, or didn't, and either way I am not ready to know which.

I closed the drawer. I locked it, badly, with the penknife. I sat back on my heels feeling like the world's most virtuous coward, which is a sensation I recommend, briefly, to no one.

* * *

Here is why my hands were shaking, and why I couldn't simply walk away and let the drawer keep its counsel. You'll need to know about Marisol.

We were nine years old, both of us, the summer her father came to Vessamare. Renzo Vail had been a cartographer of some reputation in the capital—too much reputation, as it turned out, though we didn't learn that part until later—and he arrived with a daughter, a trunk of charts, and a laugh you could hear across two rooftops. Oren took him on as a collaborator, partly for his skill and partly, I suspect, because Oren had been lonely in a way that only became visible once it was cured.

Marisol and I spent that whole summer mapping the town, in the fierce serious way of children. We surveyed the breakwater. We measured the fish market in footsteps and argued about the conversion rate of footsteps to yards. She was better at it than me, which I resented for exactly as long as it took to realize that being second-best to Marisol Vail was considerably more pleasant than being best at anything else, and after that I resented it pleasantly for the rest of our lives so far.

It was the evening before her family left—they were going back to the capital at summer's end; her father had business, her mother had roots, Marisol had opinions about both—that we sat on the harbor wall with our feet hanging over the water, and she made me promise.

"If things ever get bad," she said. She was watching the boats, not me, in the way she had of aiming sentences sideways. "If we ever have to run, or hide, or—if things ever get bad, you come find me. Or I come find you. Whichever of us still has a door."

"Things won't get bad," I said, with the magnificent authority of a boy who had never once been correct about the future.

"Promise anyway."

"I promise," I said, because refusing her anything had never once worked, and because promises made on harbor walls at nine years old carry the same legal weight as wishes on stars, which is to say none, which is why they're safe to make.

She nodded once, satisfied, and stole the last of my bread, and that was the entire ceremony. Neither of us knew we were swearing. Children make vows the way rivers make canyons—without seeming to press hard at all, and then one day you look up and the landscape has been remade.

Her family left. Letters came, fewer each year. Her father's name began appearing in the margins of things—in a magistrate's complaint about unlicensed surveys, in a rumor about a court faction fallen from favor, in the careful silence of people who had known him and now claimed to have known him slightly. The last letter Marisol ever sent me was four lines long and ended, *Do not write to the house anymore. I will find a way to find you.* Which is a sentence that reads differently at nineteen than it does at twelve.

And then, eight months before my master died, she appeared at the workshop door with a traveling cloak and her father's eyes and no explanation I was entitled to ask for. She stayed three days, sleeping in the loft, eating Oren's terrible bread with the expression of a woman conducting an investigation into it. She and Oren talked late both nights, low-voiced, in the register adults use when the subject is danger. I was not invited into those conversations, and I want it recorded that I did not sulk about this. Much. In a loft. Where sounds carry.

On the third morning she stood at the door with her hood up, and I walked with her as far as the harbor wall, because we are ourselves, and because some places insist on being returned to.

"It's bad, isn't it," I said. "Whatever it is your father did."

"He drew true maps," she said. "Apparently that's a crime now, depending on who's asking." Her jaw was set in a way I recognized from her father. "Isandro. The promise."

"It's nine years old."

"Promises don't age. That's the whole point of them." And then, quieter, with the sideways aim: "I may need to disappear someday. If I do, I need to know there's one door in the world that opens."

"You're looking at the door," I said, and meant it so completely that it frightened me.

She touched my hand—briefly, the way you check whether a surface is hot—and went, and I stood on the harbor wall like a fool with his heart hanging out, and went back and mixed ink and told no one, and told myself that was discretion rather than cowardice.

So when I knelt in front of my dead master's locked drawer, eight months later, with her father's name somewhere in the cold arithmetic of the times and her promise warm in my pocket like a coin I hadn't earned—when I looked at a book bound in grey that a careful man had hidden behind a lock for nine years—

Well. My hands shook for reasons that had names.

* * *

I inventoried the rest of the workshop by rote. Charts, 214. Instruments, complete. Books, assorted. One apprentice, uninjured except in the two places the penknife had found and everywhere else besides. I wrote it all out for the clerk in my best round hand, and I listed the drawer as "locked; contents unknown; presumed stationery," which is the finest lie I had told up to that point in my life, and I include in that count every excuse I ever gave Oren about smudged horizons.

The clerk would collect the inventory in the morning. Which meant the clerk—or whoever the clerk answered to, and lately there was always someone further up the hill to answer to—would have cause to open that workshop, and look at that bench, and notice, as I had noticed, that one drawer among a dozen was built different.

I stood in the doorway at closing time with the lamp blown out and the dark coming in off the water, and I did the sums a coward does. A man who hides papers is afraid of readers. A man who dies suddenly with a pen behind his ear has, statistically speaking, either terrible luck or excellent enemies. And an apprentice who has promised a fugitive a door, and who is now the sole keeper of a locked drawer full of folded secrets, is not really an apprentice anymore, whatever the ledger says.

Tomorrow I would go to the clerk's office and begin the work they'd assign me—copying, updating, the ordinary paper-shuffling of an ordinary port town. Tomorrow the world would resume pretending to be safe.

Tonight, I took the blue cloth off the unfinished plate one more time and looked at the coastline my master had died short of finishing. The pencil line stopped east of the salt pans, mid-stroke, pointing at nothing.

Or pointing at the drawer. From certain angles, on certain evenings, those amount to the same thing.

I covered the plate. I put the penknife in my pocket, where my thumb kept finding the nicks. And I went home through streets that smelled of low tide and woodsmoke, rehearsing, without quite admitting it, the various ways a man might pick a lock a second time—faster, this time, and with the doors barred, and with the lamp turned low.

Because whatever my master had been reading, folding, and hiding, he had hidden it below the reach of his own knee, in the dark, for years.

And things that are hidden that carefully are never hidden for their owner's sake.
