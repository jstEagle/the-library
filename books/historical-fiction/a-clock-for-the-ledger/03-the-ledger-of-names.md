## 3. The Ledger of Names

The town clerk of Vessamare was named Odil Brac, and he had the posture of a man who had spent forty years apologizing to furniture. He was narrow as a ledger spine and smelled faintly of sealing wax and the pickled herring he ate at his desk every day at noon with a devotion I can only describe as liturgical. When he came to the workshop three days after my master's funeral, I assumed he'd come about the survey fees—my master had been owed money by the town since before I could hold a pen.

He hadn't come about the fees.

"Apprentice Ishikawa," he said, using my surname the way other men used titles, which flattered me more than it should have, "the council has need of a steady hand."

I should have asked what kind of need. A steady hand is a phrase that sounds like praise and functions like a summons. But I was twenty-two, newly masterless, and the word *council* made my chest do something warm and stupid. My master had spent thirty years teaching me that maps were arguments—that whoever drew the coastline decided where the land ended—and I had learned everything except how to be suspicious of men who said *need* instead of *want*.

"The civic ledger," Brac explained, settling onto the workshop stool as though he owned it, which in a sense he did, since he owned most things in this town that couldn't run away. "It must be brought current. Every household. Every birth registered since the spring fair. Every death." He paused there, delicately, the way a man pauses when the next word is your dead teacher's name. "Every debt."

"And you want me to write it?"

"I want you to *fair-copy* it. The old book is a disgrace. Entries crossed out, margins full of gossip, half the fishermen recorded under two surnames because nobody could decide whether their mothers married once or twice." He sniffed. "You are trained to precision. Precision is what the ledger requires."

What the ledger required, I would learn, was not precision. What it required was obedience wearing precision's coat. But I didn't know that yet, so I said yes, and Brac paid me in advance—a gesture so unlike the town treasury that I should have understood immediately something was wrong. Governments pay late. Governments that pay early are frightened, or preparing, or both.

* * *

The ledger lived in the clerk's office above the customhouse, in a room that smelled the way I imagine a bank vault smells if the bank also stored onions. It was a real book—not one of Brac's working copies but the *Liber Civium*, bound in calfskin gone brown as old rope, its corners rubbed round by eighty years of hands. It sat on a lectern like a gospel, and in a way it was one. Open it anywhere and you found a person: born on such a day to such parents, apprenticed to such a trade, taxed at such a rate, buried in such a plot. Whole lives compressed into lines of copperplate, the ink faded from black to the color of dried blood.

Brac set me at a side table with loose quires—the raw material, years of slips and notes waiting to be entered into the great book—and gave me rules before he gave me work.

"Names first," he said. "A name is the whole of a person, as far as this book is concerned. Spell it as it is written in the prior entry, even if it is wrong. Consistency over accuracy. Accuracy invites dispute; consistency ends it."

"Even if it's wrong?"

"*Especially* if it's wrong." He looked at me over his spectacles with the mild patience of a man explaining tides to a gull. "The ledger does not record the truth, apprentice. It records what was written. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire art of governance."

I wrote that down in my notebook without thinking—I have always written down sentences that frighten me, the way some people touch iron—and Brac saw me do it and seemed pleased, which frightened me further.

The work itself was simple and therefore terrible. Household by household, street by street, beginning at the harbor and climbing the hill. The Serrano family, four souls, one boat, debts to the chandlery totaling eleven crowns. The widow Anselma, one soul, no boat, no debts, noted in the margin of the old book as *troublesome* in an earlier clerk's hand, a judgment I was instructed to preserve because judgments, once entered, were load-bearing. The Ferreol brothers, who shared one house and one wife between them under an arrangement the ledger called *irregular* and the town called Tuesday.

I knew these people. That was the thing I hadn't anticipated. I had drawn their rooftops. I had walked past their doors ten thousand times. Old Anselma had given me a candied fig every solstice since I was six, and here she was reduced to *one soul, troublesome*, and my hand—my steady hand, the hand the council needed—wrote her down without trembling at all.

That night I mentioned it to Marisol. She was staying with the widow Anselma, actually, in the blue house above the ropewalk, since her father's arrest had left their own rooms forfeit. She came to the workshop most evenings, ostensibly to sort her father's rescued papers, actually to sit near another human being while the town pretended she didn't exist.

"You're writing us down," she said. Not accusing. Just interested, the way she was interested in weather and hull damage and other forces larger than herself.

"I'm copying you down. You were already in it."

"Were we?" She turned her father's brass dividers over in her fingers, a habit she had, worrying the screw. "I don't remember being asked."

"That's rather the point of ledgers."

"Yes," she said, and put the dividers down, and looked at me with an expression I would spend the rest of this story learning to read. "And whose point is it, Isandro? Who wanted the book?"

I didn't have an answer then. I said something clever about civic order, and she let me be clever, which was her particular cruelty, and we went back to sorting papers in the lamplight. But walking home through the empty fish market afterward, I kept seeing the page in my mind: *Ferreol, Bastian; Ferreol, Remy; one dwelling, irregular.* Two brothers I'd known all my life, flattened into a line, and the line filed in a drawer, and the drawer locked, and somewhere at the end of that chain of custody a person existed only if the paper said so.

My master used to say that a map was a promise the land never agreed to. I was starting to understand that a ledger was worse. A map lies about places. A ledger lies about people, and people believe it.

* * *

By the second week I had developed a system, because I am my master's apprentice and systems are our love language. I copied in the mornings when my hand was freshest, entered the harbor families first because they changed fastest—sailors lost, sailors returned, babies that the priest recorded and babies that the priest conspicuously didn't—and left the hill households for afternoons, when the light through the customhouse windows went amber and Brac dozed at his desk like a cat that had appointed itself watchdog.

It was during those amber hours that I began to notice what the ledger noticed.

It noticed everything, was the answer. It noticed that the cooper Ovid Rell had bought twice the usual barrel staves in March. It noticed that the ferryman Teo had carried passengers to the north shore on four nights when no market justified a crossing. It noticed that the physician's payments from certain families had stopped in the same season that certain families' names had acquired asterisks in the margin—asterisks in a clerk's hand meaning, I eventually gathered, *watch*. Nothing was accused. Everything was recorded. The ledger never said *smuggler* or *heretic* or *traitor*. It just arranged the facts in rows until the rows themselves became an accusation, the way a mapmaker draws roads until the roads become a destination.

"Who reads this?" I asked Brac one afternoon, keeping my voice casual the way you keep a boat casual in weather you don't trust. "Besides the council."

"The council. The tax assessor. The garrison liaison, quarterly." He said it the way he said everything, as though reciting a grocery list for a household with no appetite. "And the capital, annually. A digest goes south each autumn. Names, trades, debts, dispositions."

"Dispositions?"

"Whether a household is thought loyal, indifferent, or otherwise." He caught my expression and smiled his furniture-apology smile. "It sounds sinister, apprentice. It is merely thorough. Thoroughness protects people. If everyone is written down, no one can be disappeared, because everyone is accounted for."

I want to be fair to Odil Brac, because fairness is a discipline I was practicing even then, before I understood what it would cost. He believed what he said. He believed the ledger was a shield, that legibility was safety, that a town fully written down was a town fully defended. He had spent his life inside that belief the way a snail spends its life inside a shell, and the shell was genuine, and it was also the reason anything with a boot could crush him.

But I stood in that amber light with the great book open under my hands, and I understood something else, something my hands understood before my head consented to it: a list of everyone is a list of anyone. Write down all the names and you have built a machine for choosing among them. You have not prevented the disappearance of people. You have prepared the paperwork for it.

I said none of this. I copied the next entry. The steady hand, doing what steady hands do.

* * *

There was one more thing in the ledger, and I found it the way you find most important things: by accident, while looking for something else.

Near the back of the volume, sewn in on extra guards the way appendices are sewn into atlases, was a section headed *Register of Strangers & Persons of Uncertain Domicile*. Brac had told me to skip it—"old business, closed business"—and I intended to obey, but the sewing was loose and the section opened under my thumb like a door unlatched, and I am weak in exactly the ways a mapmaker is weak: I cannot leave a blank space unexamined.

Mostly it held what you'd expect. Traveled merchants. A juggler. Seasonal crews off the herring fleet. But interleaved among them, in entries spanning the last six years, were names I recognized—not from the town but from my master's workshop. Families from up the coast. From the islands. From the border provinces where the wars had been going badly for a decade and then, officially, stopped. People who had come to Vessamare quietly, been quietly housed, quietly given work, and been recorded here not as residents but as *persons of uncertain domicile*, which was the town's way of sheltering someone while pretending to count them.

Someone had entered them carefully. Someone had chosen the category *uncertain* rather than *illegal*, had dated entries loosely, had spelled several names in ways that would make them hard to match against any northern register. This was not sloppiness. I know my master's sloppiness intimately—it lived mostly in his treatment of his boots—and this was not it. This was craft. Someone had used the ledger the way I would use a map: as an instrument, bent deliberately toward an end.

And the hand, beneath the official copperplate, was my master's.

I sat very still for a long time. Amber light moved across the desk. Brac snored gently, a man at peace inside his shell. And I understood, in the wordless way you understand a change in the wind before the sails tell you, that the ledger in front of me was two documents at once—one that counted the town for the capital, and one, hidden in its margins and categories and careful misspellings, that had been counting the town *for itself*. For the people in it. For the ones with nowhere else.

I smoothed the page flat, checked that nothing showed, and copied the next household in my steadiest hand.

That evening Marisol came to the workshop and found me sitting in the dark. I hadn't lit the lamps. She lit one, studied my face, and said, "You've been reading something you shouldn't."

"How would you know?"

"Because you look like Papa did." She said it simply, without drama, and it landed on me like a dropped anchor. "When he found things in charts. Things other people drew in and hoped no one would read closely." She sat down across from me. "Isandro. What did you find?"

And here is where I must confess something, because this account will be read someday by someone who deserves the truth even if I didn't deserve Marisol's: I lied to her. Not largely. I told her I'd found discrepancies in the tax rolls, dry stuff, clerk's indigestion. I told myself I was protecting her, which is the sentence cowards have used since the invention of sentences. Really I was protecting the knowledge—hoarding it, the way I hoarded everything, making myself small around it, keeping it where no one could take it from me or make me act on it.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and let it go, and that was worse than arguing. Marisol's father had been taken away in chains for what he knew, and she had learned exactly how much safer ignorance was, and she granted me mine like a coin she expected to collect later, with interest.

* * *

The last week of the month, Brac announced the annual digest.

"It goes south with the autumn packet," he said, laying out fresh quires with the tenderness other men reserved for infants. "Summary tables. Households, totals, dispositions. You'll rule the columns; I'll check the figures. The governor's office prefers the new format—columns, you see, rather than prose. Easier to count."

"Easier to count what?"

"People, apprentice." He said it without looking up. "Columns are for counting people."

So I ruled columns. Labor, I labeled the first, because that was the heading Brac dictated. The second he called Transport. The third he called, after a small pause I would remember for the rest of my life, Removal.

"These are for the summary only," he said quickly, catching my face. "Categories of disposition. Most households fall under labor—most everyone falls under labor, it means nothing, it means simply *here*. Transport is for those with boats or carts the province might call upon. And removal is—" he adjusted his spectacles, "—for persons whose presence is, ah, under review. It is a holding category. Almost always empty. Bureaucracies keep empty boxes the way ships keep ballast. For balance."

Almost always empty. I ruled the column anyway, straight and clean, a beautiful empty box with a beautiful printed heading, and I felt it the way you feel a splinter under the skin: small, precise, and pointing inward.

That night I dreamed of the atlas. In the dream my master was inking a coastline and kept stopping to look at me, and every place he set his pen down, the sea rose over it. I woke with the word *removal* in my mouth like salt water and lay listening to the harbor bells count the hour, and told myself it was indigestion, and knew it wasn't, and got up and worked on the atlas plates until dawn because work is the lie craftsmen tell grief.

Three days later, Marisol came to the workshop with color in her face for the first time in weeks—anger, as it turned out, not health. Her father's old coastal charts, the ones in the capital archive, had been formally examined by the provincial surveyor-general. Declared inaccurate in their soundings. Declared politically suspect in their annotations. Declared, in the language of the notice she thrust at me, *seditious cartography*, with her father's name attached in the way names get attached when attachment is the punishment.

"They're good charts," she said, and her voice cracked on the ordinariness of it. "They're *beautiful* charts. He spent his life on them. And some man in a clean collar has decided they're weapons." She pressed her palms flat on the workbench. "Isandro. If it comes here—if they come for the families, for anyone—if it gets worse—"

"I know," I said.

"Say it. Say the promise. I need to hear it in words, not in your face. Your face agrees with everyone."

I took her hands off the bench and held them, and said the thing I had said when we were fifteen on the breakwater with our feet in the water: that if it ever came to running, I would run with her, or before her, or behind her, whatever the shape of it required; that she would not leave Vessamare alone, and would not leave it unseen.

"Good," she said, and blew her nose on my good rag, and went back to sorting her father's papers as though she hadn't just handed me the weight I would carry for the rest of this story.

I went back to the digest. Columns. Labor, transport, removal. Forty-one households under labor. Nine under transport. And in the third column, in Brac's handwriting, small and provisional, as though he hoped the ink might reconsider:

Two names.

One of them was hers.

---

Outside, the harbor bells rang the turn of the tide, and far up the hill, where the road comes down from the pass, I heard—though I told myself I hadn't—the sound of many boots keeping step.
