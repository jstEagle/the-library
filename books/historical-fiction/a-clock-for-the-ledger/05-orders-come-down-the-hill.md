## 5. Orders Come Down the Hill

The morning it began, I was arguing with a herring.

Not a real herring — the harbor of Vessamare as my master had drawn it forty years ago, back when the fish market sat closer to the water and the customs house had a tower instead of the squat, embarrassed roof some later architect had given it. My task was to update the old plate to match the town as it stood now, which meant erasing a tower that no longer existed and inking one that had never deserved to. Cartography, I have since learned, is mostly the discipline of deciding which lies to preserve.

I was still deciding when the drums started on the hill road.

You grow up with certain sounds in a port town: gulls, rigging, the bell at Saint Ferren's marking the watches, and twice a year the festival drums from upcountry. These were none of those. Festival drums are festive. These were the sound of a man hitting a stretched skin because he has been ordered to, in time, without joy, all the way down the switchbacks from the garrison road. The whole harbor went quiet the way water goes quiet before something large moves underneath it.

I put down my pen. I remember being annoyed about the pen — I'd just mixed a fresh batch of ink, walnut and iron-gall, the good stuff, and I hate leaving a loaded nib to dry. That is the kind of thing you fixate on when the world tilts. Not the drums. The pen.

* * *

They came down the hill in a column of maybe sixty, bayonets catching the early light, boots striking cobbles in a rhythm no festival band ever managed. At their head rode an officer on a gray horse too fine for the dust of our streets, and behind him a clerk on a mule, which struck me even then as the truest image of an occupation I would ever see: the soldier first, but the paperwork close behind.

The column halted in the market square, precisely, like a line ruled across a page. The officer did not dismount. He unrolled a document with the crisp authority of a man who has done this in nine towns already this season and expects to do it in nine more.

"Inhabitants of Vessamare," he read, "by order of the Provisional Authority, this town and its port are hereby placed under requisition for the use of the Army of the Coast." And then he began to list us. Our houses. Our grain. Our boats. Our records.

That last one took a moment to land. Billets — yes, every household was to lodge soldiers at a rate fixed by schedule, which everyone understood to mean at no rate at all. Grain — the harvest stores, weighed and sealed, "against fair compensation," three words that have emptied granaries since granaries were invented. Ships — all vessels over twelve tons to report for inspection and possible charter. And then: *all civic registers, rolls, and records of residence to be surrendered for verification within three days.*

I felt that sentence go through me like a splinter finding its depth. I didn't yet know why. A man's instincts sometimes arrive before his understanding; mine arrived standing in the doorway of my dead master's workshop, holding a dirty pen, listening to a stranger read out the inventory of everything we were.

* * *

By noon the square had organized itself into the shapes fear always takes. There was the knot of men near the fountain pretending to discuss fishing quotas while actually discussing nothing. There were the women moving between houses with that quick, low-voiced traffic of news — Signora Becca, whose husband ran the chandlery, going door to door with the efficiency of a woman who has survived two wars by knowing things before other people do. There was old Tomaz sitting on his own doorstep looking at his own hands as though they belonged to someone who used to be him.

And there was me, doing what I do best, which is being furniture.

Being invisible is a craft. My master taught it to me without ever naming it. He'd been a small man, quiet, with the particular talent of entering a room and having no one afterward able to say he'd been there. He taught me drafting, lettering, the grinding of pigments, and along the way, without a single lesson plan, he taught me how to stand near walls, how to ask questions that invite answers rather than attention, how to be the person people forget they told things to. It seemed like an apprentice's virtue at the time. Diligence. Humility. It was only later I understood it was armor, and that a man who builds armor usually knows what kind of arrows come.

So I stood near the wall of the cooperage and listened.

"Three days for the records," said the cooper, whose name was Anselm and who had never in his life produced a piece of paper worth seizing. "What do they want with paper?"

"Names," said Signora Becca. She said it flatly, the way you'd say *rain*. "They want names. They always want names."

"What for? They can see us. We're right here."

"We're not all right here," she said, and looked at him until he stopped talking.

I thought of Marisol then. I thought of her the way you think of a bruise — involuntarily, and with a small sick drop of the stomach. Her father had been taken north in the spring, deported with a handful of other cartographers and engineers after some minister in the capital decided maps could be treasonous. Which, of course, they can. A coastline is neutral ink until someone decides where the border runs, and then every mapmaker becomes either a servant or a suspect. Marisol's father had declined, in some fashion I never learned the details of, to be a servant.

Marisol had stayed. Half-orphaned in practice, her mother keeping the house and the silence, and Marisol herself walking through Vessamare with her chin up and her father's gray eyes daring anyone to look at her sideways. Everyone knew what her family was. Nobody said so. That was the old arithmetic of the town — we kept each other by mutual agreement not to count.

I understood, standing against the cooperage wall while Signora Becca said *they want names*, that the arithmetic was changing. Someone else was going to do the counting now.

* * *

In the afternoon the billeting officers came through the streets with chalk.

This was the detail that made it real for people, more than the drums, more than the proclamation. There is something about a mark on your own door. The officers went house to house with sticks of chalk and wrote numbers — how many soldiers, how many horses — directly onto the wood. I watched Tomaz watch them chalk a *4* on his lintel, watched him open his mouth, watched him close it again. Four soldiers in a house built for a man and his late wife. He had spent the whole afternoon calculating what he could carry if he ran, and the answer, written in white above his head, was: nothing. You cannot carry your house. You cannot carry four soldiers' claim on your house.

Everyone was calculating. You could see it happening behind eyes all down the street, a silent arithmetic running parallel to the official one. The baker counting sacks. The widow Ostre counting her mother's silver, wrapped in cloth in a trunk under the bed, weighing whether silver was worth the carrying or better buried. Old Renna at the net-menders' walk measuring her boat with her arms, as if she might learn by embrace how much of her life fit inside it. What can be carried. What must be left. What will be taken regardless. Three columns, and nobody had written them down yet, and everybody knew them.

I went home — to the workshop, I mean; the workshop had been home since my master died — and found that I had left the nib in the ink, exactly as I'd feared, and it had dried into a little black stalagmite. I scraped it clean. I am telling you this because it mattered to me at the time, and I have learned that when the world tilts, you grip whatever is level. The nib was level. The nib behaved according to laws. Ink dries. Paper holds a line. These things remained true while sixty soldiers ate our bread without paying for it, and I confess I took a stupid, stubborn comfort in that.

* * *

Near dusk, a lieutenant came to the workshop.

I heard him before I saw him — boots on the stairs, unhurried, the tread of a man who has been pointed at doors all day and expects them to open. I had the good sense to be doing something legible when he entered: standing over the master's great table with the harbor plate half-inked, looking industrious, looking harmless, looking like exactly what I was, an apprentice, except that I wasn't anymore and had no idea what I was instead.

He was young. Younger than me, possibly, though rank ages people strangely. He had a list, and he consulted it, and my master's name was on it.

"Ishikawa?" He pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed.

"The master died in the spring," I said. "I keep the shop."

"You're the apprentice." Not a question. Somewhere on his list there was a note, probably in the clerk-of-the-records' hand, that the mapmaker's shop contained a junior keeper of some kind. Word travels downhill fast in an occupation; the requisition order was hours old and already I had been filed.

"Yes."

"The atlas. The town atlas. Where is it?"

I showed him. What else does one do? I lifted the plates from the flat drawer where they lived, and I watched his face change as he looked at them — the young officer, chalk-stained from a day of writing numbers on doors, looking at forty years of my master's patience rendered in ink. For a moment he was not a requisitioning officer. He was a man seeing the sea drawn so finely he could almost hear it.

"They'll want this catalogued," he said finally. "Records are records. Maps are... useful." He said *useful* the way the proclamation had said *fair compensation*, and I filed both words away in the same mental ledger, the one marked *phrases that mean their opposite*.

"Of course," I said. "Anything for the army's convenience."

I meant nothing by it. Or I meant everything by it, delivered so flatly that it passed for deference. He nodded, satisfied, made his note, and clattered back down the stairs, and I stood alone with the harbor plate and understood that I had just learned something important: they wanted the atlas. They wanted the records. They wanted the town reduced to paper, and paper, unlike stone, can be sorted.

* * *

That night I climbed to the flat roof where my master used to smoke his terrible pipe, and I looked at the harbor.

The requisition ship was already visible at anchor beyond the mole — a dark shape with a riding light, patient as a spider. Two days, the proclamation had said, for the records to be surrendered. I found myself thinking about the civic ledger in the clerk's office below the hill: every household in Vessamare, every birth, every debt, every death, kept in the town clerk's careful hand for longer than anyone alive had been keeping anything. A book that had never once been dangerous. A book that had simply been *true*.

Truth, I was beginning to understand, is not the same as innocence. A true thing in the wrong hands is the sharpest thing there is.

Down in the streets, lamps moved between houses — Signora Becca still making her rounds, or others like her, carrying whispers the way they'd carried water during the drought years. From the direction of the salt quarter came the sound of a family loading a cart in the dark, trying to be quiet about it, failing, the wheels saying everything the night tried to hide. Somewhere a child was crying, briefly, and then not.

And somewhere — I let myself think it now, fully, for the first time — was Marisol, in her house above the rope-walk, doing her own arithmetic. Counting what she carried. Which was her father's name, and her father's eyes, and every map he'd ever signed, none of which she could leave behind and all of which she couldn't carry.

The promise I'd made her was eleven years old. We'd been children on the harbor wall; she'd dared me to walk the seaward edge, I'd fallen in, and while I dripped she'd laughed and then, abruptly serious in the way children are, extracted from me a vow: *if anything ever happens, you'll get me out.* Children make vows like that the way they eat sweets — freely, without imagining the cost. But vows don't know they're childish. They wait. Eleven years they waited, patient as a seed under stone, and tonight, watching the requisition ship ride at anchor with its light burning, I felt the stone crack.

Three days. They'd given the town three days to surrender itself on paper.

I went back downstairs, cleaned my nib a second time, and sat down at the master's table in the dark, not working, just sitting, the way you sit with a sick relative. The atlas lay open beside me, my half-finished harbor gleaming faintly in the lamplight, and beyond the window the ship's light burned, and I caught myself doing something I would become very familiar with in the weeks ahead: reading the room like a map, noting exits, noting sightlines, noting where the dark was thickest.

A man who makes himself small learns to see everything. That night, for the first time, I wondered what I would do with the seeing.

Two days later, they came for the clerk's office, and I learned what the seeing was for.
