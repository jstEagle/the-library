## 6. The Requisition of Paper

They came for the paper on a Tuesday, which struck me later as the most obscene detail of the whole affair. Not at dawn, not with trumpets, but mid-morning, while Clerk Odovart was halfway through a complaint about a disputed fence line in the Salt Quarter and I was grinding ink that smelled of oak galls and patience. Soldiers do not announce themselves like weather. They arrive like a bill.

I heard the boots first — six pairs, maybe seven, coming down the narrow stair from the square with the unhurried rhythm of men who have learned that occupied towns wait for them. Then the door opened without a knock, because doors in Vessamare had apparently stopped being doors overnight.

"Which of you keeps the ledger?"

The officer who asked was young in the way that made me nervous — young enough to still believe in his own importance, old enough to have been promoted for something I didn't want to imagine. Behind him stood a thinner man in civilian clothes with a soldier's posture, carrying a leather case the way a surgeon carries instruments. He looked at our shelves before he looked at our faces. I would learn to dread that habit.

Clerk Odovart rose from his stool with the particular dignity of a man whose office has been his kingdom for thirty years. "This is the civic registry of Vessamare," he said, as if naming it might protect it, the way a mother names a child at a border crossing.

"That's what we're told," the officer said. "Verification of residents. Every household, every name, every debt owed to the crown." He said *crown* the way you'd say *weather* — an impersonal fact that happened to govern your life. "You will open everything."

*Everything* is a word that does a great deal of work in an occupation. It means drawers you didn't know were drawers, boxes labeled in dead clerks' hands, the archive of births going back two hundred years, the tax rolls, the harbor dues, the record of who owed whom what since before anyone alive was born. It means, though no one says it plainly: *we want to know exactly who you are, so we can decide what you are worth, and what you are for.*

Odovart's hand shook as he unlocked the case. Mine did not, and I remember being ashamed of that — ashamed of my own steadiness, as if calm were a form of agreement.

* * *

The thin man introduced himself as Ferran. No title offered, none needed; the leather case announced him. He opened it on Odovart's desk and laid out his tools with the tenderness other men reserve for photographs: a magnifying lens on a brass stand, a set of graduated rules, small bottles of ink in shades so similar only a craftsman could tell them apart, and a ledger of his own, pre-ruled into columns I couldn't read from where I stood.

"You are the junior keeper?" he asked me. Not looking up yet.

"I assist Clerk Odovart."

"That is what I said." He glanced up then, and his eyes went — I noted this with the professional unease of one tradesman reading another — directly to my hands. Ink-stained at the cuticles, the right middle finger calloused into a writer's ridge. "Name."

"Ishikawa. Isandro Ishikawa."

He wrote it down. Somewhere in that small act, my name left the town's keeping and entered the army's, and I felt nothing dramatic about it at all, which is its own kind of horror. A name moves between books the way water moves between vessels, taking the shape of whatever holds it.

"The master clerk will show me the structure of the registry," Ferran said. "You will copy. The army requires duplicate records. Originals remain here under seal; duplicates go to the governor's office." He said it smoothly, reasonably, the way all confiscations are described in the passive voice, as if the papers were migrating of their own accord.

So began the work. And here is the thing I could not have known that morning, standing at Odovart's desk with a fresh quill and a blank army ledger: complicity does not arrive as a decision. It arrives as a task list. You are not asked whether you will betray your town. You are asked to rule columns neatly, and you say yes, because ruling columns neatly is what you have done every morning of your working life, and the body obeys the shape of its habits even when the soul has objections.

Ferran dictated the order of transcription: households by district, then individuals by family, then debts and obligations. I copied the Salt Quarter first. The Alduntes, boatwrights, four souls, the eldest owing six crowns in harbor dues. The Peretzes, net-menders, three souls, solvent. The widow Cambeira, one soul, behind on her ground rent through no fault of her own except that her husband had drowned and drowning is expensive.

Each name passed through my hand twice — once as reader, once as writer — and each time it passed through, I felt it become slightly less like a person and slightly more like an entry. That is what ledgers do; I had always believed it was their virtue. A ledger makes a town legible. A town made legible can be governed fairly, debts settled justly, disputes resolved by reference rather than memory. My master had taught me that paper was the most honest witness ever devised.

He had never taught me what happens when a dishonest man borrows honesty's tools.

By noon my hand ached and my stomach had discovered a new and inventive way of being ill — not nausea exactly, but a cold weight, as if I'd swallowed a coin that refused to settle. Ferran worked beside me in perfect silence except when correcting me. He corrected me three times. Once for a smudged numeral, once for a date written in my own shorthand instead of full form, and once — this one I still feel — when he stopped my pen mid-stroke.

"You hesitated on this entry," he said. "Widow Cambeira. Why?"

"I didn't hesitate."

"You lifted the nib. A copyist who lifts the nib mid-word is thinking about the word." He turned the page toward himself, studied my half-finished line, and seemed satisfied by whatever he saw there — or perhaps he simply filed it away, in whatever cabinet inside himself such things were filed. "Continue. Steady hands are why you're here."

Steady hands. I copied the rest of the Salt Quarter with the steadiest hands in Vessamare, and hated every letter for how well-formed it was.

* * *

They broke at dusk, the soldiers posting themselves at the door of the clerk's office as if the building might attempt escape. Odovart walked me partway home through streets gone strangely quiet — quiet the way a house is quiet after a doctor's visit, everyone listening to their own pulse.

"They'll be back tomorrow," he said. "And the day after. There are nine districts, boy, and they'll want every one of them in that man's columns before the week is out." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, smearing ink across his brow so that he looked briefly like a man marked for something. "Do you understand what verification means? It means they intend to count us the way a merchant counts stock. And a merchant who counts his stock—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We both knew what merchants did with stock they no longer wanted.

At my door he paused. "Your master kept the harbor district's older rolls," he said, and there was something in his voice — careful, weighted, the tone of a man testing ice with one foot. "When they ask for those, and they will, you'll find some pages... damp-damaged. Water gets everywhere in a port town." He looked at me a moment too long. "Remember that. Water gets everywhere here."

Then he was gone up the street, an old man walking quickly past his own future.

I stood in my doorway a long while, turning his words over. Damp damage. Water. I thought of my master's workshop above the map room, locked since the night they found him, and of the drawer he never let me touch, and of Marisol somewhere in the capital with her father already branded a traitor, and I understood — not fully, not yet, but the way you understand a coastline from a ship before you've ever walked it — that the ledger on Odovart's desk was only the smallest of the books in this town.

Tomorrow they would come for the harbor district. Tomorrow I would sit again across from Ferran and his brass lens and his patient, terrible attention. And tomorrow, somewhere in those columns, a name would appear that I knew better than my own handwriting.

I slept badly, which surprised me. I had expected not to sleep at all.
