## 7. Categories

The clerk's office had always smelled of dust and sealing wax and the particular staleness of paper that has been argued over. That morning it smelled of soldiers. Boot leather, pipe ash, and under it all the faint iron tang of the army's own ink, which was cheaper than ours and blotted worse.

I had been in that room perhaps a thousand times. I knew which floorboard creaked under the third shelf, knew that the window on the north side stuck if you opened it past halfway, knew that old Clerk Berenguer hummed hymns when he thought no one was listening and stopped the instant anyone was. It is a strange thing to watch a room you know be made foreign. They hadn't changed anything. That was the unsettling part. The tallies simply arrived, hung their satchels on our hooks, set their lamps on our desks, and by lunchtime the office belonged to them as thoroughly as if they had built it.

The tallyman's name was Ferran. I learned it from the way the sergeant addressed him—not with warmth, exactly, but with the particular flatness men reserve for someone whose competence frightens them slightly. He was not what I expected. I don't know what I expected; cruelty, maybe, or at least coarseness. Instead there was a man of perhaps fifty with grey in his close-cropped beard, ink stains to the second knuckle of both hands—the occupational mark of my own trade, worn like a mirror held up to me—and spectacles he cleaned with a square of chamois so often it had gone shiny.

"Apprentice Ishikawa," he said, when Berenguer introduced me. He did not shake hands. He looked at mine instead, a half-second longer than politeness allowed, the way a farrier looks at a horse's hooves before deciding what shoes it needs. "You keep the fair copies?"

"I keep everything the clerk tells me to keep," I said, which was true, and which I intended to go on being true for as long as it kept me standing in that room rather than standing in a line outside it.

"Good," said Ferran. "Sit there. Ink your pen less than you want to. A heavy line is a liar's tell."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. At the time I took it for shop talk. That was the trick of Ferran, I would learn—he spoke entirely in shop talk, and only later did you discover the shop had been your house.

* * *

The work itself was simple. That was the horror of it, though I didn't have the word for *horror* yet; at the time I believe I called it *tedious*, which tells you something about how thoroughly the mind protects itself.

The civic ledger of Vessamare was, as I've said, complete—or had been, until the requisition demanded it be *verified*. Verification turned out to mean transcription into the army's own format: one name per line, one household per entry, and after each name a small ruled box in which the tallyman would write one of three words. The words were written across the top of each fresh page in Ferran's neat, upright hand, like column headings in an accountant's book, or the parts list for a machine.

*Labor. Transport. Removal.*

Labor meant you stayed and worked. Transport meant you could be moved where labor was needed, which everyone understood to mean the fortifications up the coast. Removal meant a ship.

Nobody explained this to me. Nobody had to. The words were ordinary words. That was the genius of them, if genius is the right thing to call it—you could write *removal* beside a widow's name and feel you had done nothing worse than file her under weather. The words carried their weight elsewhere, in some other office, in some other language of consequence, and left the hand free to move quickly down the page.

My job was the copying. Read the civic ledger, speak the entry aloud—Berenguer's rule, inherited now by the army, a check against error—and write it fair into the new book. Name of head of household. Names of dependents. Trade. Years at residence. Debts owed within the town, because debts, it turned out, mattered; a man who owed money to neighbors was judged likelier to stay put. Then leave the box empty, and Ferran would fill it.

He filled it fast. Faster than seemed possible, given what each word did. He would glance at the entry—two seconds, three—and write. *Labor.* *Labor.* *Transport.* Once, memorably, he wrote *labor* for the Ostero family, who ran the rope-walk, then paused, drew a small vertical tick after it, and moved on. I never learned what the tick meant. I have guesses. All of them are bad.

For the first hour I watched him the way you'd watch any craftsman, with the involuntary respect one maker feels for another's speed. By the second hour the respect had curdled into something else, because I understood what the speed meant. It meant the names weren't stopping him. He had found the level of abstraction at which people stop being people—trade, debts, years of residence—and he worked there, efficiently, the way I worked at coastlines. I drew shores; he drew fates. Both of us prided ourselves on clean lines.

I am aware of the ugliness of comparing myself to him. I am also aware that the comparison was available to me that very morning, sitting there with my steady hands and my light ink, and that noticing it did not make me put the pen down.

* * *

"Read it back," Ferran said, somewhere past midday, when I'd hesitated too long over a page.

I read it back. My voice did something strange on the third line, a small hitch, like a cart wheel finding a rut, and Ferran looked up over his spectacles.

"Which entry?"

"The Ondarza entry," I said. "It's—" I had been about to say *wrong*, which was not true, and *incomplete*, which also was not true, and I settled for, "it's crossed out in the original."

It was not crossed out in the original. But I needed him to look at the original, away from me, and for two seconds he did, and in those two seconds I discovered something about myself that I have never fully made peace with: lying came easily. Not guiltlessly—I felt the lie land in my stomach like a swallowed stone—but easily, smoothly, with almost no rehearsal. I had assumed deceit was a skill requiring practice. Apparently I had been practicing all my life without knowing it, every time I made myself small, every time I agreed when agreement was cheapest. There is a kind of honesty that is just cowardice with good posture, and I suspect its reverse exists too.

"The entry stands," Ferran said. "Crossed-out entries are flagged separately. Copy it as written."

So I copied it. *Ondarza, widow; dependent: M., daughter, seventeen.* Trade: none recorded. Years at residence: nineteen. And in the box, in Ferran's upright hand, without pause, without the tick even:

*Removal.*

There it is, I thought. There is the word, sitting under her name the way a gull sits on a mooring post, as if it had always meant to land there.

You should understand: by that morning Marisol Ondarza had lived in Vessamare since she was nine years old, when her father's disgrace arrived ahead of them like weather. Nineteen years, the ledger said, because the ledger counted from her mother's tenancy, and the ledger does not know about childhood promises made on the harbor wall with chalk in hand. The ledger knew one thing about her: that her father had drawn maps for the navy of a country we were not currently pretending to be neutral toward, and that maps, in the wrong decade, are treason. Her father had been deported four years ago on a ship much like the one now anchored in our harbor. She had stayed with her mother. She had mended nets, sold fish, taught me the names of half the boats in the harbor out of pure contempt for my ignorance. And here was her whole self, reduced to one line and one word, in ink lighter than regulation.

"Something," said Ferran—not a question, not even really an observation. Just the word, laid on the desk between us like a coin.

"Nothing," I said. "Dust."

He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he cleaned his spectacles with the shiny chamois, which I came to understand was his version of Berenguer's humming—a sound and motion that meant *I have noted something and chosen not to spend effort on it yet*—and said, "Next entry."

I gave him the next entry. My hands were perfectly steady. I remember being grateful for that and disgusted by it in the same breath, the way you can be grateful for a numb limb because it doesn't hurt.

* * *

We finished the harbor district by lamplight. The sergeant collected the pages, squared them, wrapped them in oilcloth with the tenderness other men reserve for ammunition, which is exactly what they were. Ferran stayed behind a moment, checking his sums, and Berenguer went to see about candles, and for a minute the tallyman and I were alone in the room where I had learned my letters copying tide tables.

"You read well," he said, not looking up. "Aloud, I mean. Most copyists mumble. You pronounce the names."

"It's the clerk's rule. Errors get caught faster."

"Mm." He capped his inkwell. "Do you know many of them? The families."

"Some. It's a small town."

"It is now." He said it mildly, the way you'd note the tide was coming in. Then he gathered his satchel, paused at the door, and added, without turning around: "The daughter. Ondarza. You pronounced it like a local. Like a name you'd said before."

The lamp guttered. In the space of one heartbeat I ran through every answer available to me—*everyone knows everyone here; she sells fish at the market; it's a common enough name*—and heard how thin each one was, and chose the thinnest anyway, because thinness has its own camouflage.

"Haven't we all," I said.

Ferran considered this. Whatever arithmetic he performed behind those spectacles, he kept the result to himself. "Lock up well," he said, and was gone, and his boots went down the hill with the unhurried rhythm of a man who knows exactly how many steps remain in his task.

I stood alone in the office a long while, holding the key to a room that no longer contained anything of mine. Through the north window, the one that sticks past halfway, I could see the harbor, and the black shape of the requisition ship riding at anchor, patient as a heading in a ledger.

*Removal,* the box had said. One word, in tidy ink, and beneath it a girl of seventeen who had once promised me she'd teach me to sail if I ever grew brave enough to ask.

I had grown brave enough, eventually. Just slowly. Just too slowly, the way I did everything.

Tomorrow the tally would reach the market district, and the day after the hill houses, and every name would fall into one of three boxes, and Marisol Ondarza's fate was already written in a book locked in a room where I held the key—which meant, I realized, standing there in the dark with the cold coming through the stuck window, that the only question left in Vessamare worth asking was whether ink could be persuaded to say something twice.

I had lied once today and found it easy.

That night I began wondering what else my hands could do without asking my permission first.
