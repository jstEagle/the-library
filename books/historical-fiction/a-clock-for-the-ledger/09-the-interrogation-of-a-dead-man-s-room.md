## 9. The Interrogation of a Dead Man's Room

They came for the room on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Tuesdays were for deliveries—paper stock from the upriver mill, tallow for the lamps, the fish-oil soap Master Sorrel had insisted on because it kept the ink stones clean without smelling of the harbor. A dead man's workshop should have been disturbed on a day with no associations, some blank weekday I could forget afterward. Instead they arrived while I was grinding pigment, and the smell of the tallow lamp they knocked over mixed with the smell of the soap, and now every time I catch either scent my hands remember being steady when I wanted them to shake.

There were four soldiers and a captain.

The soldiers were young in the way that frightened me most—young enough to find the workshop interesting, to pick things up, to turn the burnisher over and ask each other what it did. One of them opened a jar of gum arabic, sniffed it, and made a face, and I nearly corrected him—it's not for drinking, boy, it's what keeps your governor's precious documents from crumbling—and caught myself just in time. Correcting soldiers is a hobby for men with shorter futures.

The captain was different. Captain Aldurez. He came in last, and he did not touch anything. He stood in the doorway the way my master used to stand at the threshold of the map room, letting the room tell him about itself before he trusted it with his weight. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the particular cleanliness of a man who has decided that his authority lives in his grooming. His boots were polished to the color of dark honey. In Vessamare, only fishermen had boots like that once, before the salt ate them.

"Apprentice," he said. Not a question. He had read something about me already. That fact arranged itself in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Isandro Ishikawa, sir."

"Of the workshop of Sorrel Anzai, mapmaker and surveyor, licensed by the Crown Office of Charts." He recited it flatly, the way you'd read an inventory. "Which makes you, by succession, the current keeper of a licensed workshop. Interesting word, licensed. It means granted. And things that are granted—" he finally stepped inside, crossing the floor without looking down, though he avoided the spilled gum arabic as if he'd mapped it already, "—can be audited."

I said nothing. This was my first discovery of the morning: silence is a kind of obedience, and obedience is a costume, and if you put it on early enough no one asks whether you're wearing anything underneath.

* * *

He began with the obvious. The maps on the walls, the plates in the presses, the half-finished atlas on the bench—I had covered it with a work cloth, the way one covers a birdcage at night, and he looked at the cloth longer than he looked at anything else.

"The atlas," he said. "Progress?"

"Coastlines complete through the southern capes. Interior detail pending. My master worked coast first." True, all of it. I had learned in the last hour that truth, delivered in the correct proportion, is the best building material for lies. You build the wall out of true bricks and set one false stone in the middle, and even a mason will tap the wall and hear honesty.

"And his papers?"

"In the flat files, sir. Surveys, soundings, correspondence with the Crown Office. I've been sorting them since—" I let the pause do a small amount of work. "Since we lost him."

"We lost him." Aldurez turned the phrase over. "A generous way to describe a man found facedown in his own harbor."

"He drowned, sir."

"He drowned." He said it again, but this time it sounded like a coin he was testing with his teeth. "At night. Alone. At sixty-two years of age, in a town where he has drawn every rock and rip current for forty years. A man who knew those waters better than the fish do." He picked up a dividers from the bench—not to use, just to hold, the way another man might hold a knife while talking—and walked its points absently against his thumb. "The army's surgeon says drowning. The army's surgeon also says whatever I pay him to say. What does the apprentice say?"

My heart was doing something athletic behind my ribs. But my hands—my hands were doing what Master Sorrel had trained them nine years to do, which was to remain instruments and not faces. I folded a rag. I set it down square.

"I say he went out to check a bearing. He did it sometimes at night, when the light was wrong in his memory. He said a coastline you can't verify at night isn't fully known." Also true. Every word true. And behind the truth, tucked into its shadow, the thing I could not say: that Master Sorrel had gone out that night because someone had to move a boat, or meet one, or signal one, and that the drawer under the drafting table held proof of it, and that I had broken that drawer open two nights ago and sat on the floor among its contents until dawn.

"Sentimental," Aldurez said. "A man who trusts the sea at night more than his own bed. Well. Drowned men don't file their paperwork, apprentice, so it falls to the living. Which brings me to why I'm standing in your tallow."

He let the soldier with the gum arabic take up position by the door, and then he did the thing I would think about for weeks afterward: he sat down. He pulled Master Sorrel's stool from the drafting table—the high one, the one worn to the shape of a man who had sat in it for forty years—and he sat in it and laced his fingers and became, horribly, reasonable.

"Sit," he said, indicating a crate of paper stock.

I sat. The crate was the rag stock we used for proofs; it smelled like my master's practice sheets, the hundreds of them, the coastlines drawn and redrawn until the line lived in his wrist. Sitting on it felt like sitting on a grave, which I suppose was appropriate, since the whole interview was a grave robbery conducted politely.

"Unregistered documents," Aldurez said. "Every licensed workshop maintains them. Not out of treason—out of vanity. Surveyors keep private notes. Soundings they haven't published. Corrections they're saving to embarrass the Crown Office at the next review. It's an industry-wide disease." He smiled briefly, and it was a good smile, warm around the eyes, and I understood immediately that it was a tool as sharp as any blade in the garrison, and better maintained. "I'm not accusing your master of anything. I'm auditing him. Help me, and you're a clerk. Hinder me, and you're an heir. An heir inherits debts, Isandro Ishikawa. Debts, and questions, and in the present circumstances, possibly a voyage." He said *voyage* the way the tallyman Ferran said *removal*, with a bureaucratic smoothness that was worse than a shout. "Which would you prefer?"

"A clerk, sir."

"Good lad." He unfolded a single sheet—a list, of course, everything in this occupation was lists now—and ran his finger down it. "Then walk me through the workshop's holdings. Drawers, cabinets, presses. We'll be thorough. The thoroughness protects you as much as me."

And so we spent two hours doing inventory of my inheritance, and I committed treason for the first time in my life by omitting one line item.

* * *

Here is what I learned about lying, in that room, beside my dead master's stool: it is a craft, and I had been apprenticed to it without knowing. All those years of drawing coastlines had been training. A map is a lie agreed upon—we say it in the trade, jokingly, and it stops being a joke when you watch a border move on paper and then watch men die at the new line. A cartographer's entire discipline is deciding what to include, what to exclude, what to shade and what to leave white. The land doesn't change. The map does. The power was never in the territory. It was always in the hand holding the pen.

So when Aldurez asked about the flat files, I showed him the flat files—all except the false bottom of the third one, which I described as warped, swollen with damp, "my master meant to have it repaired, sir, he was frugal about carpentry." When he asked about the locked cabinet, I produced the key from my master's ring and opened it onto exactly what a loyal audit should find: license renewals, fee receipts, three letters from the Crown Office praising his soundings of the northern shoals. I had spent the previous night arranging that cabinet the way I'd arrange a plate for engraving—everything in its registered place, everything legible, everything *catchable*, because the great trick of the age was that no one suspected a man whose papers were tidy.

"And here?" He nodded at the drafting table itself. The table with the drawer. The drawer I had broken open with a chisel and a mallet at three in the morning, heart hammering, and then repaired with glue and clamps and a patience I did not know I possessed, so that it now slid open on a drawer full of—nothing much. Blotting paper. A spare lamp glass. Two stubs of red chalk.

"Blotting supplies, sir."

He opened it himself. He lifted the blotting paper. He held the lamp glass to the window light and looked through it at me, distorting my face into something I didn't recognize, and for one long moment neither of us moved, and the room smelled of tallow and fish-oil soap and my own fear, which I was learning has almost no smell at all if you keep your mouth shut.

"Frugal about carpentry," he said, and closed the drawer.

"Yes, sir."

"You're a calm young man, Ishikawa. Grief sits strangely in some people. In you it seems to sit like good ballast." He stood, returned the stool precisely to its scuffed place on the floor—he had noted its position, of course he had—and straightened his coat. "Here's what happens now. The workshop remains requisitioned for army use. You retain access, supervised, because you are the only person in this town who can finish that atlas, and the governor has developed an appetite for finished things. You'll report anything unusual. Documents surfacing. Names mentioned. Persons inquiring after your master's effects." He paused at the door, and the warmth left his smile entirely, and what remained was worse and more honest. "You understand that we will know. Whatever you keep back, however well it's drawn—we are very good at reading maps, my boy. It's our whole profession. People hide things in the white spaces and forget that the white space is itself information. A blank tells us where to look."

"Yes, sir."

"One more thing." He took from his coat a scrap of paper, small, torn unevenly, water-stained—the kind of scrap that washes up. He didn't hand it to me. He simply let me see it, at a distance, the way you show a dog a thing it wants. On it, faded, in a hand I knew better than my own, was a fragment of a line and what might have been a bearing notation. Then he put it away. "Found near the breakwater. The army's surgeon says it proves nothing. I agree. Proving things is expensive." He settled his cap. "Usefulness is cheaper. Be useful, Ishikawa."

They left the way they'd come, except tidier, because Aldurez made the young soldier wipe up the gum arabic before they went.

* * *

I stood in the empty workshop for a long time after the door shut. Then I did the sensible thing, which was to be sick into the slop bucket, quietly, with the professionalism my station required.

Afterward I washed my face at the cistern and looked at myself in the water, and the man looking back was a stranger wearing my apprenticeship like a stolen coat. I had lied. Not once but continuously, structurally, architecturally—for two hours, under the eyes of a man paid to detect exactly that, and I had not trembled, had not flushed, had not done any of the things my body was supposedly built to do when betraying myself. It had come easily. That was the part that frightened me. Not Aldurez, not the scrap of paper, not even the drawer with its careful repair. The ease. Master Sorrel used to say that a steady hand isn't a gift, it's a decision practiced until it looks like a nature. I had decided something about myself in that room, and the decision had gone down smooth as fresh ink.

I thought of Marisol, and her promise-shaped silence, and the way she'd said *if things worsen* as if worsening were weather rather than policy. She was still in the capital—or she wasn't; news traveled slower than fear. Her father's name was already a category somewhere in a column I hadn't seen yet. If she came back—and she would, she was her father's daughter, and stubbornness is hereditary—if she came back to a town with a garrison in the clerk's office and a captain who collected scraps of paper, then every hour I spent being merely useful instead of merely safe was an hour borrowed against her life.

The light through the workshop windows had gone long and amber. Down the hill, the garrison drums changed to evening pattern. Somewhere below, the requisition clerks were still writing, name after name, the town becoming smaller and more legible by the hour.

I went back to the drafting table and put my hand flat on the drawer, feeling through the wood the faint seam of the repair, invisible unless you knew. Then I heard footsteps on the stair—light ones, twice-stopped, hesitant, the tread of someone who knew the stairs and was afraid of them anyway—and my heart went off like a signal flare, because I knew that step, and it was not supposed to exist in this town anymore.

Marisol stood in the doorway, travel-dusted, thinner, with her eyes already moving past me to the soldiers' chalk marks on the wall—and behind her, in the street below, a squad was marching someone toward the clerk's office, and the someone was a woman, and the woman was her mother.
