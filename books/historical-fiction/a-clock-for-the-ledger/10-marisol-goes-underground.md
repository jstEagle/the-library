## 10. Marisol Goes Underground

There is a particular smell to a salt cellar that gets into everything you own and never leaves: brine and stone and old rope, the ghost of every fish that ever passed through the warehouse above. After three days I barely noticed it. By the fourth day I had decided this was a mercy. By the fifth, when I caught myself thinking *at least it masks the fear-sweat*, I understood that the cellar was beginning to make decisions about my character on my behalf, and I resented it accordingly.

My father used to say that a mapmaker's daughter could never be lost, only temporarily unrecorded. He said it the way other fathers say *eat your vegetables*, as a moral instruction disguised as a fact. I had been temporarily unrecorded for five days now, folded into the dark beneath the harbor warehouse like an erratum note slipped into someone else's atlas, and I can report that his comforting arithmetic was wrong. You are lost. The trick is deciding not to mind.

The cellar was reached by a trapdoor behind the cooper's stores, down seven steps worn concave by a century of boots. Someone—Isandro suspected the warehouse master, who asked no questions and received no answers—had laid a straw pallet against the far wall where the ceiling was highest. There was a bucket, which we will not discuss. There was a candle stub, which I rationed the way misers ration gold, burning it only long enough to read or to remind myself I had a face. And there were the charts.

I had brought four of my father's coastal charts, rolled tight in oilcloth and strapped to my body under my coat when I came down here, because they were the only inheritance I had left and because the soldiers at the harbor gate had already searched me twice without finding them, which told me something important about searching and something important about coats.

*You'll want to know what these actually are,* he'd told me once, when I was eleven and bored and lying on his workroom floor complaining about it. He spread a chart of the northern coast across the table and weighted the corners with his inkpot, his tobacco tin, my elbow, and a stone he kept for exactly this purpose. *Anyone can draw a coastline. The coast doesn't keep secrets. But look here—and here—and here.* His finger touched three marks so small I'd have taken them for flyspecks. A circle open to the east. A triangle with its point broken off. A cross-hatched crescent. *Those aren't errors, Marisol. Errors get corrected. These got repeated, chart after chart, in exactly the same places. My master taught them to me and his master taught him, all the way back to before there was a capital worth obeying.*

*What do they mean?*

He'd looked at me for a long moment, the way he looked at weather coming in off the water. *They mean where a boat can put in and be fed.*

I hadn't understood then. I understand now, in the dark, with nothing but time and four charts and my father's careful flyspecks. It had come to me on the second night, the way truth does—not arriving, surfacing, like a drowned thing giving itself up to the tide. Every symbol matched a house, a farm, a stretch of shingle. The whole network of families who had hidden people like us, drawn into the margins of official documents where no clerk would think to look, because clerks look at what is written large and my father wrote small.

The occupation called his maps seditious. They were more right than they knew, and less right than they feared. Sedition is a plan. This was only a memory, distributed across forty years of paper, waiting for someone who could read it.

* * *

My mother was taken on the third day.

I should tell it properly, in order, because order is all I have down here and hoarding it feels productive. On the third day I heard boots on the warehouse floor above—more boots than the usual night watch, and a voice I didn't know giving instructions in the tone of a man reading from a list. Then the voice next door, at Señora Betchel's, then silence there, then footsteps crossing the yard toward our lane.

Our lane. Our house, with its sagging porch and its kitchen that flooded every spring and its window where my mother kept geraniums she talked to like disappointing employees.

I could not see any of this. I want to be honest about that, because everything I am about to relate is reconstruction, and reconstruction is a kind of lying, and I have decided I would rather lie carefully than remember carelessly. What I know is this: I heard two sets of boots stop outside. I heard my mother's door. I heard her voice, once, pitched high—not frightened; annoyed, the specific annoyance she reserved for officials and seagulls—and then I heard nothing, because the walls between the lane and the cellar are thick with packed earth, and the world went on without me in muffled pantomime.

I sat on the pallet with my hands flat on my knees and performed the exercise my mother taught me when I was small and prone to fits of temper: name five things you can hear, five things you can feel, and do not throw anything at your cousin. I heard dripping, wind in the trapdoor gap, the harbor bell, rats conducting their economy behind the salt bins, my own heart behaving badly. I felt straw, cold stone, the oilcloth roll against my ribs, my fingernails cutting crescents into my palms, and a fury so clean and total that it registered less as emotion than as weather—a pressure system moving through me, bending everything flat.

She had known they would come. That morning—her last morning in our house, though neither of us knew it—she had pressed a cloth bundle into my hands at the trapdoor and said, *bread, cheese, don't argue with me about the cheese,* and then, in a different voice, the one she used maybe twice a year: *Whatever happens above you, you stay below until Isandro comes. Whatever you hear. Promise me.* I had promised. She had looked at me one second too long, committing my face to memory the way I later learned people commit prayers, and pulled the trapdoor shut, and the last thing I saw of her was her knuckles, and the last thing I heard was the bolt.

So when the boots came, I kept the promise. When I heard her voice go high, I kept the promise. When the boots left and took her voice with them, I lay down on the straw and kept the promise, staring into the dark with my eyes open until the candle stub, unlit, made no difference to anything.

People say *I couldn't breathe* when they mean they breathed badly. I could breathe. That was the insult of it. My lungs worked perfectly, efficiently, mechanically, while upstairs-in-the-world my mother was being walked somewhere by men who read from lists, and there was nothing in my body that knew how to refuse.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding: it is mostly bookkeeping.

You count your food. Bread: four days if I am honest, six if I am a liar. Cheese: three pieces, and I had promised myself one per day and already broken it. Water: the cask, refilled through a grate from the rain-barrel line when it rained, and it had not rained. You count your candle: one inch. You count your hours by the harbor bell, which strikes the watch with the indifference of a thing that has outlived every purpose it was built for.

And you count your exits, of which I had none, unless you count the drain channel that ran out under the seawall, and I did count it, nightly, measuring it against my shoulders the way you measure furniture against a doorway in a flat you're thinking of leaving. It flooded at half-tide. It flooded at most tides, honestly; my father would have called it a theoretical exit, suitable for maps but not for daughters.

So I did sums instead. Sums are a kind of courage for people who are bad at waiting.

On the fifth night—it was night; I had learned to taste the difference in the air that came down the drain, day-air smelling of tar and fish, night-air of cold and rope—I heard the bolt on the trapdoor, and my whole body became a held breath before my mind caught up, because bolts are for soldiers and my visitors knocked.

*Marisol.* Isandro's voice, low, filtered through wood. *It's me.*

I was across the cellar before dignity could lodge an objection, hauling at the trapdoor with fingers that had gone stupid from disuse, and there he was in the gap, lamplight behind him making a halo of his ridiculous hair, and I want to record that our reunion was restrained and dignified and characterized by mutual respect.

It was not. I pulled him through the trapdoor with both hands and held onto him the way drowning things hold onto driftwood, without manners and without shame, and he held back just as hard, which told me more than anything he said afterward.

*Your mother,* he said finally, into my hair. *I know. I'm sorry. The garrison has her at the customhouse—for questioning, that's what the list says. Questioning. Not removal.*

*Not yet.*

*Not yet,* he agreed, because he had never lied to me and had apparently decided not to start in a salt cellar. He sat back on his heels and I lit my candle stub, one inch of it, because I wanted to see his face while I ruined the moment.

*I have to show you something.*

I unrolled my father's charts on the flattest stretch of floor and weighted the corners, as he used to, with the cheese, a stone, my shoe, and Isandro's complete bewilderment. Then I showed him the flyspecks—the circles, the broken triangles, the cross-hatched crescents—and watched his face do the same journey mine had done, boredom to puzzlement to the particular stillness of a cartographer realizing the map in front of him is not the map he thought it was.

*Safe houses,* he said quietly. *His charts were a network. All along—*

*All along the coast. Forty years of it, hidden in plain sight, in documents the army has probably already confiscated and catalogued and found nothing in, because you need the key, and the key—* I tapped the sequence of symbols in the chart margin, the little row of glyphs my father had once told me, laughing, was his *signature*, though I had not understood until this week that a signature can also be a cipher. *The key is the order. Each symbol stands for a family. Read them in the order they appear on each chart and you have the route north.*

Isandro was silent for a long moment. In the candlelight his face did something complicated—I have thought about that expression many times since, and I still cannot file it neatly—and then he said, *This is why they killed him. Your father. Not sedition. They found out what the margins said.*

*Or they're still trying to find out,* I said, *and they took my mother to ask her.*

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water. Isandro looked at the charts and then at me, and I watched him decide, visibly, deliberately, not to say whatever had risen in him, and I understood—I understood with a clarity that hurt—that he was already carrying something he hadn't told me. His master's death. The locked drawer I'd heard him mention once, years ago, the way children mention locked doors, idly, not knowing they are describing their futures. He carried it the way I carried the oilcloth roll: strapped close, not spoken of.

I did not press him. That is the arrangement we had always had, he and I, since we were children trading contraband sweets on the harbor wall: you may keep your secrets, provided you keep walking beside me while you carry them. It is a modest contract. It has held for fifteen years.

But I will confess this, since confession is apparently the theme of my residence down here: trusting him absolutely is harder than distrusting everyone else would have been. Distrust is light. It weighs nothing and asks nothing. Trust is a cargo. Every hour I sit in this dark keeping my promise to my mother, I am also spending his—his safety, his sleep, his steadily depleting supply of innocence, spent on bread and forged papers and a girl in a cellar whose only skill is reading symbols her dead father hid in the margins of the world. If he is caught, I will have done that to him. If he is caught, it will have been for me, and I will have to live in the arithmetic of that forever.

That is its own weight. I said that already. I will say it again because it is true twice.

* * *

He came again the next night, and the next. Each time: bread, sometimes cheese (he argued with me about the cheese, taking my mother's side, which I chose to find tender rather than conspiratorial), news measured out in careful doses like medicine. The tallyman Ferran had noticed something in the ledger's inks—*don't ask how I know, eat your bread*—the ship's departure was fixed, the lists were posted, forty families. He did not say my alias held. He also did not say it didn't. He said, *Soon*, and *I'm working on papers*, and *trust me a little longer*, and I said yes every time, because what else does love say in a salt cellar?

On the seventh night he stayed later than usual, and we sat shoulder to shoulder against the cold wall with the candle unlit, sharing the darkness the way we'd once shared a blanket on the harbor wall watching storms, and after a while he said, into the black:

*Tell me about the northern route again. The third symbol. The broken triangle.*

*A farm family,* I said. *Two days' walk past the river mouth. My father stayed with them once when I was nine. The wife made a soup I still think about.*

*Good,* he said. *Keep thinking about it. We'll need you to know it perfectly. Not on paper—paper gets seized. In here.* I felt rather than saw him touch his own temple, and mine.

So I began, there in the dark, to memorize my inheritance. Symbol by symbol, house by house, I recited my father's coast to Isandro in whispers, and he recited it back, and where he faltered I corrected him, and thus, over several nights, in a hole beneath a warehouse that appeared on no map anyone official had ever drawn, the two of us committed an entire country's worth of mercy to the only archive that cannot be requisitioned.

It was on one of those nights—my voice hoarse, his hand steady on my shoulder—that we heard the bells.

Not the harbor watch. The garrison bell, ringing fast, the alarm rhythm, and then boots, many boots, on the warehouse floor directly above us, lantern-light slicing down through the floorboard gaps in long yellow blades, and a voice I recognized now, the list-reading voice, calling for the warehouse master and saying words that fell through the boards one at a time like stones through water:

*...search... cellar... girl...*

Isandro's hand closed on my wrist. Above us, the first boot found the top stair.
