## 12. The Tallyman's Eye

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept ledger that civilians will never understand and would probably find pitiable. I have made my peace with this. A man who has spent eleven years counting other people's lives into columns learns to take his joys where the paper offers them: a balanced page, a hand that slants consistently, an ink that does not lie about its age.

The ledger of Vessamare had been, until recently, one of the good ones.

I want to be precise about what I mean by *good*, because the word will do damage later if I let it stay vague. I do not mean kind. Kindness has nothing to do with a ledger. A good ledger is accurate. It is legible. It says of every person in it exactly what the authority above me needs said, in an order that can be found again at speed, because the entire value of a name — and names are the only currency I trade in — is that you can find it again. A name that cannot be retrieved is just a person, and persons are not my department.

So when Captain Aldurez assigned me to verify the residents' entries against source documents with the assistance of the junior keeper, I came down from the garrison in a reasonably good humor, the way a jeweler comes to an estate sale. There would be paper. There would be order. Someone else would fetch things.

I had not yet met Isandro Ishikawa properly. I had seen him at two days of copying, in the way one sees furniture: a thin young man at the second desk who worked very still and wrote very fast and never once looked up when the soldiers came through. Stillness in a clerk is not remarkable. Most clerks cultivate it the way farmers cultivate fences. But I remember thinking, in the idle way one thinks such things, that his stillness was of a different species — not the stillness of a man avoiding attention but the stillness of a man who had practiced being overlooked so long it had become his native weather.

I did not think much more about it. I had forty-one hundred names and a deadline measured in tides, and a deadline measured in tides is a cruel thing for a man whose whole life runs on calendar pages.

* * *

The verification work itself was tedious in the ordinary way, which is to say it was three parts tedium and one part quiet horror, and I had long ago arranged my conscience so that the horror arrived pre-filed. I checked births against baptismal records, trades against guild rolls, debts against the clerk's miserable little chits, and household against household against the census of six years past. The junior keeper fetched. He was good at fetching. He was good at everything I asked him to do, actually, which should have pleased me and did please me, right up until the moment it began instead to interest me.

Here is the thing they do not teach you in the tally schools, because it cannot be taught, only caught after enough years bent over other people's property: every hand has a gravity. Ink falls differently depending on how a wrist is built, how a pen is cut, how quickly a man was taught versus how slowly. Iron-gall darkens as it ages; the newer chemical mixtures fade toward gray-brown; a nib pressed in haste leaves a hairline burr on the downstroke that a loupe will catch like a fishhook catches light. After eleven years I can read a page of script the way a tracker reads ground — who passed, how fast, carrying what mood.

By the third day I could have picked Ishikawa's writing out of a stack of a thousand, blindfolded, with a fever. And that was fine. That was correct. A keeper's hand should be identifiable; identification is the entire religion I serve.

What was not fine — what sat down across from me on the fourth morning and refused to leave — was that his hand was *too* consistent.

Understand the problem. The ledger before the requisition was kept in the old clerk's hand and the master mapmaker's hand and, here and there over the last two years, the apprentice's hand, younger then, the letters rounder, the pressure uneven the way all beginners' pressure is uneven. The ledger since the requisition was kept almost entirely in the apprentice's mature hand, steady as a printed rule. Between the two there should have been drift. Growth. The small honest sloppiness of a living person across time.

Instead, when I laid the old entries beside the new under my lamp, the apprentice of two years ago and the apprentice of today differed only where they *should* differ, and agreed everywhere they should agree, and the agreement had a quality I can only call rehearsed. It was as though a man had studied his own younger handwriting and reproduced it — not imperfectly remembered, but deliberately rebuilt, the way a forger rebuilds a signature rather than merely imitating it.

Except that he hadn't forged anything. Not that I could find. Every entry I traced back to its source document sang true. The Verlaine birth of 1819 matched the parish roll to the day. The harbor dues, the grain debts, the widow Anser's disputed title to the smokehouse — all of it clean, all of it verifiable, all of it exactly what it claimed to be.

A forger who forges nothing. A hand too perfect doing honest work.

You see my difficulty. Accusation requires an irregularity. I had only an absence of irregularity, which is rather like arresting a man for having no shadow. And beneath the professional difficulty there was another thing, smaller and more irritating: curiosity. It is the occupational disease of my trade. We tallies are told we deal in certainty, but certainty is just curiosity that has been fed.

* * *

On the fifth morning I brought my own ink.

I did it casually, or with whatever passes for casual in a man whose face was finished pouring around age nineteen and has not moved much since. I set my flask on the shared desk between us, along with the garrison's verification forms, and said, "The office stock is poor iron-gall, half vinegar by the smell of it. Mine writes darker and dries faster. Use it. Verification entries should be visually distinct from the originals anyway — makes my job easier."

All of that was even true. It is my experience that the best lies are simply truths wearing someone else's coat.

"Thank you, sir." He took the flask. His hands were remarkable — I noticed it properly for the first time then, long-boned, nicked with the small scars of pen-knives and drafting tools, steadier than any twenty-year-old's have a right to be. He filled his pen without spilling a drop. Men who work with their hands all day usually manage that, but there was economy in it beyond competence. A dancer's economy.

He wrote his verification annotations in my ink, dark and quick, and I watched him do it out of the corner of my eye while pretending to read the parish roll, and I will confess to a flicker of disappointment, because he wrote beautifully in my ink too, and beauty proves nothing except that I am susceptible to it.

It was the drying that betrayed him. Or nearly did.

My ink dries fast — I said so myself. But fast is not instant, and there is a window, perhaps four seconds, in which fresh script smears under a careless sleeve or an impatient thumb. Everyone smears eventually. I smear. The old clerk smeared so chronically his pages look like a rain-streaked window. In three days of shared work, across some hundreds of entries, Isandro Ishikawa did not smear once.

Not once.

Now, a careful man can avoid smearing. I avoid smearing. But avoiding smearing is an active discipline, a small constant tax on attention, and I watched for the tax and could not see him paying it. His hand moved through the four-second window the way a man moves through his own kitchen — unthinking, unguarded, untouched. As though the ink were already dry when it left the pen. As though he had learned, somewhere, in some context I could not imagine, to write for an audience that would examine his pages under conditions far less forgiving than mine.

Who teaches a boy to write so that his ink survives scrutiny? Mapmakers, said a voice in my head. Cartographers' ink goes onto charts that captains trust with ships and lives and the difference between a reef and open water. Of course the mapmaker's apprentice writes like that. It is the most reasonable explanation.

I have found in my career that the most reasonable explanation is usually true, and that when it is not, the unreasonable explanation is usually worse than anyone has imagined. I turned the page and kept counting.

* * *

In the afternoon I committed my first real indiscretion of the assignment, if you can call it that, since no regulation forbids a tallyman from reading backwards. The ledger is organized forward, of course — household by household, year by year — but I have always believed a book tells you more about its keeper in the places the keeper thought nobody would look. Margins. Flyleaves. The pages between the numbered ones.

I found three things worth finding.

First, in the flyleaf of the older volume, a child's practice alphabet in faded pencil, corrected by an adult hand — the master mapmaker's, by the slant — and beneath the alphabet, in that same adult hand, a single sentence written as if for dictation: *A line drawn once may be a mistake; a line drawn twice is a decision.* Which is either a lesson in drafting or a philosophy, and I suspect the trouble with mapmakers is that they never learned the difference.

Second, a page near the front of the current volume where something had been erased — not blotted, erased, scraped with a blade and burnished smooth, the way one removes an error one does not wish seen. Scrapings are common in ledgers; guilt is written all over municipal record-keeping and always has been. What was uncommon was the scraping's position: it stood at the head of the removal column, in the space where the first entry of a list would go. Someone had erased the first name on a list, and then, apparently, no name had been written there since. A gap. A missing tooth in a smile.

Third, nothing. Which is to say, the absence of a thing I could not name but felt the shape of, the way you feel a missing stair in the dark. The ledger accounted for everyone the town said it contained. But ledgers account for what towns admit to. My whole career rests on the assumption that the record and the truth run parallel lines that never meet, and I had just spent five days in the company of a young man whose every line suggested he understood that assumption better than I did.

I closed the volume carefully, aligning its edges with the desk's edge, because order comforts me even when it accomplishes nothing.

"You've been at that page a while, sir," said a voice behind me, mild as milk. "Is there a discrepancy?"

I looked up. Ishikawa stood with a stack of parish rolls in his arms and an expression of perfectly calibrated concern — concerned enough to be respectful, not so concerned as to suggest he doubted my competence. If he had been a soldier I'd have called it drilled. From a civilian it was more interesting. Civilians have to learn their calm somewhere other than a parade ground.

"No discrepancy," I said. "Your predecessor's margins are a disgrace. I was admiring them."

"The old clerk writes like a man being chased," he agreed, setting down the rolls. "Though I suppose lately we all do."

It was the sort of thing people said in Vessamare that season — small, wry, deniable — and I let it pass the way one lets pass most of what the occupied say, filing it nowhere, because there is no column for despair and no budget line for grief, and my superiors have never once asked me to count those.

But I noted, in the private ledger I keep behind my eyes, that he had answered a question I hadn't asked, smoothly, and that I liked him, and that liking him changed nothing whatsoever about my duty, and that duty performed without appetite curdles into something I did not yet have a name for.

* * *

That evening Aldurez found me in the requisitioned customs house with the lamp turned low and the day's tallies squared and banded, and asked the question superiors ask, which is the same question in every army ever assembled: "Anything irregular?"

Consider what I might have said. *The junior keeper's handwriting is suspiciously stable across two years, Captain. The junior keeper does not smear. Something has been scraped from the removal list. I believe the boy is lying to us about something, though I cannot say what, and everything he has given us is provably true.*

Consider what Aldurez would have done with it. Aldurez collected suspicions the way other men collect debts, and settled them the same way, promptly and without sentiment. He would have posted a guard in the workshop within the hour. He would have pulled the atlas apart looking for what I could not yet name. He would, in short, have broken a machine that was still producing exactly the output required of it, on the strength of evidence consisting entirely of neatness.

"Nothing irregular," I said. "The records are in better condition than expected. The junior keeper is competent."

"He's useful," Aldurez corrected, which is the army's word for competent, and means: expendable in a productive direction. "The governor wants his atlas. Keep him happy and keep him working."

"I intend to," I said, and meant it in a way Aldurez could not have parsed and I did not examine.

After he left I sat a while with my own arithmetic, which is a habit I recommend to no one. Eleven years of sorting the sortable. I have placed laborers, horses, grain, and families into columns with the same pen, and I have told myself — it is even substantially true — that the columns are not mine, that I am a lens and not a hand. But a lens that focuses well enough will burn things whether it intends to or not.

And now the lens had found a flaw in a boy, and the flaw was excellence, and the excellence was aimed at something, and I did not know at what, and I found — this is the part I keep circling — that I wanted to know. Not to report. To know. There is a difference, and I am old enough and honest enough with myself to know the difference matters, and coward enough to notice that noticing costs me nothing yet.

Because here is the professional assessment I would write if I wrote assessments for myself: Isandro Ishikawa is either precisely what he appears — a gifted, grieving apprentice keeping honest books under occupation — or he is the most dangerous category of person I have ever encountered, which is not the fanatic or the criminal but the craftsman with a secret, the man whose skill is so complete that his lies arrive finished, polished, and true in every checkable particular.

The first sort you leave alone. The second sort you watch. And watching, I have learned, is not a neutral act. Watched things change shape. Watched men make mistakes, or commit to covering them, and the covering is where the real damage happens. Already, tonight, I had lied to Aldurez — there was no other word for it — and I had done it for reasons I could not enter in any column, for a scraped page and an unsmeared line and the memory of a sentence about lines drawn twice.

*A line drawn once may be a mistake; a line drawn twice is a decision.*

Tomorrow I would draw the line again. I had decided that much. I would request the workshop's older volumes — the survey books, the chart drafts, the master's field notes — ostensibly to verify landholdings against original surveys. Ostensibly. And I would sit in that dead mapmaker's room among his instruments and his apprentice, and I would read the ground of that place the way I read any ground, for tracks, for weight, for what had passed through carrying what.

And if I found what I half-feared I would find — if that tidy room held papers no register admitted to — then I would stand at the fork every tallyman secretly dreads, the fork with no procedure for it, and choose which book to believe: the one I am paid to keep, or the one I keep behind my eyes.

I banked the lamp. Outside, the tide was turning; you could hear the harbor change its mind.

Tomorrow, I would ask to see the workshop.
