## 16. Ferran Asks About Ink

There are three kinds of men who work late in a requisitioned clerk's office. The first kind works because he is afraid to go home and face what the day has made of him. The second kind works because he has nowhere else to be, which is a softer version of the first. The third kind — my kind, I told myself then and mostly still believe it — works because the work is the only honest thing left in the building.

It was past ten when Isandro Ishikawa came back for his inkstone.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the whole trade. He did not come back for the inkstone. The inkstone had been sitting on the corner of his table since sundown, exactly where a careful boy would leave it and exactly where a careless one would forget it, and Isandro Ishikawa was not a careless boy. I had watched his hands for three weeks by then. Hands tell you everything a ledger cannot. His were steady at the downstroke, quick at the lift, and they never hovered over a name longer than the name deserved. A man who hesitates over names is either slow or lying, and he was neither. Which meant whatever hesitation lived in him had been trained out of the hands and driven somewhere deeper, somewhere I hadn't mapped yet. I was, you understand, professionally interested. It is not the same as suspicion. Suspicion is cheap; any sergeant with a grudge can afford it. Professional interest costs sleep.

So: he came back at ten o'clock for an inkstone that had not needed fetching since sundown, wearing the particular expression of a young man who has rehearsed a reason and found it adequate. And the office was empty except for me, one lamp, and four days of transcription that would not sort themselves.

"Master Ferran." He stopped just inside the door, as if the threshold were a checkpoint. "I didn't know anyone—"

"No one ever does," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

He smiled at that — a small smile, quickly governed — and came toward his table instead of toward the door, which was the correct choice, and I noted it. People reveal themselves in their choices about distance. The guilty keep far away from authority; the innocent drift close out of simple animal comfort; the very clever do whichever is least expected. He drifted close. Either innocent or very clever, then, and I had already spent too many nights to believe the first.

"You're working late," he said, nodding at my columns.

"I'm always working late. It's the hours that are early." I moved the lamp a little toward the center of the shared table — a small hospitality, offered without looking up. "The army believes a tallyman's eyes should be fresh. The army believes many things. Sit, if you're sitting."

He sat. We worked, or performed working, for perhaps ten minutes. The scratch of two pens is a strange conversation. You can hear a great deal in it — rhythm, pressure, whether a man lifts his nib to think or drags it through the thinking. His pen lifted often. Mine dragged. Between us we sounded like a man arguing with himself, which, in fairness, we may both have been.

It was he who broke first, which surprised me slightly; I'd have bet on myself.

"The lamp," he said. "You've got it angled wrong for your light hand."

"My light hand?"

"You write right-handed but you pull your strokes from the shoulder, so the shadow falls across the column if the flame sits left of your wrist." He said it mildly, the way a man comments on weather. Then he seemed to hear himself, and something flickered across his face — the brief horror of the craftsman caught admiring another man's technique in front of witnesses. "I'm sorry. That was — my master used to say apprentices see nothing but ink. Apparently it doesn't wear off."

"Apparently," I said, and turned the lamp as instructed, because the boy was right, the shadow *had* been bothering me for an hour, and there is no point letting pride darken a column. "Your master. Sor Ondiz."

"Sor Ondiz," he agreed, carefully.

"A great loss to the town." This is the sentence we used, all of us in the occupation, for every dead man we could not explain. A great loss to the town. It covered drowning, fever, falling down stairs, and the other things. "He kept good records, I'm told."

"He kept everything," Isandro said. "Records, grudges, spare nibs going back thirty years. You could open any drawer in that workshop and find the whole history of Vessamare in somebody's marginalia."

"Could you," I said. "Open any drawer."

And there it was. I felt it land — not loudly; I am not a loud instrument — but land nonetheless, the way a stamp lands on paper, weight without violence. The boy's pen paused above the line for exactly one heartbeat, which is the correct duration for a man with nothing to hide and also the correct duration for a man who has practiced. Then he wrote on.

"I wouldn't know," he said. "Most of them are locked. Master believed in locks the way other men believe in saints."

"Did he." I set down my pen and looked at him properly for the first time that night, over the top of the lamp, so that the light fell on me and left him in the gentler dark. Interrogators think the trick is to put the light in the subject's eyes. Amateurs. You learn nothing from a squinting man. You put yourself in the light and let him watch your face while he decides how much truth the room can hold. "And you? What do you believe in?"

"Ink," he said, promptly enough that it must have been waiting behind his teeth. "Ink and vellum. Everything else in this town is temporary."

It was a charming answer. It was also, I suspected, entirely true, which made it dangerous in a way he probably hadn't considered. A man who believes in ink believes in what ink does: it fixes things. It takes the living moment and nails it flat to a page forever. Every entry I had made in the past three weeks was a nail. Forty families under the heading *removal*, each one fixed, each one nailed, and somewhere across town a girl with her father's cheekbones was either asleep or awake knowing she was one of the nails. I did not let my face do anything about this. My face has had excellent training.

"Show me your ink," I said.

He blinked. "My — "

"Your inkstone. Your mix. Humor a bored tallyman, apprentice. It's the cheapest entertainment in Vessamare and the only one the curfew permits."

He passed it over without hesitation, which I logged. A guilty man fumbles; an innocent man hands it over; a clever man hands it over *while talking*, so that the object arrives wrapped in charm. Isandro handed it over while talking — something about the harbor silt getting into everything — and I gave him full marks even as I uncapped the little pot and held it to the flame.

Iron-gall, of course. Everyone in this province writes iron-gall; it grows in our galls and costs nothing and eats through paper given fifty years, which gives our archives a pleasing air of impermanence despite themselves. But iron-gall is like bread: every baker's differs, and a man who has eaten long enough can taste the house.

"This isn't Sor Ondiz's recipe," I said.

The pause this time lasted two heartbeats. Progress.

"How could you possibly—" He caught himself, recalibrated. "It's close. I mixed it from his notes after he died. The galls here are different from the ones he used to buy, the merchant said, so I adjusted the vitriol."

"Mm." I tilted the stone. In the lamplight the ink showed that particular blue-black with a faint brown ghost at the edge where the tannin sat heavy. "His was browner. More gum arabic, less rainwater — he wrote with river water, I'd guess, some mineral in it. And he aged his batches. There's a smell old iron-gall gets, like a struck nail left out. Yours hasn't got it yet. Yours smells like a new nail."

"You can *smell* the difference?"

"I can smell everything. It's an occupational deformity." I set the stone down between us, equidistant, neutral territory. "Don't look alarmed, boy. I'm not accusing you of anything. Accusation requires paperwork and paperwork requires me to stay past midnight, and I have a bed now, courtesy of the Widow Castera, whose floorboards I am told sing."

"What are you doing, then?"

I picked my pen back up. Dipped. Drew a short stroke on scrap — his ink — and beside it, from the little traveling horn I carry in my breast pocket, drew a matching stroke in mine. Two black lines under one lamp. To a soldier they would have been identical. To the two of us they were as different as two faces.

"That," I said, "is what I'm doing. Do you know why the army sends a tallyman ahead of the provosts? Anyone can count heads. Sheepdogs count heads. The army sends a tallyman because counting is easy and *reading* is hard. Reading means knowing which hand wrote what, and when, and whether the 'when' is true. Paper lies constantly, apprentice. It's ink that tells the truth. Ink has a birthday. Iron-gall goes down blue-black and turns brown as it ages — weeks, months, years. So when I find an entry dated March written in ink that is only nine days old, the ink has just testified against the entry. Ink is the worst liar in any office. It cannot help itself."

The silence went on long enough that I could hear the harbor — the hawsers creaking, the tide turning, all the patient machinery of departure. He was looking at the two strokes on the scrap. Not at my face. Wise. My face was giving away nothing anyway, but wise.

"That's remarkable," he said finally, and his voice was admirably level. "Truly. I never thought — ink aging. Of course it ages. Everything ages. But to *read* it..."

"It's not remarkable. It's arithmetic with better lighting." I blotted the scrap and slid it aside. "Here is the trouble with being able to read ink, though. Would you like to know the trouble?"

"If you're telling me anyway."

"Wry," I noted. "Good. You'll need wry where you're going." I don't think he understood that. I'm not certain I did. "The trouble is this: once you can read it, you can never unread it. Every document in this province is now talking to me, all the time, whether it wants to or not. The clerk's ledger gossips. The grain manifests gossip. Half the transit papers crossing my desk this month were written by frightened people in a hurry, and their ink tells me so — thin mix, rushed strokes, entries added between lines. Do you know what I do with all that testimony?"

"No."

"Neither do I," I said. "That's the trouble."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him perform the same calculation I had been performing for weeks, each of us trying to price the other. I'll grant him this: he didn't flinch and he didn't grovel. He folded his hands on the table like a man settling in for a lesson, because that is what apprentices do with knowledge — they take it, gratefully, and they do not ask what it will cost.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

Because your master asked himself the same question about his own dangerous knowledge, and it killed him, I did not say. Because I sorted forty families into columns this week and remembered none of their faces, and today, watching your steady hands copy my columns, I realized you are the first face this occupation has shown me that I will remember, and I don't yet know whether that makes you a problem or an answer. Because a lamp, a shared table, and two kinds of ink is as close to confession as my profession permits.

What I said was: "Because you'll be transcribing the removal manifest tomorrow. All forty names onto fair copies for the ship's manifest. And I wanted you to understand, before you began, exactly how legible you are. Every correction you make, every date you adjust, every drop of your newer ink touching a page that claims to be older — I will see it. Not because I'm hunting you. Because seeing is the job, and the job is the man, and I have not yet decided what kind of man the job has made me."

The lamp fluttered. Outside, a patrol went by on the quay, boots keeping time with the water.

"And if there were corrections?" he asked quietly. "Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," I said, "I would advise any hypothetical corrector of one thing above all others." I leaned forward and tapped the scrap with its two strokes, his and mine, side by side. "Consistency. If you must lie in ink — and history suggests most men must, eventually — never lie halfway. Use one ink, one hand, one age, throughout. The forgers who hang are not the skillful ones. They're the ones who got tired near the end and reached for the convenient bottle."

He absorbed that. I watched it go into him like water into dry ground, all the way down, and I understood that I had just done something irreversible — that I had, in the language of my own trade, entered an item into a ledger that could not afterward be erased. I had taught the boy how not to get caught. Whatever he did with the lesson would be mine to answer for too. That is the thing about knowledge: it keeps accounts of its own.

"It's late," he said at last, rising, taking up his inkstone. At the door he paused, and for a moment the apprentice fell away and something older stood there — tired, and young, and carrying more than a boy should. "Master Ferran. The two strokes on that scrap. Whose did you like better?"

I looked at the scrap. His stroke was cleaner, truer, cut with the confidence of someone who has made ten thousand of them and expects to make ten thousand more. Mine was accurate and dead.

"Yours," I said. "Go home before the patrol changes its route."

He went. I sat alone with the lamp and the columns and the smell of a new nail, and I did not sleep, and near dawn I took the scrap with the two ink strokes and — after a long argument with myself that I lost — fed it to the stove rather than file it.

A filed scrap is evidence. A burned scrap is only a memory, and memories, unlike ink, can choose what they testify to.

Tomorrow he would copy the removal manifest. Tomorrow I would sit across from him and read his ink the way a priest reads a face. And somewhere beneath the harbor, in whatever dark place a clever boy had found for a clever girl, the tide was already counting down toward a morning neither of us had yet agreed to allow.
