## 17. The Governor's Commission

Governor Rell-Morane received me in a room that had been the harbormaster's parlor for sixty years and was now, apparently, a seat of empire. The change had been accomplished with two flags, a new desk, and the removal of every chair except his own. I took this as an instruction about posture and stood.

"You're the apprentice," he said. He did not look up from the paper he was reading, which gave me the leisure to study him, an opportunity I took the way a drowning man takes a plank—gratefully, and with no illusions about where it leads.

He was younger than his reputation. The town spoke of him as a kind of weather system, all pressure and consequence, but up close he was simply a man of perhaps forty-five with ink-stained middle fingers—the mark of someone who still wrote his own correspondence—and the particular pallor of administrators who see daylight only through windows they distrust. His uniform fit well, which told me nothing; uniforms are made to fit. But his boots were scuffed at the toes like a walking man's, not a desk man's, and I filed that away too. Mapmakers learn early that the ground truth is never in the legend.

"Isandro Ishikawa, sir. Of the Ishikawa cartographic workshop."

"The workshop of the late Master Oren Ishikawa," he corrected mildly, "which makes you its master now, I suppose, though the paperwork hasn't caught up." He set down his paper at last and looked at me directly. His eyes were the flat gray of an overcast sea, and they performed the same trick: appearing to reveal everything while concealing the horizon. "You sent me a letter. Three drafts of it, actually—you've been busy. You proposed to complete your predecessor's atlas of the Vessamare coast as a gift."

"As a service, sir. To orderly governance." I had rehearsed this sentence on the walk up the hill until it lost all taste, which I'd found made it easier to say. "An accurate survey of the coast, the harbor approaches, soundings, landmarks, roads. The old plates are half finished. It seemed a waste to let them sit."

"A waste," the governor repeated, tasting the word. "You know, apprentice, most men in this town who want something from me send petitions. They use words like *mercy* and *hardship*. You sent me a proposal with marginal notes on paper stock." He turned a page on his desk and lifted what I recognized, with a lurch, as my own handwriting. "You recommended laid rag paper over wove for durability in coastal humidity. You included samples."

"I thought it better to be useful than pitiable, sir."

"And why is that?"

Because pitiable people get processed, I did not say, and useful people get kept. Because I had spent three days watching Ferran's clerks sort my neighbors into columns and had noticed which column moved fastest. Instead I said: "Pity is spent once, sir. Usefulness pays out over time."

Something moved behind the gray eyes—not warmth, exactly, but the acknowledgment one craftsman gives another's joinery. "You'd have done well in the civil service."

"I'll take that as praise, sir, though I suspect it wasn't meant as any particular thing."

That startled a short laugh out of him, quickly suppressed, and I understood I had accidentally found the seam in the man: he was bored. Governors are bored the way harbors are wet—structurally, permanently. Every day brought him requisitions, disputes, tallies, and the slow arithmetic of occupation, and none of it required his mind, only his seal. I had offered him, of all things, a project.

"The original atlas," he said, tapping the letter. "Your master began it under the old administration?"

"Began it eleven years ago, sir. The coast doesn't change quickly, but it changes. Sandbars walk. The north channel has silted nearly a fathom since his first soundings. A map that old is a map that lies."

"A map that lies," the governor repeated again. He had the habit, I was learning, of taking possession of phrases by saying them back, the way a man pockets a coin to see how it weighs. "And you propose to finish it honestly?"

"I propose to finish it accurately, sir. Honesty is a quality of mapmakers, and you'd have to interview several before you found one."

The second laugh came easier than the first. He rose, walked to the window—the harbor spread below us, gray-green and glittering, masts like reeds—and stood with his hands clasped behind him in the posture of men reviewing property. I watched the harbor too, because I could not help it. The requisition ship sat at anchor in the roadstead, black-hulled, patient as a debt. Two days hence or three, depending on the tide tables nobody would show me, it would fill its hold with forty families and the town's future and sail north.

I had come up the hill to sell the governor a book. What I actually wanted was access: access to the customhouse records, to the harbor office, to the schedules locked in drawers and the movement of ships discussed in rooms I would never otherwise enter. Marisol was in a salt cellar that smelled of old fish and older fear, and I needed, above all things, to know exactly when the ship would sail, and I had reasoned—coldly, in the dark, in the voice I least trusted—that the surest way to be shown a fortress is to be mistaken for its engineer.

"There's a condition," the governor said to the window.

"Conditions are expected, sir."

"You'll include a military supplement." He turned from the glass. "A survey of the harbor defenses. Quays, moorings, channel depths at state of tide, warehouse capacities, the fortifications—" a small gesture that encompassed the hill batteries without respecting them enough to name them "—and a schedule annex. Ship departures, convoy dates, tide windows for loading. Nothing classified in the sense of secrets, you understand. Everything in this harbor is visible to anyone with a spyglass and patience. What's lacking is *compilation*. One document, accurate, current, bound properly." He looked at me. "Can you do it?"

Here is what I should tell you about that moment, because I have turned it over enough times since to wear its edges smooth: he handed me everything I needed and asked me politely to take it. The departure schedules. The tide windows. The exact information for which I had been prepared to forge, flatter, and if necessary burgle. The enemy opened the vault and asked if I wouldn't mind carrying it downstairs myself, nicely bound.

And my first feeling—not my second, which was triumph, nor my third, which was fear, but my first, the animal one, arriving before thought like thunder before lightning's explanation—was *pride*. Someone with authority had looked at my work and said: yes, this hand, this hand can be trusted with the harbor. I felt my spine straighten with the pleasure of being chosen, and it took me a full breath to remember that I had engineered the choosing, and another full breath after that to feel sick about how good it tasted anyway.

There is no fraud quite so complete as being genuinely proud of your own treachery.

"I can do it," I said. "It will take access, sir. The harbor office's sounding logs, the customhouse manifests, the garrison engineering returns. And time—six weeks for a proper job, eight to do it right."

"You have four."

"Four weeks produces an adequate atlas, sir. Not a good one."

"Four weeks produces a good enough one," the governor said, without malice, the way one says *the tide comes in*. "The fleet command wants it by the sailing season. Adequacy, apprentice, is the highest form of patriotism. Ask anyone employed in it."

I bowed slightly, acknowledging the mathematics. Inside my skull, meanwhile, calculations were running that I hadn't authorized: four weeks was longer than the ship would wait. Four weeks was a door standing open into a room I'd be escorted out of. Unless—

Unless the atlas became my reason to be at the quay when it sailed. Unless the schedule annex, that harmless compilation of visible facts, put the sailing date in my own ink, in my own hand, on my own drafting table, days before Ferran's sergeants knew it themselves. Unless the gift the governor was commissioning became the instrument of the thing he was commissioning it against.

"I'll need passes," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me; I had expected it to betray me the way novice linework betrays a trembling hand. "Access to the harbor office and customhouse. My master worked there often and the staff knew him; they'll work easier with a familiar face than a strange soldier asking them to read aloud."

"You'd rather deal with clerks than soldiers."

"Clerks explain things, sir. Soldiers issue receipts."

The laugh again—he was generous with it now, three in one audience, a rich man by his own standards. "Ferran said you were quick." The name landed in the room like a dropped compass, needle spinning. "Yes, he mentioned you. He handles our verification work. Sharp fellow. He says your master's papers raised questions that were never satisfactorily closed."

"My master died of a fall on the harbor stairs, sir. Gravity raises few questions."

"Ferran raises most of them instead." The governor returned to his desk, drew out a printed form, and began to fill it in with those ink-stained fingers, unhurried. "You'll have your passes. Report weekly on progress—to Ferran, as it happens, since he supervises document control. I'm told he has an interest in inks and papers. You'll find him a connoisseur."

"I've met him, sir."

"Then you know the commission comes with an audience." He sanded the page, folded it, and held it out; when I took it, he did not immediately release it, and for a moment we were two men holding the same piece of paper, which struck me then, and strikes me now, as the whole occupation in miniature. "One more thing. Your master's other papers—the ones not part of the atlas. Unregistered documents. Maps, correspondence, anything in his hand that isn't accounted for. If you come across them in the workshop, they're to be surrendered. That's in the order too. Ferran will expect the inventory to be complete."

"He kept a tidy shop, sir. I expect the inventory will match his tidiness."

"See that it matches yours." He let go of the paper. "Four weeks, apprentice. Impress me. Nobody has managed it since the arrival, and I find I miss it."

* * *

I walked down the hill through streets that had learned new habits. Women queued at the provisioner's with ration cards held face-out like psalters. Two soldiers lounged against the fountain that had anchored every childhood game I ever played, and children gave it a wide berth without being told to, some instinct passing through the young faster than any parental warning could travel. A notice had been pasted to the customs house shutter, and a small crowd stood before it not reading it, which is the townsman's way of reading: everyone memorizes by standing near, and no one by looking, because looking marks you as having something at stake.

The pass in my pocket weighed more than paper should. Governor, signature, seal: *the bearer is authorized to survey, compile, and copy such records as pertain to the harbor works.* I had rehearsed begging for this document. I had lain awake designing the approach, the humility, the precise degree of usefulness to display. And the governor had simply written it while I stood there, chatting about paper stock, and thrown in the schedules besides.

They give you the knife, I thought, and ask you to carve the roast, and thank you for the sharpness of your blade.

At the bottom of the hill I stopped at the fish market, ostensibly to price vellum substitutes from the sailmaker's offcuts—I had learned to give every errand a boring true surface—and left a wrapped parcel of bread, salt fish, and a twist of paper bearing a single line in my neatest hand: *Four weeks' work. More soon. Count nothing yet.* The parcel went into the hatch of the salt cellar by the old trick with the crate, and if Ferran's patrols watched the market, they saw an apprentice buying supplies, which was even true.

* * *

Back in the workshop, the light was going amber through the south window, catching the dust that always hung over the drafting table no matter how I swept, as though the room itself shed particles of waiting. The master's half-finished atlas lay open under its weights: plate thirty-one, the outer roadstead, his soundings marching across it in his small severe numerals, stopping mid-channel the week he died.

I sat down and looked at the blank margin where the title cartouche would go, and I let myself, for exactly the length of one lamp-filling breath, feel the size of what I'd just accepted. In four weeks I would produce, in plain sight, a document that told the occupation precisely when its ship would sail—which meant producing it also told *me*, which meant the whole commission was a machine I had built to grind out one date. And every hour I spent inking channels and compiling manifests was an hour the tallymen spent sorting, an hour Marisol spent in the dark below the waterline, an hour nearer whatever the word *removal* turned out to mean when it stopped being a column heading and became forty families on a gangplank.

But the date was coming regardless. That was the arithmetic I clung to as I trimmed the first fresh sheet and pinned it square against the board. The ship would sail whether or not I drew it; the schedules existed whether or not I compiled them; the governor's atlas added nothing to the world's danger that the world did not already contain, and subtracted something from mine. This is what I told myself, in the craftsman's voice, the reasonable voice, the one that measures twice. It is remarkable what a steady hand and a straightedge will justify.

I began to rule the border of the new military plate. Outside, the evening gun went off from the hill battery—a habit of the garrison's, marking the hour with borrowed thunder—and the sound traveled down through the floorboards into the bones of the house.

Under my hand, the first parallel line dried dark and true. And it occurred to me, somewhere between one ruled edge and the next, that I now possessed legitimate reason to open every drawer in the harbor office—including, if the inventory led there, the strongbox where the customhouse kept its register of passengers cleared for embarkation.

Which meant that sometime this week, I would need to find out exactly what name Marisol would be traveling under.
