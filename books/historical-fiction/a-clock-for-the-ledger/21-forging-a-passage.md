## 21. Forging a Passage

There is a particular hour in Vessamare when the occupation sleeps and the town pretends to. The garrison changes watch at the second bell; the new sentries spend their first half-hour on post complaining about their boots to anyone who will listen, which is no one, which means they complain loudly enough for the whole street. I had learned this the way I learned everything worth knowing: by being small, quiet, and assumed to be furniture.

Furniture does not forge documents. Furniture does not sit at its master's old drafting table with a candle unlit beside it, working entirely by touch because a man who works by lamplight has eyes that flinch at darkness, and a man whose eyes flinch at darkness cannot walk the harbor at night without betraying himself.

So I worked blind.

I want to be honest about what this felt like, because honesty is the one luxury I still permitted myself in those weeks, and only in private, and only briefly, like a man sneaking sips of wine he's supposed to be watering down. Working blind felt like prayer. It also felt like theft. These turned out to be the same sensation, and I have never fully recovered from learning that.

* * *

The transit papers of the occupation were not beautiful things. The army had commissioned them from a print shop in the capital—crude stock, a border of interlocking chains stamped in brown ink, three lines of text and two signature blocks and a seal. The seal was the whole problem. Everything else I could imitate with my hands tied behind my back, which was nearly how I'd learned, my master believing that constraint taught more than freedom. But a seal is authority made physical, and authority resists forgery the way stone resists a chisel held by an amateur.

Fortunately, I was not an amateur. And fortunately—or so I told myself, in the voice of a man negotiating with his own conscience—the atlas I was preparing for Governor Vessane required an official seal impression on its letter of commission. Captain Aldurez had pressed the governor's matrix into warm wax himself, in my workshop, with me standing usefully nearby holding the lamp like a good little instrument of the state.

The impression had gone into wax. Wax takes a negative. A negative makes a positive. Any schoolboy knows this; what the schoolboy doesn't know is how to do it well enough to survive a tallyman named Ferran looking at it under a lamp while asking pleasant questions about your evening.

I had taken the wax disc home in my coat pocket, which is the sort of sentence that should end with an execution, and had spent two evenings making a mold. Plaster was too coarse. Soap took detail but crumbled. In the end I used the old mapmaker's trick—my master's trick—a slurry of fine clay and size, packed warm around the wax, dried slowly near the banked stove so it wouldn't crack, then peeled away with the delicacy of a man defusing something. What remained was a hollow in the shape of the governor's seal: the tower, the waves beneath it, the motto worn shallow on one side where the matrix itself had been worn for thirty years.

Even the wear. That was the part that would save us or hang us. Forgers copy the ideal seal. Officials know the real one, and the real one was tired.

I mixed my casting compound in the dark—tallow, resin, a whisper of lampblack to kill the shine—and I did it by feel. This sounds romantic. It was not. It was measuring resin by pinching it between finger and thumb until the pinch felt right, the way my master had taught me to judge gum arabic, saying that the eye lies but the fingers tell the truth. My fingers told the truth. My heart told nothing but lies all night, and I let it, because a frightened conscience is still a functioning one and I needed mine running hot.

The first casting came out soft-edged. The second cracked along the tower. The third—

The third came out perfect, and I sat there in the blackness of my dead master's workshop holding a piece of the occupation's authority in my palm, freshly minted, illegal as sin, and I laughed. Quietly. There are rules even for laughter now.

* * *

Paper next. The army's transit stock had a tooth to it—sized heavy, slightly grey, made I suspected in the same mills that supplied the requisition ledgers, because bureaucracies are economical with their evils. I had three sheets. Getting them had cost me a week of careful boredom: I had volunteered—I, who volunteered for nothing—to tidy the clerk's office after the daily verification queues, and had palmed three blank forms from the bottom of the stack where the count wouldn't show. The clerk had watched me do it. I am almost certain the clerk watched me do it. He said nothing, and looked at the floor, and I understood that in occupied Vessamare there were more kinds of complicity than the army had columns for.

Three sheets. Marisol needed one. That left two, and I will confess here what I could confess nowhere then: I did not yet know who the other two were for. I only knew that a man who forges one document is a criminal, and a man who forges three is a craftsman with inventory, and the difference mattered to me in ways I didn't examine.

The text I composed by feel too, though really by memory. I had transcribed forty-one genuine transit passes for the tallyman's office, copying each in my best clerk hand, and my hand remembered what my mind pretended not to notice. The formula was rigid: *By order of the Provisional Authority of Vessamare, passage is granted to [name], [trade], traveling to [destination] for [reason], valid until [date].* The blanks were where men lived or died. *Reason* was the trap. Fishermen went out to fish. Carters went to market. Nobody simply *left*, because leaving implied that Vessamare was a place one might reasonably want to leave, and the Provisional Authority took that personally.

Marisol's reason, then. She would travel north as a widowed seamstress summoned to her sister's household in Terrassa to help with a lying-in. Sisters were unimpeachable. Lying-ins were unimpeachable. I had chosen the story the way I chose coastlines: for plausibility under scrutiny, and for the smallest possible number of details that could contradict each other. Every extra fact is another place to bleed.

Her name. Here I paused, fingers resting on the dry sheet, and thought about names the way I had been thinking about nothing else for a month. Her father's name was ash already—struck from the civic ledger, struck from the guild rolls, spoken aloud only by people who wanted trouble. Her mother was in the army's custody answering questions she could not answer safely. If I wrote *Marisol Vess-* anything, I might as well write *arrest me* across the page in letters a sergeant could read at arm's length.

She needed a name that was true and false at once. I gave her her mother's maiden name—Aldana—which appeared in no column of any list because it belonged to a woman the lists had never bothered with, wives being invisible in that particular way, and I gave her the trade she actually had, because Marisol could stitch a hem straighter than any woman in the fish quarter and a lie wrapped around a truth holds longer than a lie alone.

*Marisol Aldana, seamstress.*

I wrote it in the dark, by feel, my pen moving through the formula with the muscle-memory of forty-one honest copies. Iron-gall ink, mixed to match the office supply—I had matched it weeks ago, when matching inks was still an innocent exercise in craftsmanship. The hand was the hardest part. Transit papers were written by clerks, and clerks have hands like weather: uniform, unremarkable, slightly hurried. My own hand wanted to be beautiful. Beauty is evidence. I flattened it, letter by letter, giving each character the small impatience of a man paid by the page.

* * *

You cannot proofread in the dark. This is the flaw in the whole method, and I knew it, and I had planned for it the way I planned everything now—with a backup and a way to destroy the backup. I let the ink dry untouched for the length of a paternoster said very slowly, then blotted with clean wool, then set the finished sheet face-down on the warming shelf above the stove, where residual heat would fix the ink without the glow of coals showing through the window.

Then I allowed myself light. One candle, shuttered, for exactly the time it took to check the pass against the genuine one I had copied from memory onto scrap. Name, trade, destination, reason, date, signatures—one real, one simulated, because the army always had a junior officer countersign and junior officers' signatures were illegible by design or by despair. Seal: pressed from my mold into tallow-resin compounded to match the army's sealing wax, that particular dried-blood red, applied with a heated knife blade rather than a flame because flame leaves scent and scent carries in a town where everyone's windows are open because everyone is listening.

It was good. I want to record that without false modesty, because false modesty is just vanity wearing a hair shirt. It was good. Held at arm's length by tired lamplight, by a sergeant who had been on his feet since the second bell, it would pass. Held by Ferran under his examination glass, it would not—no forgery survives Ferran—but Ferran was a problem for a different chapter of my life, and I was learning to live one chapter at a time.

I shuttered the candle. Darkness again. My eyes had kept their night-adaptation, pupils wide, the workshop resolving around me in greys: the drafting table, the racks of plates, the locked drawer I had opened and could not stop seeing even now, its hidden ledger sleeping in the space behind the plaster where I'd re-hidden it. Forty families who had sheltered the declared enemies of the Provisional Authority. Forty families whose names I had already begun moving, entry by entry, between the columns of the official record—labor instead of removal, transport instead of labor—small treasons dressed as clerical errors.

And tomorrow night, if the tide and my nerve both held, Marisol Aldana, seamstress, would walk out of the salt cellar where she had lived for nine days smelling of brine and patience, and present this paper at the customhouse, and become a woman going north to a sister's lying-in, and by morning be a rumor on the water.

I was so busy loving the plan that I nearly missed the knock.

* * *

It was not a knock, precisely. It was the sound a boot makes against a doorframe when the boot's owner wants you to know he could have kicked. Three impacts, unhurried. Then a voice, bored and armed: "Workshop. Open."

Now. Let me describe what happens to a body at such a moment, because the manuals don't cover it. The heart does not race; it *stalls*, like a cart hitting a rut, and then catches and gallops. The mouth floods. The hands—my hands, my trained, precious hands—went cold from the fingertips back, and every joint in them suddenly felt borrowed.

On the table before me: one forged transit pass, face-down, cooling. Beside it: the mold, the compound, the heated knife, the scrap with my practice text. Around me: a room containing enough evidence to hang me twice, once for craft and once for intent.

I did not think. I want to claim I thought, that some cool strategist in my skull arranged matters, but the truth is that ten thousand hours at a drafting table had built a creature in me faster than fear, and the creature moved. Scrap into the stove—no flame, but the coals under their ash still lived, and paper goes to ghost in seconds if you feed it to them right. Knife wiped and hung among its honest brothers. Compound scraped into the clay jar with its fifty innocent cousins. The mold—

The mold was the problem. Clay takes time to break down. No time to break it down.

I dropped it into the water jar. The rinse jar where I cleaned pens, sitting full and opaque in the corner, and any apprentice might have such a jar, and clay sinks, and God forgive me but clay sinks.

The pass itself went into the one hiding place I trusted: inside the atlas. Not loose—loose is death—but slipped beneath the loose guard-sheet of the harbor plate, where a paper found would look like a draft, a working copy, the harmless litter of a man preparing documents for the governor. Which, officially, I was. The beauty of camouflage is that it works best when it's true.

Then I opened the door, and made my face into the face of a tired apprentice, which required no acting whatsoever.

Two soldiers. The tall one I knew by sight—Sergeant Brac, who had once confiscated a wheel of cheese from the widow Ondiz with the gravity of a man seizing artillery. Behind him, half in shadow, a third figure that stopped my breath before my brain caught up and identified it as merely a corporal, young, chewing something.

"Up late," Brac said. Not a question. Occupiers state; they don't ask.

"Ink," I said, and stepped aside to let him see the truth arranged convincingly: the lamp now lit—for I had lit it in the four seconds between hiding and opening, a gamble, since a man surprised in genuine innocence doesn't fumble for light, but a forger caught mid-work looks worse in the dark than in the lamplight, and I had bet on lamplight—"the governor's atlas. Plates due. Captain Aldurez checks progress Thursdays." I let my shoulders carry the weight of a man oppressed by deadlines rather than crimes. "You're welcome to look. Mind the wet pages."

Brac looked. That was the ordeal—not the searching, which he did lazily, lifting a cloth here, sliding a rack there, a soldier's search, a search for weapons and runaways and other interesting categories, not for molds and compounds. The corporal drifted to the table and I watched him with the outer edge of my vision while keeping my face aimed at the atlas with the pride of a craftsman, and the corporal picked up—God, of course he did—the rinse jar, and held it up to the lamp, and swirled it.

Clay settles. Clay had had perhaps ninety seconds to settle. The water in the jar was grey.

"Dirty water," the corporal said, to no one.

"It's a rinse jar," I said, and heard my own voice do something remarkable: it became faintly offended. "It's *supposed* to be dirty. You want I should rinse my pens in wine?"

The corporal looked at me. I looked back with the expression I imagine all apprentices have worn since apprentices were invented: the long-suffering patience of the skilled toward the unskilled. It is amazing what a profession will excuse. He set the jar down. Brac grunted, satisfied or indifferent—it hardly mattered which—and delivered his actual message, which had been waiting behind the search all along:

"Curfew's moved. Second bell now, not third. Governor's orders. There's a notice going up." He paused at the door, and something flickered across his face—not suspicion, not quite; more like a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy. "Ship's schedule changed too. Word is she sails sooner than posted. Don't ask me when. Nobody tells me anything."

They left. Their boots went down the street, unhurried, and the town resumed pretending to sleep, and I stood in my doorway counting my pulse down from wherever it had fled.

*Second bell.* Curfew tightened by a full hour. And the ship—*sooner than posted*. Rumor, probably. Soldiers love rumors the way gulls love fish guts. Probably nothing.

But I had spent a month learning to read documents, and a document is only ink, and ink is only information, and information had just walked into my workshop wearing Sergeant Brac's boots and handed itself to me free of charge: the schedule I needed most in the world was shifting underneath me, and nobody would tell me when it would land.

I closed the door. I barred it. I lifted the harbor chart's guard-sheet and looked at Marisol's pass lying there, perfect and doomed, dated for a tide that might no longer exist.

I had, by my best count, three days. Possibly two. And somewhere across the town, in a lamplit office full of columns, a sharp-eyed man was already asking questions about ink.
