## 22. Ferran's Private Arithmetic

There is an art to tallying that no one who has never done it can understand. The civilians think it is arithmetic. The soldiers think it is clerking. Both are wrong. Tallying is translation: you take a town of four thousand souls and render it into columns, and if you do your work honestly, the columns say everything the town says, only in a language that can be carried in a satchel and read by a man who has never smelled the harbor.

I had been doing this work for eleven years. I was good at it. I want to be precise about that word—good—because I have since had occasion to reexamine it.

Good meant: I could sort three thousand residents into labor, transport, and removal without my hand cramping and without my attention wandering. Good meant: I could look at a name like *Vasquez, Odile, laundress, four dependents* and assign it a column the way a man assigns a number to a bolt of cloth, by measure and not by face. Good meant that when Captain Aldurez said *ferran, I need the removal list clean by assembly day*, I did not ask what happened to people after they boarded the ship, because asking questions is a habit and habits are how tallymen stop being useful and start being inconvenient.

Three thousand people. That was the running total across the towns I had tallied before Vessamare. Three thousand names in my hand, sorted like coins. And here is the thing I would sit with tonight, in the requisitioned customs house, with the lamp turned low because low lamps are a kind of privacy: of those three thousand, I could remember perhaps two dozen faces, and most of those only because they had shouted at me.

A woman in Corvassy had thrown a boot at my head. I remember the boot better than her. A ferryman had wept onto his own ledger page and blurred his son's birthdate into illegibility, which meant the boy went into the column for adults, which meant—I understood this later, the way you understand things later, when understanding is useless—the boy boarded a ship built for grown men's labor. I remembered the ferryman's face. I have tried to forget it, and failed, and tried again, and succeeded for whole days at a time.

But the towns themselves? Gone. Corvassy was a smear of gray roofs and a smell I cannot now reconstruct. The town before that, I could not even name without consulting my own field notes, which struck me tonight as a fact so obscene I actually set down my pen to consider it.

I had rendered three thousand human lives into portable language, and the price of fluency was that none of it stayed human. That was the trade. That was always the trade. You could carry the town in your satchel or you could carry it in your chest, and a man cannot march with a town in his chest.

That is what I told myself, anyway, in the years when telling myself things still worked.

* * *

The trouble with Vessamare—and I identified it early, professionally, as a flaw in the town's legibility—was that Vessamare refused to stay translated. It kept leaking back out of the columns.

It leaked through the bread, for one thing. The baker on the salt-row put caraway in everything, and the smell got into the clerk's office through the floorboards, so that every time I entered *grain levy, household of Bruni, two sacks*, the entry smelled like breakfast. In Corvassy nothing had smelled like anything. Here the ledger itself was becoming appetizing, which is not a sentence I ever expected to compose, even mentally.

It leaked through the clerk, too. Old Salm. He had surrendered the civic records with both hands and a bow, the way a man surrenders a wallet to a knife, and I had watched him do it and thought: *sensible*. But sensible men have a way of standing in doorways afterward, holding their hats, looking at the shelves where their ledgers used to live, wearing the expression of a man attending a funeral that nobody else has noticed is happening. Salm wore that expression forty times a day. I began avoiding the doorway past his office—not for any reason I would have written down—simply because it was easier to take the long way around.

And then there were the hands.

* * *

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is the only virtue I have left that I trust.

On my fourth day in Vessamare, Aldurez sent me to the mapmaker's workshop to inventory the dead man's papers. Unregistered documents, potential sedition, the usual phrasing—we had boilerplate for it the way priests have boilerplate for funerals. The apprentice answered the door. Ishikawa, junior. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Skinny in the specific way of boys who work bent over desks, all forearms and stoop, with ink worked so deep into his fingers that the stain had its own shadow.

He was frightened. They are always frightened, and I have a professional indifference to fear—it is weather, it passes, it does not affect the columns. What I noticed, instead, was the hands.

He held them very still while I searched his master's shelves. Not clenched—that reads as guilt—but *stilled*, deliberately, the way a card player holds a good hand. And when I pulled out a half-finished chart and he could not help himself, when his whole body leaned toward it the way a dog leans toward a door, his fingers came up off the table and made a small motion in the air. Tracing something. Coastlines. He was drawing the missing section of the map from memory while I stood there holding the original.

I have sorted three thousand people and I remember none of their hands.

I remember his.

Since then I had seen him twice more—once transcribing the residency ledger under my direction, once carrying documents up the hill with that careful, apologetic walk of his—and each time I found myself watching the hands instead of listening to the answers. Which was a professional error. A tallyman watches faces and listens to words; hands are for the magistrates. And yet.

And yet there was the matter of the ink.

* * *

Here is where I must confess to a private arithmetic of my own, conducted nightly, in a column format I kept nowhere but my head, because some tallies are safer unrecorded.

Column one: facts.

Fact: the dead mapmaker had used iron-gall ink mixed thick, the old way, with enough gum to make it sit up on the page. I knew this because I had inventoried his desk. Fact: the apprentice used a newer mix—cheaper, thinner, bluer when it dried—and I knew this because I had spent an hour with the boy's transcription samples and an hour with the master's correspondence and the difference was as plain to me as the difference between two handwriting styles is to anyone else in this profession. Fact: over the past week, certain entries in the army's official copies had begun showing iron-gall characteristics in places where the army's own ink supply—which I controlled, down to the last pot—contained no iron-gall whatsoever.

I am aware how that sounds. I sat with how it sounded for most of an evening.

Column two: explanations, charitable.

Ink is shared. Workshops pool supplies. The apprentice might have found his master's old stock and used it up out of thrift or sentiment—boys do sentimental things with dead masters' belongings. Charitable explanation, plausible, cost: nothing. I wrote it in my head and looked at it and it sat there being reasonable and I did not believe a word of it, and the fact that I did not believe it was itself a datum, and I entered it in column three.

Column three: explanations, uncharitable, i.e., true.

Someone was moving names between columns. Not many. I had found four discrepancies, each small, each defensible—a date corrected, a dependent reassigned from removal to labor on the basis of an annotation that may or may not have existed in the original. Four families. Small enough to be a clerk's error. Precise enough to be mercy.

Mercy leaves fingerprints. That is the problem with mercy, from a craftsman's standpoint. Cruelty is procedurally invisible—it follows the forms, it stamps where stamping is required—but every act of mercy in a system like ours requires someone to bend a form, and bent forms show. Whoever was doing it was good. Better than good: careful. Dating falsified pages to match paper aging. Matching nib weights. Choosing targets that would never be audited twice.

And using the dead man's ink to do it.

Which meant the forger had access to the workshop. Which meant the forger was almost certainly the boy with the stilled hands, who traced coastlines in the air when he thought no one was watching, who had been present at every stage of the tally, who had—this was the detail I turned over longest—asked me, exactly once, with elaborate casualness, whether the requisition ship's departure date was fixed or subject to tidal conditions.

Subject to tides. Everyone knew that. Nobody needed to ask. Unless they needed to know *which tide*.

I finished column three around midnight and sat looking at the sum of it, which was this: the apprentice was committing treason at the rate of roughly one family per week, and I was the only person in the occupation equipped to notice, and I had known for six days, and I had reported nothing.

Why?

That was column four, and column four was empty, and an empty column in my trade is an obscenity. A tallyman with an unexplained blank is a broken instrument. So I sat there with my lamp turned low and tried to fill it, and what came, reluctantly, was not an argument but an image: the boy's hands going still on the table as I searched his master's shelves, holding themselves quiet by main force, and above them his face doing the arithmetic of a young man calculating, rapidly, what I could take from him and what he could survive losing.

Everyone in this town was doing that calculation. It was the local mathematics. I had simply never before been able to read it off a face I recognized.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I was not feeling, because a man in my position should keep honest books even about his own omissions.

I was not feeling pity, or not only. Pity is cheap; pity is what the strong feel about the weak and it costs nothing and changes no columns. Nor was I feeling sympathy in any warm sense—I had been sent to this town to make it portable, and I intended, in some dim administrative way, to succeed.

What I was feeling, I think, was a professional insult. Someone had introduced an error into my ledger, and the error was *good*, and the goodness of it offended me in a place I had not previously located: beneath the craft, beneath the career, somewhere around the region other men apparently used for conscience. Because here was the arithmetic I could not escape: if a nineteen-year-old apprentice with stolen ink could move four families out of the removal column, then the columns were not what I had spent eleven years believing they were. They were not the town. They were a version of the town, and versions can be edited, and I was the man who had been editing them, and I had told myself for eleven years that the editing was translation when it was—in at least four cases per town, multiplied by three thousand names—

I stopped there. That sentence goes somewhere, and I was not ready to live at its end.

Instead I did what I do. I tallied. I drew a line under column four and wrote, in the margin, in my smallest professional hand, a single question: *What does the boy know that makes him willing to hang for ink?*

Because that was the part that would not close. Forgers forge for money, for family, for fear. This boy forged the way he traced coastlines—compulsively, precisely, from memory—and memory was the key I lacked. He knew something about the dead mapmaker. Something worth dying for, or at least worth the risk, and the dead mapmaker had died in a manner Aldurez described as *falling down stairs* with the flat certainty of a man reading from prepared text.

One question, properly pursued, becomes five. That is the nature of my trade too, though nobody writes songs about it.

* * *

Near dawn I permitted myself one indulgence, which was to imagine the interview. Not an interrogation—I had watched Aldurez conduct those, and they ended with men shaped differently than they began—but a conversation. Two professionals, shop talk, over a shared lamp. I would set out two ink pots, the old mix and the new, and say nothing, and watch his hands go still.

And then what? Then he would lie, badly or well. Then I would pretend to believe him or not pretend. Then one of us would inform on the other, or neither would, and either outcome led somewhere I could not see clearly from where I sat, and the inability to see the end of a procedure was, for me, unprecedented and deeply unpleasant. Eleven years of columns, and every column closes. Every tally resolves to a number. This one kept opening downward, like a stairwell.

I blew out the lamp. Gray light was starting at the harbor shutters. Somewhere below, the fish market was assembling itself out of the dark, the carts and the shouting and the caraway smell drifting up through the boards, the town conducting its ordinary morning as if ordinary mornings were a thing that still belonged to it.

I resolved, in the manner of a man resolving against gravity, to watch the boy one more day. Strictly professionally. Hands, ink, discrepancies. If I found nothing further, I would let the private ledger close and report what duty required, whatever that was.

If I found more—if the pattern extended past four families—then I would have to decide what I was, and I had been postponing that decision with increasing ingenuity for six days.

The knock came before I'd finished dressing. A garrison runner, breathless, holding out the morning folder with the requisition schedules and the routine denunciations that citizens filed now the way they used to file complaints about stray dogs.

I sorted them at the window. Grain levies, billet adjustments, a complaint about a looted cellar. And at the bottom, in a careful merchant's hand, a denunciation dated the previous evening:

*A girl has been hiding in the salt cellar under the Harbormaster's warehouse. She takes extra rations. I have seen a young man visiting her. The mapmaker's boy.*

I stood very still. Below my window the market noise rose like water, and somewhere in it, I had no doubt, the boy was threading the crowd with bread in his coat, and I was the only man in Vessamare who knew he was already caught, and the paper in my hand weighed exactly what paper weighs, and nothing else, unless I chose to make it weigh more.

I read it twice.

Then I folded it, and did not file it, and went downstairs to find out what kind of man that made me.
