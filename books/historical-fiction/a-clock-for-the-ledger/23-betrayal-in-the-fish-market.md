## 23. Betrayal in the Fish Market

The trouble with a small town, Isandro thought, is that it is very well mapped already—not by cartographers, but by gossips, who survey every window and sound every wall without ever once drawing an accurate chart.

He had learned the fish market's rhythms in four days of smuggling bread. Dawn was safest, when the boats came in and every eye was on silver and haggling. The occupation had posted a sentry at the customs stairs, but the sentry was a boy from the interior who had discovered, in his first week, that he disliked the smell of fish and compensated by disliking it loudly and looking anywhere else. Isandro had mapped him too: a boy, a post, a blind spot shaped like a bread loaf under a coil of rope.

The bread itself went into the warehouse by way of old Tadeo, the salt-factor, whose loyalty had been purchased not with coin but with the memory of his master's kindness—a debt twenty years old, which in Vessamare appreciated at a better rate than any bank. Tadeo carried the loaves in with the morning's salt barrels and complained, every day, about his back. The complaint was part of the ritual. If Tadeo ever stopped complaining, Isandro had decided, something would be wrong.

On the fifth morning, Tadeo did not stop complaining. But someone else stopped to listen.

* * *

Her name was Betta Corvel, and she kept the narrow house between the chandlery and the net-mender's shed with her sister's two children and her mother's cough and a ledger of grievances she maintained more faithfully than the town clerk had ever maintained his. Her husband had been taken for questioning in the first week and returned nine days later thinner and quieter, and though he had been released, the release had not un-taken him. She had watched him sit at the table moving a spoon through cold soup, and she had understood that the army could reach inside a house and rearrange its people like furniture.

Betta was not cruel. This must be said clearly, because what she did next cannot be undone by calling her names, though the town called her plenty afterward. She was hungry. The children were hungrier. The ration rolls had her household down for two adults and one child because the clerk's ledger—the clerk's beautiful, surrendered ledger—had never been corrected after her sister died, and the army tallyman trusted his columns more than her voice.

She had petitioned twice. Both times Ferran's assistants had checked the columns, found them consistent, and thanked her for her accuracy.

So when she saw the salt-factor's boy—Ishikawa's apprentice, the quiet one, the invisible one—slipping a wrapped loaf into the warehouse where no rations were authorized, where the requisition seals on the door meant *army stores only*, she did not think *there goes a boy in love*. She thought: *someone is eating who is not on the list.*

And if there was one thing the occupation had taught Vessamare, it was that lists were the only prayers that got answered.

* * *

Marisol heard the knock below her—two soft scrapes, the all-clear—and then, an hour later, footsteps that were not Tadeo's tread and not Isandro's either, heavier, accompanied by voices with the flat vowels of soldiers trying to be polite, which was worse than soldiers not trying.

She sat in the dark of the salt cellar with her knees drawn up and her father's chart folded against her ribs, and she counted. That was the discipline now: count everything. The drips from the ceiling seam—one hundred and twelve between puddle-formations. The grains of salt that got into everything, into her hair, into the seams of her boots, so that she was becoming, grain by grain, a preserved thing. The hours since she had seen the sky.

Isandro came at dusk, most days, or sent Tadeo at dawn. He brought bread and news arranged carefully, like a man setting out a meal—only the good cuts showing. He told her about the atlas commission as if it were merely work. He told her about Ferran's questions as if they were merely weather. She had begun, lately, to translate him: whatever he reported lightly, she multiplied by fear.

The footsteps above crossed the floor twice, paused over the trapdoor—she felt the pause the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder—and moved on.

When Isandro finally came, well past dark, bringing no bread because bringing bread tonight was impossible, he knelt at the trapdoor and put his mouth close to the gap and said, "Marisol."

Just that. Which told her everything before he said anything else.

"They searched," she said. It wasn't a question. "Above me. I heard them."

"A woman talked." His voice was very controlled, the way it went when control was costing him. "Not your name—she doesn't know your name. She said *a girl in the salt cellar*, extra rations, the warehouse. They're treating it as pilfering. Ration fraud." A breath. "Which is almost good. Fraud means they're looking for food, not people."

"And when they don't find a thief?"

"Then they'll look harder for something else."

She pressed her palm flat against the salt-crusted wall. "How long do I have to move?"

"Hours. Maybe a day. I have a place—" He stopped, and she heard him decide to tell the truth, which she loved him for even as it frightened her. "There's nowhere. There's one place, and it isn't ready, and I need until tomorrow night."

"The cistern," she said. He had mentioned the old surveyor's house once, weeks ago, in the tone of a mapmaker noting a blank space on a chart. "Under the surveyor's house."

"You remember everything."

"I've had very little else to do." She heard him laugh, briefly, badly. "Isandro. Come here where I can see you."

He lifted the trapdoor a hand's width and let the lamplight from above spill down, and there he was: thinner, ink-stained to the second knuckle, eyes red-rimmed from working nights on papers she was not supposed to know he was forging. She looked at his face and memorized it—not romantically; practically, the way her father had memorized coastlines, marking the hazards, the safe anchorages, the places where the water deepened suddenly. If she lost everything else she wanted to keep this.

"You should go," she said. "Before someone sees you here."

"I'm invisible," he said. "I've spent my whole life being invisible. It's the one skill the workshop gave me that the army actually values."

"Then stay invisible somewhere far from this trapdoor."

He closed it gently, and she returned to the counting, and the salt worked deeper into her skin, and above her a town began to turn on itself.

* * *

Ferran received the denunciation at breakfast, in the ordinary way, on the ordinary form, and this ordinariness was precisely what he hated about his profession.

The form was standard: informant's name withheld (though he knew it within a sentence—there were perhaps four people in the harbor quarter with the patience to write a complaint in full sentences), accusation (*unauthorized persons in requisitioned warehouse, distribution of stores, possibly a young female*), and recommendation left blank, because informants always left the recommendation blank. They wanted the machinery to move. They did not want to watch it move.

He read it twice and set it beside his cup and considered the apprentice's hands.

That was the trouble. Three thousand souls sorted in his career—labor, transport, removal—and their faces were gone, all of them, washed away by volume the way footprints wash from a quay. But the apprentice's hands he remembered: steady on the transcription pages, too steady, steady the way a liar is steady, with effort. And last week, over the shared lamp, he had made his small demonstration about iron-gall and newer mixes, and the boy had nodded along like a student, and Ferran had gone home knowing two things: that the workshop ink appeared in places it should not, and that he had not yet decided what to do about it.

A girl in a salt cellar. Pilfered rations. Small crime, small punishment, unless the girl turned out to be a name off the removal column, in which case the machinery would want to move in earnest, and once it started it did not accept corrections gracefully.

He could assign the search himself, keep it slow, keep it stupid. Or he could pass it up to Captain Aldurez and wash his hands, and Aldurez would tear the harbor quarter apart in an afternoon and find, in some crack of it, the thread that led back to a workshop and an atlas and an apprentice with careful hands.

Ferran drank his coffee, which was terrible, and wrote on the form: *Investigate. Low priority. Warehouse theft likely local boys.* And then, because he was honest with himself in ways the job did not require and did not reward, he added beneath it, in his own private shorthand that no clerk would bother to decode: *Check the salt cellar anyway.*

He was not protecting the boy. He told himself he was not protecting the boy. He was protecting the integrity of the tally—if there was an unregistered resident in Vessamare, he wanted to find her himself, properly, on his own schedule, rather than have Aldurez's soldiers blunder into her and produce a spectacle that would make the whole town harder to sort. That was the professional explanation. He had written better justifications than that, in neater hands, for worse purposes.

He stamped the form and sent it down the hill, and only afterward noticed that he had eaten nothing, and could not remember whether that was new.

* * *

Isandro heard the news from the market itself, in the way news traveled in Vessamare, which was faster than any courier and less reliable than rumor and somehow both at once. By noon everyone knew that a search was coming to the warehouse. By mid-afternoon everyone knew it had come and found nothing but salt and spilled flour and one indignant salt-factor demanding compensation for his ruined reputation. And by evening, everyone knew—or believed they knew—who had talked.

They knew because Betta Corvel's window had been broken. Not by soldiers. By neighbors.

Isandro stood across the lane in the dusk and watched her sweep the glass, and hated her, and could not manage to hate her cleanly. He had spent enough hours with the civic ledger to know her household's arithmetic: two children, a sick mother, a ghost of a husband, and rations computed for fewer mouths than lived in the house because paper outranked truth. He had seen her name in the columns. *Corvel, B. — labor — dependents: 3.* He had copied that line himself with his steady criminal hands.

She finished sweeping. She looked up, directly at him, across the lane, and for a moment their eyes met, and hers were not defiant. They were tired in the specific way of a person who has done the arithmetic and found that survival costs more than she can pay and has paid anyway, and will pay again, and knows exactly what kind of person that makes her.

She went inside. He did not throw anything at her window. He stood in the lane a moment longer, doing his own arithmetic: how many hours until the low tide, how many until the high one, how many days until the ship sailed, how many lies remained in his purse, and whether love expressed as logistics could survive the discovery that logistics had casualties—that his careful routes through the fish market had been visible to a hungry woman with a grudge, that every act of rescue drew a circle and someone outside it.

*The occupied do not betray each other out of evil,* his master had once said, inking a coastline with the patience of a man tracing the edge of the world. *They betray each other out of hunger, and then they live beside each other forever, which is the actual punishment.*

Isandro turned toward the surveyor's house. He had a cistern to prepare, a wall to open, a letter to find, and perhaps a single night in which to do all of it—because tomorrow the tallyman's men would come back to the harbor quarter with better questions, and the blank space on his chart where Marisol hid was shrinking by the hour.

Behind him, in the darkening lane, the town began quietly nailing shut what it could not defend: shutters, doors, and the parts of themselves that still believed neighbors were neighbors.

At the corner, a soldier was posting a notice. Isandro did not stop to read it. He didn't need to; the size of the crowd gathering around it, silent, told him the shape of the words before he saw a single letter.

But he read it anyway, because a mapmaker learns never to leave a document unread:

**BY ORDER OF THE GOVERNOR: THE REQUISITION SHIP *MERIDIAN* WILL SAIL ON THE MORNING TIDE OF THE DAY AFTER TOMORROW. ALL PERSONS LISTED FOR REMOVAL WILL PRESENT THEMSELVES AT THE CUSTOMHOUSE QUAY BY FIRST LIGHT.**

Two tides. Not five. Someone, somewhere up the hill, had shortened the clock—and Isandro stood very still in the lane, doing the final arithmetic, and understood that the net was closing faster than any map he owned could show him a way out.
