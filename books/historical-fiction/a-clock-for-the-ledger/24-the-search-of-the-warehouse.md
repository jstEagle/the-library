## 24. The Search of the Warehouse

There is a particular sound a building makes when it is being taken apart by men who have no intention of putting it back together. I had never heard it before that morning. I hope I will never hear it again, though I suspect the world produces it often enough.

It is not crashing, exactly. Crashing is honest — something falls, something breaks, and the sound is finished with itself. What I heard from across the harbor was slower and more deliberate: the scrape of furniture dragged on stone, the splinter of boards pried rather than broken, boots on salt-cell stairs. A methodical sound. An inventory of destruction.

I heard it because I was on the harbor wall with my sketchbook open, drawing the warehouse.

That is the sentence I want you to sit with for a moment, because I sat with it too, and it nearly choked me. I was drawing the warehouse. For the governor's atlas. The harbor survey required elevations of every structure on the waterfront, and the warehouse — old, sagging, built by three generations of men who believed walls were suggestions — had been assigned to me as a matter of alphabetical order. And so while six soldiers tore Marisol's hiding place to pieces four hundred yards away, I stood in plain sight making a picture of it, like a portraitist sketching a man at the moment of his execution.

The irony was not lost on me. Very little irony is lost on me these days; I have developed an appetite for it, the way starving men develop appetites for things they cannot eat.

Let me go back two hours, because the morning deserves to be told in order, even if I lived it out of order.

* * *

The summons came at dawn. A corporal I did not know hammered on the workshop door hard enough to wake the dead master I still half-expected to answer it.

"Governor wants the harbor elevations by week's end," he said, when I appeared dressed and composed, which took effort, since I had spent most of the night forging transit papers by feel with the shutters sealed. "The survey officer's at the warehouse this morning. Says he needs his mapmaker."

*His* mapmaker. I noted the possessive the way one notes a change in the weather — without comment, but with a hand already moving toward heavier clothing.

The warehouse search, then, was not about Marisol. Not yet. It was part of a general sweep of the waterfront, ordered because the governor liked his requisitions thorough and because soldiers, given nothing to do, will always find something to take apart. Marisol had been in the salt cellar beneath the warehouse floor for nine days. She was supposed to move tonight — the tide, the papers, the plan we had built plank by plank over weeks of smuggled bread and whispered arithmetic through the cellar vent.

And now the army had decided, casually, before breakfast, to dismantle her ceiling.

I walked down the hill faster than a mapmaker should walk, which is to say I walked like a man with a name in a ledger, and I made myself slow down twice, and both times my legs disobeyed me until I reminded them whose apprentice I was. Patience. Proportion. A line drawn too hastily cannot be corrected without leaving a ghost of itself. Master Aldous used to say that about ink. He meant it about everything.

The harbor at dawn was gray and busy with soldiers. Ferran was there — of course he was; the man had a genius for being where the paper was — standing at the warehouse door with his tally book, directing the sweep with the mild air of a librarian supervising a reshelfing. He did not look up when I arrived. He looked up exactly when he wanted to look up, which was after a pause long enough to let me know he had known I was coming.

"Apprentice." He nodded at my sketchbook. "You're early."

"The governor wants elevations by week's end."

"Then you'll want to draw quickly." He gestured at the doorway, behind which the sounds of demolition continued their patient work. "Before the subject changes."

It was not a joke. Or it was a joke, in the way Ferran's jokes were jokes: technically accurate, humorously inert, delivered at a temperature just above room. He stepped aside and let me in, and I understood that I had been granted access precisely because granting it cost him nothing and might show him something. That was how Ferran hunted. He never closed doors; he watched who walked through them.

Inside, the warehouse was becoming a diagram of itself.

Soldiers had pulled crates into the open and split them with hatchet blows, checking grain sacks by stabbing them, rolling barrels to listen for hollows. The survey officer — a Captain Voss, precise and uninterested in anything that could not be measured — had claimed a corner table and was consulting plans of his own: garrison blueprints, older than the war, drawn by some engineer who had never smelled this harbor. I looked at those plans and felt the familiar craftsman's flinch. They were wrong. Not artistically wrong — factually wrong. They showed the warehouse as it had been surveyed thirty years ago, before the east wall settled, before the fire of '41, before Old Toma's father had rebuilt the ground floor with reclaimed stone from the collapsed customs annex. The plans showed a clean rectangle. The building was a crooked, patched, much-mended animal.

Buildings lie to strangers, my master taught me. Only someone who has listened to a building for years knows where its silences are.

And under the east stair, behind a false panel that the plans did not show and that only three people alive knew how to open, was a girl with her hand over her own mouth, listening to soldiers pry at her world.

I did not look at the east stair. I am proud of that, in the small, shabby ledger of my pride. I set my sketchbook on the table opposite Captain Voss, sharpened my pencil with a gesture of enormous calm, and began to draw the west wall, which was the farthest wall from Marisol and therefore the safest thing in the room to love.

* * *

Drawing is a kind of prayer, if you do it honestly. You cannot draw a wall and hate anyone at the same time; the hand refuses. So I drew, and my breathing slowed, and I catalogued the soldiers' progress through the warehouse the way a cartographer catalogues everything — peripherally, usefully, without appearing to.

They would find the cellar. That was certain. The question was only when, and whether the finding happened before or after they grew bored, and whether what they found in it looked like a girl's hiding place or like empty storage.

Nine days of occupation had left the salt cellar nearly bare: a blanket, a water jug, crumbs. But crumbs tell stories. Soldiers know this; tallymen know it better. A floor with bread dust and a pressed hollow in sacking says *someone was here*, and *someone was here* is the first entry in every search ledger ever kept.

A sergeant drove a bayonet into the floorboards near the center of the room. The wood complained. My pencil did not stop moving, but my heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as falling upstairs.

"Structural notes," Captain Voss said suddenly, tapping his outdated blueprint. He was speaking to himself, or to the blueprint, or possibly to the general concept of disappointment. Then he noticed me, as if I had only that instant materialized. "You. Mapmaker. Your master surveyed these buildings?"

"He surveyed most of the harbor front, sir."

"And this one?"

"This one he knew best." True. Devastatingly true. Master Aldous had known the warehouse the way a physician knows a difficult patient. "The plans you're holding predate the fire of '41, sir. The east foundation was rebuilt after. Reclaimed stone from the customs annex. It sits differently now than your drawing shows."

Voss frowned at the blueprint as though it had lied to him personally. "Differently how?"

Here is where I must be honest about myself, because no one else in this story will be: I did not decide. There was no moment of moral deliberation, no weighing of loyalty against fear. What happened was simpler and more terrible — the answer assembled itself in my mouth out of professional reflex, the way answers assemble themselves for any craftsman asked about his trade. I saw the wrongness of Voss's plan and the truth of the building and the gap between them, and into that gap my voice fell before my conscience could catch it.

"There's a void under the east stair, sir. Old construction — probably a cistern once, sealed off when they rebuilt. Your surveyors wouldn't have flagged it because the surface reads solid. But the load-bearing wall above it cracked in the settling. If your men start levering floorboards there without bracing—" I shook my head with the sorrowful competence of a man discussing weather. "The section could drop. Take a man's leg with it, or worse."

Every word true. Every word a lie. This is the forgemaster's secret, which I learned that morning and have carried since: the best counterfeits are struck from real metal.

Voss looked at the east stair. He looked at his blueprint. He looked at me, and for one long second I was back in Master Aldous's workshop being examined on projections, except that this examiner could have me shot, and the examination question was not *do you know your craft* but *why does your craft know things it shouldn't.*

"You've been in that cellar?" he said.

"For storage, sir. When I was apprenticing. My master used the space for paper stock." Also true. Also, therefore, safe. Truth was proving to be the most forgiving medium I had ever worked in. "Damp got into it two winters running. We stopped using it. I'd stay clear of the east side regardless."

He grunted — the grunt of a man filing information in a column I could not see — and turned to his sergeant. "Nothing under the east stair. Structural risk. Work the north bays and the loft."

The net moved away from Marisol by forty feet, and the distance was drawn by my own hand, in words instead of ink, and I went back to drawing the west wall while something inside me shook so hard I had to widen my stance to absorb it.

* * *

They worked the north bays all morning. They found contraband of the ordinary sort — two barrels of undeclared brandy, a coil of rope belonging to nobody in particular, a family of indignant rats — and they broke a great deal that belonged to no one worth breaking. Through it all I drew. I drew the west elevation, the south elevation, the roofline with its sag like a tired shoulder. I measured doorways I had measured a hundred times, and each measurement was a small act of defiance disguised as diligence, because every minute I spent being useful was a minute the search spent being unfocused.

Ferran watched me draw for a while. I felt it the way you feel weather changing.

"You measure carefully," he said, appearing at my shoulder with the soft tread I had come to dread more than boots.

"It's the whole of the trade, sir."

"Mm." He consulted his tally book, though I do not believe he needed to. "Your master's workshop. Iron-gall ink, mostly. He mixed his own, didn't he? Old-fashioned man."

"He was."

"Ferric tannate darkens as it ages. New entries go in pale and brown over years. It makes documents honest in a way people aren't — you can read the age of a line." He closed the book. "Useful thing to know, in my work. Ink keeps better records than men do."

"I wouldn't know, sir. I mostly draw coastlines."

"Yes," said Ferran. "Coastlines." And he moved off toward the loft, leaving behind him that peculiar sensation of having been touched very lightly by something that could have crushed.

I put down my pencil and picked up another, because a man whose hands shake visibly is a man who has stopped being useful, and I had learned by then that usefulness was the thinnest ice I walked on and the only ice there was.

* * *

By noon the search was winding down. Voss had his corrections — I copied the amended structural notes onto a fresh sheet for him, adding, with a flourish of legitimate authority, a recommendation that the east section remain unloaded pending assessment. He accepted it without reading it closely. Why would he read it closely? It was in the language of his own profession, stamped with the authority of his own commission. I had discovered the great secret of paperwork, which is that no one audits the document that solves their problem.

The soldiers withdrew. The brandy went with them. The rats stayed.

Ferran paused at the door. "The governor's pleased with the survey," he said. "He mentioned it last night. Harbor chart especially. Departure schedules included, he said." A beat, precisely measured, like everything about him. "You'll want the tide tables accurate, then."

"I always want the tides accurate, sir."

"Of course you do." He looked at the east stair once — only once, briefly, the way a man looks at a page he has already read — and left.

I waited until the boots faded, until Voss's carriage rattled up the coast road, until the harbor sounds resumed their ordinary dishonesty. Then I crossed the warehouse floor, knelt at the false panel behind the east stair, and tapped the old signal: three knocks, a pause, two more.

The panel slid. Salt-smelling darkness, and then her eyes, and then her voice, pitched so low it was almost touch.

"Isandro. There were men *on the floor*. I could see the nails moving between the boards."

"I know."

"One of them stabbed down. Right there." Her hand indicated a spot six feet from where she crouched. "Right *there*, Isandro."

"I know," I said again, uselessly, and then, because uselessness is a luxury, I added what mattered: "It's done. They're gone. The east side is condemned — officially. Nobody searches a condemned floor. You move tonight, as planned, and you walk out over ground I've just convinced the army is about to collapse."

She looked at me for a long moment in the dark. There was bread dust on her sleeve and terror drying into something harder around her eyes, and she said, quietly, "You lied to a captain. In daylight. To his face."

"I recommended structural caution," I said. "Aggressively."

Her laugh was small and cracked and the finest sound the harbor had produced all day, and I resolved, kneeling there in the salt dark with her hand finding mine, to hear it again on a boat past the point where anyone could reach us — and then, somewhere below the thought, below the resolve, I heard the other thing, the thing I had been refusing to hear since dawn:

Ferran's voice at the door. *Departure schedules included.* The governor wanted the tides. Ferran wanted to know if I wanted the tides accurate. Everyone in this town suddenly cared about the same stretch of water, at the same hour, and care of that concentrated kind is never innocent.

Tonight's tide would carry us out. Unless the schedule changed — unless the ship sailed early, on paper, silently, in some office I wasn't shown.

I kissed her fingers, told her to wait for the fish-market signal as always, and climbed the stairs into the noon light already composing my next lie: I would need to see the harbor master's sailing book by nightfall, for accuracy's sake, for the governor's atlas, for the tides.

The tide tables were going to have to be very accurate indeed.
