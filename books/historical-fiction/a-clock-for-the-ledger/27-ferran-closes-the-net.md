## 27. Ferran Closes the Net

I have counted three thousand and forty-one people in my career as a tallyman. I remember none of their faces. I remember hands, though—hands that shook, hands that were too steady, hands that hesitated a half-second over a signature because the name being signed did not belong to them.

The apprentice's hands I remembered from the second week. That was the trouble with Vessamare. It was a small town pretending to be a port, and everyone in it had known each other so long that deception had no soil to root in. Deception needs anonymity the way wheat needs rain. Here there was none. The baker knew the fishwife's grandmother's maiden name. The clerk knew which households paid their taxes in coin and which in apology. And I—a man brought in to sort four thousand souls into three columns—was the only stranger, which meant I was also the only person in town with no one to lie for and no one lying for me.

Or so I had told myself, comfortably, for weeks.

The transit papers sat on my desk under the lamp, six of them, surrendered by a sergeant at the customhouse who thought he'd found something worth a sergeant's notice. He was right, but not in the way he supposed. He thought he'd caught smugglers. What he'd actually caught, if my suspicion held, was a boy with an ink pot and a very old promise.

I held the first paper up to the flame—not close enough to scorch, close enough to bring out what daylight hides. Iron-gall ink ages honestly. It browns where it sits, darkens where it pools, and it does all this on a schedule you can set a watch by if you've spent twenty years reading other people's documents for a living. Which I had. Which was, if we're being honest about my life choices, nearly all I had done.

The ink on the transit papers was young. Younger than the dates written in it. Younger than the seals stamped beside it. Someone had written these papers within the last ten days and backdated them with the confidence of a much older criminal.

That alone would have been interesting. Interesting was survivable. A boy forging papers to move his sweetheart off a removal list—I had seen it before, in two other towns, and in both cases I had done my arithmetic, logged my findings, and let the machinery proceed. The machinery always proceeded. That was the arrangement I had with myself: I would be precise, and precision would be my excuse. *I only reported what the ink said.* The ink did the condemning. I never even raised my voice.

But then I turned the second paper over, and the interesting became the unforgivable.

There is a thing that happens to ink when a hand writes too slowly—the strokes pool at the end of each letter, little beads of darkness where the pen lingered. Forgers write slowly. They are drawing letters, not writing them. This hand hadn't drawn. This hand had *written*, quickly, fluently, in a style trained into it by years of copying coastlines and soundings and place-names in a script designed to survive salt spray and bad light. A mapmaker's hand. And beneath the forged entries, in the margin of the third paper, almost invisible—a tiny compass rose, four-pointed, drawn the way you or I would draw a breath. Without thinking. Without meaning to.

I had seen that compass rose before. I had seen it in the corner of the harbor chart the apprentice had shown Captain Aldurez, the beautiful, impertinent, entirely genuine survey that had bought him access to every document in the requisition office. The atlas commission. Of course. Give a man a reason to touch paper and he will touch all of it.

So: the apprentice. Isandro Ishikawa. Master dead under circumstances the garrison had never properly investigated because investigating costs nothing and yields nothing and Captain Aldurez preferred yield. Apprentice left with a workshop full of paper, a half-finished atlas, and—according to the requisition inventory—nothing suspicious whatsoever.

Nothing suspicious whatsoever. In a workshop belonging to a man who had died the same week the army arrived, with his door locked from the inside and his papers accounted for to the last sheet.

I put down the transit papers and poured myself the dregs of the wine I permitted myself on nights when precision felt less like virtue than like habit. Let me lay out the arithmetic, I told myself, because arithmetic was how I prayed, if I prayed at all, which I had stopped claiming in any company that mattered.

Item: a master cartographer dies inconveniently. Item: the civic ledger passes into the keeping of a nineteen-year-old apprentice who should have been nowhere near it. Item: names begin behaving strangely in my columns—families drifting between labor and transport like boats changing moorings, dates that didn't quite cohere, a clerk's handwriting that occasionally looked like it had been corrected by a firmer, younger hand. Item: an atlas commissioned by the governor himself, granting its maker the run of the office. Item: forged transit papers in a mapmaker's ink bearing a mapmaker's tell. Item: a girl, daughter of the deported traitor Ferrante-what-was-his-name the cartographer, unaccounted for since her mother was taken for questioning.

Item, and this was the item that kept me at my desk long past the hour when honest men sleep: I had watched those hands for weeks. Copying names. Steady. Too steady. No one's hands are that steady at nineteen unless they are practicing steadiness the way other boys practice swordwork or whistling.

I had liked watching those hands. That was the part I did not enter into any ledger.

* * *

Captain Aldurez received my report the next morning with the attention he gave all reports, which was approximately the attention a wall gives weather.

"You're certain," he said, not looking up from his breakfast.

"The ink is consistent with the workshop stock. The hand is consistent with the survey work in the governor's atlas. The papers are false."

"Mm." He ate. Aldurez ate the way he commanded—adequately, without pleasure, on schedule. "And the girl?"

"If the papers are hers, she means to sail."

"On our ship." He looked up at that, finally, and I saw the calculation arrive behind his eyes like a lamp being carried into a room. Not anger. Aldurez did not do anger; it was inefficient. What he did was opportunity. "A traitor's daughter, walking up our gangplank with our stamps on her papers, while our own tallyman stands at the quay verifying documents." He smiled, which was worse. "That would be a story, Ferran. That would reach the capital."

"It would reach whoever tells it," I agreed carefully.

"And who tells it depends on who catches it." He pushed his plate away. "No arrests. If the boy has made papers, the papers will be used, and I want the using, not the making. An arrest now is a fine. An arrest at the gangplank is a conspiracy—and conspiracies travel upward, Ferran. Conspiracies have names attached. Names like the name of the man whose office let this happen under its nose." He did not say *my name*. He arranged the silence around my name instead, the way one arranges furniture.

"Then we let it play," I said.

"We let it play until the tide." He stood, buttoning his coat with two economical motions. "The ship sails on the morning tide day after tomorrow. Announce it has been moved up. Tomorrow evening's tide. Tell the customhouse, tell the harbormaster, tell the clerk—tell everyone, loudly, and watch who hears it too early."

"And the apprentice?"

"Tell him yourself. Watch his face." Aldurez paused at the door, and for one moment the efficiency dropped and something almost human looked out, tired and middle-aged and far from whatever he called home. "You know the worst part of this work, Ferran? Everyone thinks it's the killing. It isn't. It's that you spend your whole life learning to predict people, and then you get good at it, and then you can't stop, and one morning you realize you know exactly what everyone around you will do next and there is no surprise left anywhere." He straightened his collar. "Predict him. Then come tell me what he did. I'm curious myself."

He left. I sat with the transit papers and the cold breakfast and the particular loneliness of a man who has just been handed permission to destroy someone and told to find it *interesting*.

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about sorting human beings into columns: the columns start sorting you.

For twenty years I had believed—had built a whole life on the belief—that a tallyman could stand apart from his tallies. That accuracy was neutrality. That if I never invented a name, never inflated a number, never wrote a column heading with relish, then I bore no responsibility for what happened downstream of my figures. The grain requisitions were not my hunger. The billeting lists were not my boots in anyone's hallway. The removal column was not my ship.

But I had begun, somewhere in Vessamare, to remember faces. First the apprentice's hands. Then the clerk's ruined dignity, the way he apologized to me for existing while handing me his town. Then the fishwife with the laugh, whose name I had moved—or rather, whose name had been moved, by a correction I had noticed and not reported, from removal to transport, and whose face I now realized I knew from the market, from bread, from an ordinary Tuesday.

Three thousand and forty-one sorted, and the first faces arriving only now, only here, only after it was too late for the arithmetic to stay clean.

I went to find the apprentice.

* * *

The workshop smelled of the whole trade—ink, salt, old glue, the ghost of the dead master's pipe still living in the rafters. He was at the drafting table with the atlas open before him, working on the harbor chart, and I will grant the boy this: he did not startle. He looked up, saw who it was, assessed, and returned to his line before speaking, which is either composure or rehearsal, and by then I could no longer tell which.

"Tallyman Ferran." He finished the stroke. Laid down the pen. Turned. Gave me the flat, polite attention of a shopkeeper greeting a customer he does not trust to buy anything. "How can the workshop serve the requisition?"

"News travels through me before it travels through the town. Consider it a professional courtesy between keepers of paper." I let that sit a moment. "The sailing has changed. Your requisition ship departs tomorrow evening. The evening tide. Not the morning after."

And there—I want to be exact here, because exactness is all I have ever had—there was a flicker. Not fear. Fear arrives in the shoulders and the breath, and his shoulders never moved. It was in the hands. Both of them came to rest on the table edge, very lightly, the way a man steadies himself against a swell he has decided not to acknowledge.

"The evening tide," he repeated. His voice did what his hands had refused to do: it wobbled, once, a quarter-tone. "That is soon. The governor's atlas—the harbor survey—I had planned to present it before departure. If I am to sail—"

"Are you to sail?" I asked, mildly. "I hadn't your name in any column."

"I finish what my master began," he said, which was true, which was also not an answer, which was possibly the most elegant non-answer I had ever been handed by a person who had presumably spent his whole previous life unable to lie about breaking a window. "The atlas sails with me or stays complete either way. But the tide—" He shook his head, and for a moment the mask slipped not into guilt but into something rawer, something I recognized from mirrors in towns I'd rather not name: the look of a man recalculating everything at once and hating the arithmetic. "Forgive me. It is only that some things take longer than a tide."

"Some things," I agreed. "Take care they don't take longer than you have."

I left him to his chart. On the street outside I stopped, and made myself admit what I had gone there hoping not to find: that I had wanted to be wrong. That I had walked up that hill carrying the evidence like a stone and set it down at his feet and waited, hoping against twenty years of professional habit, that he would pick it up and confess and make this easy, make this clean, make this somebody else's machinery.

Instead he had offered me a finished atlas and a wobbling syllable, and I had walked away knowing two things with absolute certainty.

He would be at the quay tomorrow evening.

And I would have to decide, before then, whether I was the kind of man who reported what the ink said, or the kind who read it.

* * *

That night I did the tallyman's version of prayer. I laid out the ledgers—all of them, the official ledger, my working copies, the removal list with its forty families—and I read the names aloud to my empty room, because names spoken aloud become faces, and I had three thousand and forty-one debts to begin remembering somewhere.

Somewhere in the middle of the list I stopped. Because a pattern surfaced that I should have seen weeks ago, and seeing it made me set down the page very carefully, the way you set down a thing that might go off.

The corrections in the official ledger were not random. I had noted them, weeks ago, as sloppiness—an apprentice's untidy patches over a clerk's errors. But they weren't errors. Every corrected entry involved a household that appeared nowhere else in any record I possessed. No debts. No billets assigned. No requisitioned goods taken. Families that the occupation's own paperwork had somehow overlooked entirely—as if someone had been quietly deleting them from existence, column by column, since the first week. As if the official ledger and some other ledger had been running in parallel, and the official one was being edited to agree with the invisible one.

A hidden list. Sheltering families. The words assembled themselves in my head without permission, and once assembled they could not be disassembled, and suddenly the dead master's locked room and the convenient death and the boy's too-steady hands all snapped into a single shape, and the shape was not *forgery*.

The shape was *witness*. The whole town was a witness, written down, and the boy was the binding.

I sat back. Somewhere below, the harbor muttered at its moorings, and the tide turned, and I thought: one tide. One tide left, and I am holding a stone in each hand, and both of them are evidence, and only one of them is true.

The knock, when it came, was a sergeant's knock—three blows, apologetic in rhythm, inevitable in fact.

"Tallyman Ferran? Captain's compliments. There's a girl been denounced at the fish market. Hiding in the warehouse district." A pause. "He thought you'd want to be there. You being thorough."

I closed the ledgers. All of them. I looked at my hands, which had sorted three thousand and forty-one people and could not, tonight, sort themselves into any column at all.

"Thorough," I said, to no one, and reached for my coat.

One tide. Whatever I was going to be, I had one tide to become it.
