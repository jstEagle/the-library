## 28. The Choice Between Lists

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no one teaches apprentices, though every mapmaker practices it eventually: the mathematics of what will not fit. A coastline must be simplified to be drawn. Every chart is a lie we agree to, because the truth—every rock, every shoal, every shifting sandbar—would take a lifetime per league and still be wrong by morning.

I had spent eleven years learning to leave things out.

Now, sitting in the workshop with the shuttered lamp between my knees and the contents of Master Elandor's locked drawer spread across the floor like the entrails of a dissected animal, I was finally being asked to do the same thing I had always done. Leave something out. Cut a coastline. Simplify.

The trouble was that the things on the table were not coastlines. They were people.

* * *

Let me lay it out the way Ferran would have, in columns, because that was the language the occupation had taught all of us whether we consented to learn it or not.

Column one: the hidden ledger. Forty-one names in my master's careful hand—the families who had sheltered refugees over three years, written in his private shorthand of dates and initials and small coded marks that matched Marisol's father's charts. The page I had already dissolved in seawater had bought the Vellori family their anonymity; thirty-nine names remained, each one a household that would hang, or worse, if Aldurez's men ever read them. The ledger was proof. Proof, in Vessamare under occupation, was a rope with a name at one end.

Column two: Marisol's passage. The requisition ship *Cormorant* now sailed—not at tomorrow's morning tide as every schedule said, but two tides early, because Ferran had arranged it so, because Ferran suspected my ink and wanted to see what moved when the ground shifted under it. Which meant the forged transit papers I had planned to finish tonight, working by feel in the dark with my eyes night-adapted and my hands remembering what my eyes could not see, now had perhaps six hours instead of eighteen. Six hours to draft papers good enough to pass a checkpoint sergeant who would be looking twice at everyone precisely because the schedule had changed. Six hours in which every minute spent on anything else was a minute stolen from her.

And here was the cruelty of it, the clean mechanical cruelty that I should have expected from men who kept ledgers for a living: I could not do both.

Not both well. That was the honest version. I could attempt both badly and lose everything, which is what ambitious forgers usually achieve. Or I could choose.

I sat on the floor of my dead master's workshop at roughly the ninth hour of the night, and I discovered that I did not know how to choose, and that this ignorance might be the most expensive thing I owned.

* * *

You should understand what the ledger actually was, physically, because its physicality mattered that night more than its meaning. It was a slim book, quarter-bound in calf, water-stained along the bottom edge from some long-ago flood. Master Elandor had written it in his iron-gall mix—the old recipe, vinegar and gall apples and rainwater, the ink that bites into paper like it intends to stay. His hand was distinctive the way a face is distinctive: you could know him by his descenders alone, the way the tails of his g's hooked left like little scythes.

Forty-one entries. Not just names—dates of arrival, initials of the sheltered, symbols keyed to the charts: an anchor for the warehouse families, a crescent for the cistern houses, a star for the ones who'd moved north through the hills. He had built a grammar of mercy, and he had died for it, and I had fished it out of his locked drawer like a thief and now it sat on my floor radiating consequence.

The transit papers, by contrast, were nothing yet. They were blank stock, a genuine seal impression lifted from the atlas commission documents, and the plan—which lived entirely in my head, because plans written down have a way of becoming evidence—of a harbor laborer named Teo Sandrin, conscripted for transport work, cleared for movement to the north quays. Marisol would be Teo. Marisol, who was five foot four and had never in her life looked like anyone called Teo, would need papers so clean that the sergeant's eye would slide off them before suspicion could find purchase. Clean papers take time. Time took hours. Hours were the one commodity the tide refused to extend credit on.

I picked up the pen. I put it down. I picked it up again.

This is what no one tells you about treason: it is mostly sitting still, failing to begin.

* * *

Somewhere in the fourth hour of the night—I was keeping time by the workshop's clock, which had outlived its maker and ticked on with the indifferent persistence of all machinery—I heard footsteps in the lane. Patrol boots, unhurried, two pairs. They passed. They came back. One of them tried the door.

The bolt held. I stood motionless in the dark with a blank sheet of paper in one hand and a book full of death warrants in the other, and listened to a soldier mutter something to his companion, and heard them move on. Drunk, probably. Looking for a door that wasn't bolted, for reasons that had nothing to do with sedition.

My heart took several minutes to accept that we had survived. During those minutes I noticed something about myself that I record here only because it frightened me more than the boots had: I had felt, for one clear instant, the thought *if they break in, I can only carry one.* And the instant after that, before any deliberation, before any argument—my hands had already turned toward the transit stock. My hands, without consulting me, had chosen Marisol.

I want to be honest about what followed, because this chapter of my life deserves a truthful clerk even if I was a false one. I did not feel noble. I felt caught. Choosing someone you love should feel like a sunrise; choosing her while thirty-nine other names watched me from the floor felt like closing a door on forty voices mid-sentence.

But then I sat back down among the names, and I made myself look at them properly, entry by entry, the way I'd been taught to verify a source. And that was when the arithmetic began to change.

* * *

The Corvat family, anchor symbol, sheltered six. Old Mireia Corvat could barely walk; I had carried her market basket up Harbor Lane the previous autumn and she had paid me in figs and gossip. If the ledger surfaced, she would not be questioned. She would be *processed*. Ferran's word. Removal column. The word had a bureaucratic tidiness that let you forget it meant putting an eighty-year-old woman on a ship bound for a place where the paperwork about her would be the last document anyone ever consulted.

The Danez brothers, crescent, sheltered two. Fishermen. Their boat had already been requisitioned; their names were already in the civic ledger under *labor, maritime*. They were legible, catchable, sorted. All that stood between them and the removal list was a page in my master's handwriting.

And so on down the list. I knew most of these people. That was the trap of a town like Vessamare—you cannot sort three thousand neighbors into columns without discovering that you know them, that they fed you as a child, that one of them taught you to swim, that another once beat you at cards so thoroughly you went home and practiced for a month.

And yet.

Here is the counterargument, and I gave it its full hearing, because a man who doesn't hear the argument against his own mercy is just a man rehearsing. If Marisol sailed without papers—if I abandoned the forgery to spend my remaining hours hiding or copying or burying the ledger—she would be taken. Her father was already branded a traitor and deported; her mother had been taken for questioning and not returned. Marisol's name existed in enough official memory that no amount of hidden heroism elsewhere would save her. The ledger saved thirty-nine families who might otherwise be found. The papers saved one family that was certainly going to be found, whose last living member was currently sleeping in a collapsed cistern beneath the old surveyor's house, trusting me absolutely, which—as I have noted before and will note again until it stops being true—is its own kind of weight.

One certain life against thirty-nine possible ones. Any tallyman could work that sum in his head. Ferran would have had it done before I finished stating the problem, and he would have arrived at the answer without sentiment, and he would have been correct by every measure except the ones that make a person worth being.

So why did my hands keep turning toward Marisol?

Because—and I understood this only in the small hours, holding the pen over blank paper like a man holding a knife over nothing—because the choice as I had framed it was a lie. A forgery's lie, and I should know; I make them professionally.

* * *

The lie was this: that the ledger had to be saved whole or lost whole. That it was a single object with a single fate, like a plate in an atlas, like a coin.

But I had already dissolved one page in salt water. I knew the ledger could be divided. And a man who can divide a thing can redistribute it.

I spread the entries across the floor again, but differently this time—not as a list but as a map, because maps are how I think and I had been trying to think like a clerk and it had nearly broken me. Seen spatially, the problem rearranged itself. The names clustered by symbol: anchor families near the warehouse, crescent families near the cisterns, stars scattered north. The soldiers would search where the evidence pointed, and the evidence pointed wherever the ledger was found. If the ledger was found in my workshop, every name in it became a search warrant for every street it touched.

But if the ledger ceased to exist as a ledger—if it became forty separate scraps, dispersed, memorized, sewn into different bindings in different places—then finding any piece revealed only that piece. The grammar would survive only in heads. Mine. Marisol's. Eventually others'.

Paper can be seized. Recitation cannot. I didn't know yet that I believed that sentence; I would believe it later, in a northern room, reading names aloud by candlelight. That night it was only a hypothesis with a corpse's authority behind it.

Still, division took time too. Copying forty entries accurately, in conditions where a single dropped scrap meant a patrol finding a fragment—hours again. There was no version of the night that gave me enough of anything. That is what a deadline is: not a wall you hit but a set of scales that keeps tipping no matter how carefully you load it.

I did the only sensible thing left. I stopped trying to solve it and started working, because work clarifies what thought muddles, and my hands had already voted anyway.

* * *

I copied. By feel, mostly, eyes half-closed to keep them dark-adapted, the lamp hooded down to a bead of light no watcher in the lane could glimpse. Master Elandor's shorthand was compact—one line per family, dates compressed, symbols standing in for words—and I copied his symbols exactly, because if I ever needed to pass the information on, the key lived in Marisol's father's charts, which lived folded against Marisol's ribs in the cistern below the surveyor's house. Inheritance flowing downhill through hidden channels, master to apprentice, cartographer to daughter, dead man's grammar to living mouths.

Two hours of copying produced eight entries, four scraps, folded to the size of a thumbnail and distributed: two into the false binding of a hymnal, one into the hollow heel of my spare boot, one sewn flat between the atlas's harbor chart and its backing cloth—where, I noted with the grim professional satisfaction available only to forgers and hypocrites, it would sit directly beneath the governor's own commissioned portrait of his new harbor. Let him rest his fingers on it at the presentation ceremony. Let him admire the stitching.

Thirty-one entries remained uncopied when the clock told me the truth I had been refusing to hear: the time for copying was over. The time for papers had begun, and it had begun late.

I gathered the scraps, tucked the master's ledger itself into the space behind the workshop's loose hearthstone—found, not solved, deferred—and turned to the blank stock and the seal impression and the persona of Teo Sandrin, harbor laborer, who had been born in my imagination roughly six hours ago and already had less time to live than most mayflies.

And that was when I admitted the real shape of the choice, the one underneath the arithmetic.

It was never ledger versus papers. It was this: I could spend my remaining skill making Marisol safe, or I could spend it making the truth durable. Loyalty and honesty, wearing costumes, asking for the same coin. Every stroke of the pen on Teo Sandrin's papers was a stroke not spent preserving what Master Elandor died to write down. Every entry copied was an hour subtracted from the life of the woman sleeping in a cistern who trusted me absolutely.

The theme of my life, crystallized at last, and it fit on the head of a pin: you cannot chart every coastline. You choose your scale, and whatever falls outside it simply does not exist—not out of malice, but because paper is finite and the world is not.

I chose my scale. I am not proud of how easily my hand chose it, and I am not ashamed either, and both of those facts surprised me more than the boots at the door.

* * *

The forgery went badly at first and then, as forgeries do, went suddenly well—the way a current takes a hull. Teo Sandrin acquired a birth date, a conscription stamp, a movement authorization for the north quays effective with the *Cormorant*'s sailing, and a set of small deliberate imperfections—a coffee stain aged with tea, a corner softened by handling—because perfect papers are the loudest papers at any checkpoint. I worked the seal impression warm so the wax would take the governor's crest cleanly, breathed on it like a blessing, and set it aside to cool with a tenderness I usually reserve for living things.

Then I sat back and audited the night, the way you audit any ledger, and the audit came back with a figure I had not planned for and could not pay.

Ferran had moved the sailing. Ferran suspected my ink. Ferran had done all of it quietly, professionally, without accusation—which I had told myself was mercy, or caution, or mere thoroughness, whichever flattering word fit best. But sitting there in the ash-smelling dark with a finished forgery drying beside me and a hidden ledger bricked behind a hearthstone, I saw the shape of the trap with sudden terrible clarity: he hadn't set a trap for the papers. Papers are evidence; evidence can be argued with. He had set a trap for a *choice*. He had shortened the clock to see what I would reach for first, and I had reached, exactly as predicted, and the reaching itself was the confession.

He didn't need to catch the ink. He only needed to watch which thing I saved. A man who saves a girl is a fool in love. A man who saves a ledger is a conspirator. Either way, by dawn he would know which he was dealing with—and I had less than one tide to decide what to do about a man whose private arithmetic I had never once been able to check.

Unless I changed the sum myself.

I looked at the forgery. I looked at the hearthstone. I thought about the tallyman who had sorted three thousand people and remembered none of their faces—except, he had let slip once, over a shared lamp, an apprentice's hands.

There was one move left on the board, and it was the maddest one, which by that hour of the night recommended it strongly. It would require me to do the thing I was worst at: tell the truth. Most of it. To the last person alive who could use it to kill everyone I was trying to save.

The clock ticked. The east window showed the first gray suggestion that the night intended to end whether I was ready or not.

I put Teo Sandrin's papers inside my coat, over my heart, where contraband travels best, and I went to find Ferran.
