## 29. Confession to a Stranger

I have heard it said that a man about to drown reviews his whole life in an instant. I can report that this is false, or at least that it was false for me. What I reviewed, sitting in the workshop with the lamp turned low and Ferran's footsteps somewhere on the stairs, was not my life but my inventory: one atlas, nearly finished; one hidden ledger, one page of which was already dissolved into the harbor and one page of which now slept sewn into the binding of the atlas itself; one forged set of transit papers; one girl in a collapsed cistern whose location I would carry out of this room or carry nowhere.

And one tallyman coming up those stairs who had, I was increasingly certain, already counted every item on that list except the girl.

I could run. Let us be honest with ourselves, since I am apparently being honest with everyone else tonight: I considered it. The window at the back of the workshop opens onto the tanner's yard, and the tanner's yard opens onto Cutpurse Lane, and Cutpurse Lane opens, at this hour, onto nothing much at all. But Ferran had come alone, without soldiers, without lantern-men, without the theatrical apparatus of arrest. A man does not climb a dark staircase by himself to catch a criminal. He climbs it by himself to buy something, or to sell something, and I had very little left worth buying except the truth.

So when he knocked—knocked, as if I were a shopkeeper and he a customer—I opened the door before he could knock twice, because a man who hesitates at his own door has already told you everything.

"Apprentice Ishikawa," Ferran said. He held no papers. His hands were empty, which frightened me more than irons would have. "You're awake."

"I work at night."

"So I've observed." He stepped past me into the workshop the way a man steps into a church he doesn't believe in—politely, cataloguing everything. His eyes went to the drafting table, to the atlas under its cloth, to the ink pots arranged in their row like soldiers at inspection. They did not linger anywhere. That was his craft, of course. The clerk's art and the tallyman's art are both arts of looking, and Ferran looked the way my master used to draw coastlines: quickly, once, correctly.

"You've come about the ink," I said.

He turned. For the first time since I'd known him—and I had known him for weeks, in the way one knows a blade that hangs on a wall, admiring it and hoping never to need it—for the first time his face did something other than calculate. It listened.

"Yes," he said. "The ink."

* * *

Ferran:

Let the record show—I keep a record even of this—that I did not expect him to confess. In eleven years of sorting the empire's population into its useful columns, I have interviewed perhaps four hundred men caught in falsification, and of those four hundred, the number who confessed before the third question was small enough to count without removing my boots. People lie the way water runs downhill: not from wickedness but from the path of least resistance. I had built my career on the assumption that everyone lies, and I had been promoted twice on the accuracy of that assumption.

But I had also, in recent weeks, begun keeping a second kind of ledger, unofficial, uncolumned, and in that ledger the entries were stranger. The apprentice's hands, for instance. Steady hands. Hands that copied three hundred names without a tremor and then, on the three hundred and first—a boy of nine, bound for the removal column with his mother—hesitated over the descender of a single letter. Not enough to prove anything. Enough to remember.

And there was the matter of the iron-gall. The old master's ink browned with age in a particular way, and the pages of the civic ledger had begun browning in places where they had no business browning, in columns where the entries were dated too recently to have earned that color honestly. Someone was writing the past. I had seen it done before, in the northern provinces, by a woman who moved two hundred names across three counties before anyone thought to hold her paper to a lamp. They hanged her eventually, but she died knowing arithmetic, and I have always suspected she considered the trade fair.

I climbed the stairs to the mapmaker's workshop alone because I wanted to know which kind of man I was dealing with before I decided what to do about him, and a man accompanied by soldiers tells you only what soldiers permit. Also—and this entry goes in the unofficial ledger, in pencil, lightly—I climbed them alone because I was tired. Three thousand names. I remembered none of their faces. I was tired in a way that sleep does not address.

He opened the door before I finished knocking. That was my first surprise of the night. It would not be the last.

* * *

Isandro:

"The ink," I said again, and then, because the moment had arrived and I had spent three nights rehearsing it and rehearsals are worthless, I did the thing outright. I took the civic ledger from its shelf, carried it to the table, and set it down in front of him. Then I fetched the atlas and laid it beside the ledger. Two books. One hand each, if you knew where to look.

"Sit," I said. "This will take some time, and I'd rather not do it standing."

Ferran sat. He sat the way he did everything, economically, folding himself onto the workshop stool as though stools had been invented for his convenience. I poured wine—not the good wine, which was gone, but the wine that remained—and he watched me pour without touching his cup, and I understood that he would not drink until he knew what I was doing, because a man who accepts a host's cup has accepted a host's terms, and Ferran accepted nobody's terms.

"My master kept two ledgers," I said.

"I know."

Of course he knew. I should have begun there. "Then you know what's in the second one."

"I know what was reported to be in it. Reports are not knowledge." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, aligning it with nothing. "Reports say your master kept a list of families who sheltered the refugees from the eastern coast. Reports say that list is why he died. Reports do not say where the list is, whether it still exists, or how many names are on it. Those are the questions I climbed your stairs to ask."

"And if I don't answer?"

"Then tomorrow I answer them myself, with men who ask differently than I do." He said it without heat. That was the terrible thing about Ferran; he never needed heat. "I'd prefer your answers. Mine cost more."

I looked at him for a long moment. The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere below, the harbor muttered to itself—the sound Vessamare makes in its sleep, ropes against masts, water against stone—and I thought: this is the town that taught me to be small. This is the town where I learned that if you stand still and lower your eyes, the world will pass over you like weather. And the world had passed over me for twenty-two years, and here was the end of all that quiet, sitting on a stool, waiting to be paid.

So I paid him.

"There were forty-one families on the list," I said. "Forty-one households that sheltered refugees after the eastern edict. My master wrote them down in order of danger—most endangered first—so that if the list ever needed to be destroyed, it could be destroyed in pieces, worst losses first." I heard my own voice do something strange on that sentence, something steady, and realized I was proud of the dead man's design even while betraying it. Grief is an apprentice too. It learns its master's habits. "One page is gone. Dissolved. Seawater, the night the garrison arrived. Seven names. The Corvane family among them."

"Seven saved."

"Seven saved. Thirty-four still written." I put my hand flat on the atlas. "And thirty-four people in this town who will be on a removal column within the month, because every one of those families appears in the civic record under categories your office created. Labor. Transport. Removal." I made myself say the word plainly. Removal. As though people were gravel. "I didn't dissolve that page to protect myself. I dissolved it because it was the page I could reach."

Ferran's face did not change, which I had expected, and his hands did not move, which I had not. Men's hands confess before their faces do; I had learned that watching him copy lists. But his hands stayed flat and still as drafting weights, and I understood suddenly that I had guessed wrong about something fundamental. He had not come to confirm a suspicion. He had come hoping to be wrong.

"Why tell me this?" he said quietly.

"Because you already know about the ink. Because you've known for days—you held pages to the lamp in front of me, you asked me about iron-gall over that shared lamp like a man discussing weather, and a man discussing weather wants company, not information. You'd have reported me by now if reporting were what you wanted." I turned the atlas toward him, to the harbor chart, and did not open it further. Some doors I would keep shut even in confession. "And because I have one night left, and I would rather spend it being exactly what I am than pretending to be what you'd find convenient."

* * *

Ferran:

He offered me the truth the way a debtor offers coin—counted out, deliberately, watching my face for the exchange rate. And it was good coin. That was the difficulty. Every detail matched my own arithmetic: the forty-one families fit the gaps in the census, the seven dissolved names fit the damp-stained absence in the records I had audited, the master's death fit the timeline of the requisition orders. He was not lying to me. After eleven years I know the texture of a lie the way a blind man knows a doorway, and Isandro Ishikawa was handing me solid fact with both hands, and asking—in words so plain I nearly missed them—for me to decide what facts were worth.

"You understand," I said, "that confession is not absolution. You've told me what you did. You haven't told me why I shouldn't act on it."

"No," he agreed. "That part isn't mine to give."

"Then what is?"

He looked at me then with an expression I have been turning over since, the way one turns a stone found on a beach, and he said: "Your own arithmetic. You've sorted three thousand people, Tallyman. You've put numbers next to names until the names stopped meaning anything—I've watched you do it, and I've done it myself, and I'm telling you it stops meaning anything, and that is precisely how it becomes easy. So don't take my word for any of this. Take yours. One family per name. One kitchen, one bed, one child learning letters at that table. You're good at counting. Count them."

And I want it recorded that I did not look away, and that this cost me more than anything else in that room.

Because I have counted. That is the obscene secret of my profession, the one we do not write in ledgers: we all count, at the beginning. The tallyman who trained me wept at his first removal list, and by his tenth year he ate his lunch while reading them aloud. The numbers do not harden us. We harden ourselves against the numbers, deliberately, methodically, because feeling them would make the work impossible and the work must be done—or so we tell ourselves, in the voice of every man who has ever found his duty coincidentally aligned with his convenience.

Three thousand names. I remember none of their faces. I remember the apprentice's hands.

"What do you want?" I asked him. It was, I think, the first honest question I had asked in the empire's service in six years.

"To finish the atlas," he said. "To deliver it to the governor, as commissioned. To account for every name on that hidden list—by memory if the paper burns, by paper if it survives." A pause, small as a hairline crack in porcelain. "And to see that a certain transit document, of which you may be aware, reaches the morning ship."

There it was. He had given me everything except the one thing, and now he named the thing without naming it, testing whether I would pretend not to hear.

"The transit papers," I said, "bear an ink that matches this workshop. The seal impression bears an impression that matches the governor's commission materials, which passed through these hands. The dating bears a hand that matches the ledger entries I have been auditing for nine days." I let him see that I had counted. "Shall I go on? I can. I'm very thorough. It's my only virtue."

"Then you know I forged them."

"I know you forged them beautifully. That's worse."

* * *

Isandro:

Here is what I had bet everything on, laid out plainly so that whoever reads this history—if anyone reads it, if it survives the tide and the war and my own cowardices—can judge whether I was a fool or merely desperate, which in my experience amount to the same thing viewed from different distances.

I had bet that Ferran was a man who had done his sums and found himself owing. Not to me. To the three thousand. That somewhere beneath the tidy columns and the professional calm there was a private ledger with a deficit in it, compounding for years, and that my confession was not a plea but a payment plan—an offer to let him settle his account through mine. You cannot ask a man like Ferran for mercy. Mercy insults him; mercy implies he might be small. You can only ask him for consistency. Show him the numbers. Let him balance them.

"You could arrest me," I said. "You could burn the atlas and the list and march me to the customhouse, and by noon you'd have a clean report and I'd have a short drop. And the thirty-four families would still exist, Ferran. Arrest changes their column from *hidden* to *found*. That's all it does. That's all it has ever done." I turned the wine cup, finally untouched, in a slow circle, aligning it with nothing, his own gesture returned to him like a coin he'd dropped. "Or you could walk down those stairs and log the hidden ledger as lost to damp. Damaged beyond recovery. An administrative note, half a line, filed and forgotten. And tomorrow morning the requisition ship sails, and one more person leaves Vessamare who the columns say should not, and thirty-three households stay unexamined, and your arithmetic comes out—" I stopped. I had prepared a number and found I couldn't say it. Saying it would make it a bargain, and this was not a bargain. It was a wager on a soul.

Ferran was silent for so long that the lamp burned down a full finger's width.

"It doesn't come out," he said at last. "Whatever you think my arithmetic says, it doesn't come out. If I spare you, I spend thirty-four families against my own comfort, and I am not entitled to make that trade with other people's lives. If I report you, I spend you and the girl and the truth itself against my career, which is worth less than any of them, and I know it's worth less, and I would do it anyway, because that is what the empire pays me for—to do the thing I know is wrong because the column says so." He stood. He straightened his coat with two precise tugs, a man reassembling himself. "You've made your confession, apprentice. You've told me everything except where she is."

It was not a question. I gave it silence, and silence was answer enough.

"Good," he said softly. "Keep it that way. There are things I don't want to know; a tallyman's ignorance is the last honest currency this occupation has left." He walked to the door, stopped with his hand on the latch, and stood with his back to me. I watched his shoulders rise and fall once, the way a man breathes before lifting something too heavy, and I understood that whatever he carried down those stairs, he was carrying it up first, now, in the space between one breath and the next.

"The ink evidence," he said, without turning. "Where is it?"

"The trial pages. My practice sheets. The brush rag. The—"

"In your stove?"

"Under the floorboard by the wash basin. Wrapped in oilcloth."

"Get them."

* * *

Ferran:

He fetched them without argument, which told me he had already decided to trust me, which was either the wisest thing he had done all year or the most fatal, and I confess I did not yet know which, and neither, I suspect, did he. A bundle of oilcloth, tied with twine. Inside: eleven trial sheets, a rag stiff with dried ink, a broken nib. Everything I would need, laid before the empire's officer, wrapped by the empire's forger, handed over like bread from one hungry man to another.

I weighed it in my hand. Paper is light. It has always astonished me, the disproportion between the weight of paper and the weight of what paper decides.

"Tomorrow," I said, "the requisition manifests for the harbor warehouse will require revision. Damp damage. Extensive. Unrecoverable documents, logged and disposed." I looked at him at last, and I made sure he saw my face do what it did, because he deserved to see it: not triumph, not mercy, just a tired man closing an account at a loss he intended to keep private. "You have until dawn, Ishikawa. The tide will not wait for your courage and neither will I. If you are on that ship, I never saw you. If you are not—" I handed him back his evidence, all of it, the whole light unbearable weight of it. "Then none of this happened, and I will deny you to my grave, and you will owe me nothing, because debts between strangers don't count."

"And if someone else checks the ink?"

"Someone else would have checked it already." I opened the door. The stairwell breathed cold salt air up at us; the harbor was muttering; somewhere east the sky was thinking about gray. "One more thing, apprentice. The governor reads his atlases. All of them. Cover to cover. He was a surveyor before he was a soldier, and he notices everything—which means whatever you've bound inside that harbor chart, you had better pray he is the man I have spent six years believing he isn't."

I went down the stairs. I did not look back, because looking back is how men get caught, and because I had, at long last, counted.

Dawn was four hours off.
