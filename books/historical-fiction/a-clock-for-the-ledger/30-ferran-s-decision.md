## 30. Ferran's Decision

I have burned papers before. It is a professional skill, like arithmetic or forgetting. You learn early in the tallying trade that a fire is just a column with no names in it—the cleanest ledger there is. So when I stood in the workshop that night with the apprentice's forged transit sheets in one hand and the lamp in the other, I told myself this was nothing new.

The lie did not even survive the lighting of the wick.

Let me be precise, because precision is all I have ever had. My name is Ferran. For eleven years I have counted people for the Crown—sorted them into labor, transport, removal—and I have done it well, which is a sentence that should end careers and mostly ends dinners. Three thousand souls, give or take, passed through my columns. I could not tell you a single face. That was always the trick of it: you survive this work by making the numbers do the feeling for you. A number does not look at you. A number does not have a daughter in the salt cellar.

Except one number kept looking at me, and it had ink on its hands.

* * *

I should explain how I came to be standing there, because a man who decides badly ought at least to decide honestly.

Three days earlier I had sat across from Isandro Ishikawa over a shared lamp, in his dead master's workshop, and shown him something I probably should not have. I dipped a pen in two inks—the old master's iron-gall, gone brown at the edges like an old bruise, and the boy's newer mix, blue-black and stubborn—and I drew two lines on scrap paper and let him watch them age. Shop talk. Chess by candlelight. *I know your hand is not only your own,* I said, without saying it. He understood. He was quick; he had been quick all along, which was the problem. The best forgers are never the ones with steady hands. They are the ones who understand what a document *wants to be*, and this boy understood documents the way priests understand scripture—too well, and too seriously.

He lied to me afterward, of course. Badly, then well, which is the usual progression. And I let him lie, and I went home, and I laid the evidence out on my table the way I lay out everything: in columns.

Column one: the apprentice had forged transit papers using the governor's own seal impression, lifted—cleverly—from the atlas commission itself. Column two: the ink matched the workshop. Column three: somewhere in Vessamare there existed a hidden ledger in the dead master's hand, a list of every family that had sheltered the refugees we now called enemies, and if that list reached my superiors, the town would not lose forty families to the removal ship. It would lose the town. I had seen what happened at Brasse when a similar list surfaced. Brasse has a fine new garrison now and no fishermen.

Column four—the column I kept trying not to write—I did not want to add it up.

That is the honest arithmetic. Everything after that was me looking for an error in my own sums.

* * *

I found him at the workshop near midnight, because I knew he would be there. Men like Isandro return to the scene of their crime the way penitents return to churches—not to confess but to sit near what they have done and see if it still fits inside them.

He opened the door before I knocked the second time. He had been expecting arrest for days; it was written in the way he held his shoulders, coiled and courteous, a man rehearsing his own trial.

"Tallyman," he said. "It's late."

"It's exactly late enough," I said. "May I come in and ruin your evening properly?"

I will grant him this: he did not grovel. He stepped aside, and I walked past the drafting table where the atlas lay open—the governor's atlas, the great gift, the beautiful alibi—and I set my satchel down on his master's desk with the deliberation of a man unpacking evidence, which I was.

"You know why I'm here."

"I can guess," he said. "You're here about ink."

"Ink," I agreed. "Ink is the whole world, apprentice. Names are just ink that learned to walk." I took out the forged transit sheets—I had lifted two of them during the commission presentation, palmed like a card-sharp, another professional skill—and I laid them side by side under his lamp. "Two passages. One for a girl traveling as a cartographer's assistant. One for her escort. Sealed with the governor's own impress. Do you know what happens to me when the governor learns his seal has been whoring around town signing tickets for removal-list cargo?"

"I imagine you'd be removed yourself," Isandro said quietly.

"Filed under transport," I said, "with a note." And then, because I am apparently incapable of conducting even a blackmail without teaching, I added: "Your spacing gives you away. You leave too much room between the seal and the signature. Honest clerks crowd the seal. Only forgers respect it."

He looked at the papers for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face was not fear—or not only fear—but a kind of terrible readiness, the look of a man who has already decided how much he will pay and is only waiting to hear the price.

"Then report me," he said. "Before the tide. If you report me now, they'll search the warehouse district tonight, and—" He stopped himself. Too late. The word *she* had almost made it out of his mouth wearing its address.

"There she is," I said softly. "I wondered when you'd show me where you keep her."

And he shut it, that door in his face, fast as a clerk closing a ledger at the end of a bad quarter. "There's no girl," he said. "There's no anything. There's an atlas I owe the governor and a workshop I owe nobody, and you, tallyman, standing in both."

It was a good lie. It was nearly a good enough lie. But I had spent three thousand faces learning to find the one that didn't match its column, and his face was doing arithmetic behind his eyes—he was calculating whether he could reach the letter opener, whether I was alone, whether drowning me in the harbor would buy her the tide.

"Don't," I said. "Not because I'd win. Because I'd log it, and I log everything."

Something in him broke, very slightly, very quietly, the way ice breaks in spring—one crack, muffled, the whole sheet weakened. He sat down on his master's stool and put his hands flat on the desk, ink-stained fingers spread like a man surrendering the only weapons he had ever owned.

"What do you want, Ferran?"

And there it was. The question I had rowed across the dark harbor to be asked.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about sorting people for a living: the work gets into your hands before it gets into your conscience. For years my hands stayed dry and my sleep stayed easy, because a column is a kind of anesthesia—you move ten thousand names and feel none of them, the way a surgeon's knife feels nothing it cuts. I told myself I was a scale, not a judge. Weigh, record, release. The Crown decides; I merely count.

But scales are supposed to balance, and mine had stopped balancing years ago, and I had kept filing the discrepancy under *not my department*.

Now a nineteen-year-old mapmaker was sitting in front of me asking what I wanted, and the honest answer—the answer I had carried across the harbor like contraband—was: *I want to stop being accurate.*

"The hidden ledger," I said. "Your master's second book. Where is it?"

Silence. Long enough that the lamp fluttered.

"You should understand," I went on, conversationally, because cruelty and kindness use the same voice at this hour, "that if I report its existence—even its suspected existence—they will send more than a tallyman. They will send the men from Brasse. And they will not read your ledger, apprentice. They will read the town. Every name in that book is a house, and every house is a confession, and they will take the confession out of Vessamare one family at a time until the book balances. That is not a threat. That is a procedure. I have watched it run."

"And if I give it to you," Isandro said, "you'll burn it."

"If you give it to you, *he'll* burn it," I corrected. "You've already started, haven't you? A page dissolved in saltwater. The Ossoline family. I logged the damp damage myself last week and wondered why the damp was so precisely shaped like a page."

He went very still. I watched him decide whether to be astonished, terrified, or furious, and watched him arrive at some port where all three shared a harbor.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the saltwater," I said. "Since the ink inconsistencies. Since your hands, frankly. I have sorted three thousand people, Ishikawa, and I remember none of their faces. Yours I cannot stop remembering, and it is entirely your hands' fault. They are honest hands. They were trained by an honest man. And honest hands make the worst liars in the world because they hate the work, and the hatred shows, and I—a professional hater of my own work—recognized the expression."

"Why come to me, then? Why not just burn the workshop and file the ashes?"

"Because," I said, and here I had to pause, because the true thing was larger than my mouth was built for, "because I have decided, late and badly and against every instinct of self-preservation, to make an error. One error. In three thousand entries. And errors require accomplices, or they get corrected."

* * *

We worked until the small hours, and I will record what we did, because records are all I am good for and this one I intend to keep somewhere, even if only in the burning.

He brought the hidden ledger up from wherever he had sewn it—into the atlas binding, as it turned out, inside the harbor chart, which was audacious enough that I laughed aloud, the first laugh I had produced in that town in eleven years, and it startled us both. But the loose pages, the working copies, the drafts and duplicates a careful man keeps—those he surrendered to my lamp.

I burned them one at a time. Not in haste. Haste looks like guilt, and I wanted this to look like housekeeping. Each page curled and blackened and gave up its families to the dark, and I read each name as it went—Ossoline, harbormaster's widow, the Vay brothers, the woman who kept the lighthouse and had hidden six children in the lamp room—that was my ritual, my private liturgy: *I will not count you. I un-count you. Go.* It is the only blessing I know, and I invented it that night, at fifty-one, standing in a dead cartographer's workshop watching thirty years of careful goodness go up in a stove.

Isandro watched every page. He thought I couldn't see him watching, cataloguing, memorizing—which was correct, and necessary, because paper burns but recitation doesn't, and the boy understood archives better than any clerk I have ever served beside. Let him carry the list in his skull northward. Skulls clear no checkpoints. Skulls are the one ledger no requisition reaches.

When it was done, I opened my official field book—the one with the Crown's stamp in the cover, the book that outranks every other object in Vessamare including the governor's patience—and I wrote, in my own impeccable, unforgivable hand:

*Unregistered documents recovered from workshop of deceased subject M. Ishikawa. Water damage extensive. Contents illegible. Remnants destroyed per damp protocol. No further action warranted.*

*Destroyed per damp protocol.* Four words. I have sent men to the removal ships with fewer.

"Do you know what you've just done?" Isandro said. His voice had a strange grain in it, the sound of a man watching someone else commit treason on his behalf.

"I have completed a report," I said. "It is accurate. The documents were indeed extensively damaged by water. I applied additional water. From a flask. The protocol is satisfied." I closed the field book. "This is how corruption actually works, apprentice—not as fire and drama, but as a footnote filed correctly. The Empire is not defeated by heroes. It is defeated by clerks who misplace things on purpose."

"And you?" he said. "What happens to you?"

"Nothing, if we are both perfect. Something, if either of us blinks. Which brings me to the arithmetic." I stood, and put on my coat, and became once again the tallyman, because the tallyman was the only armor I owned. "The ship sails at morning tide. That is what everyone believes, because that is what the manifest says. At dawn I am announcing a change of schedule—engine trouble, insurance politics, whatever fiction the harbor office prefers. New departure: this tide. Within the hour. I will announce it loudly, to the wrong people, slowly."

He stared at me. "You're giving us the tide."

"I am giving you a scheduling error," I said. "Errors happen. I have decided to be capable of one." I gathered my satchel. My hands, I noted with distant interest, were shaking—the hands of a man who had just un-weighed three thousand plus one, and found the difference unbearable. "Listen to me, because I will not say this twice and I will deny saying it once. You have until dawn. At dawn I close the net I have spent three weeks weaving, because if I don't close it, someone else will inspect it, and they will find your footprints everywhere and mine on top. So: the quay, before first light. Papers ready. Luggage minimal. And boy—if anyone stops you, do not lie cleverly. Lie boringly. Clever lies invite questions; boring lies invite yawns. It is the only professional advice I will ever give you free of charge."

At the door he stopped me. Of course he did. The good ones always need to ask.

"Why?" he said. "Truly. Three thousand people, Ferran. You sorted them without blinking. Why does number three thousand and one change anything?"

I considered lying to him—boringly, as instructed—but the hour deserved better.

"Because I remembered their faces eventually," I said. "All of them. Late, in the dark, one per night, like a debt collector visiting. It took me eleven years and I am not finished. And then one day I realized the faces weren't haunting me. They were waiting. Queued. Patient as entries. Waiting to see what I'd do when it finally mattered." I pulled the door open onto the cold harbor smell. "Tonight, one of them got to go home. That's all. That's the whole of it. Don't thank me. Thanks are just receipts, and I'm done issuing receipts."

* * *

I walked back along the seawall the long way, past the warehouse, past the customhouse where the sergeant on duty saluted me and I saluted back and both of us believed, for one more night, in the same empire.

The sky over the water was doing that gray thing it does before dawn—not light yet, just the memory of light arriving ahead of schedule. Behind me the town slept, all its households folded into their beds like entries into a ledger, legible, catchable, saved—for tonight—by a footnote.

At dawn I would stand in the harbor office and announce the early tide in my flattest voice, and watch the gangplank rise, and log two passengers whose names I would not read. At dawn I would begin the rest of my life, which is the life you live after you have learned what you are willing to lose, and it is a smaller country than the one you lived in before, and the air in it is better.

Until then, I had one more entry to make. I took out the field book one last time, and under the damp-damage report, in the margin where margins keep the truth, I wrote a single line that no auditor would ever flag and I would never explain:

*One error, deliberate. Balance restored by 3,000 minus nothing.*

Then I closed the book, and turned toward the harbor office, and began walking fast—because somewhere behind me, in a workshop full of burning-smell and borrowed courage, an apprentice was checking the clock, and the tide, as I had promised it would, was coming early.
