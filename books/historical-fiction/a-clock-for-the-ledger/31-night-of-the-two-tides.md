## 31. Night of the Two Tides

The garrison bell rang the wrong hour twice that night, and I have never decided whether it was incompetence or Ferran's hand at work. Either way, it gave us a gift: confusion. In a town where everyone had learned to set their hearts by bells, two conflicting hours meant that no one was sure when anything was supposed to happen — including the patrols.

I had sewn the ledger page into the atlas binding three days ago, in the hour after I chose which list to save. I want to say I did it with a craftsman's calm, but the truth is my hands shook so badly the first stitch went through my thumb instead of the linen tape. The second went through the atlas's spine properly, though, and by lamplight you could not find the seam. The page lay flat against the harbor chart of Vessamare, forty families pressed invisibly against the coastline they had lived on for generations. If you wanted to hide a town's conscience, there were worse places than inside a map of the town itself.

Now the atlas was wrapped in oilcloth under my arm, the forged papers were in Marisol's coat, and the tide would turn in a little under four hours.

"Say it again," Marisol whispered.

"Customhouse first, then the north quay, then the governor's door, then the ship."

"And if the customhouse goes badly?"

"Then we improvise, and we are both terrible at improvising, so let's hope it doesn't."

That earned me half a smile in the dark, which I counted as a small victory against the end of the world.

We left the collapsed cistern through the gap behind the old surveyor's house, the one I had found only because I had once drawn its foundations from a neighbor's description and then, out of professional honesty, erased them when I couldn't verify them. The unmapped place. My master used to say a mapmaker's first duty is to know what not to put down. I had spent twenty years thinking that was a lesson about humility. It turned out to be a lesson about survival.

The streets above were wet with the night's fog, and the lamps burned low — the occupation had ordered every other lamp extinguished to save oil, which meant they had also ordered every other shadow created. We moved lamp to lamp, shadow to shadow, the way I had moved through this town since I was a child making myself small. Except now I was not making myself small. Now I was carrying something the size of forty families, and it changed how a body walks.

* * *

You learn things in a salt cellar that they do not teach in any school. You learn that fear has a taste — old brine and copper, mostly. You learn that your own heartbeat is loud enough to give you away, and that after three days of listening to it you can make it quiet by sheer stubbornness. And you learn that the person you love becomes legible to you in the dark, the way a map is legible: by touch, by habit, by the small certainties. Isandro's elbow. Isandro breathing before he lies, a little inhale like a man stepping off a ledge.

He had come for me an hour past midnight, smelling of ink and cold air, and he had said only, "Now," and I had understood that now meant everything: leave the cellar, leave the town, leave my mother's empty house, leave the life where I was the cartographer's daughter and become someone with no name anyone could find.

Except I did have a name. Two, now. The false one sewn into the transit papers, and the true one, which I intended to keep the way my father kept his chart-keys — folded small, hidden in the lining, carried anyway.

The fish market was our first gauntlet. By day it roared; by night it lay gutted and silver-smelling, the stalls shuttered, the scales hung up like sleeping birds. A single lantern burned at the far arch, and beneath it sat a sentry doing what sentries do at that hour, which is lose the war against sleep.

Isandro had taught me the route the day before, drawing it in spilled salt on the warehouse floor: *the ice gutter runs the length of the market, waist-high on the east side, dry this time of year. Walk it bent. He'll hear water if he hears anything, and nobody questions the sound of water.*

So we walked the gutter bent, and the sentry slept, and I thought: my father mapped coastlines his whole life, and in the end his daughter survived by learning where the water wasn't.

Halfway across, a cat knocked over a basket of mussels.

It is absurd, afterward, how loud small things are at night. The clatter went through the market like a musket shot, and the sentry came up out of his chair with a sound halfway between a word and a cough, and his lantern swung its light across the stalls in one long yellow blade.

Isandro's hand found my wrist and pulled, and we went down behind the ice gutter together, folding ourselves into the dark between a scale and a post. His heart was going like a bird's. So was mine. Above us the light swung back, paused, and the sentry muttered something about cats and the army and his mother, and sat down again.

We stayed folded there longer than we needed to. Not from fear. Or not only fear. There was something in the staying — his shoulder against mine in the dark of a gutted market, the whole occupied town holding its breath around us — that I wanted to keep. You carry very little when you run. I wanted one thing that weighed nothing.

"We should go," he breathed.

"In a moment."

"In a moment they'll shoot us."

"Then in a moment," I said, and stood, and pulled him up after me.

* * *

The customhouse square was worse than I remembered planning for, because memory is a mapmaker's tool and maps are always flattering. In my plan it contained one patrol, moving on a predictable loop. In reality it contained one patrol, moving on a predictable loop, plus two soldiers building a bonfire of requisitioned furniture, plus a sergeant — *the* sergeant, I realized with a drop in my stomach, the one from the harbor gate who noticed things — standing at the customhouse steps with a stack of papers, reading by firelight.

But Ferran's early-tide announcement had done one thing in our favor: the garrison believed the ship sailed at dawn, and dawn was hours off, and nobody guards a door that many hours early. The customhouse itself stood dark except for the clerk's window, where a single candle showed the night-duty man half asleep over the transit register.

We could not go around the square; the only other route to the north quay passed the governor's residence, which now had more lamps than a feast day. We had to go through.

I did what I had been trained to do, which is to say I made a map in my head and then trusted it more than my eyes. Patrol's loop: forty paces along the colonnade, ten at the fountain, turn, return. The furniture fire threw flicker across the cobbles, and flicker is a friend — nothing reads as movement in flickering light. I timed the patrol through two full loops while Marisol counted her own breathing beside me, steady as a metronome, and I thought absurdly, *she counts the way I draw coastlines,* and loved her for it.

"Now."

We crossed on the diagonal, oilcloth bundle tight, papers inside coats, walking — this was the hard part — *not running.* Running is what guilty people do. We were, by every document in Vessamare, a junior cartographer escorting a licensed draftswoman delivering a commissioned atlas to the governor at first light. People delivering atlases walk. They walk like the world owes them a desk.

The patrol passed within six feet of us at the fountain. One soldier yawned. The other complained about herring. Neither looked down, because people look at faces and at weapons, and I had spent twenty years ensuring no one ever looked at either.

Then we were across, and in the shadow of the chandlery, and Marisol laughed once — a tiny, shocked sound, quickly swallowed — and I understood I had just done the bravest thing of my life and it had felt exactly like walking.

* * *

The night-duty clerk at the customhouse window was a boy of maybe seventeen, promoted because every man older than him had been requisitioned, deported, or buried. He looked at our transit papers the way you look at a sum you've been told is correct but can't quite make come out.

"Two names," he said. "One destination. It says here you're carrying survey materials for the governor's commission." He looked up. "Where is it, then?"

I unwrapped the atlas and laid it on the sill between us, open to the harbor chart. Let him look at the thing I'd bled over. Let the beauty do its work — beauty is bribable currency everywhere, but especially at candlelight, especially at this hour, especially from a boy who probably missed his bed.

"That's the new survey," I said. "Soundings, mole depths, berth assignments. The governor wanted it bound before sailing."

The boy ran his finger along the coastline of his own town, drawn better than it had ever been drawn, and something moved behind his eyes — homesickness, maybe, arriving early, the way it does for people about to be left behind by history.

"It's good," he admitted. And then, because he was seventeen and honest: "They say the ship comes back empty. For the next removals."

I kept my hands flat and still on the counter, where he could see them. Twenty years of making people trust my hands.

"Then the harbor chart matters," I said. "Doesn't it? Somebody ought to know the water."

He stamped our papers. He did not check the second name against the removal list lying on his desk, face-down, forty families thick. I took the atlas back with both hands, feeling the ledger page press against the chart like a heartbeat against a rib, and we walked out of the customhouse into the last dark hour before the tide.

Marisol didn't speak until we reached the quay stairs. Then she said, quietly, "You lied to a child."

"I flattered a child."

"You lied to a child *well.*"

"There will be a reckoning for it," I said. "Later. With myself. I've scheduled it for somewhere with better wine."

She squeezed my arm, and we went down to the water.

* * *

The north quay at slack tide smelled of tar and rope and the particular loneliness of boats. The requisition ship rode low in the harbor, lamps lit along her rail, loading the last of her cargo by lantern — grain sacks, mostly, and crates marked with the army's stencil, and a thin line of passengers' luggage that looked, from shore, like toys abandoned mid-game.

And at the foot of the gangplank, checking papers by lantern-light, stood a figure I knew by his posture before I knew him by his coat.

Ferran.

Marisol saw him too. Her hand found mine and gripped it, and I felt everything in her — three days in a salt cellar, a mother taken for questioning, a father across the sea branded a traitor — arrive all at once in her fingers.

"He knows the ink," she whispered. "You told me. He matched it."

"He matched it days ago."

"Then why—"

"Because I don't know," I said. It was the truth and it was the whole plan: I had bet a family's worth of lives on the private arithmetic of a man who sorted strangers for a living, and I would not know until the lantern reached my hands whether the sum came out to mercy or to the firing squad.

Ferran saw us coming. Of course he did. He had probably seen us at the top of the quay stairs, and let us walk the length of the pier anyway, which was either calculation or courtesy, and with Ferran the two had long since become the same thing.

"Papers," he said.

I handed them over. He read them — actually read them, top to bottom, which the boy at the customhouse had not done — and I watched his eyes move across the forged seal impression I had made by lamplight with a warmed wax blank and a stolen matrix, and I thought: *this is the man before whom all my crimes are legible,* and I stood still and let my hands be steady, because they were the only argument I had left.

"The atlas," Ferran said.

I unwrapped it. He turned pages: coastlines, soundings, the military survey of the harbor he himself had commissioned through the governor, departure schedules included — the machine's own knowledge, bound in calfskin, about to sail out of the machine's reach. He lingered on the harbor chart of Vessamare. His finger rested for a moment on the spine, near the seam where forty families slept inside the binding, and the lantern flame leaned in the wind off the water.

Somewhere behind us, up the hill, a bell began ringing the right hour at last. Dawn was coming whether the garrison believed it or not. Down the quay, a gate slammed; voices; boots on stone. The garrison waking. Ferran heard it too — I watched his head tilt toward the sound like a man hearing his own name called from a house he no longer lived in.

He drew out his stamp.

He pressed it to our transit papers without reading another word, and held them out, and as I took them he said, in the flat tone of a tallyman closing a column:

"They're mustering the quay watch early. You have perhaps five minutes before this pier fills with soldiers who are not me." A pause. The lantern light caught something in his face I had never seen there and have never stopped seeing since. "Carry less next time."

Then he turned back to his ledger, and wrote something — I will wonder forever what — and the gangplank groaned under the first feet of morning, and Marisol's hand closed on my sleeve, and up the hill, coming down through the fog toward the water, marched the sound of the entire occupation.
