## 35. Landfall North

*Isandro*

The first thing I did when we passed beyond territorial waters—beyond the reach of any ledger, column, or tallyman's arithmetic—was vomit over the side of a fishing boat called, with the optimism common to small vessels, the *Fair Return*.

I would like to report that I was composed about it. That I gripped the rail like a man in an engraving, salt wind in my hair, gazing nobly toward freedom. I did not. I heaved until my ribs ached while a fisherman named Odo watched me with the mild contempt sailors reserve for landsmen who cannot even be seasick with dignity.

"Two hours out of port," he said, "and you've already given back everything you ate."

"Not everything," Marisol said from behind me. She had not been sick once. She sat on the netting with her knees drawn up, eating dried cod as though she were at a festival. "He's had nothing since yesterday. So technically he gave back more than he took."

"I'm glad," I said, between retches, "that one of us is keeping accounts."

The phrase came out before I could stop it, and something crossed her face—not quite a flinch, but the shadow of one—and then she laughed, and I laughed, and Odo shook his head and went forward to swear at his nets, and that was how we left Vessamare behind: with a joke about ledgers that neither of us found entirely funny.

We had been running since before dawn. The checkpoint at the customhouse. The gangplank rising. Ferran at the quay with his stamp and those four words—"Carry less next time"—which I have turned over so many times since that they have worn smooth, like a coin handled too often. Then the *Fair Return*, and the harbor falling away, and the lighthouse, and the hill above it where the workshop stood with its windows dark, and after that the town itself folding into the morning haze like something being put away carefully by hands I couldn't see.

I kept expecting to feel triumphant.

What I felt instead, four hours out, with my stomach finally settled and the sun high enough to show us nothing but water in every direction, was a strange hollowness, as though I had been carrying something heavy for so long that my body had forgotten how to stand upright without it. Fear, it turns out, is load-bearing. Remove it suddenly and you find out what else was holding up the roof.

Marisol noticed before I did. She always does. She looked up from the cod, studied me the way she used to study her father's charts—tilted head, narrowed eyes, reading coastlines invisible to everyone else—and said: "You're going to do inventory, aren't you?"

"Do what?"

"You have the face. The workshop face. You're about to count things."

She was right, of course. She usually is. It is one of the many things I love about her and one of the many things that terrifies me.

So we did it. There on the deck of the *Fair Return*, in the flat northern light, with Odo pretending not to listen while he mended nets ten feet away, we took stock of everything we owned in the world.

One atlas. Bound in calfskin, its spine resewn twice—once by Master Corvane, once by me, in the last desperate week, with a page hidden in the harbor chart binding that no governor will ever know is there unless he takes a knife to his gift, which I pray he does not, because the names of nine families are sewn into that seam and they are worth more than every chart in the book.

One transit paper each, stamped and already worthless. We agreed to burn them rather than let them drift into someone else's records. Even paper can betray you if you leave it lying about; this is the first lesson of my profession and the last lesson of my old life.

My father's knife—no. Not mine. Master Corvane's knife, which is nearly the same thing now, though I keep reaching for it with the wrong hand's memory. A stub of pencil. The clothes we stood in, which smell of salt cellar and bilge and no longer bear close inspection. Marisol's mother's ring, which she wore on a cord around her neck and did not mention and which I did not ask about, because some inventories are private.

And one name each. Mine in the civic ledger, officially entered as *removed, destination unrecorded*, which is the kindest lie the occupation ever told about anyone. Hers nowhere at all, erased so thoroughly that for official purposes Marisol Arriaga has never existed—which means, as she pointed out with a smile that didn't reach her eyes, that she is now the freest person either of us knows, and also the easiest to lose.

That was everything. One atlas, two names, a knife, a ring, a pencil, and the clothes on our backs. When I said the total aloud, Odo snorted from his nets.

"That's more than most bring out," he said. "Most bring out nothing but their skins. Some don't bring out those whole." He threaded his needle without looking up. "You want to know what I brought out, twenty years ago? A spoon. Still got it. Don't use it. Just keep it, to remember what enough looks like."

I think about that spoon now. I suspect I will think about it for years.

* * *

*Marisol*

Here is what nobody tells you about escape: it is mostly sitting still.

For eleven days I had been moving—cellar to cistern, cistern to warehouse wall, warehouse to boat—every step planned, every hour accounted for, Isandro's maps folded against my ribs like a second heartbeat. And now there was nothing to do but sit on a deck and watch the water change color, and the stillness was worse than all of it. The fear had somewhere to go before. Now it just pooled.

So I counted instead. I come from a family of mapmakers; we count when we're frightened. My father taught me that a coastline is only grief made measurable, and that if you measure it carefully enough, the grief becomes something you can hold.

I counted the things I lost.

A mother, taken for questioning, whom I have not seen since and may not see again—I made myself say this plainly, in my own head, because Isandro tiptoes around it and his tiptoeing makes it larger. A father, deported north two years ago, address unknown, alive as far as anyone knows, which is both everything and nothing. A house. A language of kitchen sounds and street names that I did not know was a language until the people who spoke it were sorted into columns. My name, which I gave up myself, freely, and would give up again, and which I miss anyway, at odd moments, like a tooth.

Then I counted what I had, and here is the strange part: the list kept getting longer the longer I looked at it.

Isandro, asleep against the rail with his mouth open, looking approximately twelve years old, holding the atlas even in sleep the way other men hold weapons. His hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle—he never did get the stain fully out, and I hope he never does. The fact of him: that he broke open a locked drawer, forged a governor's seal, lied to a captain's face, dissolved another family's death warrant in seawater, and did all of it badly frightened and completely certain, which is the only kind of courage worth anything.

The nine names sewn into the atlas. Families who sheltered strangers when sheltering meant removal, who will never know that their list survived, that a boy they barely knew carried it out under a conqueror's nose bound inside a pretty gift-book. I read them once, by match-light in the hold while Isandro slept, and I made myself memorize three of them before he woke. Three a day, I decided. By the time we make landfall I will carry all nine in my head, where nobody's tallyman can reach them.

Odo's soup, which was terrible and hot and the best thing I have ever eaten.

And the town. This is the one I did not expect. I thought I had finished grieving Vessamare—I had eleven days and ample cause—but grief, I have learned, waits for safety the way tide waits for moon. All through the escape I had been too busy to mourn. Now, with nothing to outrun and the water going that deep green that means real sea, real distance, it caught up with me all at once, standing at the stern with my soup, looking south at nothing.

I cried for the harbor wall, where I asked him to keep his promise and hated myself for asking. I cried for the salt cellar, which smelled of my childhood summers and my recent terror in equal parts. I cried for the clerk, broken little man, who surrendered our names because he believed paperwork could buy mercy, and who waved to me from his office window the morning we left as though nothing had happened—as though, for him, nothing had. I cried for the fish market, which had betrayed us, and for the neighbor who denounced us, whom I had known since I was six and whose daughter I had taught to swim, and I could not hate her, that was the terrible thing, because I had seen her counting too, seen her lips move over her own inventory, and I knew exactly which column fear puts people in.

I cried until Isandro woke up, which took an unconscionably long time, because the man can sleep through weather.

"You're crying," he observed, with the diagnostic precision he brings to everything.

"Observant."

"It's a professional skill." He came and stood beside me at the stern, not touching me—we had not yet relearned how to touch each other without urgency, without it meaning *hold on, we're still alive*—and looked south at the same nothing I was looking at. "Which coastline?"

"The harbor wall," I said. "And the market. And—" I ran out of specificity. "All of it. It's all one coastline now."

He nodded slowly. He understands coastlines better than anyone I've ever met. "It doesn't get smaller," he said. "I keep thinking it will. That distance works like scale. But it doesn't. The town stays full size. You just get further from being able to walk home."

That is the most honest thing either of us said for the first two days, and I held onto it the way you hold onto a bearing in fog.

* * *

*Isandro*

On the second night, Odo asked to see the atlas.

I hesitated—the reflex of the past month, when showing anyone anything was a calculated risk—but there was no reason left to hesitate, and the habit felt suddenly heavy, so I opened it across a crate by the lamp and let him look.

He went through it slowly, with a care that surprised me. Fishermen read water the way clerks read paper, and Odo read charts like a man checking them against memory. At the harbor chart he paused longer than anywhere else, and I felt my pulse tick up—one degree, two—until I realized he wasn't studying the soundings at all. He was studying the margin.

"Good ink," he said finally. "Even in the corners. Whoever bound this cared about the parts nobody sees."

"He did," I said. It was true, and it was also the safest true thing available, and I have learned to live in that narrow strait between honesty and confession.

"My grandfather was a pilot," Odo said, closing the atlas gently, the way you close something that might be listening. "Worked these waters before the survey ships came. When the navy charts arrived, he looked at them and said: *they've mapped everything except where things actually happen.*" He banked the lamp. "Get some sleep. Landfall tomorrow, weather willing."

North. A free port, Odo said, which mostly meant a port with different masters, but I had stopped believing in clean categories somewhere around chapter seven of my life, so different masters seemed like wealth enough.

I lay down next to Marisol under the netting and did not sleep. Instead I did what I have done every night since the drawer came open: I recited names. Master Corvane first, always first, who taught me that a map is a promise that places exist, and who died for a list of people who kept that promise better than he ever drew it. Then the nine families, one by one, and beside each name I set the house I associated with it—the blue door on Tanner's Row, the widow Ondiz's bakery, the attic above the cooperage—and I found that the houses were becoming ghosts already, going soft at the edges, and that frightened me more than anything Ferran had ever done.

Because paper can be seized, but recitation cannot—that was the plan, that was the whole point of sewing the page into the atlas—and yet memory erodes too, quietly, without witnesses. A forgotten name is its own kind of removal. I resolved, lying there in the dark with the water talking underneath me, that erosion would not win. Whatever came next—whatever port, whatever work, whatever new masters with new columns—I would keep those names the way my master kept coastlines: redrawn regularly, checked against the ground, corrected by whoever still remembered the truth of them.

Beside me, Marisol murmured something in her sleep. I leaned closer. She was counting. "...the blue door on Tanner's Row... the widow Ondiz..." She had gotten further than me. Of course she had.

I lay back and looked at the stars, which are the one territory no one has ever successfully requisitioned, and allowed myself—for the first time in a month—to wonder about tomorrow instead of surviving tonight. What does an exiled mapmaker do in a free port? Draw coastlines for people who need to leave, I supposed. There would be no shortage of customers. There never is, anymore.

The atlas sat between us under the netting, one page heavier than all the rest.

Tomorrow, landfall. Tomorrow, the nine names spoken aloud somewhere safe enough to speak them. Tomorrow, the question I had been refusing to ask since the quay:

Whether the man who signed himself apprentice in Vessamare could learn to sign himself maker in exile—and what it would cost to stop being invisible on purpose.

The water talked. I let it talk. For once, I was not taking notes.
