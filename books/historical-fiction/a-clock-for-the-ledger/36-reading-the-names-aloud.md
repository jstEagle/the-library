## 36. Reading the Names Aloud

The safe house was a herring fisher's cottage two miles inland from a harbor whose name Isandro had deliberately not learned, because a man who cannot be asked where he came from should also not know where he is. It had one room, a stove that smoked unless you swore at it in a particular dialect, and a window that looked out on a field of turnips going to seed in the cold. Marisol pronounced it the finest house she had ever lived in. She was, Isandro suspected, only half joking.

They had been there eleven days. Long enough for the fisher's wife who owned the place to stop counting them as strangers and start counting them as mouths. Long enough for Isandro's hands to heal from the rope burns of the gangplank and begin, traitorously, to miss the feel of a pen. Long enough for both of them to sleep through an entire night without waking at every dog's bark — though neither had yet managed to sleep through a gull.

The atlas sat on the shelf between a chipped bowl and a jar of lamp oil. To anyone who opened it, it was a handsome commissioned work: the coastlines of the occupied south, surveyed with love by a dead master and finished by his apprentice, presented to a governor who would never understand what he was holding. But sewn into the binding beneath the harbor chart, flat as a second skin, lay a single page of a different ledger entirely — the page Isandro had cut from his master's hidden list on the night he had chosen between saving a document and saving a person.

For eleven days neither of them had touched it.

It was Marisol who broke first, on the twelfth morning, when rain came in off the sea and made the turnip field look like something out of her father's oldest charts.

"We can't just let it sit there," she said. She was mending a net she had no obligation to mend; the work kept her hands honest while her mind went wherever it went. "Like a letter unopened."

"It's not a letter," Isandro said. "It's forty-one names."

"Then we should know them."

"Paper can be seized." He said it the way his master used to say *measure twice*, out of habit rather than argument. He had been saying it for eleven days, mostly to himself.

"Paper can be seized," Marisol agreed. "So learn it. Then the paper stops mattering."

Isandro looked at her. She had spent three weeks in a salt cellar learning patience the way other people learn a language, by total immersion, and she had come out of that dark with a certainty he was still acquiring drop by drop, like water through limestone. She was right, of course. She usually was, which he found alternately comforting and infuriating depending on the hour.

He took down the atlas. His hands did not shake. He noted this with a kind of professional surprise, the way he had once noted that his own voice stayed level while he lied to Captain Aldurez about unregistered documents. There was apparently a part of him that would remain steady through anything, and he had decided not to examine whether that steadiness was courage or damage.

He cut the stitches with a fish-boning knife. The page came free — iron-gall ink in his master's hand, cramped and urgent, the letters of a man writing fast because he believed he was being followed. Forty-one households. The baker Ondiz and his wife. The Widow Ferréol, who had sheltered three children in her loft above the chandlery and told her neighbors she was storing sailcloth. The Vintar family, all seven of them, who had taken in strangers though their own roof leaked. Down the page, in smaller and smaller script, as if the writer were running out of room or time or both.

Marisol pulled her chair beside his. Outside, the rain thickened. The stove, unprovoked, behaved itself.

"Read them aloud," she said. "Both of us. Until we can say them without the page."

* * *

They began after supper, by lamplight, with the door barred and the fisher's wife paid to ask no questions — paid, Isandro thought wryly, exactly what the occupation had paid him, in coin on one side and silence on the other. The economics of discretion were apparently constant across all known coastlines.

"I'll go first," Marisol said, "so you can correct my pronunciation of your countrymen."

"They're your countrymen now too."

"Then correct me gently."

She read: "Ondiz, the baker, and Laria his wife, and their son Tello." She paused. "I bought bread from him. The morning before they posted the first notices. He gave me the heel loaf for nothing because I looked thin. He didn't know why I looked thin."

"He knew," Isandro said quietly. "Bakers always know. They see everyone at their hungriest."

"Ondiz, Laria, Tello," Marisol said again, and closed her eyes, and said it once more without looking. Then she nodded at him, and it was his turn.

Isandro read the next entry aloud. The sound of his own voice saying names into a stranger's kitchen in a foreign country struck him as absurd and holy at once. These were not great names. No governors, no captains, no men whose signatures moved armies. A roofer. A midwife. Two brothers who ran a ferry that no longer ran. Families who had done nothing except, when a frightened person knocked, open the door — which Isandro supposed had always been the whole of the law, before anyone thought to write it down.

"The Widow Ferréol," he read. "Loft above the chandlery. Three children passed through, autumn before last."

"You knew her?"

"Everyone knew her. She chased boys off her dock with a broom." He smiled despite himself. "She'd have chased soldiers off too, if the broom were longer. Say it back."

"Widow Ferréol. Loft over the chandlery. Three children."

"Good."

They worked through the evening the way sailors work a pump, in shifts, steadily, each taking turns reading and repeating until a name could stand on its own without the page beneath it. It was slow, and it was strange, and somewhere past midnight Isandro realized that he had never in his life committed anything to memory so deliberately — maps, yes, coastlines, soundings, the shapes of harbors, all the geography that could be redrawn by someone else if lost. But names he had always trusted to paper. Paper and ledgers and tidy columns. That trust had gotten forty families marked for removal, so perhaps it was time to carry the important cargo differently.

Around the twenty-ninth name, Marisol stopped.

"This one," she said. Her finger rested under the entry, and even upside down Isandro recognized the smallness of the script, the way his master had squeezed the words in as though ashamed to give them space. "The Pellam family. Harbor row. They sheltered—" She looked up. "This is the family my father sent to us. The ones who knocked on our door with nothing."

Isandro remembered. Of course he remembered. He had been fifteen, and a cartographer's daughter had come to his master's workshop at dusk to ask for a map with no roads marked on it, and he had fallen halfway in love before she finished the sentence.

"Pellam," Marisol said. "Harbor row. Father a caulker, mother a net-mender, daughter named Sefi who was afraid of gulls." She said it like a litany, like something rehearsed in a salt cellar through months of darkness. "I've carried them since the cellar. Now you carry them too."

"Sefi, who was afraid of gulls," Isandro repeated. And then, because grief and humor live in the same room in his chest and have always answered each other's knocks: "She'd hate it here. The gulls are enormous."

"She'd be brave about it," Marisol said. "She pretended not to be afraid, very loudly."

They laughed, briefly, and then the laugh turned into something else, and Marisol put her forehead against his shoulder, and Isandro understood that reciting the dead and the dispersed was its own kind of burial rite, and that no one had yet buried these people properly — not the ones gone north on the requisition ship, and not the ones still hiding behind walls in Vessamare, waiting for the war's attention to move elsewhere. Memory was the only grave marker they were going to get, and grave markers, in his experience, were meant to be visited more than once.

So they went around again.

* * *

The second night, the fisher's wife's brother joined them — a gray-bearded man named Rurik who had run contraband through three wars and considered the fourth merely a slow season. He had ferried them north; he knew what the page was without asking. When he heard what they were doing, he pulled up a stool and said, "Read them to me too. My memory is better than yours and worse than hers, which averages out useful."

So the circle widened by one. Rurik proved to have a shocking gift for it — he had memorized tide tables and smuggling routes for thirty years, and forty-one households settled into his skull like ballast. By the third night he was correcting *them*, insisting that no, the ferry brothers' surname ended in -ez not -az, and producing from some inner ledger the detail that the younger brother had a wooden leg carved with mermaids, which was not on the page at all but which Isandro, closing his eyes, could suddenly see, and suddenly the entry was not ink anymore but a man limping down a quay with mermaids on his knee.

"That's the trick of it," Rurik said, pleased with himself. "Ink is flat. Faces aren't. Give every name a face and a fault and you'll never lose them."

"The occupation gave them numbers instead," Isandro said. "Columns. Labor, transport, removal."

"Aye, well." Rurik spat, expertly, into the stove. "Numbers are how cowards count people. Names are how the rest of us do."

On the fifth night Marisol brought out something else: her father's old coastal charts, the ones declared seditious by men who had never held a compass. In the margins, in symbols that had looked like decoration to every official eye that ever frowned at them, lay the key — the anchor that meant safe house, the lighthouse that meant a doctor would help, the cormorant that meant passage north. She spread them on the table next to the rescued page and began matching, entry by entry, symbol to name.

"Some of these houses are still standing," she said. "Some of these people are still in them. If the war ends the way wars do — slowly, badly, then all at once — someone will need to find those families again. Prove they existed. Return what was taken." She tapped the chart. "My father built the map. Your master built the list. We're just the ones carrying both halves."

Isandro looked at the table: charts and lists, codes and names, the whole improbable archive of ordinary decency spread across a fisherman's table in a house full of turnips. His master had died keeping a page like this. Ferran had burned evidence to let a page like this survive. Somewhere south, a clerk who had surrendered his ledger believing cooperation would spare the town was presumably still cooperating, still hoping, still wrong. Paper had failed all of them and saved all of them, sometimes in the same week.

"So we become the archive," Isandro said.

"We become the archive," Marisol agreed. "Until there's somewhere to put it back."

They drilled the names a final time that night, all forty-one, standing, the way you'd recite an oath. Rurik insisted on formality. Marisol added faces to names as she went — Sefi loud against her fear of gulls, the baker with his heel loaf, the widow with her broom — and by the end Isandro found he could walk the page blindfolded, could start anywhere in the alphabet of it and travel the whole coastline of those families without landfall, without error, the way he could draw the Vessamare headlands with his eyes shut.

"Once more," Rurik said.

"Once more," they said.

And they read the names aloud into the smoky room, and the rain came off the sea, and somewhere far south a ship rode at anchor and a tallyman slept with clean hands and a complicated conscience, and none of it reached the cottage, none of it mattered in the cottage, where the only census being taken was the old kind — the kind taken by voices, door to door, heart to heart, and filed nowhere that fire could follow.

When they finished, Marisol folded the page along its old creases and handed it back to him.

"Burn it," she said.

Isandro stared at her. Eleven days he had guarded that page like a relic, sewn it into the atlas, planned its future in some imagined free archive. "Burn it?"

"You heard me. We know them now. All forty-one. You said it yourself the first night — paper can be seized." She held his gaze, and he saw that she had been planning this longer than he had been resisting it. "Every day that page exists, it endangers whoever shelters us next, and Rurik, and his sister, and everyone in this chain. The safest archive is the one that leaves no ash." A small smile. "Besides. You already erased one page in seawater and couldn't sleep. Think how well you'll sleep after burning one on purpose."

"That was guilt. This would be—" He searched for the word.

"Trust," Marisol supplied. "Trusting yourselves to remember. Which is harder, I know. Ink doesn't forget. People have to *choose* to, every morning."

Rurik, wisely, went out to check on a boat that needed no checking.

Isandro stood at the stove a long time with the page in his hands. He read it through one last time, silently, the maker's farewell inspection — every line true, every name load-bearing, the best craftsmanship he had ever been peripherally responsible for. His master had written it knowing it might cost him everything. It had. Handing it to flame felt like a second funeral, and a birth, and the settling of a debt, all pressed into one act like flowers in a book.

He fed it to the fire a corner at a time, watching the iron-gall ink flare a brief offended green — his master's mix, always too much vitriol — and then it was heat and light and gone, and forty-one households lived thenceforth in exactly three heads in a herring cottage, and nowhere else on earth.

He slept, as promised, magnificently.

He woke before dawn to Marisol shaking his shoulder, and it took him a moment, in the gray light, to read her face — not fear exactly, and not grief. Something closer to recognition.

"There's a boat in the bay," she said. "Small, dark, sitting low in the water like it's carrying more than fish. Rurik's gone down to meet it." She paused. "And Isandro — the man steering it waved. Not a fisherman's wave. He waved like he knew us."
