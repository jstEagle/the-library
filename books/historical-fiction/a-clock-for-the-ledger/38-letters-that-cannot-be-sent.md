## 38. Letters That Cannot Be Sent

### Marisol

The safe house in the north had a table that wobbled, a window that faced a wall, and a supply of paper that arrived, like everything else here, by way of people who did not ask questions. Three sheets. I counted them twice, because three sheets felt like an inheritance and I wanted to be sure of the size of it.

My father had written letters his whole life. That was the joke of it — the man who mapped coastlines for a living spent his evenings writing to places he'd never been and people he'd never met, correspondents scattered across ports like buoys marking some channel only he could see. When the soldiers came for his charts, they took the maps and left the letters, because letters, in their arithmetic, weighed nothing.

I sat at the wobbling table with my three sheets and understood, finally, why he'd done it. A map tells you where a thing is. A letter tells you where *you* are — which is a much more dangerous piece of information, and much harder to requisition.

*Papá,* I wrote, and then stopped, because the next word was supposed to be an address, and there wasn't one. He had been deported in the spring, put on a transport with a manifest and no destination announced, and in the months since, no line of his handwriting had reached us. Somewhere north of Vessamare — or east, or wherever the removal ships went when they were full — there was a man who taught me to read latitude before I could read scripture, and I had no way to aim a piece of paper at him.

So I wrote it anyway. *Care of no address,* Isandro had said once, half laughing, describing how his master sent complaints to the Cartographers' Guild. It seemed as good a postal system as any.

I told him about the harbor wall, how the gulls still argued over the same territory as if the occupation were a change of weather. I told him the salt cellar smelled of him now, or I imagined it did, which amounted to the same thing. I told him I had kept the coded key, that his old coastal charts — the ones they called seditious, the ones worth a cell or a rope — had turned out to be exactly what he'd always claimed: just maps. Honest ones. The sedition was in what other people needed them for.

I did not tell him about the forty families on the removal list, because if he learned that from a letter intercepted somewhere between here and nowhere, it would be used against whoever held it. This was the new grammar of writing, which I was learning quickly: you composed two letters at once, the one on the page and the one in your head, and prayed the wrong reader never got the right one.

Three sheets. I filled the first with small things — the wobbling table, the wall outside the window, the way Isandro measured distance now in days of walking instead of miles, which was a habit he'd caught from grief and couldn't shake. On the second sheet I ran out of small things and got honest. I told him I was afraid I was forgetting the sound of his voice and keeping only my memory of remembering it, which is a copy of a copy, and copies degrade.

On the third sheet I wrote the thing I had actually sat down to write, the thing underneath everything: *You drew the coast so that people could find their way home. They called it treason. I am going to finish drawing it.*

Then I folded all three together, addressed them to *my father, care of no address*, and realized I had nowhere to put them. There was no post here. There was nothing here but safety, which turns out to be its own kind of exile — a room where nothing can reach you, including the person you are writing to.

Isandro found me at the table that evening, holding the folded sheets like a bouquet of something that wouldn't keep.

"No way to send them," he said. Not a question. He'd watched me count the paper. He counts things too; it's how we recognized each other, years ago, two children tallying the harbor boats for the pleasure of it.

"I know."

"You could read them aloud," he offered carefully. "Like we do with the names. Commit them to—"

"Memory." I almost smiled. "Paper can be seized. Recitation cannot. Yes, he's heard the sermon too."

"It's not a bad sermon."

"It's a terrible sermon," I said, "because it works." And that was true, and we both knew it, and knowing it didn't make the folded pages in my hands weigh any less.

### Isandro

I have burned things before. Ink off a page in seawater, mostly — chapter and verse of that particular liturgy is written into my hands somewhere, in the stains. But burning a letter is different. Seawater takes away information. Fire takes away intention, and intentions are louder.

I wrote to Ferran at midnight, because that's when the honest sentences come, the ones daylight would have censored.

It began as an accounting, because I didn't know how else to begin with a man whose entire soul was arithmetic. *You sorted three thousand people and remembered none of their faces,* I wrote, and then crossed it out, because it wasn't fair — he had remembered mine, or at least my hands, and his memory of my hands had saved us. That was the debt I couldn't reconcile. Every column in my ledger wanted to balance. Gratitude to the man who hunted me sat in the same row as rage at the machine he served, and the sums refused to come out even.

*You stamped our papers without reading them,* I tried again. *I have wondered every day since whether that was mercy or efficiency, and I've decided the distinction doesn't matter, which frightens me more than either answer.*

That was closer. Ferran would have appreciated the precision. He'd sat across from me with a lamp between us and demonstrated, casually, conversationally, that he knew my ink from my master's — iron-gall browning at the edges like autumn against my newer mix — and he'd made it sound like shop talk between colleagues. Menace dressed as craft. I had hated him for it and admired him in the same breath, and I never resolved which came first, and I suspect he never resolved it either.

I told him about the atlas. About the ledger page sewn inside the harbor chart, invisible unless you knew to look for a binding a hair too thick, which of course he would have known, because he noticed everything. I told him the page was safe now, recited nightly in a house with a wobbling table, and that his name was not on it — neither the saving list nor the damned one — because he belonged to a third category no tallyman had invented yet.

I told him, near the end, the truest sentence I owned: *You let us go and then warned me to carry less next time, and I have thought about what "next time" means for a man who has already carried everything he owns onto a fishing boat, and I think you meant that carrying less is a discipline, not a deprivation. I think you were teaching me the thing you learned too late, sorting faces you'd never remember.*

Then I signed it. Then I looked at the signature — *Isandro Ishikawa, maker* — the first signature of my life that wasn't an apprentice's mark under a master's name, and I understood what the letter actually was.

It wasn't for Ferran. Ferran would never read it. A letter to a man whose profession was making people legible cannot be delivered, because delivery would undo the only gift he ever gave us: our disappearance from every record he kept. If I mailed it, I would un-erase myself. If I kept it, I was keeping evidence of a correspondence that existed only in one direction, addressed to a man who might be dead, transferred, or promoted into someone I wouldn't recognize.

Some debts are paid by silence. My master had taught me that without meaning to — he'd died protecting a list, and his last letter had named me custodian, and custodianship, I was learning, is mostly the art of holding things you cannot show anyone.

Marisol came down the narrow stair while the candle guttered. She had her three unsentable pages tucked under her arm, and she looked at my letter, and my hand hovering over the stove, and she didn't ask permission. She'd stopped asking permission around the time she answered a suspicious sergeant with fluent, dangerous truth and walked through a checkpoint on the strength of honesty alone.

"Burn it?" she said.

"Burn it."

"Read it first," she said. "Out loud. Both of ours. Not because it helps. Because it's the only ceremony we have."

So we did. She went first — her father's letter, the wobbling table, the salt cellar smelling of memory, the vow to finish drawing the coast. Her voice stayed level until the third sheet, and then it didn't quite, and I looked at the wall and gave her that.

Then mine. The crossed-out lines and all — I read those too, because Ferran would have appreciated the record of correction, the visible arithmetic of a mind changing itself. *Mercy or efficiency, and the distinction doesn't matter.* Marisol flinched at that one. She'd never liked Ferran. She trusted him the way you trust weather that has already passed — gratefully, and without forgiveness.

We fed the pages to the stove together, hers and mine, and the fire took the intentions, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet after a confession nobody witnessed.

"Years from now," Marisol said, watching the embers, "when this is over, if it's ever over — will you write to him? Find out where he went?"

"If he wants to be found."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then the letter stays burned," I said, "and the debt stays paid."

She nodded slowly, and I could see her filing it — she files things too, another way we recognized each other — filing it under debts, under silence, under the long inventory of what the occupation cost that no requisition form had a column for.

### Marisol

That night I dreamed of my father's workroom in Vessamare, the smell of sizing and sea-damp charts, and in the dream he was at his desk writing a letter, and the address on it was my name, care of no address, and I woke up certain — not hoping, *certain*, the way you're certain of a bearing you've plotted twice — that somewhere he was writing to me, that the correspondence existed in both directions, held open by two people burning their halves of it.

Which meant, if I followed the logic, that the letters weren't lost. They were just undelivered. And undelivered is a condition, not an ending. Maps taught me that. A coastline doesn't stop existing because no ship has charted it yet.

I got up in the dark, careful of the floorboard that complained, and found Isandro at the table already, because of course he was. He doesn't sleep well in safe houses; he sleeps the way he draws coastlines, provisionally, ready to revise.

"The blank book," I said.

He looked at me.

"We read the names aloud. We memorized them. But memory degrades — I told my father that myself, copies of copies." I set the blank book on the wobbling table between us. "Paper can be seized. But a new book, started fresh, in a free port, with names chosen freely — that's not an archive. That's a choice."

Isandro opened the cover. In the gray light before dawn he dipped the pen, and I watched my husband-to-be — though neither of us had said it yet, and perhaps neither of us ever would, since some things, like some letters, are truer unsent — write the first line.

He started with his master's name. Of course he did.

And then he paused, pen lifted, and looked at me across the table, and I saw that he had arrived at the question I'd been carrying all night: not *whom* to write down, but what kind of book this was going to be — a ledger of the dead, or a register of the living. A memorial, or a beginning.

"Your turn comes after mine," he said quietly. "But decide tonight what name you'll give it. Because whatever we write first, the whole book will follow."

Outside, the northern harbor was waking — gulls, rigging, a language we were only beginning to learn. Inside, the pen waited above the second line, empty and exact, like a coastline not yet drawn.
