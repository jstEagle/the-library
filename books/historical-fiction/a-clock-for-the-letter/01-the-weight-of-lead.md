## 1. The Weight of Lead

There were four hundred and twelve pieces of type in the wrong compartments of the upper case that morning, and August Hartwell knew this because she had counted them the evening before, and because she had counted them again now, and because the number had not changed — which meant her father had not touched the case since she'd sorted it, which was either a mercy or an omen.

She decided, as she generally decided, to call it a mercy and get on with the work.

"August," Elias called from the composing stone, in the voice he used when he wanted to appear authoritative and knew he was about to be contradicted, "have you seen my reading spectacles?"

"You're wearing them."

He paused, touched his own face with the delicacy of a man discovering a bird has landed on him, and said, "So I am. I put them there so as not to lose them."

"A sound strategy."

"One learns these things." He bent back over his stick of type, squinting at a line of fourteen-point Bodoni that August could see, even from across the room, had been set upside down. She did not mention it. There were kindnesses a daughter owes a father, and chief among them, in a print shop, is the pretense that his eyes are what they used to be.

The shop smelled the way it had always smelled — ink and oil and the particular cold sweetness of lead — and August found, sorting the morning's corrections into their cases, that the smell itself had become a kind of clock. Not the tall case in the corner that Tomas had repaired twice before they took him away; the other kind of clock, the one inside a person, that tells you by the weight of the air whether it is early or late in some season you cannot name.

It was late March, 1848, and something was coming. Everyone said so the way people say rain is coming: not with fear exactly, but with the small adjustments of people who intend to keep their hats dry.

* * *

The Hartwell Printing Office occupied the ground floor of a narrow building on Kessler Lane, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold ribbons to no one anyone had ever identified. Elias had bought it thirty-one years ago with money saved from a journeyman's wages and a bride's dowry, and he liked to say — every year, on the anniversary, whether or not anyone asked — that he had printed his first broadsheet on a Tuesday and his first mistake on a Wednesday.

August had been born over the shop in the way printer's children are born over shops, which is to say surrounded by letters before she could speak, and she had learned her alphabet not from a hornbook but from the type case itself, running her fingers along the compartments the way other children ran theirs along fence rails. *A* in its corner box, most plentiful, like gravel. *Z* alone in its sliver, hoarded like gold. Her father claimed she'd spoken her first word at the case — "more," reaching for the *M*'s — though Elias told that story differently each time, and once had her saying "no," which August privately considered more likely.

By twenty-six she could set a column faster than any journeyman who'd passed through, could read reversed type as easily as printed, could tell by the feel of a piece of lead whether the foundry had cut corners on the alloy. These were not accomplishments anyone would toast at a wedding. They were, however, hers entirely, and she had come to suspect that a person's real possessions are always of that sort: unphotographable, unprizable, load-bearing.

The town outside the window did not know what to make of her, which suited her fine. A woman who worked cases was odd; a woman who worked cases *well* was unsettling; a woman who owned no husband, no ring, and no visible ambition beyond the next job's deadline was regarded by the matrons of the market square as a problem awaiting a solution. August had heard herself described, through the open window, as "still unmarried, poor thing," delivered in the tone one uses for a roof that leaks only in spring.

She had bitten back the reply that came to hand — that she was married to four hundred pounds of type and the marriage was holding up better than most — and had instead smiled, because a printer's smile costs nothing and buys silence, and silence was worth more than coin that winter.

Because the town was murmuring. You could hear it under everything, the way you hear water under ice.

* * *

The murmuring had a geography. It lived in the taverns first, then migrated to the bakery, then to the church steps after service, where it stood about with its hands in its pockets pretending to discuss the price of flour. The flour was genuinely expensive; that much was true everywhere. But nobody discussed flour for forty minutes, and the men on the church steps discussed flour the way diplomats discuss weather — meaningfully, at length, while looking past each other at something neither would name.

Students had come down from the university in February, three of them, young men with pamphlets in their coat linings and the shining eyes of boys who have discovered injustice the way one discovers a loose tooth — with fascination and a constant probing tongue. They had come to the shop twice, asking after rates for a "circular," and Elias had quoted them a fair price and taken no deposit, and afterward August had swept the floor and said nothing, and her father had said nothing, and the nothing between them had been very articulate.

"We print what we're paid to print," Elias said at last, hanging his apron on its nail. "That's all a press is. A mouth that doesn't choose its words."

"And if someone pays us to lie?"

"Then we lie beautifully." He said it lightly, but his hands, she noticed, were not light. They were doing the thing they did when he was frightened — smoothing themselves along the frame of the old Albion press, stroke after stroke, the way another man might stroke a dog or a rosary.

Her father had printed the town's news for thirty years: council notices, auction bills, the shipping lists, obituaries set with genuine care because Elias held that the dead deserved good kerning if nothing else. He had never once printed sedition, and he had never once printed flattery, and the narrowness of that road had kept the shop alive through two changes of government and one fire. It was a respectable road. It was, August reminded herself daily, the whole of the road.

Revolutions, she had concluded by twenty-six, were things that happened to other people's towns and got printed about afterward, in papers like hers, in ink mixed by women like her, read aloud in coffeehouses by men who would have run from the actual event at a dead sprint. History, properly conducted, stayed on the page, where it could be proofed, corrected, and filed. She believed this the way she believed in the metric system of the type case: completely, professionally, and without ever having examined the foundation.

* * *

The forgetfulness had begun — or rather, August had begun *noticing* it — eighteen months ago, and it announced itself not in grand failures but in small displacements, the way a slow flood announces itself in the migration of furniture.

First it was the spectacles, which turned up in the flour bin, in his boot, once folded into the copy drawer beneath a manuscript he swore he'd never seen. Then it was names. He called Herr Brandt the inspector "Brenner" for a full season, and Brandt, a nervous man who seemed to find the error flattering, never corrected him. Then dates. Elias set the date-line of the weekly paper two days ahead in October, and the paper went out announcing events that hadn't happened, and two subscribers wrote in to say the Hartwell paper had achieved prophecy, and Elias laughed, and August did not laugh, and took to checking the date-line herself thereafter.

She kept the catalogue privately, the way a physician keeps notes. *Tuesday:* salt in the coffee. *Thursday:* called me by my mother's name, then covered it with a joke about how all Hartwell women scold identically. *Friday, worst day:* set an entire funeral notice for Widow Ammer with the wrong name entirely — a living woman's name, a neighbor's — and had to be shown it three times before he saw it, and when he saw it he sat down slowly on the composing stool and said, "Well," in a voice August would hear for the rest of her life.

"It happens," she'd told him. "Everyone misreads. The light's bad in the afternoon."

"The light's bad in the afternoon," he agreed, gratefully, and both of them knew the light had been fine.

What frightened her was not the errors. Errors could be caught; she caught them; the paper went out clean. What frightened her was watching him *know*, in his lucid hours, that the fog would come back, and watching him arrange himself against it — tying a string to the cashbox lid, writing *LOCK THE DOOR* on a scrap and pinning it inside his hat, telling the same story twice within ten minutes and watching her face carefully, hopefully, to see if she'd noticed. He was cataloguing too, she realized. His own decline, itemized in strings and scraps. Two printers in one family, both keeping records, both filing them where the other wouldn't look.

Once, in January, she'd come down before dawn and found him standing at the type case in his nightshirt, sorting type in the dark, perfectly, by touch, his fingers moving through the compartments with total confidence. When she lit the lamp he blinked at her and said, "I couldn't sleep. Thought I'd get ahead on the week."

"You've sorted the entire *e*-case into the wrong tray."

He looked. His face did something complicated. "Ah," he said. "Well. At least it was done with conviction."

They re-sorted it together in silence, shoulder to shoulder, and it occurred to her that this — this, here, the two of them and the lead and the lamp — was the whole of what she wanted out of the world, and that wanting it made her small, and that she did not care.

* * *

At noon the ribbon-seller's boy came in for the auction bills, and stayed leaning in the doorway to deliver the news, which was his true trade, the bills being mere cover.

"They're saying Voss doubled the levy on the mills again," he said. "And that the weavers at Millbridge mean to strike it, come April."

"Saying who?"

"Saying everyone." He grinned. He was fifteen and found the whole era delicious.

"Tell everyone," August said, counting his change, "that everyone should spend less time saying and more time buying ribbon, or his mistress will sack him."

"She's not my—" But he was laughing, and gone.

Voss. Magistrate Voss, whose notices the shop printed quarterly — tax schedules, curfew reminders, the dry grammar of authority — and whose name, lately, was said in the town the way one says the name of a tooth that aches: precisely, quietly, with the tongue pressed against it. August had met him once, at the ceremony when the new courthouse clock was installed. He had shaken her father's hand and looked over it, at the shop sign, and said, "Useful business," in a tone that managed to make usefulness sound like a character flaw. She had disliked him instantly and completely and had never examined the dislike, because disliking magistrates required no examination. It was like disliking wet rot.

But the name was in the air now, more often, lower. And April was coming, and the students' circular had never materialized — they'd never returned with their deposit — and she wondered sometimes what it was they'd wanted to print, and whether somewhere in the town that circular existed already, printed on some other press, in some other shop where the owner had said yes where Elias had said *bring a deposit.*

She did not envy that shop. That was the thing she wanted noted, if ever anyone asked. She did not envy it. A press that printed sedition was a press that attracted inspectors, and inspectors, and then soldiers, and then the particular arithmetic by which a shop becomes a pile of type scattered in the street and its proprietor becomes a name in someone else's paper. She had seen the arithmetic done in neighboring districts. She had read the results. Reading the results was, in her view, participation enough.

"August." Her father again, from the stone. "Read me this galley. Tell me if it sings."

She came and stood beside him and read the morning's job — a bill for the chandler's spring stock, tallow and wicks, prices bold — and pronounced it clean.

"Sings," Elias repeated, satisfied, patting the galley. "Your mother always said a well-set line hums. I thought it nonsense for twenty years. Now I think she could hear things the rest of us can't." He smiled at the type, not at her. "She'd have liked you in the trade."

"I'm *in* the trade, Father."

"So you are." He frowned suddenly at the case, at some private ledger opening behind his eyes. "Was there... I had a thought this morning, walking up. Something about Thursday. Was there a Thursday thing?"

"It's Wednesday."

"Is it." He absorbed this. "Then perhaps it was last Thursday. Or next. Never mind. Never mind, girl, your face will freeze like that."

"My face isn't—"

"It does that little fold. Your brother used to say—" And there he stopped, the way he always stopped, the sentence ending like a road washed out, and busied himself violently with a rule of brass.

Tomas. Two years gone now, officially. Fever, the letter had said, contracted in the east, buried where he fell; the government's letter was courteous and sealed and contained, in its second paragraph, a sentence about his service that suggested services rendered rather than services suffered, and August had read that sentence four hundred times and still didn't know what it meant. Her father had aged a decade in the week after the news, and had never spoken of it since except in these broken-off roads, sentences that set out toward the boy's name and drowned before arrival.

She missed him the way you miss a wall you'd leaned on without knowing it was bearing anything. He'd been the loud one, the quick one, the one who charmed the customers and argued politics in the tavern and came home smelling of other people's opinions. She'd been the quiet one at the case. Between them they'd made one complete printer, her father used to say, and then he stopped saying it, because half of the complete printer was dead, and jokes don't survive division by grief.

August went back to the cases and worked. The afternoon light came long and yellow through the window and lay across the leads in bars, and the town outside murmured its low water-under-ice murmur, and she set a price list for ribbon and corrected her father's date-lines and believed, with the whole professional weight of her being, that history was ink for other papers.

It was nearly dusk when the knock came — not at the front door, where customers knocked, but at the loading door in the alley, where no customer ever came, and where, in thirty-one years, the knocker had always been the coal man or the devil.

The coal man, she reflected later, would have been preferable.

The knocking came again, weaker, arrhythmic, the knock of a man spending his last coins of strength on it. Through the wall she heard her father stir and start to rise, and she was at the loading door before him, bar lifted, before any sensible part of her had voted on the matter —

— and a stranger fell into the doorway, fever-bright, gray-lipped, clutching two leather satchels to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood, and behind him, far down the alley, came the boots of the night patrol, keeping time, keeping order, keeping on.
