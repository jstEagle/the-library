## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The trouble with a print shop, August Hartwell had long ago decided, was that it taught you exactly how much of the world was made up.

Every word that came through her hands had been set by someone — some tired compositor in some smoky room, choosing letters one at a time out of a case, making decisions about what was true and what was merely printable. Kings were made of lead type. Wars were made of lead type. The price of bread and the death of princes arrived at her father's shop in identical crates, smelling identically of ink, and she had stopped being impressed by any of it somewhere around her fourteenth year.

Revolutions, in particular, struck her as a great deal of fuss over what would eventually be a paragraph in somebody else's paper. *In the town of — , unrest among the lower classes was suppressed by the authorities with commendable firmness.* She could set that sentence in her sleep. She had, in a sense, been setting versions of it for years, because Elias Hartwell's little press printed whatever paid: almanacs, hymnals, bills of lading, funeral notices, and once — memorably — an advertisement for a tonic that promised to cure both deafness and excessive cheerfulness, which August considered the most honest lie they'd ever run, since it failed at both jobs equally.

So when the knocking came at the loading door that Tuesday evening, she assumed it was the paper merchant, come late and apologetic as usual, and she wiped her hands on her apron without hurry and went to let in more raw material for other people's history.

Except it wasn't the paper merchant.

* * *

The man in the doorway had been tall once. Now he was folded against the doorframe as though the frame were holding him upright out of politeness, and his face was the color of tallow, shining with the particular sweat that August had learned — from obituaries, mostly — meant fever. He had a satchel strap clenched in one fist so hard his knuckles had gone the color of candle wax, and he was saying something.

"You'll want to speak up," August said. "I don't do deafness or cheerfulness."

That got a flicker of something across his face — surprise, maybe, or the last gasp of a sense of humor fighting hard against whatever was trying to kill him. "Are you Hartwell's?"

"I'm Hartwell's."

"Prints... anything?"

"Anything that pays."

He tried to smile at that and mostly managed it, which was when his knees quit entirely. August caught his arm — he weighed nothing, which frightened her more than the fever did — and half-carried, half-lowered him onto the stack of rag bales beside the loading door, where the paper merchant usually sat and complained about the roads.

"Satchel," he said. It came out as mostly vowels. His hand found the bag and pulled it against his chest like a child with a coat in winter. "Don't let them take the satchel."

"Who's them?"

And that was when August heard the boots.

Not paper-merchant boots. Boots in rhythm, several pairs, coming down the alley behind the shop with the unhurried confidence of men who carried authority in leather. Patrol boots. She had heard them plenty of times from the front of the shop; hearing them at the back was different in the way that a word is different when you see it set wrong on the stone.

She looked at the feverish stranger. The stranger looked at her, and his eyes — brown, absurdly clear given everything else about him — asked the question plainly enough.

August swore under her breath, in the manner of her father, who swore beautifully and had taught her all of it, and hauled open the lid of the paper chest by the door. It was half full of unsold stock.

"In," she said. "Breathe quietly. If you die in there, die small."

He went in like a man who had stopped asking questions of the universe some miles back. She piled three reams of spoiled almanac sheets on top of him — pages forecasting mild winters and fair winds, which she thought, if he survived, might be worth mentioning to him as a form of apology — and shut the lid just as the boots arrived.

There were three of them. She knew the middle one by sight: Sergeant Brock of the garrison, a broad, pink man with the face of someone perpetually smelling something he'd been assured was soup. He had inspected their press registration twice before and always looked at August the way a man looks at a dog that might be purebred but has suspicious manners.

"Mistress Hartwell." He touched his cap. "Open late."

"Deadlines," August said. "Funeral notice for the Kessler infant. Grief keeps no business hours." This was true, and also effective, because no man alive wants to argue with a grieving printer. She watched Brock's face perform the small collapse of a man losing his appetite for the inspection.

"We're searching for a fugitive. Feverish fellow, may be injured. Seen anyone?"

"Seen the paper merchant at four o'clock, and before that a woman I strongly suspect of wanting to sell me buttons." August leaned against the doorframe, arranging herself into the posture of a woman whose evening had already been ruined by commerce and would not tolerate further interruption. "Is this about the unrest in the capital? Papa read me the gazette's version. Very restful, the way they tell it. Everyone discontented, then everyone firm, then everyone suppressed."

"The gazette," said Brock, "is the gazette."

"It is that," August agreed. "Would you like to see inside? I should warn you the only exciting thing in there is a case of apostrophes Papa misplaced in '39 and refuses to admit to."

For one dangerous moment she thought he would take her up on it — the paper chest stood four feet inside the door, and behind its lid a dying man was doing his best impression of silence, breathing in increments, like a clock with a bad escapement. But Brock was a sergeant, and sergeants are promoted out of curiosity early in their careers. He glanced at the dark interior of the shop, at the great silent mass of the press, at the aproned woman in the doorway radiating inconvenience, and decided that Hartwell's was what it appeared to be: a small, failing, honest trade.

"Firmness," he said, apropos of nothing, and touched his cap again, and the boots went on down the alley.

August waited until she couldn't hear them, and then until she couldn't hear anything but the river and the shop clock, which had run four minutes fast since the spring and resisted all correction. Then she opened the chest.

"You," she said, "are going to explain yourself. After you've explained yourself to some broth. In that order. I'm told fever responds to broth, and I have never known a man to respond to explanation without it."

* * *

Corvin — he gave the name the way a man gives a name he has worn too long, like a coat gone shiny at the elbows — sat wrapped in Elias's good blanket by the stove in the composing room, holding a bowl of broth in both hands as though it might make a break for freedom. He was perhaps thirty. Fever had burned off most of him already, but it had left behind sharp cheekbones and those clear eyes, and hands that even now kept drifting to the satchel at his feet to confirm it was still there.

"You hid me," he said. It wasn't gratitude, exactly. It sounded more like a man checking his arithmetic.

"I hid the satchel," August said. "You happened to be attached to it. What's in it?"

"Pamphlets."

"And?"

"Pamphlets," Corvin repeated, with dignity, and drank his broth.

August set type while he ate. It was easier than staring at him. She was setting a handbill for an auction — two cows, a wagon, the estate of a man who had evidently died owing money to everyone including, possibly, the cows — and her fingers moved through the case without instruction, pulling letters the way other people breathe. Her father said she composed faster than any man he'd apprenticed in forty years, which he delivered as a compliment and which August heard as a fact about her prospects.

"Elias Hartwell," Corvin said at last, into the quiet. "This is really his shop. The old gazette work — the bridge contract scandal, eleven years back. He printed the true figures. Before they closed him down."

"They didn't close us down." August pulled a capital A. "They declined to renew our municipal contracts, our distributor found religion, and two men broke our front window with a brick they later claimed to regret. Entirely unrelated events, occurring in sequence."

"That's what closing down looks like," Corvin said gently. "Where I come from."

"Where do you come from?"

"Tonight?" He looked at the broth. "Tonight I come from a ditch outside Millbridge Ford. Generally speaking, I try to come from somewhere else entirely."

August set her line and locked it in the chase. There was a silence in the room that had weight to it, and she found she didn't want to fill it, and filled it anyway.

"There were soldiers tonight looking for a feverish man. They'll come back. Whatever you're carrying, this is a bad place to carry it to. We print almanacs. My father can't remember Tuesday. I am not part of whatever this is." She turned and looked at him directly. "I have seen revolutions, mister. In type. They're very tidy on the page. Fourteen-point bold, *LIBERTY OR DEATH*, and underneath in six-point agate, *several persons were hanged*. I know which size the bodies get set in. So you can drink your broth and go back to your ditch, and tomorrow I'll have forgotten you, because forgetting is a craft like any other, and I practice."

Corvin set down the empty bowl. He looked at her for a long moment, and there was something in his expression that she would spend years afterward trying to name — pity, she supposed eventually, though at the time it looked almost like relief. As though he had been carrying his secret up a long hill and here, at last, was someone offering to tell him he could put it down, and he was grateful, and he couldn't.

"You should look at one of the pamphlets," he said. "Before you forget me. That's only fair. You shouldn't have to forget something you haven't seen."

"That's not how forgetting works."

"It's how mine does." He crouched, wincing, and opened the satchel, and took out not pamphlets but a bundle of letters, tied with string, soft-cornered with handling. "The pamphlets are for the cause. These are for Hartwell's specifically. For this shop. Because of who wrote them."

He held them out.

August almost didn't take them. Later she would understand that there had been a full second — the length of a falling typeface, the time it takes a letter to travel from case to composing stick — in which she could simply have turned back to the auction bill, and Corvin would have gathered his letters and his fever and gone back to his ditch, and everything after would have been different. Her whole life balanced on that second, and she spent it reaching out her hand, because she was curious, and curiosity was the first craft she had ever learned.

She untied the string. There were seven letters. The topmost began, in a strong slanting script she knew better than her own—

*Dear friend at the old press,*

*I write to you across the distance they think they've made.*

The looping g's, each one swinging below the line like a boy leaning over a fence. The t's crossed twice, quick and impatient, so that every *the* wore a little flag. The capitals that started too large and apologized for themselves halfway down. She knew them the way you know a voice through a wall. She had received dozens of such letters, years ago, from a boy apprenticed away in the capital, letters full of big talk and worse spelling, letters she had burned in grief along with everything else in the wooden box on the shelf above the type racks, because keeping them had hurt more than the fire did.

Tomas Hartwell had been dead for two years. She had read the official account herself — sedition, tribunal, execution, *the sentence was carried out with appropriate severity*, agate, six-point. She had not wept at the news, because she had done all her weeping in private beforehand, on no evidence whatsoever, purely on the suspicion that a boy who wrote letters like that would eventually write himself into a hole too deep for letters. Her father had set the death notice himself, in a steady hand, and had gone to bed, and had slept, and August had lain awake listening to the clock run four minutes fast.

The handwriting on the letter in her own two hands was Tomas's. Not similar. Not forged well. His — down to the way the crossbar on the final *t* trailed off where he always lifted the pen too soon.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice came out quite level, which surprised her; she would have expected it to shatter things. "He's dead."

"No," said Corvin, softly, watching her face the way a man watches a dam in flood season. "No, miss. That's rather the point of everything I've carried through three checkpoints and one ditch. He's alive. And these letters say he means to come home."

Outside, the shop clock ticked on, four minutes fast, toward a dawn that August no longer believed in.
