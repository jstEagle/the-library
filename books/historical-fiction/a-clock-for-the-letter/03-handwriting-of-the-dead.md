## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

August had laid the letters out on the composing stone the way her father laid out type before a job: sorted, squared, ready to be interrogated.

There were seven of them. Seven sheets of thin, cheap paper, water-stained and folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Corvin sat across from her in the chair they kept for customers who wanted wedding announcements — a chair that had never once hosted anything this strange. He had stopped shivering somewhere around midnight, which she decided was either a good sign or a very bad one.

"You went through my pockets," he said. His voice was better now. Steadier. It had the rasp of a man who had been shouting pamphlets in market squares.

"You bled on my floor," August said. "I went through everything."

She had found the satchels first — two of them, heavy as sleeping cats, stuffed with pamphlets she had read twice by candlelight before her outrage caught up with her curiosity. *The Voice of the Ford*, they said across the top, in type she recognized. She owned matrices like those letters. Half the underground presses in the valley bought their sorts from suppliers who bought from Elias Hartwell, and a compositor knows her own alphabet the way a mother knows a child's cough.

But it was the third thing she'd found that had kept her awake until the sky went grey at the edges. A bundle of letters, tied with string, addressed to no one, signed by everyone's ghost.

"Tomas Hartwell," she said, and watched Corvin's face do something complicated. "You have letters from Tomas Hartwell."

"I do."

"My brother has been dead for two years."

"Has he?" said Corvin, who was either very brave or very new to this town.

* * *

Here is what August knew about her brother's death, in the order she had learned to carry it:

Tomas had left in the spring of 1846 with a printer's apprentice's kit and a head full of speeches. He wrote home twice a month — funny letters, mostly, full of jokes about bad lodging houses and worse coffee, because Tomas could make even politics sound like a comedy act if you let him. Then in June he was arrested near Millbridge Ford. Then in September there was an execution notice in the official gazette, small as a widow's announcement, and their father had printed a copy of it himself, standing at his own press with tears running into his beard, because the law required it be set in local type and Elias Hartwell would not let a stranger's hands touch his son's death.

August had composed that notice. She remembered every word. She had set it letter by letter, and when it was done she had stood in the yard behind the shop and thrown up into the gooseberry bushes, and then come back inside and cleaned the type, because grief is one thing and a dirty galley is another.

So no. She did not believe in ghosts. But she believed in handwriting.

She picked up the topmost letter and put it on the stone between them, smoothing it flat with both palms like a woman calming a startled animal.

"Read me the second line," she said.

Corvin leaned forward, wincing. "'The coffee here could dissolve a horseshoe—'"

"The g." She tapped the paper. "In *horseshoe*, there isn't one, but look at *dissolve* — no. Look here. *Longing.* Every g loops below the line and comes back up crossed, like someone tying a knot. That's not taught in any school. That's a boy who decided at twelve that plain g's were boring and invented his own, and our father told him it would ruin his penmanship exams, and Tomas said exams were a form of tyranny." Her voice was doing something she didn't authorize. "The t's are crossed left to right, backward, like a man writing in a wind from the wrong direction. He started doing that after he broke his wrist falling out of the mulberry tree. I watched him learn it. I watched him practice it at this very table."

The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, the river worked at its pilings, patient as ever.

"It's his hand," August said. "It's his exact hand. And you will now explain to me, slowly, with no cleverness, how a man's exact hand exists on paper dated four months ago, when I set that man's death notice myself."

Corvin looked at the letter for a long moment. When he spoke, all the feverish flippancy had drained out of him, and what was left was just tired and honest.

"Because he wrote it," he said. "Four months ago. In a cellar under the tannery, eating soup I made badly, complaining about it in exactly that hand."

* * *

He told it badly, at first. He was a pamphleteer, not a storyteller; he kept reaching for rhetoric and she kept slapping it down.

"Start with the execution," she said. "I held the notice. I know the date. The eighth of September, eighteen forty-six, the courtyard of the district prison, name of Tomas Hartwell among eleven others."

"There were eleven men in that courtyard," Corvin agreed. "And one empty cart."

August's hands went still on the stone.

"Voss needed an execution," Corvin said. "That's the part nobody prints, so listen. Millbridge Ford happened in July. Fourteen strikers at the weir, and Voss ordered the sluices opened, and fourteen people went into the water and didn't come out. You've heard whispers. Everyone has. Whispers don't convict magistrates. Documents do. There was a witness list — men who saw it happen, men who could swear to the order being given. Eleven names. Voss couldn't arrest them all without making the list public, so he did something smarter. He executed them."

"He—" August's voice cracked sideways. "They're alive?"

"One of them is my cousin," Corvin said. "One of them taught me my letters. One of them," and here he paused, and looked at her with something almost apologetic, "is your brother."

The stone was cold under her fingers. She noticed that the way you notice things when the world tilts: irrelevantly, precisely, the chill of the brass, the smell of ink and lamp oil, a loose sliver of type wood digging into her thumb.

"Say it properly," she said. "All of it. No rhetoric."

"So: the tribunal was bribed, the notices were printed, the prison yard was dark, the cart left empty. Eleven men declared dead by the state and breathing quietly in cellars from here to the border. Dead men can't be subpoenaed, August. Dead men can't testify. Voss didn't kill the witnesses. He killed their testimony. As far as the law is concerned, everything any of them might say about Millbridge Ford is spoken by a ghost, and ghosts have no legal standing." He smiled without humor. "It's elegant. I hate how elegant it is."

"And the letters?" She gestured at the bundle. "Why does a dead man write letters?"

"Orders. Reports. The committee corresponds, and it corresponds in his hand, because half the network knows Tomas Hartwell's writing from his organizing days and trusts it on sight. A letter in that hand gets read. A letter in mine gets burned." He rubbed his eyes. "He writes constantly. He's — well. You'll see. He's the funniest corpse in the province. He keeps sending me corrections to my pamphlets. My punctuation, if you can believe it. A dead man critiquing my commas."

August did not laugh. Something in her chest had come undone and was swinging loose, and laughter would have been dangerous anywhere near it.

* * *

Hope, she discovered, arrives in the body before the mind. Her hands had gone warm. Her breath had gone shallow and quick, the way it did running downhill as a girl. Hope was physical. It was disgraceful. It took up space in her ribs like a drunk uncle at a wedding.

Fury arrived more decorously, but it arrived.

"You let us mourn him," she said.

Corvin had the grace not to flinch, though he deserved to flinch. "I wasn't there. It was before me. I only carried what I was given."

"You let my father print his own son's death notice." Her voice rose and she pushed it down, because the patrol still walked its rounds past the loading door and walls in this town had opinions. "Do you understand what that did to him? He was never the same after. His memory — the doctors say the grief accelerated it, whatever it is that's eating his mind. He forgets whole years now. He forgets where he puts his spectacles and then he remembers the ninth of September, the day after, when we got the confirmation in writing, and I watched him go white and quiet and old. You let that happen to protect witnesses. Fine. Noble. And none of you thought to send one anonymous note? One unsigned scrap? *Your son lives, say nothing?*"

"We couldn't risk—"

"You couldn't risk. No. That's the whole architecture of it, isn't it? Every choice in this conspiracy was weighed against the cause, and my family's grief simply didn't tip the scale." She stood up. Her knees were shaking, which annoyed her. "Two years. I lit candles for a living man. I said Kaddish-adjacent things over a dinner table for a living man, and he was alive, and writing letters, and correcting strangers' punctuation."

"To be fair," Corvin said, with the survival instinct of a man who had already been shot at that evening, "your punctuation probably needs work too—"

"Get out of my shop."

"You hid me from a patrol an hour ago."

"And I regret it already. Out."

He didn't get out. He couldn't; he could barely stand, and they both knew it, and the knowledge sat between them like a third person. August turned away so he wouldn't see her face do what it was going to do. She busied herself at the type cases, sliding the drawer open, closing it, performing industry.

"Why tell me at all?" she asked finally, to the wall. "You've kept this for two years. Why break it in my print shop at three in the morning?"

Silence. Then, quieter than before: "Because I came here looking for you, August. Not for the shop. For you." She heard him shift in the chair, fabric dragging, the small sounds of a hurt man rearranging himself. "The committee needs printers abroad. Foreign presses, foreign paper, beyond Voss's reach. And they need someone who can prove what's real — someone who can authenticate documents. Who better than a compositor who's known a man's hand since he was twelve years old?" A pause. "Your brother wouldn't ask you himself. He forbade it. He said — and I'm quoting the letter, I have it memorized, God help me — 'she'll want to help and helping will kill her, keep her in the shop, keep her setting wedding announcements.'"

The fury crested again, but it crested differently now, carrying something else underneath, something that felt horribly like pride.

"He always did think he got to decide things for other people," August said.

"He mentioned you'd say that."

"Of course he did." She laughed despite herself — one short, broken-off sound, like a type stick dropped on stone — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "God. Two years of grief, and the first thing I feel when I find out he's alive is the urge to strangle him personally."

"That seems healthy," Corvin offered.

"It's not healthy. It's the most normal I've felt since September of forty-six." She lowered her hand and looked at him, really looked: the fever-bright eyes, the bloodstained shirt, the stubborn jaw of a man who ran toward patrols for a living. "If this is a lie, if you're some kind of provocateur baiting printers into the open — I will find out, and I will set your confession in fourteen-point type and post it on every door in this town."

"Noted," Corvin said. "Though for the record, fourteen-point is a bit shouty for a confession. Twelve is plenty threatening."

* * *

She should have slept. Instead she sat by the banked stove with the letters in her lap and read them all, twice, three times, learning her brother back into existence line by line.

He complained about soup. He asked after their father with a care that dodged every question directly — *does the shop still take the gazette, does the old man still argue with the almanac* — as though asking plainly might somehow travel backwards through time and undo the empty cart. He signed off each letter the same way: *Yours, deceased,* followed by a small drawn clock with the hands at odd hours, a joke whose meaning she couldn't yet crack and refused to ask about.

Near dawn, a floorboard creaked on the stairs and her father appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, spectacles nowhere in evidence, hair like a struck match.

"Papa. It's early. Go back to bed."

Elias squinted at the papers in her lap, then at the stranger asleep in the customer chair with his boots still on, and August braced herself — for alarm, for confusion, for one of the long grey fogs that swallowed him lately.

Instead her father walked to the composing stone, picked up the topmost letter, and held it at arm's length the way he read everything, lips moving.

Then he began to laugh. Softly, helplessly, tears standing in his eyes, laughing the way a man laughs at a funeral when the grief finally breaks sideways.

"That scoundrel," Elias said. "That absolute scoundrel. Look at those g's." He tapped the page with a trembling finger, and for one shining hour he was entirely, lucidly present. "I set that death notice myself, Augustin. Did I ever tell you? I set it, and the whole time, the whole time, something smelled wrong about it. They sent a clerk with the copy, a clerk who watched my hands like a hawk watches a field mouse, and I thought it was because they feared sympathies. But I know that boy's hand better than scripture, and I remember thinking — I remember thinking the notice was set too neatly. Too much like him. As if someone wanted it believed." He looked up at her, and his clear old eyes were wet. "Child, I believe we have been party to a fraud. A magnificent, criminal fraud."

"Yes, Papa," August managed. "I believe we have."

"Good," said Elias Hartwell, and patted the letter like the cheek of a returning son. "I never liked printing lies. Even necessary ones sit poorly in the case."

He wandered back upstairs, humming, and August sat alone in the grey light with seven impossible letters and a decision taking shape in her chest — unwelcome, unformed, already heavy.

Because if Tomas lived, then he had witnessed Millbridge Ford. If he had witnessed it, his testimony was worth more than every pamphlet Corvin had bled to deliver. And if Voss learned — if the magistrate ever suspected that one of his elegant corpses was walking around with a memory full of drowned strikers —

She looked at the letters, and the letters looked back.

Then the knock came at the loading door: three slow raps, a pause, two fast ones. Corvin woke instantly, all pretense of sleep gone, hand diving for the satchel.

"That's the committee," he whispered. "And August — whatever they ask you, whatever they show you — remember that I told you he's alive before you hear it from anyone wearing a hood."

Through the boards came a voice, low and level and unmistakable, a voice she had last heard across a supper table two years ago, arguing about exams:

"Corvin. Open up. And whoever's in there with you — I can hear a composing stick moving. Nobody handles lead that nervously unless they're a Hartwell."
