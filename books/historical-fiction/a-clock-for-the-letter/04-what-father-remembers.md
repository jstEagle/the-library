## 4. What Father Remembers

There was a trick to sorting type with your eyes half on the clock, and August had never learned it. Elias had. He could set a column of funeral notices in the time it took the kettle to sing, and he had done it for thirty years, and now — now he held a capital H up to the window as though it were a coin of doubtful currency, and asked her, for the third morning running, whether it was an H or an M, because from certain angles, he said, they were twins.

"They're not twins, Papa."

"Everything is twins if you squint hard enough." He set it down anyway, in the H compartment, which meant some part of him still knew, even while arguing. "Your brother used to say that."

"Tomas said nothing of the kind."

"He said something of the kind. It's the same thing at this distance." Elias smiled at the type case the way one smiles at an old dog that has begun to snap. "Memory is just printing, August. You pull a sheet too many times and the letters go soft."

She let that pass, because there was no winning it, and because she had learned — these two years since Tomas, these eighteen months since the forgetting began — to treat her father's mind like a press that jammed: you didn't kick it, you coaxed it, and sometimes you simply walked away and came back when the machine had settled its own opinions.

But that morning, the machine did something unexpected. It cleared.

She would think about it afterward the way one thinks about weather — that it came without warning, out of a sky that had promised nothing. She was at the stone, locking up the chase for the grain merchants' weekly notice, when she heard her father laugh.

Not the small polite laugh he kept for strangers. The real one, the rusty gate of it, the one she hadn't heard since before Tomas.

"August," he called. "Come here and tell me why I'm holding a galley proof of my own handwriting from 1846."

* * *

He was standing at the back of the shop by the morgue — the cabinet of old pages, not the other kind, though God knew the word had taken on weight in this town — and he had in his hands a proof sheet gone brown at the edges. She crossed the room slowly, the way you cross a room toward a bird that has landed indoors and might startle.

"Papa?"

"Sit down, girl, you hover like a mother hen with a mathematics degree." He patted the stool beside him. "I've had an hour of perfect clarity once every season for a year now. Like a clock that strikes true only at midnight. You learn to use the hour." His eyes were bright and terrible in their steadiness. "This morning I woke up and the whole shop was there. Every drawer. Every debt. Every name."

"Elias Hartwell," she said, testing it.

"The very man." He folded himself onto the bench, joints cracking like type being distributed. "Now. Do you remember the death notice?"

The words went through her the way a bodkin goes through paper — clean, quick, and then the hole was just *there*, permanent.

"Tomas's notice," she said.

"Tomas's notice." He turned the proof sheet toward her, and she saw it: the old layout, the black border, the type she knew better than her own hand. *Died in custody, the 14th of March, Thomas Hartwell, printer's son, aged twenty-three.* Below it, the formal line the magistrate's office required. And below *that* — she had forgotten, or had made herself forget — a second paragraph. A statement of regret from the magistrate's office, expressing confidence in the due process of the law.

"I printed that," Elias said quietly. "You were at market. I told you afterward it was routine."

"It wasn't routine."

"No." He smoothed the sheet with both hands, an old habit, the gesture of a man making peace with paper. "It wasn't routine, and I knew it then, and I have been trying all morning to reconstruct what I knew and what I only suspected, and I'll tell you the truth, August: I can't always tell them apart anymore. But this morning I can." He looked up. "Bring me the ledger. The job book, March of '46. Third shelf."

She brought it. He found the page with a finger that did not shake, which frightened her more than shaking would have.

"There," he said. "See the entry? *Notice, per order of the magistrate's office. Copy supplied. Set and run, 400.* Now look underneath."

Beneath the entry, in the margin, in pencil so faint she had to tilt it to the light: *Paid double. No proof pulled.*

"Papa, what does that mean?"

"It means," Elias said, "that a man came to the shop the evening before the notice ran. Not Voss himself. A clerk — a little ferrety man with ink on his cuffs, which offended me professionally, because a clerk who can't keep ink off his cuffs has no business carrying orders. He brought the copy already written. Set copy, ready for the stone, and four hundred reichstaler above my usual rate, and instructions that no proof sheet was to be shown to anyone, including family, and that the edition would be collected by the office directly."

"That's not how notices work."

"That is *exactly* what I told him." Her father's mouth twisted. "I told him a death notice is set from the family's information, that the family comes to the counter and pays and weeps a little and I charge them for the border and nothing extra for the weeping. And he put his hand flat on my counter — flat, like this, see — and he said, *Herr Hartwell, the magistrate admires your work and wishes to spare the family distress.*"

"Spare us distress."

"At quadruple rate. With a clause about due process that nobody had asked me to set before." He tapped the penciled note. "And then he said the thing I have been chewing on for two years without knowing I was chewing on it. He said: *And you'll want to be careful with the boy's effects. Some things burn cleaner than others.*"

The shop was very quiet. Outside, someone led a horse past at a walk, and the hooves sounded like slow setting of type, one letter at a time.

"What things," August said. It was not really a question.

"That's what I asked." Elias closed the ledger gently, as if it might bruise. "He didn't answer. He paid, and left, and the next day the edition was collected by two soldiers who stood in my doorway looking at everything, and I printed the notice, and I never pulled a proof, and I told you it was routine because — " He stopped. Started again. "Because your mother was six months gone with grief already over the arrest, and because a man tells himself the story that lets him keep working, and because — " he gestured at his own temple, rueful, terrible — "because some of us have machinery that takes things away whether we consent or no. Maybe I hid it where even I couldn't find it. Who's to say. The Lord, probably, and He doesn't pull proofs either."

"Papa." August took his hand. It was warm and spotted and steady. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you I may have helped bury your brother twice," Elias said. "Once in ink and once in silence. And this morning, for one clear hour, I remember enough to know that I don't remember everything, and that the not-remembering was *arranged*. A ferrety clerk. Double pay. No proof. Effects that burn clean." He shook his head slowly. "When a man offers you money to print a thing quickly and quietly and to forget the paperwork, August, he isn't sparing you distress. He's buying your part in it. And I took the money and bought bread with it and I cannot even properly repent because half of me lost the receipt."

* * *

She made tea, because her hands wanted employment and because Elias, in his lucid hours, liked the ritual of it — the warming of the pot, the precise spoonfuls, the way he supervised her pouring like a foreman though he'd taught her none of it, she'd learned it watching. For a few minutes neither spoke. Rain had started against the loading door, soft and businesslike.

"You thought it was grief," she said at last. "The forgetting."

"I thought it was age. Then I thought it was grief. Then I stopped thinking about it, which is the luxury of the afflicted." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "Though I'll say this — there are mornings I wake and the shop is whole but the year is wrong. I look for Tomas and I am angry with him for being late to supper, and the anger is so *specific*, August. So well-made. Like it was set from good copy." He smiled without humor. "A man's memory shouldn't have gaps with straight edges. Gaps should be ragged, like torn paper. Mine are cut with a knife and a rule."

She thought of the letters hidden under the floorboard beneath the stone — Corvin's satchel, Tomas's looping g's, alive, alive, alive — and kept her face still. Two days she had known. Two days she had carried it alone in the house with him, and now he handed her this.

"There's something else," Elias said. "In the lucid hours I get the whole ledger, debts included. And one of the debts is a lie I told you."

"Papa—"

"Hear it out; the hour is short and I don't know when the clerk comes back to collect." He set down the cup. "The night after the notice ran, soldiers came back. Late. They said the magistrate's office required the *forme* — the locked type of the notice itself — for record purposes. Nobody requires a forme for record purposes; you melt it or distribute it, everyone knows. But they took it. And I let them take it, and I told you they'd come about the tax assessment, because you were nineteen and furious at the whole world already and I thought — I thought if I gave you one more thing to be furious about you'd do something they'd punish you for." He looked at her steadily. "So I lied. Small lie. Kind lie. Same ink as the big ones, though, isn't it? Just less of it."

"Why would they want the forme?"

"To prove it was set and printed and done, I expect. Or—" he hesitated, and in the hesitation she saw the young man he had been, the one who'd apprenticed in a city shop and come home full of Latin and opinions— "or to make sure it could never be compared against anything. A notice is evidence, August. Of a death, yes, but also of a *printing*. If someone ever claimed the death was false, the first question is: who set the type, when, from whose copy? The forme answers those questions. They removed the questions."

The rain picked up. Somewhere down the street a drum was practicing — the student corps, the town murmured, drilling in the meadow past the tannery, boys with sticks and ideas. Elias cocked his head at it.

"There'll be trouble," he said. "There's always trouble when drums come to river towns. In '30 there were drums, and before I was born there were drums." He refilled his own cup, spilling slightly, and the spill seemed to cost him something, because his hand, which had been steady all morning, wavered now at the edge of the hour. "August. Listen to me while I'm still myself to listen."

"I'm listening."

"If someone comes asking about that notice — if anyone, ever, comes asking — you tell them the truth about me. That I was coerced, or fooled, or cowardly, whichever word fits the facts best. Don't polish me. I've seen what polishing does; it makes a lie you can read in the dark." He gripped her wrist, suddenly fierce. "Promise me. A printer's worst sin isn't a bad page. It's a bad page you refuse to correct."

"I promise," she said, and meant it, and hated that meaning it required her to keep hiding Tomas from him — the correction he demanded, withheld by his own daughter.

He seemed to sag all at once, like a form going loose in the chase. The brightness drained from his eyes not dramatically but the way water leaves a lock, level by level.

"Mm," he said, looking at the ledger. "Whose book is this? These aren't our rates." He peered at the penciled marginalia. "*No proof pulled* — who writes things in margins? Ruins the resale value." He looked up at her, mild and vague and kindly confused, and asked the question he asked most mornings now:

"Is Tomas coming by today? The boy's late. He knows I don't like the type left out overnight."

August looked at her father across the table — the man who had buried her brother in ink, who had hidden the burial from her, who had lost the receipt for his own guilt — and heard herself say, gently, "Not today, Papa. He'll come when he comes."

Afterward she stood a long time at the window, watching the rain stitch the street together, turning over the arithmetic of it: a coerced notice, a vanished forme, a clerk who spoke of burning things clean. Her father had set the lie that killed Tomas. And somewhere out in that rain, the living proof of it was fevered and hiding, waiting to hear whether the Hartwell shop was a refuge or a trap.

Two knocks at the loading door. Soft. The rhythm Corvin used.

She wiped her hands on her apron, and went to answer.
