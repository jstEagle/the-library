## 5. The Committee of the Undersigned

Corvin came for her at dusk, which August thought was theatrical of him but did not say so, because he had brought a fish barrel on a handcart and a look of a man who has been argued with by professionals and lost.

"Get in," he said.

"In the barrel?"

"In the barrel."

August looked at the barrel. It smelled of herring and old salt, and it had, she noted, two air holes bored near the bottom with what must have been considerable optimism about her willingness to use it.

"I want you to know," she said, pulling on the coat her father had mended twice that winter, "that I once smuggled a full ream of paper past the excise men in exactly this fashion, except the barrel was smaller and so was I, and I swore on my mother's grave I would never do it again."

"Your mother's grave is in the churchyard where the patrol drinks its coffee at seven," Corvin said. "Which is why we're going now."

It was, she had to admit, a reasonable answer. She got into the barrel.

The ride through the town was long and bruising and gave her a great deal of time to think, most of it spent on the same thought she had been turning over for three days since the letters: *Tomas is alive.* It did not behave like other thoughts. Other thoughts sat still and let you examine them. This one moved. It slipped out of her hands every time she reached for it and came back wearing different faces — Tomas at nine years old with ink to his elbows because he'd insisted on helping pull the Tuesday edition; Tomas at seventeen arguing politics with their father over the composing stone until Elias threatened to set the boy's opinions in eight-point and print them as a correction; Tomas walking to the scaffold in a woodcut she had printed herself and hated herself for printing well.

The cart stopped. The barrel tipped — gently, someone had hold of it — and Corvin's face appeared above hers against a sky going the color of pewter.

"We're here," he said. "Don't tell anyone I apologized in advance for the smell."

* * *

"Here" was a cooperage on Tanner's Lane, disused, or pretending to be. Corvin led her down an outside stair, then through a door that opened onto darkness and the smell she knew better than any smell on earth besides bread: ink, oil, damp paper, hot metal. A press was running somewhere below, its rhythm muffled but unmistakable, that steady exhale of the platen that she had fallen asleep to as a child the way other children fell asleep to lullabies.

Then a lantern was unshuttered, and she saw it.

The cellar was long and low, brick vaulting overhead furred with white mineral bloom, and in the middle of it stood a press — not a toy, not a jobbing press cobbled from spare parts, but a proper Columbia, iron eagle at its crown, its bed gleaming with use. Type cabinets lined one wall. A drying rack hung with sheets. Six people, maybe seven, turned to look at her, and none of them smiled.

August had spent her whole life in print shops and had never once been frightened in one. She was frightened now, and understood that the fear was not of the room but of what the room meant. Her father's shop was a business. This was an argument.

"You're the sister," said a woman from beside the press.

She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, sleeves rolled over forearms that had clearly thrown a lever ten thousand times, gray hair cut short in a way August had only ever seen on widows who had decided mourning was no excuse for impracticality. She wiped her hands on a rag with the unhurried thoroughness of someone who believed clean hands were a moral obligation.

"August Hartwell," August said.

"Ilsa Reiner." The woman did not offer to shake. "Corvin says you read the letters."

"He says a great many things," said a thin young man near the type cabinet. "He said the barrels were watertight."

"The barrels *were* watertight," Corvin said, with dignity. "You are not a barrel."

Ilsa ignored this. She crossed to a shelf, took down a ledger — an ordinary shop ledger, the kind Elias kept his accounts in, red spine, marbled edges gone soft — and opened it on the composing stone. "Come here."

August came. The lantern light fell across the page, and there, among a list of names written in several different hands — names with columns after them for sums and tasks and dates — she saw it.

*T. Hartwell.*

Not printed. Written, in the looping, leaning, impatient hand she had learned alongside, the g's that swung below the line like bells, the t's crossed with a single decisive stroke as if the letter could not be bothered to wait. He had signed his name the way he did everything, quickly and completely. There were entries beside it. Dates. Deliveries. The last one was eleven days old.

Her hand went out before she decided anything and touched the name. The paper was real. The ink had dried two years' worth of ago and eleven days' worth of now.

"He wrote that himself?" Her voice came out strange, small, as if it had already been set in type and someone else was reading it aloud.

"He writes his own name," Ilsa said. "We insist on it. Everything else can be copied, forged, stolen. A man keeps his own signature. It's the last thing they can't take without killing him." She closed the ledger, and August's fingers had to withdraw. "Where is he?"

"Not here," Ilsa said. "He's never here. That is the arrangement, and the arrangement keeps him breathing. You will not ask me again."

"He's my brother."

"Yes," Ilsa said, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes, some old grief filed away but not discarded. "And I buried a brother too, Frau Hartwell, and a husband, and I would trade every press in this cellar to have either of them back at a table complaining about the soup. Which is precisely why we do not make exceptions. Grief makes exceptions. The work cannot afford them." She looked August up and down, not unkindly, but the way a printer looks at a font — counting how many characters, checking for damaged sorts. "Corvin says you hid him from a patrol and asked no questions until the danger passed. Corvin says your father taught you the trade and you can set a page faster than Weber here."

"It's true," Weber admitted glumly. "I've made peace with it."

"Corvin says a lot of things," August said. "You trust him?"

"No," said Ilsa, and several people in the cellar laughed, and the laugh broke something open in the room, let the warmth in around the edges. "But he's usually right, and when he's wrong he's wrong loudly, which is nearly as useful." She picked up the rag again, wiped hands that were already clean. "You want to know if you can belong to this. You haven't asked yet, but you want to know. So I'll tell you what belonging costs, and then I'll give you one errand, and we'll see what kind of metal you are before I decide whether to cast you."

* * *

The errand came the following night, and it arrived, like most bad ideas, dressed as a simple one.

"There's a coffin," Ilsa said, "going to the cemetery at Lichtenthal tomorrow morning. Undertaker named Probst owes us more than he wants to. Under the false floor of the coffin is a stereotype plate — the first fourteen pages of an account we're compiling. You will ride with the coffin. You will be a grieving cousin. At the cemetery gate there is a mason, old Ferenc, who will take the delivery. You will hand him nothing, say nothing, do nothing but stand there looking sad, which I'm told runs in your family."

"And what am I grieving?"

"A cousin. Anna Weiss. Died of the fever, poor girl, twenty-six years old. There's a card with the details. Memorize it and burn it. If anyone asks, you weep. If they ask questions, you weep harder. Nobody interrogates a crying woman at a funeral; it's the one courtesy this century allows us."

August studied her. "And if they open the coffin?"

Ilsa met her eyes steadily. "Then you were smuggling seditious material, and you'll go to the house of correction, and we will all be very sorry, and the plate will have been worth it. That is the arithmetic. I won't lie to you about it. Lies are for Voss; among printers, we deal in exact figures."

"That's a terrible recruiting speech."

"It's the only honest one I have." Ilsa paused, and then, in a different voice, quieter: "Your brother stood where you're standing six months ago. I gave him the same speech. He said, 'My sister will do it better than me, she's steadier.' He talks about you. I want you to know that, whatever else happens tonight. In our line of work, being talked about is how we love people."

August looked away, because her eyes had decided to do something unsanctioned, and she had been raised to believe one should never cry in front of a press — the humidity alone.

* * *

The coffin rode in an open cart, and August rode beside it with her hands folded in her lap and her face arranged into grief, which required less acting than she expected, because the morning was gray and the road ran along the river, and the river was low, and she could see the ford at Millbridge in the distance, brown stones showing like the backs of sleeping animals, and she found herself thinking: *he stood there. He saw it there.* Fourteen people had gone under that water on a magistrate's order, and her brother had watched, and lived, and died, and lived.

The card in her pocket said *Anna Weiss, fever, twenty-six*. She recited it to herself the way she memorized copy. Anna Weiss. Fever. Twenty-six. She was beginning to genuinely mourn Anna Weiss, who had never existed, when the cemetery gate came in sight, and with it a problem.

There was a checkpoint. Not the usual lone gendarme picking his teeth — three of them, plus a sergeant, plus a table set up across the road as if funerals were now subject to tariff.

"Mourners to the left," the sergeant called. "Coffins come to the table."

August's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her throat. She guided the cart forward, dismounted, and wept. This part was easy. Three days of letters and cellars and her brother's handwriting had given her a deep reserve of material.

"Name of the deceased?"

"Anna Weiss." Her voice cracked on purpose. "My cousin. The fever took her. She was twenty-six."

The sergeant consulted a list, running a thick finger down it. "Weiss. Weiss. No Weiss."

"She died Tuesday," August said. "Perhaps your list is Tuesday's." And then — God help her, the shopkeeper's instinct, the printer's instinct, the instinct of a woman who had spent her life correcting errors — she added, "May I see it?"

The sergeant looked up. For one long moment the whole machinery of her life balanced on the point of that finger. Then, behind him, the undertaker Probst appeared, sweating through his black coat, waving a document. "Sergeant, Sergeant, burial permit, all in order, the family is waiting, the ground is dug and the fee is paid—" and there was money moving between hands with the smooth discretion of long practice, and the sergeant, who had perhaps seen the fee's cousins before, grunted and waved the cart through.

August walked beside the coffin through the gate, delivered it to old Ferenc the mason, said nothing, handed over nothing, and stood looking sad while a stranger pried up a false floor within arm's reach of a gendarme. Then she walked back out the gate, mounted the cart, and did not begin shaking until they were half a mile clear, at which point she shook so thoroughly she nearly fell off the seat, and laughed while doing it, a wild unlovely laugh that she clapped both hands over and could not entirely suppress.

She understood then, with the clarity of the newly converted, what Ilsa had known all along: that this was the seduction. Not the cause — the cause was grim and just and heavy. The seduction was this feeling. The feeling of mattering at a scale larger than one shop, one town, one life. Of history coming off the press with your fingerprints in the margin.

She had always believed revolutions happened in papers printed elsewhere, for other people. Now her fingertips still smelled of coffin varnish and gunpowder-adjacent fear, and she wanted, with an appetite that alarmed her, to do it again.

* * *

Ilsa was waiting in the cellar, and did not ask how it went, because the answer was standing in front of her upright and smelling of herring-adjacent adventure.

"You added a step," Ilsa said. "Probst says you improvised at the checkpoint."

"You have a man inside the checkpoint?"

"I have a man inside everything eventually. You asked to see his list. That was either very brave or very stupid."

"My father says those are the same thing in this trade."

For the first time, Ilsa Reiner smiled. It changed her whole face, took twenty years off it and put ten back in scars. "Your father," she said, "printed the death notice for your brother. Did he tell you that?"

August went still. "He set it. I know he set it. He doesn't remember it — he remembers almost nothing now —"

"He remembers more than he says, I think, and says less than he knows." Ilsa turned to the ledger again, opened it, dipped a pen, and held it out across the composing stone. "We're called the Committee of the Undersigned. Old joke; the authorities think it means signatories to a petition. We let them. Everyone who works here signs. Not a loyalty oath — I don't believe in those, they select for liars. Just a name, so that everyone knows whose risk this is. Yours as much as mine, from tonight onward. Voss doesn't distinguish between the pen and the press."

August took the pen. Her hand hovered over the empty line beneath her brother's written name — close enough that the loops of his letters almost touched the space where hers would fall.

"If I sign," she said slowly, "and you find him — when he comes — he'll see it. He'll know I'm in."

"He already knows," Ilsa said gently. "Why do you think he hasn't come? A man can refuse an enemy. Refusing his little sister, who he taught to set type by putting her fingers on the wrong letters first — that he can't do. He's staying away to keep you out." She tapped the ledger. "Sign, and take that choice away from him. Or don't sign, and go home, and we'll never trouble you again, and you can spend the rest of your life setting wedding announcements wondering."

August signed. The pen scratched, the ink took, and there they were together on one line of a secret book: *T. Hartwell, A. Hartwell*, side by side the way they used to stand at the case, his hand guiding hers, before history decided to happen to them personally.

She had barely blotted the page when Corvin came down the stair three at a time, breathless, hatless, and pale in a way that had nothing to do with barrels.

"Ilsa," he said. "Brandt's men were at the cooperage an hour ago. Asking about deliveries. He didn't search — but he *counted the carts*." He caught his breath. "And there's more. Someone left a note at your father's shop, August. Under the door. Addressed to you — in your brother's hand."

Ilsa's smile was quite gone. "What does it say?"

August's own voice sounded far away when she answered, because she had read it three times already and knew it whole:

"It says: '*Stop digging, little bird. They watch the ones who love me. — T.*'"
