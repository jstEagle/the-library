## 6. Ink Under the Fingernails

The errand came wrapped in a shroud, which Ilsa Reiner seemed to consider the natural order of things.

"You'll take it to the cabinetmaker on Wren Lane," she said, laying her hand flat on a wooden box no bigger than a hymnal. "He's expecting a delivery of hinges. You are delivering hinges. Say it enough times and even Brandt's men will believe it."

"And what am I actually delivering?"

"A hinge," Ilsa said, with the faintest smile, "between one world and the next."

I had decided, somewhere in the small hours between leaving the cellar press and waking in my own narrow bed above the shop, that I did not trust these people — and that I would go back anyway. This struck me as an entirely reasonable position. A person can distrust a stove and still put her hands on it when the house is cold.

Father was at the composing stone when I dressed, arranging type into words that dissolved under his fingers like frost. He looked up at me with the pleasant, unfocused courtesy he offered strangers and sometimes, now, his own daughter.

"Morning, miss. Is my daughter about? August? She does the sorting."

"She's here," I said. "She's always here."

"Good girl, that one." He returned to his letters. "Terrible sorter, though. Lets her mind wander off with the em quads."

There are two kinds of grief, I have since learned: the kind that arrives like weather, sudden and loud, and the kind that comes to live with you and pays no rent. Father's mind had taken up residence in some earlier decade where Tomas still needed his supper and I was still learning to set my first sentence without dropping half a case. Some mornings I wanted to shake him. Most mornings I wanted only to sit down beside him inside whatever year he was living in, because the rent there was cheaper than grief.

"I'm out on deliveries," I told him, which was true in the way that matters least.

* * *

Wren Lane smelled of glue and shavings and, beneath it, the river — the town's oldest perfume, equal parts trade and rot. The cabinetmaker's shop stood at the end of the row, its windows fogged, its sign reading simply *MERTENS*, the final S hanging by one screw like a man reluctant to let go of the edge.

Herr Mertens himself was a slab of a man with sawdust in his eyebrows and the gentle voice people seem to acquire around coffins, as though the wood might be sleeping. He received me without surprise, which unsettled me more than any interrogation could have.

"The hinges," he said gravely.

"The hinges," I agreed.

He led me through the workshop, past coffins in various stages of becoming — bare boxes, planed boards, one polished affair with brass rails that looked ready to attend its own funeral with some pride. At the back sat a plain pine coffin, unadorned, destined for some family that could afford nothing better than pine and prayer.

"For the Kessler boy," Mertens said quietly, catching my glance. "Fever. Eleven years old. The family wanted him shipped home to his mother's village before the roads close for winter."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and felt immediately the strangeness of standing condolences beside contraband.

"He'd have gone today regardless." Mertens opened the false bottom of the coffin with the ease of a man lifting a lid off soup. "Your cargo goes here, under the boards, against the head panel. Weight it right and it rides like sorrow — nobody questions what a coffin carries."

I knelt and fitted the box into the cavity. It settled with a sound like a book closing. My hands were steady; my fingernails were not clean. There is ink under every printer's fingernails, permanently, a black crescent moon on each finger that scrubbing only fades. I thought of that ink traveling now under a dead boy's coffin toward some other press, some other pair of hands, and how the whole conspiracy of truth seemed to rest on people being willing to carry things they could not read.

"What's in it?" I asked Mertens. "Truly."

"Better you don't know." He closed the false bottom and ran his palm across the pine, an oddly tender gesture. "That's not an insult, miss. It's arithmetic. A man who doesn't know can be surprised but not betrayed."

"A man who doesn't know," I said, "can also spend her whole life printing other people's truths without ever once knowing whether they were worth dying for."

Mertens looked at me then — really looked, the way a man appraises a joint he's about to cut. "You're Hartwell's girl."

"I'm Hartwell," I said.

"That too." He lifted the coffin lid and lowered it again over the emptiness that was not empty. "For what it's worth — yes. Worth it. Every plank of it." Then, because German men of his generation are constitutionally incapable of ending on sincerity: "Mind the cart wheel on the bridge cobbles. The boy's earned a smooth ride."

* * *

The route to the shipping office took me past the tannery and along the canal path, and I drove the handcart slowly, a grieving sister in a black shawl borrowed from Frau Ostmann upstairs, who had lent it without asking questions because in this town a black shawl is passed door to door like sugar.

The morning was doing its best. Weak sun on wet cobbles. Gulls arguing over something in the canal. Two students from the gymnasium walked past deep in animated argument about Bakunin, pronouncing the name with the reverence boys reserve for names they learned last week. Revolution was in the air the way rain is in the air — everyone sniffing at it, nobody carrying an umbrella.

At the corner of the tannery wall I stopped to fix the shawl where the wind had pulled it loose, and that is when I saw him.

He stood in the mouth of the alley opposite, half in shadow, wearing a hooded coat against the damp. He was watching the coffin cart — watching *me* — with an intensity I felt on my skin before I understood it, the way you feel someone's gaze cross a room. And though the hood shadowed his face, though he stood differently, heavier in the shoulders, older — I knew the angle of his jaw the way I knew the weight of a ten-point em. You set type beside a man's name for twenty years, you learn the shape of everything he was made of.

"Tomas," I said. Not loudly. The word barely cleared my own teeth.

But he heard it. Of course he heard it. And he flinched — I saw the flinch travel his whole frame like a crack through ice — and then he turned and went into the alley at a walk that very badly wanted to be a run.

I do not remember deciding to follow. The cart stayed where it was; the coffin stayed; the boy inside it stayed, poor mite, having his last journey delayed by the living's bad manners. My feet carried me into the alley after that retreating hood, past stacked tanning barrels, and my heart was doing something complicated and ugly — hope and fury braided together so tight I couldn't tell which strand was pulling.

"Tomas Hartwell!" I said, louder now. "You stop, or so help me—"

He stopped. At the far end of the alley, where it opened onto the canal steps, he stopped, and for one moment he half turned, and the hood shifted, and I saw his face entire.

Older. Bearded. A scar at the hairline I didn't know. But his eyes were our mother's eyes, and I had spent two years mourning them.

Two years. Two years of visiting a grave that held — what? Sandbags? An empty box and official lies? Two years of setting the memorial notice for the anniversary edition while he was presumably alive within a day's walk, breathing, eating, letting his sister grieve on a schedule convenient to conspirators?

He raised one hand — not a wave. A gesture of *stop*, or perhaps of *forgive*, or perhaps merely the reflex of a man pushing away a thing he wanted too much. And then he went down the canal steps and along the water and was gone among the barges, moving with the practiced speed of someone who has fled from many alleys and gotten good at it.

I stood in the tannery stink and discovered that I was shaking — not with fear. With a rage so clean it frightened me.

* * *

I delivered the coffin. I want that recorded. Whatever else I was, whatever I became in those weeks, I was a woman who finished the job. I wheeled the cart to the shipping office, signed Mertens's name in the manifest with a fair imitation of his clumsy capitals, and watched the Kessler boy go onto the barge with his secret cargo riding above his small heart like a second soul. Then I walked back to Wren Lane, returned the shawl to Frau Ostmann, who pressed a honey cake on me because she believed I'd been at a funeral and grief requires feeding, and ate the cake standing in the street, furiously, all of it.

Then I went to find Corvin.

He was at the cellar, of course, hunched over the little press like a man warming himself at a stove that gave no heat. He looked up when I came down the stairs and something in my face must have told him everything, because he said, "Ah," and set down the roller, and added, "I'm sorry," before I had said a word.

"You knew." My voice came out low and level, which surprised me; I'd expected shouting. "You've known since the night you fell through my loading door."

"Yes."

"He's alive."

"Yes."

"And he watched me grieve him. He read—" My voice cracked finally, on the specific grievance, the smallest and largest one. "Every year on the anniversary Papa prints a notice. In memoriam. I compose it myself. I choose the border ornament. Last year I chose lilies, because he liked lilies, and he was *alive*, and somewhere out there he may have *seen it*—"

"August." Corvin rose, hands out, palms forward, the posture of a man approaching a dog he suspected of biting. "It wasn't cruelty. It was the whole architecture. Voss believes Tomas died at Millbridge Ford. That belief is the only reason fourteen families have their compensation, the only reason the witnesses sleep, the only reason *he* sleeps. If Voss learns otherwise, Tomas isn't a ghost to be haunted — he's a loose thread to be cut. Everything we're doing hangs on his death being real."

"Then why tell me at all?" I demanded. "Why break it over my kitchen floor if the lie was so precious?"

Corvin had the grace to look away. "Because you deserved a brother more than we deserved a secret. And because—" he hesitated, and I have thought many times since about that hesitation, and what it cost him, "—because Ilsa wants you. Your shop, your presses, your father's registration. We need you inside the lawful world, August. And I judged — perhaps wrongly — that a woman works harder for people whose lies she shares."

"So you made me complicit in my own grief."

"No," he said quietly. "We made you free of it. There's a difference. It's just not a comfortable one."

I turned away from him. The cellar press sat in its corner, squat and patient, and above it hung their roster, that absurd roll call of the undersigned, and halfway down it, in his looping hand, *Tomas Hartwell*. Alive in ink. Dead in every public record. Resurrected in an alley long enough to run from his own sister.

"Where is he kept?" I asked.

"Not kept. Hidden. There's a difference too, though I grant it's thinner."

"Where."

"August—"

"*Where.*"

Corvin was silent a long moment. Down here the river seeped through the stones, and somewhere overhead a cart rattled past, ordinary life going about its business above our conspiracy of cellars.

"I can't," he said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because he asked. He asked one thing of us — that you not be brought to him until *he* was ready. He says—" Corvin stopped, restarted. "He says he can't survive your eyes yet. Those were nearly his words. A man who faced Voss's dragoons and won't face his sister."

"Then he's a coward," I said, and heard how untrue it was even as I said it, and said it anyway, because anger is a garment and I was cold.

"He's ashamed," Corvin said. "Which is worse for him, believe me. Cowards get to stop running. Ashamed men carry the alley with them everywhere."

I left without another word. On the stairs I met Ilsa coming down, and she stepped aside with that unnerving composure of hers, and said only:

"Well?"

"He ran," I said. "From his own ghost. I'm apparently the most frightening thing in this town."

Ilsa studied me the way Mertens had studied the coffin — appraising what lay underneath. "Anger keeps," she said at last. "Grief spoils. I'd rather work with your anger." And she went down into the dark toward her press, satisfied, as though she'd concluded a transaction.

* * *

That evening the shop was warm and ordinary, criminally ordinary. Father sat by the stove reading yesterday's paper aloud to no one, skipping lines and improving others, so that the grain report contained a passage about a horse he had once owned. I set an order of handbills for the chandler, mindless work, good work, the kind that lets a body pretend its hands are busy with the only things that matter.

But my eyes kept going to the case marked with the worn brass plate: **T**. Half a drawer of capital T's, each one identical, each one him, and none of them running away.

I thought about alleys. About a raised hand. About the fact that he had been near the coffin cart at all — near *me* — which meant either coincidence, which I did not believe in, or that he had come to look at me the way you look at a lit window from a cold street. He had come to see his sister, and lost his nerve, and fled, and I was supposed to file that under *shame* and wait politely for his readiness.

I had waited two years without knowing it. I declined to wait two more.

And it occurred to me — sorting T's in the firelight, father murmuring about his horse, the town outside muttering itself toward some terrible spring — it occurred to me with the calm of a composed line locking into its chase, that I now knew how to reach him. Not through cellars or committees or men with fever-bright eyes. Through the one medium Tomas Hartwell had never been able to refuse.

Ink. Words in a known hand. A message set where only he would understand it, printed where only he would dare come looking.

I was still turning the idea over, feeling its edges like hot type fresh from the mold, when boots sounded on the loading step — three pairs, unhurried, official — and a folded paper slid under the door with the government's seal upon it, and Father looked up from his newspaper and said, pleasantly, "Are we expecting the militia, August? Shall I put the kettle on?"
