## 7. Voss Summons the Printers

The summons arrived the way bad news always arrived in Hartwell & Son — by a boy who didn't want to be there.

He was maybe eleven, wearing the oversized sash of a municipal runner, and he stood at the loading door holding the envelope at arm's length as though it might bite him. August wiped her hands on her apron and took it. The seal was heavy wax, the color of old blood.

"Magistrate's compliments," the boy recited, staring at his shoes. "All printing presses within the town limits are to be registered and inspected. Your shop is first on the list." He paused. "First, ma'am. They said first."

"They said that twice, did they?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Well. You delivered it very bravely. Tell your mother you were brave."

He left at a run. August turned the envelope over twice before opening it, as if the back might contain better news than the front. It did not.

*By order of Magistrate Voss: all presses, type, plates, and printed matter to be made available for inspection by the Office of Public Order, Tuesday next, nine o'clock in the morning.*

Tuesday next was tomorrow.

She read it three times while her heart performed a slow, deliberate drop through the floorboards, and then she folded the letter into quarters, tucked it into her apron pocket, and walked — not ran, walked; running attracted eyes — down the cellar stairs where Corvin was mending a shoe by candlelight and pretending, badly, that he was only a man who liked cellars.

"Corvin."

He looked up. He'd been in the shop six days now and had learned to read her face the way she read galley proofs: quickly, and with growing dread.

"What happened?"

"Voss is inspecting the presses. Ours first." She set the letter on the crate beside him. "Everything from the cellar press comes up here tonight. Every plate. Every proof."

"The coffin shipment isn't back yet—"

"Then we have until nine o'clock tomorrow morning, and so does every plate in this building."

* * *

They worked through the night, and it was the kind of work August would remember afterward not as fear but as strange intimacy — she and Corvin passing plates hand to hand up the cellar stairs, wrapped in printer's felt like infants, laying them out on the composing stone in candlelight. There were eleven of them. Eleven engraved plates, each one a small metal argument that Voss had ordered strikers drowned at Millbridge Ford, each one bearing dates, names, the testimony of a man officially two years dead.

Her father came down once, in his nightshirt, drawn by the light under the door.

"Are we printing?" he asked hopefully. "I could stand at the press. I'm good at standing at the press."

"Not tonight, Papa. Go back to bed."

He considered this. His eyes fell on the plates gleaming on the stone, and for one lucid, terrible moment something moved behind them — recognition, or its ghost.

"I printed a death notice once," he said quietly. "Two years ago. For a Hartwell." He looked at her. "I never set my own son's name before. I would have remembered setting it. I think I would have remembered."

August's throat closed. "Go to bed, Papa."

He went. And she stood among the evidence of everything, and understood that tomorrow the law was coming into her house, and that her father's memory was a room full of unlocked drawers, and that any inspector with half an eye and an hour's patience could open every one of them.

The false bottoms were her idea. The type cases were old — her grandfather's, beechwood, with frames deep enough to spare a finger's width below the true bottom. Her father had used the space once, she knew, to hide brandy from her mother during a Lent neither of them observed seriously. August pried up the bottoms of four cases, lined the cavities with felt, and laid the plates flat among them, then replaced the wood and shingled the cases high with type so that no weight or knock would sound wrong.

By four in the morning her fingers were bleeding at two cuticles and she had invented three new swear words, which she did not say aloud because her father slept above and had once washed her mouth with lye soap for considerably less.

Corvin surveyed the finished work. "You know," he said, "for someone who told me revolutions are ink for other papers, you're getting remarkably good at this."

"That was before the papers came for me."

* * *

Inspector Brandt arrived at nine o'clock precisely, which August later learned was the most punctual thing about him.

She had expected a thug. What climbed down from the municipal carriage was a thin, damp-palmed man of perhaps forty, in a coat slightly too big for him, carrying a leather folio he held the way a nervous man holds a hymnal in a church he doesn't attend. Behind him came two constables who looked bored in the aggressive, professional manner of men paid by the hour.

"Hartwell print shop," Brandt said, consulting the folio. "You are... the daughter?"

"I am the shop."

"Ah." He blinked at her. "Yes. Well. The registration order—"

"Is on the composing stone, Inspector, copied out fair and signed. Press particulars, founding dates, inventory of type by weight. My father keeps meticulous records when reminded. I remind him."

Brandt looked at the document. Something in his shoulders loosened by a degree, the relief of a man who had feared he would have to *do things*. "This is... thorough."

"We're printers, Inspector. Thoroughness is the trade."

And so the inspection began, and August discovered that watching a search of your own home is a particular torture: every drawer he opened was innocent, but the space between drawers was not, and she had to stand there composing her face into polite boredom while Brandt's long white fingers drifted along the shelves like a man reading braille.

He was not, she decided, a fool. That was the problem. Thugs could be fed drink and flattery; fools could be misdirected with a pretty broadside. But Brandt examined the press frame with genuine knowledge, ran a thumbnail along the ink ducts, noted — she saw him note — that the rollers were dressed for a job though the day's work sat finished and dry.

"You've been printing at night," he said, not accusingly. Almost conversationally.

"My father wakes at odd hours. Printing settles him." This was even true. "Better than laudanum, and cheaper."

"Mm." He wrote something in the folio. She could not see what.

* * *

Brandt, in his own private accounting of the morning, was deeply unhappy.

Not with the Hartwells — the daughter was courteous and the father, when produced, was a sweet, ruined gentleman who offered to show Brandt how the tympan worked and then forgot midway through what a tympan was, patting the machine fondly and calling it "the old girl." Brandt had an uncle like that. It had not made him suspicious; it had made him homesick.

No, what made him unhappy was the arithmetic.

Eleven plates missing from the cellar press on Wexler Lane — the raid there was scheduled for Thursday, but Brandt had seen the requisition, because nobody guarded paper from a junior inspector with clean boots and no ambition. Eleven plates of a size to print a pamphlet, and here was a shop with false-bottomed type cases.

He had found the first one by accident. A case on the lower shelf sat proud of the wall by the width of a fingernail, and when he'd pulled it out to check the foundry mark, the bottom had given the smallest sound — not a hollow sound, nothing so crude — but a *dense* one. Type rings when you shake the case. These rang differently. Lower. With felt between the layers, perhaps. Or lead sheeting. Or—

He had put the case back exactly where it sat and said nothing.

Because here was the trouble with being Brandt: he could report the case, and there would be a second visit, and soldiers, and a girl with ink-stained cuticles standing very straight while men with rifles went through her dying father's life work. And if he was wrong about the case — and he might be, he *hoped* he was wrong, he had built a career on hoping he was wrong — then he had summoned soldiers to a print shop over an acoustical suspicion, and Inspector Weiss would make a joke about it at dinner, and the joke would be Brandt-shaped and permanent.

And if he was right?

If he was right, reporting it meant arresting a girl who had offered him coffee, and an old man who called presses "the old girl," and finding in those hidden plates the truth about Millbridge Ford, which everyone in the Office suspected and no one in the Office said, because saying it was a career executed at dawn.

Brandt stood in the doorway of Hartwell & Son, folio under his arm, and wrote his report in his head: *Presses conform to registration. Printed matter unremarkable. Proprietor cooperative. Recommend no further action.*

It was, he told himself, technically accurate. He had recommended no further action. He simply had not mentioned the case.

The distinction felt thin as foil and just as easy to see through. He climbed into the carriage and gave the driver the next address, and spent the whole ride doing what he did best, which was being a minor man negotiating with his conscience and losing slowly, on installments.

* * *

The constables left at noon. August bolted the loading door behind them and leaned against it and breathed for what felt like the first time since the boy with the sash.

Her father emerged from the back room buttoning his vest over his nightshirt, having apparently concluded the inspection required formal dress at some point after everyone left.

"How did I do?" he asked.

"You were perfect."

"I thought so," he said serenely, "though I don't entirely recall it. Was the tall one angry?"

"No, Papa. The tall one was frightened."

"Frightened." Elias considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "Then he knows something. Angry men only think they do."

August looked at her father — at the ruin and the wisdom occupying the same skull, taking turns — and felt the cold slide of Brandt's thumbnail along the ink ducts all over again. The inspection was over. It would happen again. And again. Voss's order was not a search; it was a siege conducted politely, one Tuesday at a time.

She went to the window. Across the square, the municipal carriage was pulling away, and in it, visible for one moment before the corner took him, sat Inspector Brandt, looking straight back at her through the glass with an expression she could not name — apology, maybe, or arithmetic.

That evening Corvin came down from hiding long enough to bring word from the cellar press: the raid on Wexler Lane was coming, Ilsa wanted the shop ready to move everything at an hour's notice, and — delivered flatly, without commentary, in the voice of a man handing over something heavy —

"There's someone who wants to meet you. Tomorrow. At the press."

"Who?"

Corvin hesitated, and in the hesitation August already knew, the way you know a typeface by its shadow alone.

"Your brother," he said. "Tomas wants to explain himself."

August stood very still among her type cases, each one hiding its secret beneath a false and quiet bottom, and heard her own voice come out level and cold:

"He can start with why he let me grieve."
