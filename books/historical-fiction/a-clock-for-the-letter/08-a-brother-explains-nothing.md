## 8. A Brother Explains Nothing

The trouble with being dead, Tomas Hartwell had discovered, was that nobody ever let you explain anything.

For two years he had been a rumor to himself — a name spoken in cellars, a cautionary tale, a martyr with no opinions of his own. The conspirators had buried him with great ceremony: a sealed coffin, a weeping sister who was never told the truth, and a government so pleased with its own cruelty that it never thought to check whether the corpse had walked off. He had watched his own funeral from behind a tanner's fence, hat pulled low, hands gripping the rail until the wood groaned. August had worn his mother's black shawl. She had not cried loudly. She had cried the way she set type — precisely, one small sob at a time, like letters dropped one by one into a composing stick.

He had wanted, more than anything in that moment, to cross the road and tell her.

He had not. And every day since, he had rehearsed the explanation he would someday give her, and every day it grew thinner, like a coat worn through too many winters.

Now she was here — *here*, in Voss's own town, setting type for Ilsa's press, asking questions with her whole stubborn face — and the explanation had run out of winters entirely.

* * *

He found her at dusk on the lane behind the chandler's shop, where the puddles held the last grey light and the smell of tallow hung thick as wool. She was walking home with a wrapped loaf under her arm, humming. That nearly broke him before he'd said a word. Two years dead, and she was *humming*.

"August."

She turned. He watched recognition move across her face in stages: first the polite blankness she gave strangers, then the narrowing of the eyes, then the bread slipping from under her arm and him lunging forward to catch it, because some things were reflex even between the living and the dead.

"You," she said. Not a scream. Worse. A flat, careful statement, as though she were checking the spelling of a word she had seen printed wrongly all her life.

"Don't say my name," he whispered. "Not here. Please."

"You don't have one," she said. "You're dead. Dead men can't be named."

A door opened somewhere up the lane; a woman emptied a basin into the gutter without looking at either of them. Tomas took August's elbow — she stiffened but did not pull away — and steered them both down the alley behind the chandler's yard, between stacked barrels that smelled of pitch and old fish, until they stood in a pocket of shadow where the town could not hear them.

"You've been following me," she said. "Since the coffin shipment. You ran."

"I know."

"You looked *at* me and you ran."

"I know." His voice came out cracked. "August, I need you to stop. Stop asking Corvin questions. Stop helping Ilsa. Go back to Father's shop and be a printer's daughter and print almanacs and wedding bills until this all blows over, and then — "

"And then what?" Her voice stayed low, which was how he knew how angry she really was. August loud was merely annoyed. August quiet was a press coming down on your thumb. "And then I go home and eat supper across from Papa, who still sometimes asks me if you'll be home by Sunday? And then I go to church and walk past the stone with your name on it? And then what, Tomas?"

"Then you live," he said. "That's the whole of it. Then you live."

"That's not the whole of it." She stepped closer. Up close, in the dark, he could see she had their father's eyes exactly — the same pale grey, the same way of looking at a person as though reading them for errors. "You let me bury you. You stood — where? Behind that fence by the tanner's? I saw the fence shudder, afterward. For two years I thought I imagined it. I thought grief had made a lunatic of me. And it was *you*. It was you shaking the fence rails trying not to call out."

He hadn't known she'd seen. Something in his chest folded like wet paper.

"It was the only way," he said. "Voss wanted me hanged with the others. There were men — good men — who spent their own money and risked their own necks to buy me a death instead of a rope. If anyone learns I'm alive, they learn it eventually too, and then they hang, and their children starve, and it will have been for nothing. So yes. I let you bury me. I'd do it again tomorrow."

"No, you wouldn't," August said. "Because you couldn't. You can't even look at me while you say it."

He looked at the barrels. The pitch. The grey water in the wheel ruts.

"Look at me, Tomas."

He did. It cost more than he could have said.

"I saw what I saw," he said quietly. "At Millbridge Ford. That's the beginning and end of everything you want from me, so let me save us both the night: I saw it, I ran, I am the only one left who saw it, and the only reason I'm breathing is that fourteen people are not. Every hour I stay hidden is an insult to them and I know it. Now go home."

She didn't go home. Of course she didn't go home. Instead she reached into her coat and drew out one of the letters — *his* letters, carried halfway across the country by a feverish boy named Corvin — and held it up between two fingers like evidence at a trial.

"The looping g's," she said. "You used to sign your school exercises with that flourish, like a peacock trying to get through a keyhole. I sorted type beside you for eleven years, Tomas. I know your hand better than you do. These letters are why I'm in this at all. Corvin nearly died bringing them. Ilsa is planning something enormous around them. And you stand there telling me to go print wedding bills?"

"They're not ready. The testimony isn't ready. It needs — "

"What does it need?"

"Witnesses who aren't dead," he snapped. "Corroboration. Names. Dates. A tribunal that actually sits, someday, when the country turns over — and it *will* turn over, August, you can smell it in the streets — and when it does, my word has to hold like set lead, not like ink smeared in a panic. If I come out now, tonight, and shout it in the square, Voss hangs me by morning and calls it disorder, and my testimony dies with me unsworn. Unwitnessed. Worthless. Is that what you want? Do you want me *useful* or do you want me *comfortable*?"

The moment the words left him he wanted to gather them back like spilled type.

Her face went very still. "Comfortable," she repeated.

"August —"

"You think that's what this is. That I want you home so I can be comfortable. That I've lost sleep over you because my bed was too soft."

"No. No, that came out wrong —"

"Fourteen people," she said. "Say their circumstances. You've said the number four times now like it's an argument. Tell me one of their names."

He opened his mouth. Nothing came.

Because here was the thing he had told no one, not Ilsa, not Corvin, not God: he could not remember them all. He had seen fourteen people die in the water at Millbridge Ford and in the terror of surviving he had failed to learn half their names, and every attempt to summon them produced only bodies, faces, a woman's boot turning over and over in the current like a thing deciding whether to right itself. The number he had. The names fled him. And a testimony built on a number and not a name was worth less than the paper it would ruin.

"One," August pressed. "Give me one."

"Marta Kessel," he said, hoarsely, because that one he had, that one he said to himself every night like a prayer with a debt attached. "The weaver's wife. She pushed a boy out of the water and the dragoons — " His throat closed. "She pushed a boy out of the water."

August's anger faltered. He saw it happen — saw the exact moment his sister's fury collided with his shame and neither knew which should give way. She hugged the recovered loaf of bread against her chest as if it were a child, or a shield.

"Papa printed the death notice," she said, quieter now. "Did you know that? They brought it to the shop with the magistrate's seal on it. He set it himself. He's never said so, but I've watched him avoid case seven for two years, the one with the bold faces, and I finally understood last month. He suspects he helped forge the record of you. He carries it like a stone he can't put down, and he doesn't even know what it truly weighs." Her voice shook once, and steadied. "We buried you with Mama's shawl. Do you understand? That shawl is the last thing I have of hers and I draped it over an empty box because you were hiding behind a fence."

"I know," he whispered. "I saw the shawl."

"And you let them close the lid."

The alley was silent except for water dripping somewhere, patient as a metronome. Tomas found that his eyes were hot and did not trust them.

"If I come out of hiding," he said, slowly, "they won't just kill me. They'll kill the truth with me, tidily, in the same act. Voss will stand at the trial of whoever they catch holding these pamphlets and say, *this witness is a fantasist, a dead man invented by seditionists* — and he'll be *right*, August, technically, gloriously right, because I am legally, officially, beautifully dead, with a printed notice to prove it, set by our own father. Don't you see the joke of it? Our family already testified that I don't exist. In ink. On the record."

"Then unprint it."

"With what press? With whose blood?"

"With yours, apparently," she flared, "if Ilsa has her way, and you'd thank her for the honor —"

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair!" The bread fell again and this time neither of them moved to catch it. "You talk about testimony holding like set lead. You talk like a man drafting a legal brief. But I watched your face just now when you said Marta Kessel's name, and that wasn't law, that was a man drowning in his own guilt and calling it strategy. You don't want justice, Tomas. You want penance. Those are different animals and only one of them eats you alive."

He stared at her. Eleven years at the cases together, and he had never once heard his sister say anything so true or so cruel, and he could not decide which impulse came first — to embrace her or to walk into the river.

"Maybe both things can be true," he managed. "Maybe I want to live and I'm ashamed of wanting to live. Maybe I want to testify and I'm terrified. Did you consider that? That I'm not a martyr or a coward, just a man doing arithmetic in the dark and getting different sums every night?"

Something in her gentled then, briefly, dangerously — the old August, the girl who had once sat up all night picking metal splinters out of his fingers by candlelight and called it *quality control*. She reached out and took his wrist, the way she used to when correcting his grip on the composing stick, and he felt two years of exile collapse into nothing at the touch of one familiar hand.

"Then let me carry some of it," she said. "That's all I've ever asked. Not for you to die. Not even for you to confess. Just — stop carrying it alone in the dark where I have to dig it out of you sideways. Tell me about the ford. All of it. I'm setting type for Ilsa now anyway; my hands are in this regardless of what you'd prefer. Give me the truth and let me make it permanent. That's what we do, Tomas. It's the family trade. We make things permanent."

And God help him, he wanted to. He wanted it so badly his knees ached with it. To hand it over — the ford, the order, Voss's calm voice saying *let the current do the work, gentlemen, we mustn't bruise the town's reputation with bullets* — to feel it leave his body and become *type*, become *record*, become something heavier than memory and harder to drown —

But if he told her, she would act. He knew her. She would carry pages, plates, names; she would stand next to Ilsa at the press; she would be within reach of Voss's constables on the very night the whole fragile conspiracy came apart, as it must, as everything did. His death would buy nothing then. His silence was the only wall between his sister and the rope, and walls, whatever else you said about them, kept the rain off.

"I can't," he said. "Not yet. Please, August. Not yet."

She let go of his wrist. She picked up the bread, brushed it off with great care, and when she straightened, her face had closed like a shop shutter at the end of a bad day.

"Not yet," she repeated. "You know, that's what Papa says when I ask him where he's misplaced his spectacles. *Not yet, they'll turn up.* Everything turns up eventually in this family. Usually broken."

"August —"

"There's a committee meeting Thursday," she said. "Ilsa wants to discuss the testimony. Your testimony, the famous one, the one that holds like lead. She asked me to bring the letters." She paused at the mouth of the alley, silhouetted against the lamplit lane, and did not look back at him. "I'll tell her you refused. Unless you'd rather I tell her you're afraid. Which story keeps a dead man safer, do you think?"

Then she was gone, and the bread smell faded after her, and Tomas stood alone among the pitch barrels understanding, with the particular clarity of the damned, that he had just lost an argument without winning a single point — and that by Thursday he would either have to give his sister the truth entire, or watch her walk into Ilsa's cellar war without him.

Down by the river, faint on the wind, the town clock struck the hour. Eight bells. Somewhere beyond the water, at a ford he could not stop seeing, the current went on doing its patient work.

Thursday, he thought. Three days.

It was not enough time to become brave. It was, he suspected, exactly enough time to be asked.
