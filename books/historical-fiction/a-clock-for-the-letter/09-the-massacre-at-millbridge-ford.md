## 9. The Massacre at Millbridge Ford

He would not sit down to tell it.

August understood why only afterward — that some stories are easier carried than set down, and if he sat, he might never rise again. So they stood together in the back room of the cellar press, between the ink barrels and the drying rack, and Tomas kept his feet as though at attention before some tribunal that had not yet convened.

"I'll say it once," he said. "Once is all I have in me."

* * *

It had rained for three days in March of '46, and the river at Millbridge Ford came up brown and fast, carrying fence posts and a drowned ewe and one entire chicken coop past the mill like a parade of everything the valley could not hold on to. The strikers had been camped at the ford a week by then — mill men, mostly, and their wives, and a few boys too young to know what a wage dispute was but old enough to carry a placard and feel important doing it.

"I went out there," Tomas said, "because Voss sent me. That's the part I want you to hear plainly, August, because I've heard people tell it since like I was some hero who stumbled into hell. I was sent. I was his clerk. I carried his papers and I polished his boots and I thought — God help me — I thought being near power meant understanding it."

He laughed then, a small dry sound with no floor under it.

"He gave me a ledger to deliver to the mill owner. A ledger! As though arithmetic were the business of the day. And I walked out to the ford because it was a fine excuse to leave the office, and I wanted to see the strikers for myself, because I'd been reading the pamphlets you used to mock me for." He pointed a finger at her without heat. "Don't deny it. You said, *Tomas, you'll be a radical when you learn what a week's wages buys.* Well. I learned."

August did not remember saying it. She believed him anyway. Memory, she had learned from her father, was a compositor who set type by feel; sometimes the line read true even when the case was wrong.

"There were about forty of them at the ford when I arrived. They'd chained the ferry. Old man Kessel — you wouldn't know him, a mill hand, sixty years old, hands like tree roots — he'd organized it all. He used to bring his granddaughter out in the mornings because she liked to watch the water. She was eleven."

Tomas stopped. He looked at the drying rack as if the pamphlets hanging there might be counted, ordered, made sense of.

"Voss arrived at noon with thirty dragoons and a magistrate's writ nobody could read in the rain. And a proclamation. He stood up in his stirrups and he read it out — *dispersal by any means necessary* — and then he said, quietly, to his captain, so that only those of us standing near heard it — *the river does the work cheap.*"

"Did he mean it?" August asked, and hated the question even as it left her, because she already knew.

"He wasn't a man who said things twice," Tomas said. "They drove them toward the water. Not shot them — understand that, it matters to the shape of the lie — they didn't shoot. Shooting leaves bullets, and bullets can be dug out of walls, and walls can testify. They used the flats of sabers and the horses themselves, herding, like cattle, like the coop floating downriver, and the crowd broke the way crowds do, all directions at once, and the direction that mattered was the ford."

He was still standing. His hands were still. Only his voice moved.

"The rain had come up so high that the stepping stones were under. But the strikers didn't know the ford the way the mill men did — no, that's wrong, the mill men knew best of all, that's the horror of it, they knew exactly how deep it was, and they went in anyway because behind them was steel. Kessel went in carrying his granddaughter above his head. He got three steps. The current took her out of his hands like it had been waiting all spring for something small."

August found she had put her own hand out, flat, palm up, as if to catch something. She lowered it slowly.

"Fourteen," Tomas said. "Kessel. The girl. Two brothers named Vogel who held hands going in, which saved neither of them. A woman named Anna Brandt — no relation to the inspector, before you ask, though I've wondered since whether God has a sense of humor or just poor filing. A boy of fifteen who'd been carrying a placard that said *WE ONLY ASK* — the river didn't read it. Fourteen. I counted them after, in my head, over and over, because counting was the only thing left I knew how to do. I was a clerk. I counted."

"And you?"

"I hid." He said it flatly, without decoration. "I got off my horse — I'd dismounted when the reading started, I don't know why, some instinct told me I didn't want to be mounted among them — and I lay under the ferry chain anchor in the reeds, and I watched. All of it. From below, from the water level, which is a vantage point I'd recommend to no one. And when it was done, Voss rode along the bank with his captain, and I heard him say, *regrettable accident, seasonal flooding, note it for the record,* and the captain said *yes, Herr Magistrate,* and that was the whole of the law that day. Two sentences. I could fit them in half a column of eight-point type."

The printer's daughter in her caught that — the measure of things in columns — before the sister in her caught the rest.

"You ran."

"I walked, at first. Walking is invisible. Then I ran. And a week later I was dead." He almost smiled. "Ilsa's people faked it well. A body pulled from the river two towns over, unrecognizable, convenient. Your father printed the notice — did you know? I think he suspected. He set my name himself, letter by letter, and he wept over the composing stick, and he never said a word, which is either cowardice or the bravest silence I ever benefited from."

"He doesn't remember," August said. "Some days he remembers nothing."

"Then he's paid enough," Tomas said quietly. "More than enough."

* * *

They had moved, somehow, to sitting after all — both of them on upturned crates beside the press, knees nearly touching, the lamp turned low because low lamps were habit down here now. Corvin was asleep in the corner with his mouth open, snoring gently, a sound so ordinary and foolish that it almost made the room bearable. Almost.

"Why didn't you come home?" August asked. "Two years, Tomas. Two years I set type next to your death notice. I dusted your name every morning like it was a grave marker. Papa talked to your empty chair. Mama's picture fell off the wall the day after the notice and I took it as an omen, I was so far gone with grief I took *a falling picture* as an omen, and you were—" Her voice cracked and she was furious at it. "You were alive. You were probably twenty miles away eating sausage."

"Farther," he said. "The sausage was terrible."

"Don't joke."

"If I don't joke, August, I will lie down on this floor and not get up, and you need me upright, so the jokes stay." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Why didn't I come home. Because a dead man can walk places a living man can't. Because the moment I knocked on our door, Voss would know his witness was breathing, and the first knock would be followed very shortly by other knocks, worse ones, and not only at our door. Ilsa keeps fourteen names on that roster over there. Every one of them is family to someone drowned at Millbridge Ford. My survival isn't mine anymore. It's evidence. Evidence stays hidden until trial."

"And when is the trial?" she asked. "Who tries a magistrate?"

"That," said Tomas, "is what the revolution is for."

He said it simply, without the fire she'd expected, without the pamphleteer's music. Just a fact, laid out like a form to be filled in: cause, condition, remedy.

"The testimony alone," he went on, "my sworn account, written, witnessed, plus the list of the fourteen with ages and next of kin — Ilsa has been collecting it for two years, parish records, employers' ledgers, the girl's baptism paper — that document is worth more than every barricade in this town. Barricades fall. Ink travels."

"Ink travels," August repeated. It was the catechism of her childhood; her father said it every morning before opening the shop, like a prayer or an apology.

"You understand, then." He took her hands. His were ink-stained too now — he'd been helping at the cellar press, she realized, setting the very declarations she'd been forbidden to ask about. "What I saw is the whole case. Without me it's rumor. With me it's law. Which means every day I stay hidden is a day justice waits, and the moment I stand up is the last day of my life, whichever way the tribunal rules. Voss's people don't forgive witnesses. Dead men make inconvenient habits of resurrecting."

August looked at her brother — thinner than she remembered, gray at the temples at twenty-six, with a scar on his chin she didn't know the story of and suddenly understood she never would unless she asked soon, soon being a word with new weight now.

"Then we keep you alive until we don't have to," she said. "We print it abroad. We get you out with the testimony. There's a boat coming — Corvin said—"

"I know about the boat."

"Then it's simple. You go. You testify from safety. Switzerland prints it, or Strasbourg, and it comes back on the wind and Voss rots."

"Nothing about you was ever simple," Tomas said, and smiled, and for a moment he was twelve years old again, showing her how to ink a brayer without streaks, patient, infuriating, beloved. "But thank you for trying. It's the family trade, isn't it? Making the impossible look typeset."

Corvin snorted in his sleep, rolled over, and muttered something that sounded like *not the blue ones, they're for Ilsa.*

* * *

That night August climbed the cellar stairs into the rain-washed street and walked home the long way, past the river, and stood on the bank looking at black water moving in the dark.

She thought: fourteen. She tried to count them as Tomas had, nameless, because she didn't know the names yet — but Ilsa's roster had them, she'd seen it, she would copy them tomorrow in her own fair hand, the way clerks do, the way her brother once did for the wrong side. She thought about a girl of eleven who liked to watch the water, and about her father at home asleep, who had perhaps wept while setting a dead boy's name and could no longer remember why, and about a boat with one berth, though she did not yet let herself think about the boat directly, only let it sit at the edge of her mind like weather on the horizon.

Ink travels, she thought. But first it must be carried.

She went home and slept without dreaming, which felt like a mercy and was probably just exhaustion. In the morning there were red cockades in the market square and soldiers on the bridge, and the town smelled — she would remember this always — the town smelled like her father's shop: ink and oil and something about to catch fire.
