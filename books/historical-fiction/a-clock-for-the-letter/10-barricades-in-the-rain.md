## 10. Barricades in the Rain

The first stone was thrown at half past four in the afternoon, and by six the town had divided itself neatly into two kinds of people: those who were boarding up their windows, and those who were taking the boards *off*.

August Hartwell stood in the doorway of her father's shop with a composing stick in one hand and an apple in the other, watching a squad of university students drag a wagon axle into the middle of Kessler Street while a grocer — old Hahn, who had once refused to sell them cabbage because Elias paid three days late — carried paving stones to the students on his back like a man forty years younger.

"They'll want posters," she said to no one.

Elias appeared behind her, squinting through his spectacles at the street as though the barricade were a misprint he'd been asked to approve. "Is that Hahn?"

"It is."

"Hahn hates students."

"Hahn hates Voss more," August said, and went inside to ink the press.

* * *

She had imagined revolution, insofar as she had imagined it at all, as something that happened in capitals — somewhere with an opera house and a proper garrison, where men in good coats read declarations aloud from balconies. It was not supposed to happen in a river town where the principal exports were coal, eels, and grievance. And it was certainly not supposed to smell like this: wet wool, gun oil, and the particular sharp sweetness of fresh ink, which for her whole life had meant safety, routine, supper approaching — and now meant something with teeth in it.

By eight o'clock the cellar press below Ilsa Reiner's bakery was running hot enough that the metal of the bed rang when you touched it. August knelt at the type cases with a candle stub guttering beside her, setting the declaration for the fourth time — they kept amending it, every hour, as though liberty were a serial publication.

"Comma after 'tyranny,'" Ilsa said over her shoulder, reading upside down, which she could do, which August found frankly indecent.

"There is no comma after tyranny."

"There is tonight." Ilsa reached past her and dropped the comma into the stick herself. Her hands were steady. Everything about Ilsa was steady; August suspected that if you cut her, she bled in straight lines. "We are declaring a republic, Hartwell. We can afford punctuation."

"You can't afford paper," August muttered, but she set the comma.

It was true. The cellar's stock was pitiful — a ream and a half of bad rag paper, some of it so thin you could read the next page through it, which Corvin had suggested was a feature ("the authorities will have to read our declaration twice"). They printed two hundred copies of the first declaration and sent runners out into the rain with bundles under their coats, and by ten o'clock August saw one pasted to the door of the customs house, and by eleven someone had pasted another directly over the magistrate's official notice of curfew, so that Voss's stern injunction against unlawful assembly was buried beneath the words *THE PEOPLE ASSEMBLE*, which gave August a satisfaction so pure and childish she had to lean against the wall for a moment.

"You're grinning," said Ilsa.

"I am not."

"You're grinning like your brother used to when he got a joke into print before anyone caught it."

August stopped leaning. That was the trouble with this cellar — Tomas was everywhere in it, in the roster pinned to the beam, in the jokes the others told that he had apparently originated, in the way Ilsa said *your brother* as though he were simply in the next room, delayed. She had still not seen him. The hooded figure from the coffin shipment had not reappeared, and no one would speak of him except sideways, in the future tense: *when Tomas sees this, when the town hears him*. As if the dead were merely punctual.

She bent back to the case. The letters rose under her fingers in their familiar geography — lower left the e's, upper right the t's — and she thought, not for the first time that night, that she knew the location of every letter in the alphabet better than she knew the layout of her own heart, and that tonight the alphabet was winning.

* * *

Around midnight the shooting started.

Not near — east, toward the ford, a scattered popping like green wood in a fire, punctuated by something heavier that made the jars on Ilsa's shelf shiver and Corvin, who was feeding the press, drop his rag.

"That's muskets," Corvin said unnecessarily.

"Thank you, Corvin. I mistook it for applause."

He looked at her stricken, and she softened. Corvin took everything literally; it was part of what made him a good courier and an exhausting companion. "They're saying the students held the bridge at Millbridge," he offered. "Held it all evening."

Millbridge. The word went through the cellar like a draft through a broken sash. Fourteen names hung unspoken in the air — the fourteen from the ford, two years drowned, the reason any of them were down here at midnight inking a press with shaking hands. Ilsa crossed the room and put both palms flat on the forme, as though steadying it, or being steadied by it.

"Then we print faster," she said.

So they printed faster. August worked the cases, Corvin fed, Ilsa pulled the bar, and a boy of perhaps fifteen named Piet — who claimed to be a student, though August had her doubts about any student whose knuckles were that acquainted with labor — ran copies up the ladder into the rain. The rhythm of it became almost peaceful, in the way of all machine work: set, lock, ink, pull, wash, set again. August had spent her whole life believing history was ink about other people, printed elsewhere, arriving in her town already dried and finished. Now it came off the bed damp enough to smudge if you weren't careful. You could put your thumb through a revolution if you weren't careful. She was careful.

Somewhere around two in the morning Elias appeared at the top of the ladder.

Nobody had told him anything, of course; nobody ever remembered to tell Elias anything, and August's heart did its usual complicated gymnastics — fear first, then guilt, then a fierce tenderness she didn't have time for. He descended the ladder with surprising care, looked around the cellar at the press, the piles of declarations, the candle-lit faces, and said:

"This is Ilsa Reiner's bakery."

"It is, Herr Hartwell," Ilsa said gravely.

"Where are the bread?" asked Elias.

"We've sold out," Ilsa said without a flicker. "Revolutions are very hard on pastry."

"Revolutions," said Elias, nodding slowly. He lowered himself onto a flour barrel with the sigh of a man settling in for a long wait at the barber's. "There was one in 'thirty. Or 'thirty-one. There was a pamphlet. I printed the pamphlet." He frowned. "Or I refused to. One of those."

August's hands stopped on the case.

*You printed it,* she wanted to say. *And later you printed the death notice too, Father, and we have never once spoken of what they made you do, and now here you are sitting on a barrel in the middle of the thing itself.* But Ilsa caught her eye across the cellar and shook her head, very slightly, and August understood: let him have whichever memory is kindest. Memory was a compositor too, always dropping letters, always justifying lines that didn't fit.

"The bread will be along in the morning," Ilsa said kindly.

"I'll wait," said Elias, and waited, and watched them print, and at some point — August never learned exactly when — began handing Piet the damp sheets to carry up the ladder, counting them aloud in his careful schoolmaster voice, and so became, for one night only and entirely without knowing it, a printer of revolution. When August looked over at him, at the candlelight on his white hair, at his lips moving with the count — *forty-one, forty-two* — she felt something in her chest crack slightly, the way ice cracks on the river in March, not breaking but announcing that it would.

* * *

At four, with the rain easing to a fine drizzle and the shooting gone quiet again, Ilsa called a halt to wash the type. August cleaned the cases by feel, sliding the letters home into their compartments, and found Ilsa standing beside her holding two cups of something that was technically chicory and spiritually dishwater.

"To the free press," Ilsa said, and clinked her cup against August's.

"To commas," said August, and drank.

For a while neither spoke. Above them the floorboards creaked with the bakery's ordinary morning business beginning — the ovens lighting, the girl who worked the counter humming — and the ordinariness of it, stacked directly on top of the night they'd had, struck August as either the funniest thing in the world or the most terrible.

"He won't come up while you're here," Ilsa said quietly. No name. No explanation needed.

"I know."

"He wants to. He stands outside sometimes. In doorways. Like a cat deciding whether to come in out of the weather."

"I don't want a cat, Ilsa. I want a brother who tells me why he let me bury him."

Isla drank her chicory and considered August over the rim. She had a way of looking at people that made you feel a proof being pulled — every letter of you examined, inked, judged fit or unfit for the page. "When this is done," she said, "when Voss answers for the ford — then he'll tell you everything. He made me promise. He's a coward about exactly one thing in this world, Hartwell, and it isn't Voss."

August said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a scream, and one does not scream in a cellar full of freshly locked type.

Instead she asked the question she'd been carrying since sundown: "What happens tomorrow? If the town holds. If the students really did hold the bridge."

"Then we go from declarations to trials," Ilsa said. "A declaration is a promise, Hartwell. Tomorrow we start keeping it. We'll need testimony. Witnesses. Sworn statements, printed, distributed, undeniable." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a woman who has been loading a weapon for two years and has finally been told the war has started. "Your brother saw everything at Millbridge Ford. Everything. When he stands up and says the fourteen names aloud — with the date, with the order given, with Voss's signature on the order — there is no tribunal in Europe that can pretend it away."

"And Voss?"

"Voss is currently barricaded in the courthouse with thirty dragoons and his correspondence," Ilsa said. "Which, incidentally, we should very much like to read. Corvin says Brandt keeps the keys to the records room."

"Brandt?" August looked up sharply. "The inspector? The one who—" The one who had inspected her father's shop, who had run his fingers along the type cases within inches of the hidden plates, and who had — she was nearly sure of it, she had replayed it a hundred times — noticed the false bottom and said nothing. Who had written in his report, in beautiful copperplate, *nothing of note at the Hartwell premises.*

"Brandt," Ilsa confirmed. "Who burns more reports than he files. A small man, doing small disobediences. But tomorrow, Hartwell—" and here she drained her cup and set it down with a click, "-tomorrow the small ones decide everything. They always do. The big men make the speeches; the small men open the doors."

Above them the bakery bell rang six times. Dawn came down the ladder in a thin gray line. August stood, and her back announced every hour of the night, and through the little grate at street level she could hear the town waking — carts, voices, someone laughing, actual laughter, on Kessler Street, at six in the morning, in the rain, in a republic one day old.

Elias was asleep on the flour barrel, upright, dignified, a stack of declarations cradled in his arms like a grandchild. Forty-nine, he had counted last. He had been right; she checked.

"Piet," she said, "run these last twelve up to the corner. Mind the drizzle." Then, softly, to Ilsa: "He'll want to see it. Tomas. When he testifies. Tell him—" She stopped. Telling Tomas things through intermediaries was how they had communicated for two years now; the family art of the forged letter had expanded to include the forged errand, the invented message, the whole postal system of avoidance. "Tell him I'm proud of him. And furious with him. Both, in that order."

"I'll tell him exactly that," Ilsa said, "though knowing him he'll only hear the first part."

August climbed the ladder into the gray morning, and stood in the doorway of the bakery, and looked down the long wet length of Kessler Street at the barricade — wagon axle, paving stones, a church pew somebody had donated, and, absurdly, Hahn's delivery cart flying a red tablecloth from its rail — and she understood, with the calm of a woman who has just pulled her five-thousandth perfect sheet, that the thing she had once believed was ink about other people had a typo in it somewhere, had always had one, and the typo was her.

History was not happening elsewhere. It was under her fingers, and it smelled of ink, and it was not yet dry.

She was still standing there when Corvin burst out of the cellar door behind her, breathless, hatless, holding a scrap of paper that had come, he gasped, from a boy at the courthouse — a boy paid by nobody anyone could name — and which he pressed into her hand as though it were burning.

It was a single line, in a hand August knew better than her own: the looping g's, the t crossed with a slight upward kick at the end like a man raising his hat.

*Tomorrow,* it read. *The records room, at first light. Come alone. — T.*

Below it, smaller, hurried, in a different hand entirely — precise, copperplate, unfamiliar until she remembered the fingers trailing across her father's type cases:

*He doesn't know I'm sending this. Bring help. — B.*
