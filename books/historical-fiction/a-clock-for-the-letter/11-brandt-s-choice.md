## 11. Brandt's Choice

Inspector Brandt had been a minor man for thirty-one years, and he had come to regard it as a kind of craft. There were men who burned to be major — Magistrate Voss, for instance, who polished his ambitions the way other men polished their boots, twice daily and before witnesses. Brandt did not burn. Brandt filed.

He was a small, tidy man with ink-stained cuffs that he could never quite explain to his wife, because the truth — that he sometimes copied out passages of his own reports just to admire how the sentences sat together on the page — seemed an embarrassing confession from a man whose profession was supposed to be sentences of a very different sort. He wrote *the premises were found in order* forty times a year and he had never once believed it meant anything, including on the days when the premises were, in fact, in order.

So when the order came down on a wet Thursday — *raid the cellar behind the cooper's shop on Fetter Lane, all presses destroyed, all persons detained, Voss's compliments* — Brandt read it three times at his desk and felt something shift in his chest like a type case jostled by a passing cart.

It was not surprise. He had known about the cellar press for weeks. That was the trouble with being a good inspector: you found things out, and then you had to live being the sort of man who knew them.

* * *

The knowing had started innocently, as knowing usually does. A boy selling chestnuts near the cooper's yard had mentioned, in the way boys mention things they've been paid not to mention, that the coopers were working odd hours. Brandt had gone to look. He had stood in the rain across the lane and watched through a grating as lamplight moved below, and heard the rhythmic thump of a press, and seen — this was the part he kept returning to, the part that had lodged under his skin like a splinter — a woman's silhouette bending over the forme, her hands moving in the quick, sure geometry of someone who knew her trade.

She had reminded him of no one. That was not true. She had reminded him of himself at nineteen, hunched over a clerk's desk, copying out inventories with the furious precision of a young man who believed that accuracy was a form of virtue. He had believed, then, that if the records were true, the world would follow. He had spent thirty years learning otherwise, slowly enough that he could pretend the lesson hadn't taken.

The pamphlets coming out of that cellar were seditious. He knew that too; he'd confiscated two copies from a student who wept so hard Brandt had let him go and then bought the confiscated copies back from the evidence room with his own money, which was, he supposed, the moment the splinter had gone in past the skin entirely. He had read them by candlelight in his study while his wife slept upstairs. They were well set. Whoever composed the pages knew her spacing the way a musician knows fingering. And the content —

The content was the fourteen names.

Brandt had been at Millbridge Ford. Not at the water's edge — a minor man is never at the water's edge — but in the second row of officials, close enough to see the strikers herded toward the weir, close enough to hear the sound the river made afterward. He had spent four years telling himself the official account: a crowd panicked, a bridge gave way, an accident of crowds and weather. He had been promoted moderately for his part in the subsequent reports. The promotion had felt, at the time, like a coat that fit in the shoulders but pulled everywhere else.

The pamphlet listed the names. All fourteen. With trades. With ages. There was a boy of eleven on it, a bobbin-threader, and Brandt remembered him — remembered the boy's cap coming off in the crush, remembered thinking, absurdly, *someone will want that cap back.*

He had checked the names against his own private notes, made the night of the disaster in a hand shakier than his usual one, notes he had hidden inside the false spine of a sermon collection his wife used for pressing flowers. Every name matched. The cellar press knew things only a witness could know, and printed them in a finer hand than any government report he had ever filed.

* * *

On Friday morning, Voss received him in the magistrate's office, which smelled of lemon polish and, beneath it, of something older and less pleasant, the way certain rooms do when important decisions have been made in them too often.

"Tomorrow night," Voss said, not looking up from his correspondence. "The cooper's cellar. Take six men. I want the press broken in front of whoever you find there — let them watch the screws come out. And I want the woman who sets their copy identified particularly. She has a family connection of some interest to me."

Brandt waited. Voss did not elaborate, which was itself an elaboration: the magistrate had learned that Hartwell girl was setting type for the conspirators, and he wanted her name in a report he could hold over the print shop like a knife over a knuckle.

"You'll deliver your findings personally," Voss said. "Not through the bureau. To me."

"To you, sir." Brandt heard his own voice say it, mild and bureaucratic, a voice designed to end conversations rather than begin them.

Walking home, he took the long route along the river. The town was electric — barricade timber disappearing from building sites, students laughing too loudly in the coffee houses, the smell of a storm everyone could taste but nobody would name. In ordinary times Brandt would have noted it all in his little book, *unrest, general, low-grade,* and filed the note under U for uselessness. But these were not going to be ordinary times much longer, and he knew it the way a barometer knows it, in the ache of old injuries and older compromises.

He stopped on the bridge and looked at the water. Millbridge Ford was two miles upstream. He didn't need to see it. He could hear it, some nights. That was not a figure of speech; it was simply what memory did to a man who had spent thirty years taking accurate notes.

His wife, Greta, had the supper on when he came in, and she took one look at him and said, "You've been given something you don't want to carry."

Greta had this talent. Thirty-one years of marriage and she could read him faster than he could read a report, which was saying something, because he read reports beautifully.

"It's a raid," he said. "Tomorrow night. A printing cellar."

"And?"

"And there's a girl there who sets type better than anyone in this town, and fourteen dead people whose names she prints correctly, and a magistrate who wants her identified particularly." He sat down heavily. "And six men tomorrow who will do whatever I tell them to do, because I am Inspector Brandt, and Inspector Brandt has never once given an unusual order in his life."

Greta put the pot down. She was a large calm woman who had buried two children and outlived three governments without changing her opinions, which took a strength Brandt had never had and had always quietly worshipped.

"So don't give it," she said.

"Greta—"

"Don't give it, or give it wrong, or give it late. You're a clever minor man, Aldous. It's the only kind of cleverness you've ever had — finding the gap in the paperwork." She brought the supper to the table and sat opposite him. "But listen to me. Whatever you do, do it whole. Half-measures get honest men killed twice over."

They ate. The stew was good. He thought, absurdly, about how many more suppers like it he might have, and how many a girl in a cellar might not.

* * *

Saturday night came down gray and dripping. Brandt assembled his six men at the guardhouse — good men, dull men, men who followed orders the way rivers follow channels, without philosophy. Korl was the sharpest of them, a corporal with a widow's stoop and a habit of asking one question too many.

"Fetter Lane, sir?" Korl said. "That's the cooper's yard. What are we breaking up back there?"

"A press," Brandt said. "An unregistered press."

Korl nodded, and then didn't move. "My brother was at the Ford," he said, to the floor. "Corporal Jens Korl. He didn't drown. He came home after, and he couldn't say why he'd lived, and in the spring he hanged himself in our mother's barn." He looked up. "Is it that press, sir? The one printing the names?"

The five other men had gone very still. Rain ticked at the guardhouse window. Brandt felt the whole weight of his thirty-one years settle onto his shoulders, and found, to his astonishment, that they held.

"Yes," he said. "It's that press."

Another silence. Then the youngest of them, a boy called Fenner, said, "Corporal, my mum buys bread from a bakery where the printer's daughter gets her flour," which made no sense at all, and yet every man in the room nodded as though it were a resolution passed unanimously.

Brandt understood then that he had not assembled six instruments. He had assembled six accomplices waiting, all this time, for someone to go first.

"Here is the operation," he said, and his voice sounded strange to him, clear and unhurried, the voice of a man reading his own eulogy and finding it acceptable. "We proceed to Fetter Lane at the appointed hour. We make considerable noise approaching — boots on cobbles, whistles, the full theater of it. We will find, upon arrival, an empty cellar containing a cold stove and some debris consistent with a recently cleared-out printing operation. We will search thoroughly. We will break nothing of value, because there will be nothing of value. At some point during the search, a lamp will be knocked over and a fire will start in the debris, which we will regrettably fail to control until it has consumed everything, including any papers remaining on the premises. My report will state that the press was removed prior to our arrival — most likely by cart, most likely days ago, most likely abroad by now. Voss cannot hang us for arriving after the birds have flown."

Korl said, "And the girl, sir? The one he wants identified?"

"The cellar will be empty, Corporal. Empty cellars contain no girls."

Fenner grinned. Korl did not, but something in his face loosened, the way a knot loosens.

"There's a problem with your plan, Inspector," Korl said slowly. "Afterward. When it's done. Voss will know, or guess. A man doesn't stay an inspector after a thing like this."

"I'm aware," Brandt said. "Go home first. Say goodbye properly. Report here at half past ten."

* * *

He went home and told Greta, and she listened while stirring a pot she wasn't really tending, and at the end she said only, "I'll pack the blue trunk. We can be at my sister's in Wellerfield by Sunday coach if the roads allow." Then, after a moment: "Aldous. The sermon books. The ones with the pressed flowers."

He looked at her.

"If you're going to burn your papers," she said, "burn those notes too. Whatever you decide to become after tonight, become it clean."

So that was how, at nine o'clock on a Saturday night in a year that would go down in history — though not, he suspected, in any official record he'd care to sign — Inspector Aldous Brandt sat in his own study and fed his career into the stove page by page.

The report was easy. He had written it already, the true one, the accurate one — *premises located, press confirmed, personnel observed, recommend immediate action* — and he read it once more with a craftsman's sorrow, because it was, he admitted, the finest report of his career: precise, complete, damning. Then he fed it to the flames and watched thirty-one years curl and blacken.

The sermon book was harder. The pressed flowers fell out — a violet Greta had put there the summer their daughter died, flattened to a stain of purple — and he laid the violet aside with ridiculous gentleness, and then he fed his own handwriting into the fire. The shaky hand. The fourteen names, written the night of the Ford, in ink that had never dried properly in his conscience.

When it was done, he took out a fresh sheet and wrote the report he would submit: *Cellar premises searched per order. Press absent, removal estimated several days prior. Debris burned due to overturned lamp during search. Nothing of value recovered.* He signed it. It was the first lie he had ever put his name to, and his hand did not shake at all, which surprised him, and pleased him, and frightened him more than shaking would have.

Greta came in and stood behind his chair. "Well?"

"It's done." He turned the signed page toward her like a schoolboy presenting sums. "Thirty-one years, and my masterpiece is a fiction."

"Your masterpiece," Greta said, kissing the top of his head, "is what you're about to do next."

* * *

At half past ten he collected his men, and they made their theatrical approach up Fetter Lane, boots hammering, whistles shrieking, a parade of manufactured urgency loud enough to wake every sympathetic soul within a quarter mile. He saw shutters twitch. He saw, or imagined he saw, a figure slip from the cooper's yard door and vanish down an alley with the unhurried urgency of someone who had been warned by exactly the right amount of noise.

In the cellar they found the cold stove, the debris, a scatter of broken type — deliberately broken, he noted, cheap sorts sacrificed to look like wreckage — and one sheet of paper left squarely in the center of the floor, which Brandt picked up with a rising heart and read by lantern light.

It was a printed declaration. Fourteen names. Set in beautiful, even, lovingly spaced type.

Somebody had left it for him. Somebody had known.

"Careful with the lamp there, Fenner," Brandt said, and knocked it over himself.

The fire took nicely. Six men fought it with the thorough incompetence of professionals, and by midnight the cooper's cellar was a smoking ruin containing absolutely nothing, and Corporal Korl stood beside Brandt in the rain watching it burn and said quietly, "Jens would have liked that, sir. The noise we made coming. Like a band."

"Dismissed, all of you," Brandt said. "Report tomorrow at noon. Tell them I stayed to oversee the scene."

Then he walked, alone, through the wet streets to the guardhouse, and wrote his resignation on official paper — *for reasons of health* — and left it on the desk of a sergeant who would find it in the morning and not understand it for years.

There remained one last piece of business, and it was the piece that mattered, and it required him to walk back up Fetter Lane in the small hours, alone, unarmed, feeling younger and more terrified than he had since his first day in uniform. He found the alley door he had watched weeks ago, the one with the grate above it, and he did not knock so much as stand in the rain and let the light from within fall through the doorway onto his boots.

The door opened a hand's width. A young woman looked out — sharp-eyed, ink to the wrist, unmistakably the hands he had seen through the grating. Behind her, a cellar full of faces, and one face among them that he knew from files and photographs and a death notice he had helped file away two years ago.

"Inspector Brandt," August Hartwell said. Her voice was not afraid. That undid him more than fear would have.

"Miss Hartwell." He removed his hat, which felt correct, and stood dripping. "Your cellar was raided tonight. Thoroughly. By six men and a fool. Nothing was found and nothing will be reported. I have resigned my post. I intend to leave town on the morning coach, and I am telling you this for two reasons. First, so that when Voss learns what happened — and he will learn it — you know it was not betrayal from within. Second—" he hesitated, and found the second reason had grown larger in his mouth than the first, "—second, because I was at Millbridge Ford. I was in the second row of officials. And I have notes. Had notes. Accurate ones. Names, times, positions, the order Voss gave and the hour he gave it. I burned them tonight, because my wife is wiser than I am, and I should have copied them out for you first instead."

From the shadows behind August, the dead man spoke. His voice was rough with disuse and something worse. "Why burn them at all?"

"Because I was a minor man keeping major secrets for thirty years, and the keeping had become the point," Brandt said. "I'm done keeping." He looked at Tomas Hartwell — alive, impossible, and bearing the particular exhaustion of a man carrying testimony — and then back to the girl. "Voss believes your press is destroyed and scattered. He believes you have nothing. That belief is worth more to you now than any document I could have given you, and it will last exactly as long as it takes him to discover otherwise. Which brings me to my last piece of information, freely given, worth every bit as much as my testimony: he is not waiting for you to act. Reinforcements reached him yesterday. Within days — I would give it a week, perhaps less — he will retake this town with force sufficient that no cellar, no committee, and no boat schedule will matter. Whatever you mean to do with the truth, Miss Hartwell — whoever you mean to send it abroad, and on whichever vessel — you must decide now. Tonight. Before the choosing is taken out of your hands."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere across the town a clock struck two, and August Hartwell looked at him with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic that no ledger could hold.

"One boat," she said, mostly to herself. "One berth."

"Then God go with whichever name you write down," Brandt said, and put his hat back on, and turned into the rain — a minor man no longer, walking toward a morning coach and a life beginning at fifty-three.
