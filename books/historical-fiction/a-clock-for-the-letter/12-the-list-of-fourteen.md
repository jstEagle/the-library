## 12. The List of Fourteen

The cellar had changed since my first visit. That first night it had been a conspiracy; now it was a printing office that happened to be underground, which is to say it was a home. Someone — Ilsa, I suspected, though she would never admit to domestic sentiment — had swept the floor daily and stacked the paper against damp in neat, squared piles. A kettle sat on a spirit stove. There were four chairs now instead of two, and a fifth crate that served as a chair for whoever arrived last.

I arrived last most nights. My father slept badly since the barricades went up, muttering at the sound of distant drums as though they were apprentices dropping type trays, so I worked evenings at the shop and came down after he'd finally gone under. Corvin's shoulder had healed crooked but healed, and he'd taken to standing watch at the cellar stair with a cudgel and a look of tremendous self-importance, as if the entire revolution depended on a man who could no longer raise his arm above his waist.

"You're late," he said, admitting me.

"I'm punctual," I said. "The night is early."

Ilsa looked up from the press. She had ink to her elbows these days and a smear across her jaw she either didn't know about or wore deliberately, like a badge. Beside her, setting type with the careful slowness of a man handling something holy, was my brother.

Tomas. Alive. Two years dead and alive, thinner than I remembered, with a scar through one eyebrow he hadn't earned when we were children. Every time I saw him the same strange arithmetic ran through me: grief subtracted, joy added, fury carried over from the previous column. He glanced up, gave me the small half-smile that was ours alone — the one we'd used across the dinner table when Father made one of his pronouncements — and returned to his composing stick.

We still hadn't said everything to each other. Perhaps we never would. But he was there, and he was real, and every evening I watched his hands move among the letters and thought: those are the hands that wrote to me for nineteen years, and then stopped, and I buried them without a body.

"Sit," Ilsa said. "Tonight we finish it."

* * *

What she meant was the list.

She cleared a space on the crate beside the stove and unrolled a sheet of paper weighted at the corners with four pieces of scrap lead. It was written in her own hand — that blunt, upright script that looked like it had been set in type already, all serifs and certainty — and at the top it read, in letters slightly larger than the rest:

THE FOURTEEN OF MILLBRIDGE FORD.

Below it, fourteen names. Below each name, in smaller writing, what was known of them: a trade, an age, a wife or mother or child, the manner in which they'd gone into the water.

I read them the way you read anything you know will change your life — slowly, twice, and then a third time because the first two readings felt like someone else's misfortune.

Anselm Brandt-Reuter, millhand, twenty-two.
Josef Kellner, millhand, thirty-one. Wife Marta. Daughter aged three.
Lukas Fenner, millhand, nineteen.
— and so on down the page, fourteen lines, fourteen small biographies compressed into the space a newspaper gave to shipping news.

Fourteen men who had gone to the ford to ask for wages they were owed, and who had been ordered into the current by Magistrate Voss when they refused to disperse, and who could not swim, because millhands from our town did not swim, because the river was where work died and not where people played.

"Tomas swore the testimony six days ago," Ilsa said. "Before witnesses. Before God, if God was paying attention." She tapped the paper. "These names go with it. The full account. What he saw, what Voss ordered, who carried the order, where the bodies were taken afterward — because some of them were *taken*, August, they weren't left in the river like accidents, they were carted upstream and buried under the new embankment fill, and Tomas can say where the spades are."

The cellar was quiet except for the kettle beginning its low complaint.

"And the plan?" Corvin asked from the stair. His voice had gone careful.

"The plan," Ilsa said, "is that this account reaches printers abroad before Voss's masters send him enough soldiers to take this town back. Which they will. Everyone in this room knows the barricades are a bonfire, not a fortress. Bonfires are for light, not for warmth. We print while we can, and we send the truth somewhere it can't be burned."

"Where?"

"Basel. There's a press there — Brenner & Sons. They've printed for exiles before; Corvin knows the contact." She nodded at him. "A sworn testimony, signed, witnessed, with the names and the burial site, published in Basel, means Voss can't bury it here. It means every chancellery in Europe has it. It means when the trials come — and they'll come, sooner or later, these things always come — there is a document. Not rumor. Document."

"It's good," Corvin said slowly. "It's the best thing this room has ever made."

"There's a complication," Ilsa said.

There is always a complication. In my experience the word *plan* simply means *complication with better posture*.

"The river is watched. The roads out are watched. There is exactly one way this gets through: the *Marta*, a coal barge running downstream tomorrow night, before the blockade closes completely. The captain owes Ilsa—" she paused, corrected herself with visible distaste, "—owes *us*. She'll carry one passenger and one satchel. One berth, August. One."

I heard the words. I understood each of them individually. It took a moment for them to arrange themselves into meaning, and when they did, the cellar seemed to tilt very slightly, like a floor in a house settling.

"One berth," I repeated.

"For the testimony," Ilsa said. "Someone carries it, delivers it, stands in Basel while Brenner sets it, and stays until it's done. It can't be posted. It can't be trusted to strangers. It has to be a person who understands what they're carrying."

Her eyes came to mine, and I saw — not unkindly, but unmistakably — that she had already decided whom she wanted.

"Tomas goes," she said. "His testimony, his delivery, his name on the publication. If anyone asks him a question in Basel, he can answer it. No one else in this room can."

And there it was. Hope, arriving with its price tag already stitched on, the way fine goods come from the city.

Because Tomas going meant Tomas leaving. And Tomas leaving meant Tomas living. And some treacherous, exhausted part of me — the part that had spent two years grieving a brother and three weeks being angry at him for existing — leapt up at that like a child at a window. *He lives. He gets on the boat. He lives.*

But another part, quieter and less flattering, did the sums. Because Ilsa had said *before Voss retakes the town*, and everyone knew how soon that might be, and everyone knew what happened to witnesses who stayed. And I knew my brother. I knew the set of his shoulders as he stood over the list of names, reading them again and again, mouthing them like penance.

He wasn't going to get on any boat.

* * *

Afterward — after Ilsa rolled up the list and put it inside a oilcloth packet and told us the departure was tomorrow night, after Corvin went up to spell himself at the door and the kettle had been passed around in tin cups that tasted faintly of ink — I found myself alone with Tomas at the composing stone.

He was distributing type, sliding letters back into their cases, the old rhythm of it, our father's rhythm. Our father had taught us both that a compositor should never look at the case while working; the hand finds its letter the way the tongue finds its teeth. Watching him do it, I could have been twelve years old again, standing on a crate beside him, both of us learning that words were made of metal and metal was heavy and therefore words must be worth carrying.

"One berth," I said quietly.

"Mm," he said, not looking up.

"Tomas."

"I heard you."

"Do you intend to be on that boat?"

His hand paused over the case, a piece of type held between finger and thumb — an *e*, I noticed, the commonest letter, the one every language leans on. Then he set it home and kept working.

"That depends," he said, "on whether the town is still standing when it's gone."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only kind I have tonight."

I took the composing stick gently out of his hand and set it down, because I wanted his eyes and he was a man who could hide behind type forever. He let me. That was something — that he let me.

"They need you in Basel more than they need you here," I said. "You know that. Your signature on the page is the whole weapon. Dead men can't testify, and neither can arrested ones. Ilsa knows it. You know it."

"Ilsa wants me safe because I'm useful," he said. "That's not the same as wanting me. And August —" he finally looked at me, and his face in the lamplight was older than twenty-six, older than our father's sometimes, "— fourteen men went into the water because they asked for their wages. I stood on the bank. I ran. Every day I've been dead, I've been dead for a reason, and the reason wasn't to die *properly* in some skirmish on a barricade. It was to stand up in front of God and everybody and say their names. Here. Where they drowned. If I run to Basel with the paper, the paper says it, yes. But nobody in this town hears it said out loud in this town's own voice. And Voss walks free until some foreign tribunal gets around to him, which is never."

"So you stay to testify."

"So I stay to testify. At the trial, if there's a trial. On a barricade, if there isn't." He almost smiled. "You always said history was ink for other papers. It isn't, August. It's ink for whoever's brave enough to hold the brush."

"I never said that."

"You said it every day of your life without saying it."

I turned away and busied my hands with nothing — straightening paper, squaring the tin cups — because my face was doing things I didn't consent to. Fear, mostly. Fear wearing anger's coat, the way it does in families.

"There will be a boat," I said. "One boat. One berth. And if you don't take it—"

"If I don't take it, it goes empty, or it goes to someone else who matters less to the cause and more to somebody." He said it lightly, but I felt the weight underneath, the way you feel a stone under moss. "Don't worry about the berth. Worry about the packet getting finished. Ilsa wants the final pages set by noon tomorrow."

* * *

I walked home through streets that smelled of rain and woodsmoke and gun oil. Barricades are strange things in the dark — furniture grown enormous, the whole town rearranged overnight by students and stevedores who only agreed on one thing, which was *no further*. A boy of perhaps sixteen stood sentry on the corner by the tannery, a musket taller than he was, and asked me my business, and when I said "printer," he stepped aside with such reverence you'd have said I carried relics instead of a tired woman with ink permanently under her nails.

Perhaps I did carry relics. We all did, that week.

At the shop I let myself in quietly. Father was asleep in the back room, and I stood in his doorway a while watching him breathe, the way you check a fire you're not ready to lose.

He had been worse lately. The barricades frightened him without his knowing why — he'd wake shouting about a press run due in 1841, about a customer named Weissmann who had been dead five years and owed us eleven gulden. Some evenings he was perfectly himself and read the conspirators' declarations aloud with a typesetter's critical eye ("comma splice on line four; revolution or no revolution, we don't ship slop"), and those hours were gifts I hoarded greedily. Other evenings he looked at me with polite puzzlement, the expression of a man greeting a lodger, and once — once, terribly — he had called me *boy* in Tomas's voice and asked why I was out so late.

I stood in the doorway and did arithmetic I didn't want to do.

One berth. Tomorrow night. The last boat.

If Tomas wouldn't take it — and he wouldn't, I knew it the way you know weather coming in your knee — then the berth would sit empty while the *Marta* slipped downstream carrying nothing but coal and cowardice, and everything Ilsa had built, everything Tomas had stayed dead for, would depend on one oilcloth packet and the goodwill of a Swiss printer none of us had ever met.

Unless someone else carried it.

The thought arrived the way thoughts do in the small hours — not knocking, just suddenly present, sitting in the room like a relative who lets himself in.

Someone else could carry it. Someone who knew the trade, who could stand in Basel and talk to Brenner & Sons in their own language of em quads and forme-locks. Someone expendable enough that the movement wouldn't starve without her, and stubborn enough to see it through.

And someone with a father who could not be left behind in a town about to fall back into Voss's hands. A father who had printed, God help him, the original death notice — coerced, maybe, or deceived, but printed in *our* shop, in *his* hand — and who would answer for it the moment the soldiers came back and started asking old questions of old men who couldn't remember their own answers.

One berth.

My brother needed it to save the town. My father needed it to save himself. And I —

I stood between them in the dark of the shop that raised us both, smelling of ink and lamp oil, and understood that whatever I decided, I would decide it alone, and live with it alone, and that no committee of the undersigned could sign this particular document for me.

From the back room, Father stirred and spoke clearly into his sleep, in the tone he used for correcting apprentices:

"Mind your kerning, boy. Letters need room to breathe."

I went to my own bed and did not sleep.

Somewhere past three in the morning I heard the front bell of the shop — the little bell over the loading door that hadn't been oiled since spring — and I was on my feet and down the stairs with a poker in my hand before I was properly awake, because no honest person rings a bell at three in the morning during a revolution.

But it was no soldier. It was Father, in his nightshirt and one boot, standing in the open doorway with yesterday's newspaper folded under his arm, looking out at the rain with an expression of mild urgency.

"August," he said — my name, my right name, thank God — "the boy will miss his supper. He's waiting by the river. Help me find him, there's a good girl, it's nearly dark."
