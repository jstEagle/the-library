## 13. Elias Wanders

The trouble began, as most troubles in our shop did, with a missing em quad.

I noticed it at half past four in the morning, standing at the stone in the cellar press with Ilsa's declaration half-set before me, my fingers reaching for the little box where Father kept his spacers sorted by size the way other men kept their sins sorted by excuse. The box was there. The em quads were not.

"Corvin," I said, "did you borrow from the em case?"

Corvin looked up from the ink slab, his bandaged shoulder making him list to one side like a ship that had taken on opinions as well as water. "I don't even know what an em quad is."

"Then you're the only man in this cellar who can say so honestly."

But even as I said it, a colder thought arrived and sat down beside it, uninvited, the way bad news does. The em case lived upstairs. Upstairs meant Father's workbench, and Father's workbench meant Father, and Father — I cast back through the night, through the thunder of barricades going up in the rain, through Ilsa shouting for more copies, more copies, *more* — Father had not come down to check on me once. Not once all night. And my father checked on things the way rivers checked on downhill: constantly, inevitably, whether anyone wanted him to or not.

"Ilsa," I said. "When did you last see Elias?"

She didn't look up from the galley she was locking. That alone told me something. Ilsa Reiner noticed everything — she noticed which of her conspirators ate last, which slept least, which had begun to glance at the door too often — and when she declined to notice something, it was because noticing would require acting on it.

"Some hours ago," she said. "He was helping Corvin fold pamphlets. He folded them very well." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "Very slowly."

"How many is some?"

"August." She set the quoins with a wooden key, tap tap tap, giving herself somewhere to put her hands. "There are men on the barricades who have been awake forty hours. There are women carrying ammunition in bread baskets. If your father has wandered off in a town currently full of soldiers, drunk students, and burning furniture, then yes, I am concerned, but I cannot spare a single body to go looking, and you know it."

She was right, which was the worst quality a person could have at that moment. The press behind us thumped like a heart with opinions. Outside, the rain had stopped an hour before dawn and the whole town had that rinsed, held-breath sound of a place between thunderclaps.

"He knows the town," I said, mostly to myself. "He's walked these streets fifty years."

"He knew them," Ilsa said gently, "when they were the same streets."

* * *

I went up the stairs two at a time and into the shop, and I knew before I crossed the threshold that he was gone. You spend a lifetime in a room, you learn its silences the way sailors learn weather. This silence had an empty shape in it.

His coat was gone. His hat — the grey one, soft as an old apology — was gone. On his workbench, someone (himself, presumably; he had not been himself in months, but whoever he had been, he was tidy) had left a note in pencil, in that careful copperplate that had taught Tomas and me our letters both:

*Gone out. Back presently. Do not fuss.*

Beneath it, in smaller letters, added later, shakier:

*I have forgotten what I went out for. Going anyway.*

I stood holding that piece of paper for longer than any printer should stand holding paper, because paper is meant to be printed on and moved along, and grief that stands still is just weight.

Then I put on my own coat, took the lantern, and went out into the town my brother had died in twice.

* * *

Dawn in a revolutionary town is a strange light. The barricades stood black and dripping against a sky the color of dishwater, and the students who manned them had the glassy, heroic look of people who had discovered that heroism was ninety percent staying awake. A boy of perhaps sixteen offered me a flag. I declined. He offered again, as though flags were being distributed whether wanted or not, like taxes.

"Have you seen an old man?" I asked. "Grey coat, grey hat, walks like he's arguing with the cobblestones?"

The boy considered. "That describes every second man in this town."

"He'd have asked about newspapers."

"Oh," said the boy, with sudden recognition, "the newspaper gentleman. Yes. He came by the east barricade around midnight. Told Captain Ferenc his typesetting was disgraceful and his spelling worse. Then he corrected the spelling."

"And then?"

"Went on toward the river, I think. He said—" the boy frowned, working to recall it exactly, and I understood that he would be recalling it for the rest of his life too, everyone in this town collecting small pieces of my father without knowing they were assembling a man— "he said he had a delivery that couldn't wait."

A delivery. My stomach performed a maneuver I will not describe in a family chronicle.

The river lay east and down, past the tanneries, past the burnt shell of the customs house, to the long stone quay where the boats knocked their fenders against the pilings all day like impatient horses. It was no place for a man who sometimes, on bad mornings, forgot which end of the street his house was on. It was, however, exactly the place for a man on a mission, because missions were the one thing that still organized him. Give Elias Hartwell a task and he became briefly, wonderfully, terribly competent. He would cross fire and flood to deliver a newspaper. He had, in fact, done both, if you counted the flood of '41 and the time the alderman's son threw things at him over a libel suit.

I walked fast. The town woke around me in fragments — a baker's window glowing, a patrol of government soldiers still, impossibly, tolerated at their post by some unwritten truce, a drunk student asleep in a doorway hugging a bottle like a sweetheart. Somewhere a cockerel was having opinions. I did not run, because running attracts eyes, and eyes were dangerous now; but I walked the fastest walk of my life.

* * *

I found him at the quay, sitting on a bollard with his legs crossed like a schoolboy, the newspaper folded on his knee with a care that made my chest hurt. The river ran brown and quick behind him, carrying last night's rain to wherever rivers think they're going. The mist stood on the water in columns. His hat was on straight. His coat was buttoned wrong by one, so that the whole garment hung askew, a man dressed by his own ghost.

"Tomas," he said, when he saw me. Brightly. Without a flicker of doubt. "You're late."

And there it is — the thing about grief nobody warns you, that it can ambush you wearing your brother's name out of your father's mouth. I stood very still on the wet cobbles and let the moment pass through me like a swallowed knife, and then I smiled, because he needed the smile, and because in the grey half-light, God help me, I suppose I could pass for Tomas if you only looked at the shape of a person and not the whole history inside it.

"Am I?" I said. "What are we late for?"

"Supper." He tapped the newspaper. "Your mother made dumplings. Well — she *calls* them dumplings. They're ammunition with ambitions. But your brother will eat six of them and complain about seven, and if we miss supper he'll have started without us and there'll be nothing left but the accusation." He patted the bollard beside him. I sat. The stone was cold enough to make a point about mortality. "I brought him the paper. He likes the shipping news. He says it's the only honest part — ships are either in or they're out, none of this parliamentary waffle."

"Tomas likes the shipping news," I repeated. My voice behaved itself. Somewhere in the last two years I had learned to make my voice behave itself, and I no longer remembered whether that was a skill or a wound.

"He reads it aloud," Father said. "Every vessel, every cargo. Grain, timber, pig iron. He makes up lives for the captains." He chuckled, a private, worn-in sound, the chuckle of a man who had told the same story to himself so many times it had gone smooth as river glass. "The *Marta*, out of Rostock, captain with a wooden leg and a sweetheart in every port, according to Tomas. Your mother tells him to stop. She doesn't mean it."

The mist shifted. Out on the water, early, riding low with whatever desperate cargo the times permitted, a boat moved toward the quay and I watched it with the other half of my mind while the half that mattered stayed seated on the cold bollard next to my father, in the year he was living in, where Mother was alive and Tomas was fourteen and hungry, and the worst thing that had ever happened to our family was a burned batch of dumplings.

"Father," I said. "Do you know what year it is?"

He gave me the look. Every child of a certain parent knows the look — the one that says *I raised you better than to ask foolish questions.* "It's Thursday," he said.

"That wasn't the question."

"It's the answer that matters. Nobody ever got anything done on a Tuesday worth mentioning." Then the look faltered, the way a lamp falters when the oil runs low, and I watched him reach for something and find the shelf empty. His hands moved on the newspaper, smoothing it, folding a corner, unfolding it. "They've changed the typeface," he said quietly. "In this paper. Look at the serifs. That's not how they cut the e's. Someone's been cutting e's and I don't know who, and I don't—" His voice climbed a fraction, and I heard underneath it the thing he fought every day now, the vast foggy ocean just offshore of his mind. "August. When did they change the type?"

"A while ago," I said. "You know how printers are. Always meddling."

"You're not listening to me." He gripped my wrist. His hand, once strong enough to lift a full forme without trembling, shook against my sleeve. "I know that type. I set that type. I have set that type for thirty years and it is *wrong*, and if the type is wrong then I have lost days, and if I have lost days—" He stopped. Looked at me properly, at last, with the terrible brief clarity that came to him maybe an hour a week now, arriving like a creditor. "How many days, August?"

The truth sat in my throat. Two years since Tomas died. Three years of fog, thickening season by season. Words like *dementia* that the doctors used and I refused to.

"More than a few," I said.

He nodded slowly. He looked out at the river. A gull expressed itself overhead in the language of gulls, which is complaint.

"And Tomas," he said, in a different voice, a voice with all the floors removed, "Tomas is not waiting at home for dumplings."

"No."

"They shot him."

"Yes."

"For a printer," he said, "they used remarkably cheap ink. The notice. The death notice. I printed it, you know."

"I know."

"I printed it and I didn't read it closely. Or I read it and didn't ask. One or the other, and I've spent—" he counted on his fingers, lost count, started again, abandoned the project— "a long time now trying to remember which, and do you know what the fog does? The fog takes the question away entirely. Some mornings I wake up and there is no question. Just breakfast. Just the smell of ink. Just a boy waiting for the paper who isn't—" His jaw worked. "I delivered papers to him for years. Force of habit outlives the reason for it. Isn't that a damnable thing to say about love."

"Father—"

"Isn't it." Not a question. He refolded the newspaper with enormous precision, creasing each edge with his thumbnail. "Well. Now you know the state of the account. Debts on both sides, no clear ledger." He stood, using the bollard, then using me. "Take me home, daughter. Whoever you turned out to be, you have your mother's way of standing there letting a man finish a sentence, and I find I've missed it."

We walked back west together, slow, his arm in mine, and he pointed out things as we went — *that awning is new, that wall used to be the tallow chandler's, I set the funeral bill for old Frau Kessler in that house, forty-two words, she paid in eggs* — laying down the town's history like paving stones ahead of his own feet, as if by narrating the past he could keep his footing in the present. At the corner of the tanners' lane he stopped.

"There's going to be a choice," he said.

The word went through me like a misregistered strike, ink where it shouldn't be. "What choice?"

"The boat." He was looking at the river again, though we couldn't see it from here. "People talk in cellars. Old men hear everything; it's the only advantage of being assumed deaf. There's a boat, and there's a berth, and there's a boy who ought to be on it." He turned to me and his eyes were perfectly clear and perfectly kind, which is the most devastating combination I know. "And a daughter who will try to carry everyone on her back across the water, because that is what Hartwells do, we carry presses, we carry grudges, we carry each other. So I am telling you now, in advance, while I still know what I'm saying: do not choose me."

"Father—"

"If you choose me over the truth, I will never forgive you, and worse, I won't remember it, so you'll have to be forgiven fresh every morning, which is more than any daughter should bear." He patted my hand where it lay on his sleeve. "Besides," he added, and the fog rolled back in over the clear water of his voice, gentle as tide, "besides, I can't swim, and Tomas will miss supper."

We went home. I put him to bed with the newspaper, and he read the shipping news aloud until he fell asleep mid-sentence, the *Marta* outbound, cargo unspecified, captain's fate uncertain.

I sat on the stairs outside his door for a long time with the dawn coming up grey through the window, and I understood that Ilsa had been right about something she hadn't said: the choice had already been made, weeks ago, in every hour I spent cataloguing what he'd lost instead of what he was. The question was not whom I loved. The question was which love I could bear to turn into a lie.

And downstairs, through the floorboards, I could hear Ilsa's boots. She was coming up to ask me for my answer.

She had brought paper.
