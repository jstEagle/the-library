## 14. Two Names, One Berth

Sleep had become a rumor. August had heard of it, the way she'd once heard of Paris, and entertained about the same likelihood of visiting.

She sat in the cellar press with a single candle and the list of fourteen names spread before her on the composing stone, weighted at the corners with quoins because the draft from the stairwell kept trying to carry them off — as if even the air of this town knew those names were contraband. *Brandl. Kessner. The two Ostrava brothers. Little Anna Vrba, who was seventeen.* Fourteen names, and one berth, and one boat, and one sister sitting awake between her brother and her father like a woman stretched across a crack in the ice.

The boat left at dawn tomorrow. Or dawn the day after — no one could say precisely, because the pilot would not say precisely, because saying precise things in wartime was how men ended up floating facedown past the flour mill. What was certain: one berth. One seat on the last packet out before Voss's troops closed the river.

One name to be written on the manifest.

August picked up the pen and put it down again three times without making a mark. The fourth time she made a mark anyway — a small blot, accidental, right beside Tomas's name, like a period after a sentence that hadn't been written yet.

"You'll ruin the stone," said Ilsa Reiner from the stairs.

August hadn't heard her come down. She never did. Ilsa moved through the world the way water moved through cracks — quietly, and ending up everywhere eventually.

"I couldn't sleep," August said.

"Neither could I. But I had the decency to lie about it upstairs." Ilsa came and stood across the stone from her, arms folded, hair bound back for whatever hour of work she'd decided this was. "You've been staring at those names for six hours by my count."

"Four."

"I rounded up out of respect." Ilsa pulled a stool over and sat, which she almost never did; standing was her native posture. "So. Decide."

"That's what I'm doing."

"No, you're not. Deciding is what happens when a person chooses. What you're doing is performing the *idea* of choosing so thoroughly that the choice will make itself out of sheer embarrassment." Ilsa tapped the list. "I've watched you do it with type, too. You hold a piece of ten-point over the case for a full minute before committing."

"A full minute is *craft*."

"A full minute is fear wearing an apron." Ilsa said it without malice, which was somehow worse. Then her voice gentled, or did the thing that passed for gentle in a woman who ran a conspiracy out of a tannery cellar. "August. I know what I'm asking."

"Do you?" It came out sharper than August intended, and she found she didn't mind. There was a supply of sharpness in her tonight that wanted using. "Because I think you know exactly what you're asking. You're asking me to choose which member of my family dies."

"I'm asking you to choose which one lives."

"The same sentence, Ilsa. Same words, different order. I set type all day. I know how much order changes things, and I know when it doesn't."

The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere above them the town creaked through its insomnia — a shutter banging, a dog with opinions, the far-off clatter of students pulling cobblestones for barricades they were building faster than anyone could tear down.

"Tomas testifies," Ilsa said quietly, "and Voss hangs. Not resigns. Not retires to his estate to write memoirs blaming everyone else. Hangs. Fourteen families get to stop calling their grief a rumor. And the account goes out — Millbridge Ford, in full, under sworn testimony from a living witness — and no magistrate ever again looks at a crowd of strikers and thinks he can simply open the sluice gates and be done with them."

"And Tomas swings for it."

"Tomas has already decided that, so you'd only be agreeing with him."

"He hasn't decided anything! He hasn't been asked!" August stood, and the stool scraped, and both of them pretended that wasn't nearly a shout. "That's what nobody in your committee seems to grasp. He'll walk onto that boat if I tell him to. He'll do whatever I write down for him — God knows I've learned I can forge his hand well enough to order him around like a compositor. But nobody has asked him whether he wants to die. You've all just arranged the room so dying looks noble and then admired him for standing in it."

Ilsa took that. August watched her take it — saw the hit land somewhere behind her eyes and be absorbed rather than returned. It was, August thought, the first honest thing she'd seen the woman's face do in weeks.

"You're not wrong," Ilsa said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me. I won't say it twice, my reputation can't afford it." She leaned forward over the stone. "Yes, we've built the stage. Yes, Tomas stepped onto it with his eyes open and has refused every exit we've offered. Corvin offered to drug him and drag him aboard last week — did you know that? Tomas threatened to shout for the patrol. So no, we haven't asked him whether he wants to live, because asking would be cruelty dressed as a question. He's already answered."

"When?"

"Every day since Millbridge Ford. You just weren't there to hear it."

August turned away. She went to the far wall where the plates hung in their oilcloth wrappings, fourteen little lead coffins, and touched one without unwrapping it. Under her fingers, through cloth and grease, the ridges of reversed letters. Names printed backward so the world might read them forward. That was the whole trade, really. Her whole life.

"My father," she said, to the plate rather than to Ilsa, "thinks it's 1843 most mornings. He asks me whether Tomas has eaten. He asks me to fetch Tomas home before dark. Yesterday he called me Mother." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, dry as a broken forme. "If I put Elias on that boat, I save a man who cannot remember the massacre, cannot testify to it, cannot tell you the year or the magistrate or which of his children is alive. I save him because I love him. And the committee gets nothing but an old man's nightmares and a daughter's relief, and Voss keeps his office and his neck, and in ten years some other magistrate opens some other sluice gate on some other crowd and nobody prints a word about it because nobody survived to describe the water."

"Yes," said Ilsa. "All true."

"And if I send Tomas—"

"You lose him either way," Ilsa said. "That's the arithmetic nobody wants done aloud. If he stays and testifies, he dies in a cell at the end of it, maybe, or on a barricade, or shot in a ditch by a lieutenant anxious for his supper. If he goes, he lives — but he lives *there*, in exile, safe, and every morning he wakes safe he'll hate himself a little more, and every morning you wake beside him you'll see him hating himself, and that is also losing someone. Just slowly, with better weather."

"You have a gift for comfort, Ilsa."

"I have a gift for accuracy. Comfort is your father's department. Was." A flicker crossed her face — guilt, quickly filed away. "Is. When he can find it."

August came back to the stone and sat. The blot of ink beside Tomas's name had dried. It looked, in the candlelight, like a small black heart.

Here was the thing she had been circling all night, the way a moth circles a lamp with increasingly sincere intentions:

She loved her father more. That was simply a fact, like the weight of lead, like the smell of ink, like the direction water flows. She loved Elias Hartwell with the whole of herself, without effort, the way she breathed. And Tomas — she loved Tomas too, fiercely, but it was a love that had learned to share a body with anger. He had let her grieve him for two years. He had let her stand at a funeral printing his own death notice with steady hands while something inside her quietly died and was buried in the same hole. There was a debt between them now, and love with a debt attached is a different animal than love alone — thinner, harder, more likely to bite.

And yet.

And yet if she sent her father, she saved the person she loved most and failed everything else she believed in. And if she sent her brother, she served justice and history and fourteen dead strangers, and committed, personally, with her own hands, the act of choosing which Hartwell survived.

Revolutions are ink for other people's papers. She had believed that her whole life, comfortably, the way you believe in winter — it happens somewhere, to someone, annually, and then it passes. Now the revolution had climbed into her type cases and was setting itself in thirty-two-point across her family, and the proof was coming off the press tonight, and there was no correcting the galley after.

"Say I choose Tomas," she said slowly. "Say I let him testify. How does it happen? He surrenders? Walks into the tribunal with his testimony and a hymn?"

"Surrenders publicly, at Ilsa's trial. They've charged me — did Corvin tell you? Sedition, incitement, operating an unlicensed press. Flattering, really. They got the unlicensed part wrong; mine's licensed, just under a false name." Ilsa permitted herself the ghost of a smile. "He gives testimony before the tribunal. Sworn, witnessed, copied and dispatched abroad within the hour by riders we've already placed. Even if Voss's people shoot him by evening — and they will — the copies exist. You can drown a witness, August. You cannot drown paper. Your own shop taught me that."

"Don't," August said. "Don't use my shop against me. It's low."

"It's effective."

"It's *low and effective*, which is worse." She rubbed her eyes until colors bloomed. "And if I choose my father. Say it plainly. Say the ugly version."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment, and for once did not reach for the elegant phrasing. "Then an old man who cannot remember yesterday boards a boat and lives out his years confusing you with your mother, and a witness who saw fourteen people murdered drowns in exile by inches instead of a cell by feet, and Magistrate Voss presides over the assizes next spring with a commendation. That's the ugly version. I don't enjoy it any more than you do. I liked Elias. He set a clean page, and he once hid me in the paper store for three days in '44 and never once asked why."

"He told me about that," August said. "He thinks he was hiding a crate of Bibles."

"He was hiding me. I was reading the Bibles. Everyone was very uncomfortable."

The laugh escaped before August could stop it, and once out it turned traitor, wobbling into something else entirely, and Ilsa — tactician, conspirator, wanted woman — reached across the composing stone and gripped August's wrist, hard, and held on, and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

When August could speak again she said, "You want me to choose Tomas."

"I want the truth to survive. I would prefer it didn't cost me another friend." Ilsa released her wrist and stood, resuming her native altitude. "But yes. If it must cost someone, I want it to cost the one who's already spent himself deciding. Tomas chose Millbridge Ford the moment he climbed out of that water. We are only arguing about the paperwork."

"Paperwork is my entire life, Ilsa."

"Then do it before dawn." At the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, silhouetted against the thin gray light beginning upstairs. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you to your craft. Whatever you decide — decide it as yours. Don't forge your way out. I've watched what forgery costs the forger. It's the one crime where the victim and the perpetrator sleep in the same skull."

Her footsteps went up and away. The door at the top did not quite close — it never did; the frame had swollen years ago — and the rectangle of gray light lay across the floor like a dropped rule stick.

August sat alone with the candle, the list, the pen.

She thought about her father's hands, which still knew their work even when his mind had wandered off. Some mornings he could set a perfect line of type and have no idea whose shop he stood in. The hands remembered. Only the story was gone. Was a man his memory, or his hands, or neither, or both? If she saved Elias, what exactly was she saving — the man, or her need for him?

She thought about Tomas at nine years old, walking into the river at Millbridge Ford — the swimming hole, the innocent one, decades before the other ford earned its name — and coming up shouting, fearless, the first one in every spring. *Come in, August, it's not as cold as it looks.* It was always exactly as cold as it looked. He went in anyway. That was the whole boy, and the whole man, summarized: he went in anyway.

And she thought about the letter she would have to write. Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it — she had understood it somewhere in the last hour, the way you understand a knot by finally seeing where the end goes through. If she chose, she could not announce the choice. Tomas would refuse to go if ordered by the committee; he'd sooner fight Ilsa physically, and he might win. Elias would refuse to leave without Tomas, gently, stubbornly, forever. No. Whoever went would have to believe the choice was their own — or the other's. And she was the only person in this town who could manufacture belief out of lead and ink. She had been forging her brother's hand since she was eleven years old, signing his school exercises, signing his apology to the baker, signing, once, terribly, a valentine to the miller's daughter. She knew his loops the way she knew her own pulse.

She could write a letter that sent Tomas into exile believing it was his own idea.

She could write a word in the margin of a manifest that sent her father up the gangplank believing Tomas was already aboard.

Two names. One berth. One pen. Hers.

The candle burned low. Outside, the gray had thickened toward actual morning, and with it came sounds that did not belong to ordinary dawn — boots, many of them, in rhythm, somewhere beyond the flour mill. Drums. And under the drums, distant but unmistakable, the flat concussions of artillery being unhorsed and emplaced on the ridge road.

Voss was not waiting for the tribunal. Voss was coming to collect the town.

August snuffed the candle, folded the list of fourteen into her bodice against her heart, and ran for the stairs.

* * *

She met Corvin halfway up, breathless, hatless, alive with terrible news. "Troops on the ridge — thousands — Ilsa says the press comes apart in twenty minutes or we all—"

"Where's my father?"

"With Ilsa. August—" He caught her arm. "The boat. Dawn's gone, but the pilot will wait till the shelling starts, not past it. One berth. It has to be *now*."

Above them, through the swollen doorway, the town began to ring its bells — not the measured peal of Sunday but the ragged urgent clangor of alarm, all the churches at once, out of time with each other, like fourteen clocks all struck at random.

August took the stairs two at a time.
