## 15. Voss Retakes the Town

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and August Hartwell was asleep on a stack of unbound declarations when the first cannon spoke.

She came awake all at once, the way she always had — her father said she'd been born that way, arriving in the world like a dropped tray of type, everything scattered and immediate. For one merciful second she did not know where she was. Then the smell told her: ink, tallow, wet stone, the cellar of the old tannery with its ceiling low enough to brush your hair. Then the sound told her the rest.

Not fireworks. Not quarry blasting. Cannon.

Ilsa was already standing at the foot of the stairs, fully dressed, listening the way a blind woman listens, her head tipped as though reading something written in the air above them. She had not slept, August realized. Perhaps she never did.

"How many?" August asked.

"One gun so far." Ilsa's voice was flat and quick, the voice she used for facts. "One gun means they're ranging. Two means they've found their distance. After that it's arithmetic."

"And after arithmetic?"

"Arithmetic is what loses revolutions, girl. Get up."

The cellar stirred around them — six printers and two students who'd been setting type since midnight, blinking at lamps, hands already moving to cover the stone with its leather apron out of pure habit, as though the press were a sleeping animal they mustn't startle. Corvin came down the ladder last, moving stiffly. He'd been on watch at the grating behind the coal yard, and his coat was dark across one shoulder with more than rain.

"You're bleeding," August said.

"I'm leaking," Corvin corrected, lowering himself onto a crate. "It's different. Bleeding is dramatic; leaking is just a body expressing an opinion." He grinned at her, feverish and thin, the same grin he'd worn collapsed at the loading door a lifetime ago, clutching satchels of pamphlets like a man delivering scripture. "They're in the streets by the Custom House. Dragoons first, then foot. They learned from the bridge — they won't be kind about it this time."

August's hands found the type case without her telling them to. The testimony plates — fourteen names, sworn statements, the whole terrible ledger of Millbridge Ford — lay wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom beneath the twenty-point founts, where she herself had hidden them while Brandt's nervous fingers hovered over her father's cases a world of weeks ago. Fourteen names weighing less than four pounds. Everything the dead had left behind, fit under one arm.

"August." Ilsa stood over her. "Listen to me now, because there will be no good moment later. If the town falls — *when* the town falls — those plates leave. Not the press, not the declaration, not us. The plates. Do you understand? A press can be rebuilt. A witness cannot be reprinted."

"Tomas—"

"Tomas knows his own arithmetic." Something crossed Ilsa's face then, quickly, like a shutter closing against weather — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's coats. "He made his choice before you ever knew he was alive. All you have to do is carry what he saw."

*All.* August thought of her father at the riverbank with his newspaper, calling Tomas to supper in a house that no longer existed. She thought of one berth on one boat. But that decision belonged to another night, another self, and this morning had no use for her yet.

Above them, the second gun spoke.

"Two," said Ilsa. "There's the arithmetic."

* * *

Corvin ran because running was the only part left to him.

He had done the mathematics of his own body weeks ago — the cough, the shoulder wound that wouldn't close, the fevers that arrived now on schedule like rent. A man in his condition could carry a bundle, hold a door, or stand somewhere inconvenient. This morning he chose the door.

The retreat went up Tanner's Lane toward the river: students with muskets older than their fathers, printers with pikes improvised from press irons, Ilsa ahead with the calm of a woman crossing her own kitchen. Behind them, at the mouth of the lane, the dragoons came on in that awful unhurried way cavalry has, as though the street itself had agreed to cooperate with them.

Corvin stopped at the corner where the cooper's yard jutted out and made a wall of himself and a handcart full of barrel staves.

"Go on!" he bellowed upward, to the retreating backs, to nobody, to the rain-dark sky. "I'll hold the literary discussion!"

A trooper fired. The ball took him high in the chest and sat him down among the staves with a strange gentleness, as if the yard had decided to catch him. He found he could still laugh, which surprised him. He thought — with the peculiar clarity of a man whose accounts are nearly settled — that August would understand the joke. He had come to this town dying of fever at a stranger's back door, and he would leave it dying of gunfire in a stranger's street, and between those two doors he had been alive in a way the fever years had never allowed. That was the whole of it. That was enough of it.

They came around the cart at last. He threw a barrel stave at the nearest one and missed by a distance that would have shamed him at any other point in his life.

His last thought was entirely practical, which would have pleased him: *she has the plates. She has the plates.*

* * *

August ran through lanes she had walked since childhood and did not recognize a single one of them.

Smoke had rewritten the town overnight. The bakery on Corn Row was a black tooth. The schoolhouse had a hole in it large enough to walk through, and through the hole she could see the alphabet cards still pinned above the empty desks — *A is for Apple, B is for Bell* — absurd and unbearable. Somewhere a woman was calling a name over and over with the rhythm of a pump handle, drawing grief up from a deep well, bucket after bucket.

Elias. The thought arrived like cold water. Her father had gone out at first light — gone out *alone*, gone out into *this* — because in his mind it was some other morning entirely, some morning years deep, when papers still needed delivering and Tomas still missed his supper.

She turned toward the river, and a hand caught her sleeve. Ilsa.

"The quay," Ilsa gasped. "There may be a boat. There will be nothing else after today."

"My father—"

"Corvin?"

August shook her head. She didn't know. Ilsa read the truth off her face anyway, the way she read everything, and for a moment the commander vanished and only a tired woman in her fifties remained, holding a girl's sleeve in a burning street.

"Then go find your father," Ilsa said. "And then, whatever else you do — however else God tests you — get the truth onto paper that outlives us. Promise me that much, August. It's the only promise worth making this morning."

Then Ilsa Reiner turned and walked back down the lane toward the dragoons, alone and upright, carrying nothing but herself, and August lost sight of her in the smoke, and did not see her taken, and would learn of it later the way we learn most things: too late to help, too soon to forget.

* * *

Brandt stood in the doorway of the customs office and watched the town he had spent eleven years keeping orderly disappear into disorder, and felt — of all things — relief.

He had burned his report nine days ago. Nine days of waiting for the knock on his own door, the summons, the quiet room where careers ended. Instead the army had come and made his small treason irrelevant, swallowed it whole along with everything else. There was a freedom in catastrophe, he was discovering. When the house burns down, no one audits the pantry.

An officer strode past, young and magnificent, mud to the thighs. "You! Civil authority? Where are the ringleaders?"

"Dead or scattered," Brandt said, which was even true.

"The printer. Reiner. Voss wants her alive."

"She went toward Tanner's Lane," said Brandt, pointing in precisely the correct direction, because he had discovered that the safest lie was a true thing said at the wrong time, and Ilsa Reiner had been gone from Tanner's Lane for half an hour.

The officer clattered off. Brandt remained in the doorway, a minor man in a wet coat, performing the last duty his conscience would permit him: occupying space, seeing nothing, being useless with great precision. He thought of the print shop on Cooper Street, of the girl with ink permanently under her nails, of the old man who had once set him a wedding announcement free of charge thirty years ago and gotten every letter of Brandt's difficult name right on the first try.

When the firing stopped near midday, Brandt walked down to the river to look for survivors, and found instead a woman's shoe, a broken pike, and — floating against a mooring post, face down, patient as driftwood — a fat man who had once carried satchels of contraband pamphlets through the rain and grinned about it.

Brandt stood a long moment. Then he removed his hat, which was against regulations, and said, to the river, to the man, to the account book of his own life: "You held the door."

Then he went back to work, saving what could be saved, beginning with the truth about where Ilsa Reiner had *not* been.

* * *

August found Elias at the print shop.

He was standing in the doorway of Cooper Street with his apron on, watching the smoke rise over the rooftops, and when he saw her he smiled with such ordinary delight that she nearly fell apart in the street.

"There you are," he said. "I woke and thought — well. Thought I'd open up. Saturday." It was Thursday. "Bad morning for it, seems."

"Papa." She took his arms. His wrists felt like bundled sticks. "We have to go now. Right now, down to the water."

"Is it Tomas?" he asked suddenly, sharply, lucid as a struck bell. "I heard guns. Is it my son?"

And August — who had forged one letter already and would forge worse before this story finished — looked her father in the eye and gave him the only mercy she had left in stock.

"He's ahead of us," she said. "At the boats. We don't want to miss him."

"Then why are we standing here talking?" Elias said, and gathered his coat about him with the dignity of a man going to church, and let her lead him into the smoking streets, and did not ask again.

Behind them, as they reached the quay, they both heard the crash — distant, unmistakable, iron meeting stone with malice — and August knew without turning that the soldiers had found the tannery cellar, and that the little press which had spoken for the town all these weeks was being broken into scrap and scattered in the yard like seed.

*Print another,* she thought, with a fury that steadied her rather than shook her. *Break one, and we print another. It's the one trade you can't undo by force. Every fool who smashes a press proves how frightened he is of a sentence.*

The boat was real. Small, foul-bottomed, taking on cargo and refugees in equal disorder, bound downriver on a tide that waited for no one. One berth had become three spaces among crates, if you paid, and she had her father's savings sewn into her petticoat, because she had been planning catastrophes in her spare time for weeks without admitting it.

She got Elias aboard. She got the oilcloth bundle aboard, wrapped now in spare shirts, riding against her ribs like a second heart. She stood at the rail scanning the crowd on the dock — every hat, every shoulder, every limping silhouette — looking for one walk she would know anywhere, a walk like handwriting.

The tide bell rang. The mooring lines came off the posts one by one, heavy and final, coiling into the water.

The deck moved away from the dock. A hand's width. An arm's width.

And there, at the far end of the quay, pushing through the crowd with his collar up and his face turned from the soldiers' torches, moving fast — Tomas.

Their eyes met across widening water.
