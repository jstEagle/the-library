## 16. The Forgery

The shop at midnight was the only place in the world where August Hartwell still believed in order.

Every case had its compartment, every compartment its letter, every letter its slot, and if you asked any single piece of lead where it belonged it would tell you without hesitation: *here*. She wished her family arranged itself so neatly. Her brother belonged to a cause that wanted him dead. Her father belonged to a past that was dissolving under him like sugar in rain. And she belonged, apparently, to neither — belonging instead to this bench, this lamp, this hour, and to the terrible arithmetic she had been doing since Ilsa said *one berth* and stopped talking.

She pulled the drawer of composing sticks toward her and looked, for a long moment, at nothing but type.

*Choose Tomas,* Ilsa had said, as though choosing a man were like choosing a font. *His testimony convicts Voss. The old man remembers nothing. It is no choice at all.*

That was the cruelty of it. Ilsa was right, and being right made her monstrous in a way August could not forgive yet could not argue with. Elias would board that boat and arrive somewhere safe carrying nothing but himself — his hat, his coat, the small habit of humming while he read — and none of it, not one ounce of it, would be evidence of anything except that he had once been loved. Whereas Tomas aboard that same boat would be a weapon. A living affidavit. Fourteen names spoken aloud by the one mouth Voss could never silence.

One berth. One cargo worth more than the other.

And one sister who could not, would not, let the cause do her choosing for her.

She lit a second lamp. Then, because the work ahead required it, she did something she had done ten thousand times without ever once thinking of it as preparation for anything: she fetched out the specimen book.

Her father's specimen book. Forty years of faces cut, cast, and printed by Hartwells, each page a small monument. And tucked at the back, in an envelope gone soft with age, samples of handwriting — because printers, her father liked to say, must know every kind of mark a page can carry, including the marks that are not ours. Among them, folded separately, wrapped in tissue like something embalmed: three letters from Tomas, written home from his apprenticeship in the city when he was nineteen.

August unfolded the first one and felt the paper's particular resistance, the way grief has textures you forget until your fingers remind you.

The looping g's. He made them grandly, swinging below the line like a man leaning over a fence. The t's crossed with a slight downward slash, impatient, left to right, always a hair too long. The r's narrow as fence pickets. And at the bottom, that signature — *Tomas*, the T flourishing up and back over the whole word like a hand shading its eyes against the sun.

Two years dead, said every document in the province. Alive, said three letters in a satchel carried by a feverish stranger through a loading door. Dead again by dawn, if she did what the cause demanded and put him on that boat alive; dead forever in every way that mattered, if she let him stay without ever knowing why he stayed.

She spread the letters flat and weighted their corners with quoins.

Then she sat down to learn to be her brother.

* * *

It took four false starts.

The first was too careful — every g looped to exact specification, every crossbar measured — and it read instantly as what it was: a forgery, a copy, a thing afraid of itself. Tomas had never been afraid of anything on paper. His writing leaned forward into the page the way he leaned into doorways, taking up room, certain the room was glad of him.

The second attempt she made angry, which was truer, and therefore worse, because anger made her press too hard and the nib tore, leaving a blot like a period after a sentence nobody had finished.

The third attempt she burned. Not out of failure but out of a superstitious horror at how well it had gone — how easily her wrist remembered him, how the fourth word in already sounded like his voice saying it. There are doors you should hear yourself open slowly, so you know what you're walking into.

For the fourth attempt she stopped trying to copy the letters and tried instead to remember the man. Tomas at supper, arguing politics with a mouth full of bread. Tomas repairing the shop clock with a jeweler's screwdriver held between his teeth, telling her that a mechanism was just an argument that had been settled so well it could repeat itself forever. Tomas, two years ago, standing in this exact spot, saying — and she had thought it a joke then, God help her — *"If they ever come for me, Augie, don't believe the paperwork."*

She dipped the pen. Her hands, she noticed with a strange professional calm, were perfectly steady. Printers' hands. Hands trained to hold a quoin key at the precise torque that locks a form tight enough to survive ten thousand impressions. It occurred to her, distantly, that she might hate those hands a little by morning.

*My dear friends of the Committee,* she wrote, in her brother's forward-leaning hand.

She made him decisive. That was the essential thing — Ilsa would have to believe it, and Ilsa believed very little that wasn't decisive. She made him cite duty, because Tomas had used that word sincerely even when it was inconvenient, especially then. She made him name Voss, because the letter needed to look like a man planting a flag. And she made him — here her pen hesitated for the length of one heartbeat, one tick of the shop clock — *order himself* to stay.

*I will not run,* wrote the dead man, in ink applied by his sister. *Fourteen people are owed a reckoning, and I am the last coin left to pay it. If I go abroad, I am only a rumor with a signature. Here, I can stand before a tribunal and say what I saw. Keep the plates moving. Get them out by whatever means. Do not argue with me — I learned stubbornness at our mother's knee and improved upon it.*

There. The humor was right, too — that little sideways grin of a phrase, *a rumor with a signature.* Tomas would have liked that line. That was the worst part, the part she would not let herself think about while she still had work to do: that somewhere in the middle of betraying him she was also, in some way she couldn't fix or file, giving him back to himself. Every looped g was a resurrection. She was printing her brother in the only medium left to her, and the edition size was one, and the distribution plan was a tribunal, and the price of the whole run was everything.

She signed it. The T flourished up and back over the word like a hand shading its eyes.

Then she blotted it, set down the pen, and put her head in her ink-stained hands, and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds — she watched them off the clock face, the clock whose ticking was the oldest sound in her life — to be a girl crying at a workbench.

Sixty seconds was all the budget allowed. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, took up the pen again, and addressed the envelope in her own plain hand, because the letter would travel through other people's pockets before it reached the Committee, and a forged letter sealed by a forged hand invites scrutiny that a forged letter under an honest seal does not.

The wax pooled red as anything else in this town lately. She pressed her father's seal into it — the little hart, the print-shop emblem, antlers like a crown nobody had voted for.

One letter finished. One deception to go.

The second one was easier, and for that reason it broke her heart more thoroughly, the way the second blow of any hammer does the real damage.

* * *

Elias was awake at five, as he always was, dressed before sunrise as though punctuality were a rope he could haul himself up hand over hand out of the water he was drowning in. He was in the kitchen attempting to make coffee and had gotten as far as putting the beans in a saucepan, dry, which he was watching with the expectant patience of a man awaiting a scientific result.

"Augie," he said, relieved. "Or—" A flicker crossed his face, the familiar awful shuffle of the card catalog in his head. "Tomas?"

"August," she said gently. "Tomas isn't here."

"No," he agreed, troubled. "He's late. He'll miss his supper."

She took the saucepan from him and started the coffee properly, and he sat down at the table and folded his hands and waited, with the dignity of an emperor in exile, for the world to resume making sense. In the gray light he looked smaller than yesterday. Everything about him was slightly worn now, like type that had been overprinted — the letters still legible, but the edges softening, the serifs thinning, a face gradually returning to the blankness of uncut metal.

"Papa," she said, sitting across from him. "I have news."

"Good news?" he asked, wary. He had learned, sometime in the past year, to ask that question first. It was one of the cruelest things about memory's decline: he'd lost the details of his life but kept the shape of its disappointments.

"I think so." She set the boat ticket on the table between them. She had bought it the previous evening from Corvin's contact at the harbor office, paying in plates' worth of silver, and it lay there now — one ticket, one berth, the last boat out at dawn — looking absurdly small for the weight it carried. "You're going on a trip."

"A trip." He picked up the ticket and studied it upside down, then turned it right side up, which didn't help much. "Where?"

"Across the river. To friends. They'll take care of you."

"I have a shop," he said, with sudden firmness. "I have a press. I have— " and here his voice did the thing she could never prepare for, dropping into uncertainty mid-sentence, "—I have responsibilities. Don't I?"

"You do," August said. "And the most important one right now is to be safe. Papa — the town is in danger. There's fighting coming. You remember what happened at Millbridge Ford?"

Something moved behind his eyes. For a moment — one lucid, terrible moment — he looked directly at her, and she saw the man who had printed the death notice two years ago, the man who had suspected even then that his hands had been borrowed for a lie.

"I remember a notice," he said quietly. "I remember not asking enough questions about a notice. Is that the danger? Am I being punished for it?"

"Oh, Papa. No."

"The boy, though," he said. "If we're traveling, the boy comes too. He'll miss his supper otherwise."

Here it was, then. The moment she had rehearsed in the dark and failed to rehearse adequately, because there is no rehearsal for lying gently to a man who cannot hold the truth.

"Tomas sent word," she said, and was astonished at the steadiness of her voice, given that the words were true in the most sideways fashion imaginable. "He wants you to go ahead. He says it's better this way. He says—" and she gave her father the sentence she had composed at three in the morning, the sentence she hated most in the world and had crafted with more care than anything she had ever set in type, "—he says a printer protects his family by keeping them out of the story."

Elias considered this for a long time. Outside, the first gray was coming up over the rooftops, and somewhere near the river a bell was ringing — not fourteen bells yet, not the alarm, just the ordinary hour, but ordinary hours had grown teeth lately.

"That sounds like him," Elias said at last, and smiled, and the smile undid her completely, though her face held. "He always did decide things on my behalf. Even when he was small. I'd come to take him somewhere and find he'd already told his mother we weren't going." He chuckled, then faded. "He decided this? Himself? You're sure?"

"He decided it," August said, "above my head entirely. I'm only the messenger. I argued with him, if that helps."

It helped. She could see it help — the idea that his children were still children, still squabbling and deciding things over his head, restored to him a version of the world in which he was the father being managed rather than the man being managed, and there was comfort in that, or something shaped like comfort, and she took it, because it was all there was to take.

"And you?" he asked. "Are you coming?"

"I'm staying to close up the shop properly," she said. "Someone has to lock the cases. You taught me that."

"I taught you that," he repeated, pleased, and drank his coffee, and hummed — the little tuneless hum he made while reading — at the empty air where a newspaper should have been.

She let him believe it. That was the unforgivable sentence, she thought, listening to him hum: *she let him believe it.* Not that she lied — lies were currency in a besieged town, everyone spent them — but that she had built the lie so carefully, so lovingly, that her father would carry it onto the boat and across the river and into whatever years remained to him, and it would hold its shape the whole way, and he would die, someday, comfortably, believing his son had sent him to safety as a matter of filial routine.

Some forgeries you burn. Some you frame. And some you simply live inside, like a house you built with your own hands, knowing you can never move out.

* * *

She walked him to the dock as the sky went the color of old newsprint.

The town was quiet in the wrong way — shutters closed, streets empty except for patrols and one dog with strong political opinions that barked at both sides impartially. Smoke stood over the far quarter where the barricades had been. Somewhere beyond it, in a cellar she could not think about too precisely or her feet would turn of their own accord, Ilsa was already in custody, and Corvin was wherever the dead wait, and Tomas—

Tomas was a letter in her pocket. That was all she could safely feel. She had delivered it to the Committee's fallback drop at midnight; by now it had been read, weighed, argued over, and — if her wrist had remembered him truly — obeyed. *Do not argue with me.* He wouldn't. Nobody could forge stubbornness that convincing unless it ran in the family.

At the gangplank Elias stopped and gripped her shoulders with both hands, and for a moment his eyes were perfectly clear, perfectly present, the man from forty years of specimen pages looking straight at her.

"You're a good compositor," he said. "Better than me, if I'm honest, which I try to be on Tuesdays." A pause. The clarity wavered like lamp-flame. "Whichever one you are today — August or Tomas — your mother and I are very proud."

"August today," she managed.

"August today," he agreed happily, and kissed her forehead, and went up the plank with his ticket in his fist like a man boarding a train to a routine appointment, humming his humming song, and did not look back, and she loved him so much for not looking back that it felt identical to grief.

The boat cast off. The river took him, gray and slow and indifferent, carrying away the only cargo August had ever cared about protecting.

She stood on the dock until the boat was small. Then smaller. Then a suggestion. Then hers alone to imagine.

And it was then — turning away, pulling her shawl tight, composing her face into the blank efficiency the streets demanded — that she saw him.

Down the wharf, running. Coat flying, satchel slapping his hip, taking the puddles without slowing, twenty years falling off him with every stride — a man sprinting for a boat that had already let go of its moorings, and failing, and stopping at the end of the pier with his chest heaving, staring at the wake.

Tomas.

In his hand, crumpled now, the letter. Her letter. His handwriting. His decision, written by another hand entirely, arriving too late to be disobeyed.

He looked up. Across fifty feet of wet planks and two years of deliberate silence, his eyes found hers — and she watched him read her face the way a compositor reads a galley proof, letter by letter, and reach the last line, and understand everything: the berth, the ticket, the forgery, the sixty seconds of crying at the workbench, all of it, published in a single glance between siblings fluent in each other.

He did not shout. He did not wave. He pressed the crumpled letter once to his chest — *do not argue with me* — and shook his head, almost laughing, almost weeping, and turned back toward the town where fourteen bells were waiting to be answered.

August stood a moment longer. Then she walked home to lock the cases, as her father taught her, past the clock tower striking six — one tick at a time, all the way up.
