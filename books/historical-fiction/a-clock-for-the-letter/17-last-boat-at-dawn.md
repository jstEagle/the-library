## 17. Last Boat at Dawn

The river wore a skin of fog that morning, the way it did on the coldest days, as if it were ashamed of what it carried and preferred not to be seen doing it.

August stood at the dock with her father's coat over her arm and his hat in her hand, and she watched the steam of her own breath rise and vanish, rise and vanish, like a lesson in impermanence she had no intention of learning. The boat — a squat, stubborn paddle-steamer called the *Resolute*, which struck her as either the funniest or cruelest name available — lay against the pilings with its boiler already muttering to itself like an old man waking up grumpy.

"August." Her father patted his pockets with the methodical anxiety of a man who has misplaced something important and cannot remember what category of thing it was. "August, I've lost my—"

"You haven't lost anything, Papa." She settled the hat on his head herself, tilting it slightly left, because Elias Hartwell had worn his hat tilted left for forty years and if the world was ending, it would end with him properly hatted. "Everything you need is in the bag. I packed it twice."

"Twice?"

"In case I forgot something the first time."

He considered this and found it reasonable, which was itself a small heartbreak; there had been a time when Elias Hartwell would have caught the illogic of that sentence before it finished leaving her mouth. He had taught her the logic of type — every letter in its compartment, every compartment in its case, everything answerable to the composing stick. Now the cases in his mind were jumbled, sorts spilled everywhere, and some mornings he set whole paragraphs from memory only to have them dissolve by noon.

But this morning — she checked, the way she always checked, reading his face for the flicker of the stranger who sometimes lived behind her father's eyes — this morning he was mostly himself. Enough of himself. Enough to be embarrassed by the fuss.

"You should come with me," he said suddenly, gripping her wrist. His hands were still printer's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles no matter how he scrubbed. "A girl your age shouldn't stay in a town like this. It's not — August, it's not safe here. There's been trouble."

"There has," she agreed gently.

"I read about it." He hesitated. "Did I read about it? I remember reading something."

She remembered him printing it. Three nights ago, in the cellar press, while shells fell somewhere past the eastern ridge and Ilsa's typesetters worked with candles guttering in the draught. But that was not a memory she could give him, so she gave him another instead.

"You read about it," she said. "And now you're going to go and tell them about it. All the way across the water. You're going to tell everyone."

"I am?"

"Papa." She took both his hands. Somewhere behind her, on the dock, a sailor shouted something about the tide, and the *Resolute* coughed a fistful of steam at the sky. "Listen to me. There is a man who is going to meet us here, or try to. And when you see him, you mustn't ask him any questions. Can you do that? No questions."

Elias frowned. "Is it someone I owe money to?"

Despite everything — despite the sleepless night, despite the forgery drying in her satchel, despite the fact that her brother was at this very moment walking toward a dock from which he would never depart — August laughed. A short, cracked laugh, but real.

"No questions," she said. "Promise me."

"I promise," said her father, who had promised a magistrate two years ago that he had printed a death notice honestly, and who could no longer remember whether he had lied or simply obeyed, and who was, of all their family, perhaps the only innocent left.

* * *

They waited on a bench beneath the customs shed, where the paint peeled in long curls and somebody had chalked *LIBERTÉ ÉGALITÉ FRATERNITÉ* and then, more practically underneath, *AND WARM BOOTS*. August held her father's bag between her feet and felt the shape of the forged letter through the canvas, folded into Tomas's coat pocket upstairs in the room they'd rented, where Tomas believed it waited for him along with instructions he thought were his own.

That was the part she kept turning over, the way her tongue finds a broken tooth: he would believe it was his idea. That was the mercy of it, or the cruelty, depending on which hour of the night you asked her. She had sat at the borrowed desk by candlelight with specimens of his handwriting spread around her — letters Corvin had carried, letters from before, a grocery list from 1844 that she'd kept because even then she'd loved his hand — and she had written, in looping g's and t's crossed with that particular impatient slash, an order from Tomas Hartwell to Tomas Hartwell: *Stay. Fight. Testify. It is the only thing worth doing and we both know it. Do not follow me. A.*

The *A.* at the end had taken her twenty minutes. Four attempts. Because signing his letter with her name meant he would know, instantly and forever, exactly what she had done, and she could not make her hand produce those two small characters until she pretended she was setting them in type — just sorts, just lead, nothing alive in them at all.

"Eh." Her father nudged her. "You're frowning at the river. You'll get wrinkles."

"You're one to talk. Your face looks like a proof pulled too soon."

"It does not." He touched his cheek with genuine concern, and she squeezed his hand and told him he was handsome, and he said, "Well. Obviously," and for a moment they were entirely themselves again, two printers sharing the family joke, and August had to look out at the fog because her eyes were doing something unforgivable.

The bells started at half past five.

Not all of them, and not for long — a ragged handful of strokes from the cathedral, cut off mid-peal, the sound of a town being repossessed. Government troops had entered the western quarter in the night. Everyone at the dock knew it and nobody said it; they stood in little clusters, holding bags and children, pretending the tide was the only thing that mattered.

"They're ringing early," Elias said. "Bells don't ring at half five. Unless—" He turned to her, and for one terrible moment she thought the lucidity had arrived at the worst possible door. "August. Where is Tomas? You said he'd be here."

Had she said that? She couldn't remember saying it. She might have. In the night, in his hearing, she might have let slip anything.

"He'll come," she said. "He's coming."

And God forgive her, she looked down the road and hoped he wouldn't. Hoped the checkpoints had stopped him. Hoped the fog had thickened, the roads closed, the world conspired to keep him away from this dock — because the letter was in his coat, but letters can be ignored, and brothers are only human, and if Tomas reached this quay and saw his father boarding alone, what was stopping him from tearing up her forgery and shoving the old man back onto dry land?

Nothing, she realized. Nothing but his own good heart, which had never once obeyed anyone, including her.

The *Resolute*'s mate came down the shed steps calling names off a manifest. "Hartwell! Elias Hartwell! One passenger, one berth, papers in order?" He said it loudly enough for the whole dock to hear, which was the point; the boat was leaving under Voss's successor's nose precisely because it looked like nothing — one old man, one daughter seeing him off, a bag of clothes and, beneath the false bottom August had built into it at midnight with a chisel and shaking hands, fourteen names and a sworn testimony and enough evidence to hang a magistrate.

"That's me," said Elias, standing, and then, with the sudden piercing clarity he was granted sometimes like a cruel joke: "August. Look at me. Am I doing the right thing?"

The question hung there. He didn't know what the right thing was. He only knew that his daughter's face was answering it wrong.

"Yes," she said. "Papa, yes. This is right. I promise you this is right."

He kissed her forehead, both cheeks, forehead again, the old pattern, and muttered into her hair, "Mind the shop. Mind the embers. Don't let the type freeze — cold cracks the lead—" and then he was crying without appearing to notice, the way old men cry, as though the weather were merely bad, and she walked him down the gangplank with her spine straight and her hand steady on his elbow, and the sailor took his bag, and the mate helped him over the coaming, and Elias Hartwell turned at the rail and waved.

He waved at her. Just her. And she waved back, and smiled, and kept smiling, and kept waving, because if she stopped waving she would have to start screaming, and there was no berth for screaming on the last boat out.

The *Resolute* backed water. The paddles churned the fog into rags. The horn gave one long mournful note, and the boat swung its bow downstream, and grew small, and became a smudge, and was gone.

* * *

Then there was nothing to do but stand on the emptying dock and wait for her brother.

The fog thinned as the sun came up, reluctant and red. Gulls argued over something in the shallows. The customs shed clock — a public clock, municipal, indifferent — crawled through the minutes, and August discovered that she could feel each one physically, ticking up through the boards into her shoes.

She rehearsed what she would say. That was the absurdity; she had committed treason, fraud, and forgery in a single night, and what she was rehearsing was an apology. *Tomas, I'm sorry. Tomas, it had to be one of you. Tomas, don't hate me. Tomas, please, please understand—*

She heard him before she saw him. Running. That uneven, urgent slap of boots on wet planks that she had known since childhood, when he'd run everywhere, late for everything, arriving breathless and delighted. He burst out of the fog at the landward end of the dock with his coat unbuttoned and his hair wild, and he stopped dead when he saw her, and the distance between them — forty yards of grey boards — suddenly contained the entire war.

"Tomas—"

He didn't run to her. He looked past her, at the river. At the empty river. At the place where the boat wasn't.

She watched it happen. Watched him scan the water as if the *Resolute* might still materialize out of the mist, watched his shoulders drop half an inch, watched his hand go to his breast pocket and find the letter — her letter, his letter, the letter in his own handwriting that ordered him to stay — and she saw him draw it out and unfold it and hold it in the flat red light.

He read it. She knew he read it, because his lips moved; they always moved when he read, ever since he was six. And then the lips stopped moving, and his eyes came up from the page slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water, and they found hers across the forty yards of dock, and everything in them changed.

It was not anger. She had braced for anger. It was worse and better than anger: it was recognition. He read the letter again — she saw him do it, a second pass, faster — and she could see the exact moment he understood it, because his hand began to shake and he pressed the paper flat against his leg to stop it, the way their father pressed proofs flat, and then he looked up at her a third time and he knew.

He knew she had written it. He knew why. He knew that she had chosen — that when the choice came down to two names and one berth, her hand had picked up the pen and made herself the author of both their lives. And he knew, too — she saw this arrive in his face last of all, like dawn reaching the deepest room — that she had done it so that the decision would be his. So that whatever happened next, happened to a man who had chosen it. Not to a sacrifice. To a signature.

Forty yards. Neither of them moved.

What was there to say that wouldn't shatter it? If he crossed to her he would have to curse her or forgive her, and forgiveness spoken aloud can be revoked. If she crossed to him she would have to explain, and explanation is the enemy of understanding. So they stood in the ruin of the morning and said none of it. He raised the letter slightly, an accusation or an absolution, he didn't say which and his face refused to decide. She put her hand flat over her heart, which was either an apology or a promise, and probably both.

A gull screamed. The tide sucked at the pilings. The municipal clock ticked off another minute of the new order, and somewhere west of the river, faint as a pulse, the guns went on about their business.

Then Tomas folded the letter — carefully, corner to corner, the way you fold something you intend to keep forever — and put it back inside his coat, over his heart, and squared his shoulders, and turned toward the town.

Toward the tribunal. Toward the testimony. Toward the dawn that was coming for him in a few days' time, though neither of them yet knew how few.

At the head of the dock he stopped once and looked back, and called out — his voice carrying clear across the water and the years:

"Mind the shop! Don't let the type freeze!"

Their father's words. Their family's benediction. August laughed and sobbed in the same breath and called back, "Cold cracks the lead!"

He raised one hand, high, palm open — and was gone into the streets.

August stood alone on the dock for a long time after the footsteps faded. The sun cleared the ridge finally and laid a rough gold road straight down the middle of the river, all the way to the horizon, all the way to wherever her father was at that moment, tilting his hat to the left and telling some stranger that obviously he was handsome.

Then she picked up her satchel, and turned, and walked back toward a town that belonged to Magistrate Voss's successors — back toward the cellar where Ilsa sat in chains, back toward the press she would have to rebuild piece by piece, sort by sort.

Back toward the work. Because her brother had given her his answer without a word, and the answer was four letters long, and it was not *stay*.

It was *print*.
