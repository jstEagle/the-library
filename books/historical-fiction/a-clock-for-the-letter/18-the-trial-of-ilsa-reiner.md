## 18. The Trial of Ilsa Reiner

There is a particular silence in a courtroom before the accused is brought in, and I have decided it sounds exactly like the silence after a press has been inked and set, in the moment before the lever comes down. Everything that will be printed already exists. The paper only waits to receive it.

I sat in the third row with my hands folded like a man at his own funeral, which, in a sense, I was attending. Two years dead, one week resurrected, and already practicing for the second go. The tribunal occupied the magistrate's bench that Voss had occupied before them — three men in gray coats, weary from the retaking of the town, who had the look of clerks handed an arithmetic problem too large for their columns. Behind them, on the wall, the government crest had been pried off in the night by persons unknown, leaving a pale rectangle. Someone had chalked a small bird in the empty space. Nobody had rubbed it out.

The gallery was full. Printers mostly, and their wives, and the students who had survived the barricades, sitting very straight as if posture were a kind of ammunition. At the front, flanked by two soldiers whose youth embarrassed everyone present, sat Ilsa Reiner.

She looked smaller than the woman who had once tested my sister with a dangerous errand and a level stare. The weeks since the cellar press was smashed had not been kind — her hands were bandaged where they had broken something in the raid, though whether plates or fingers I never learned — but her spine was the same spine. When the president of the tribunal read the charges — sedition, incitement, the operation of an unlicensed press, the distribution of false and inflammatory matter — she listened the way August sorts type: cataloguing, weighing each letter, finding none of it worth arguing over.

"How do you answer?" the president asked.

"Guilty of all of it," Ilsa said, "and proud of most."

A sound went through the gallery like wind through a case of loose type. One of the soldiers shifted. The president removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on, having bought himself four seconds in which he did not have to decide anything.

"The tribunal will hear evidence," he said.

And then I stood up.

* * *

I want to record, for whoever prints this afterward, that my legs shook and my voice did not, and that I found this arrangement acceptable. A man's legs may do as they please so long as his testimony holds.

"I am Tomas Hartwell," I said, "son of Elias Hartwell, printer, brother of August Hartwell, compositor. By the record of this town I have been dead since the spring of 1846. The record is wrong."

Every face turned. I had expected the sensation of it, the weight of two hundred stares, and I had expected it would feel like drowning. It did not. It felt like setting a difficult line: you place each word where it must go, and your hands are steady because there is no other work for them.

"Fourteen men drowned at Millbridge Ford on the ninth of June, 1846," I said. "They were strikers. They were unarmed. Magistrate Voss ordered the sluice gates opened while they stood in the channel bed clearing debris, on the pretext that the water could not be stopped in time. The water could be stopped in time. I watched it not be stopped. I was the only witness who got out alive, and the government hanged a man in my name to make certain no one else ever heard about it."

The president said, "You will state your evidence formally and in order," which was the voice of a man clinging to procedure the way a drowning clerk clings to his ledger.

"I intend to," I said. "It will take some time. I apologize to the tribunal in advance for both."

* * *

I told it all. The meeting at the mill, the promise that the repairs were routine, Voss in his good coat standing on the bank with his hands clasped behind him like a gentleman admiring the weather. The engineer who begged for ten minutes to close the gates and was refused. The sound the water made — I made myself describe it, because the tribunal needed to understand that fourteen men did not simply die, they were *removed*, the way a bad line is lifted from the stone, and what remained was a ford that ran quiet and a payroll dispute that resolved itself overnight.

"Their names," I said, and I read them. All fourteen. I had written them out the night before in August's shop, in the new typeface she was cutting, tracing the letters with my finger until I could have recited them upside down and mirrored. Brandt — yes, Inspector Brandt, formerly of the magistrate's office, currently a deserter with clean hands and a haunted expression — had obtained me the coroner's list from '46, and against it I laid the names the survivors' families had given the committee. They matched, all but one, a boy listed as *unknown, approx. seventeen years*, whom I knew as Ferenc, because he had once beaten me at cards behind the tannery and cheated doing it.

"Ferenc Kovacs," I said. "Seventeen. He owed me eleven kreuzers. I would give a great deal to collect it."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't expected them to. But Ilsa turned in her seat and looked at me — the old level stare, worn thin at the edges now — and something passed between us that needed no words. *You should not be here,* she mouthed. *Neither should you,* I mouthed back, and she almost smiled.

Voss was not present. He had been taken three days after the town fell back into government hands, attempting to leave with two trunks and a forged passport so incompetent that even August, who was becoming something of an authority on forgery, pronounced it an insult to the craft. He sat now in the room beneath us, in the cell where Ilsa had spent her weeks, awaiting his own proceeding. The tribunal had scheduled it with the grim efficiency of men tidying up after an earthquake. His lawyer had arrived from the capital, a smooth fellow named Herr Reis, whose entire strategy, as far as anyone could tell, consisted of saying the word *hearsay* with great conviction.

He said it eleven times during my testimony. I counted, the way you count g's in a galley when you cannot sleep. On the twelfth occasion I stopped, faced him, and said:

"Herr Counsel, you may call it hearsay. I call it Tuesday. I was there. Ask me anything about that day — the color of the sky, the smell of the channel, the order in which the men went under — and I will answer, and every answer will hang your client a little higher."

Reis opened his mouth. The president said, rather gently, "The witness will confine himself to facts," but his eyes, when they met mine, were not unkind, and I understood that the hearing had already been decided somewhere above our heads, in ministries and back rooms, and that we were all of us merely setting type for a page already composed. This did not trouble me as much as it might have. Composed pages still need someone to print them. That had always been the work of Hartwells.

* * *

They called Ilsa to speak after me. She rose, declined counsel — "I have read enough law to know when it is decoration" — and gave her account plainly: the press in the cellar, the pamphlets, the declarations during the barricade week, the fourteen names she had meant to send abroad.

"We printed because no one else would," she finished. "The tribunal sits today because a press sat first. Sentence me how you like, gentlemen, but do me the courtesy of noticing whose ink you are reading your own verdict from."

The president looked down at the document before him — typed, in a government office, on a government press — and had the grace to look briefly embarrassed.

Before she sat, she paused beside me in the aisle. She was shackled at the wrists, and she used the chain's slack to touch my shoulder, once, lightly, the way you'd test whether bread had cooled.

"You know what testifying costs," she said quietly.

"I've had it itemized," I said.

"Your sister will hate the tribunal for it. Don't let her hate herself. That's an instruction from a condemned woman; those carry weight."

"She doesn't take instructions well."

"No," Ilsa said. "I noticed. It's why I trusted her." And she went back to her seat with her chin up, a woman entirely finished with pretending to hope.

* * *

The verdict came the following noon. Guilty on all counts against Reiner — the tribunal, to its faint credit, refused the capital charge, sentencing her to twelve years in the fortress at Stein, which in the arithmetic of 1848 might as well have been forever but left the arithmetic of mercy intact. For Voss, tried the same afternoon in a proceeding so swift it seemed apologetic, the verdict was different: guilty of the murders at Millbridge Ford, stripped of office, sentenced to death.

When it was read, the gallery did not cheer. I had thought perhaps they would. Instead there was that courtroom silence again, the pre-lever silence, two hundred people feeling the same thing and finding it too heavy to pass between them. Justice, it turns out, does not feel like triumph. It feels like a debt finally entered in the ledger after years of the book refusing to balance. You do not celebrate. You exhale.

Voss himself did not exhale. They brought him up in irons for the reading, and he stood very straight in his ruined good coat, and when the president finished, Voss looked directly at me — across the room, past the soldiers, with the same mild interest he had shown from the riverbank — and inclined his head perhaps half an inch. Acknowledgment. One professional to another, as if we had merely disagreed about the closing of gates.

I did not nod back. But I did not look away either, and I think, between the two of us, that was the greater discourtesy.

* * *

That evening August came to the safe house where Brandt and I lodged, carrying bread, cheese, a bottle of the rough red wine Father used to thin for winter, and an expression that meant argument.

"They'll commute him," she said, meaning me, setting the bottle down hard enough to slosh. "Voss is convicted; the point is made. You could leave. There are boats. There are always boats, August Hartwell finds them, it's her single talent—"

"Her single talent is forging my handwriting," I said. "Boats come second."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny. You wrote a letter ordering me to stay and fight. In my hand. With my looping g's. I've read it forty times. You crossed the t's too hard — you always cross them too hard when you're lying — otherwise it's flawless. I'm considering an apprenticeship."

"Tomas."

"August." I took the bread and cut it, because hands that are doing something can carry on a conversation their owners cannot. "Sit down. Eat. Listen to me once, all the way through, which I believe is a first in the history of this family."

She sat. She ate. She listened, arms crossed, jaw set, looking so exactly like Father reviewing a misprinted proof that I nearly lost the thread.

"Ilsa goes to Stein tomorrow," I said. "Twelve years, for printing the truth that I am the only man alive able to swear to. If I run, the sentence stands, and Voss's lawyers will spend the next decade picking at my absence — a witness who fled, they'll say, a witness who could not bear cross-examination. Every year they'll grind the verdict thinner. But if I stand in that room, on the record, sworn, examined and cross-examined and *still* standing — then it holds. Not just for Voss. For the next Voss, and the one after him. A verdict is only type, August. It says nothing unless somebody prints it. I am the printing."

"And the cost?"

"The cost is known." I poured the wine, two glasses, because refusing one would have been cruel. "Father asked me yesterday — lucid, a whole hour of him — what day it was. I told him. He nodded and said, 'Good, the boy will be home for supper,' and went back to watching the river. Do you understand? Somewhere in him I am still nine years old and punctual. That is where I intend to live from now on. It's a better address than the one the tribunal is offering, and the rent is free, and you visit often."

She was crying, silently, furiously, the way she did everything.

"He'll call me by your name," she said. "He does it more now."

"Then answer to it," I said. "Answer kindly. He means one of us, and we were always the same person anyway; you just got the steadier hands."

We drank the wine. It was terrible. We finished the bottle.

At the door she stopped and said, without turning around: "The bells. Afterward. I want you to know I'll be listening for them."

"Fourteen times," I said. "Once per victim. Make it fifteen if you like. I won't tell anyone."

"That's not funny either."

"No," I admitted. "But it's true, and you've always preferred true."

* * *

The night before the end, I walked the town. This required no great courage; the curfew patrols knew my face now and saluted awkwardly, uncertain whether I was a hero or a nuisance, and settled for nodding. I went past the shop, dark, the press sleeping under its canvas like a big loyal dog. I went down to Millbridge Ford, because it seemed important to see it running quiet and harmless, the water going about its business with no memory at all. Water forgets everything. That is its privilege and its flaw.

On the way back I passed the courthouse clock, which had stopped during the fighting and been repaired — I will note, with modest pride, by me, in an afternoon, since a town that has just convicted a magistrate deserves to at least know the hour — and I stood beneath it a while, listening to it tick, thinking: *this is the whole trick of it.* Grief and time and justice, all of it just machinery, weights and escapements, and it only works if somebody keeps it wound.

Somewhere across the roofs, August's lamp was burning. She was at her cases, I knew, cutting steel letters into the shapes of a dead brother's living name, and I loved her the way you love a fire in winter: helplessly, and from a careful distance, knowing you cannot hold it without burning.

Dawn would come at six minutes past five. I had checked. I am a printer's son; I check such things.

The bell overhead gathered itself and struck the hour, clear and unhurried, and I pulled my coat tighter and started home to keep my appointment with the morning.

Fourteen times, August. Count carefully.
