## 19. Fourteen Bells

I came back to the town on the evening of the day they were going to kill my brother, and nobody stopped me at the gate.

That was the strangest part — not the soldiers with their new armbands and their old rifles, not the smoke still rising in gray threads from where the cellar press had been, but the sheer ordinariness of the checkpoint. A corporal glanced at my travel papers, stamped them without reading them, and waved me through as though I were a seamstress returning from her mother's funeral. Which, in a sense, I was. He even wished me a good evening.

"You as well," I said, because one learns manners the way one learns type: by repetition, until the hand does it without the heart.

The town smelled of wet ash and bread. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and somewhere else a woman was singing to a child — badly, off-key, the way mothers are permitted to sing. The uprising had lasted eleven days and had ended the way most things end, which is messily and with paperwork. Voss's troops had retaken the square on Thursday; by Friday the barricades were firewood; by Saturday there were already men in the taverns explaining loudly that they had always thought it was a mistake, that they had said so at the time, that everyone could remember them saying so.

But tonight the taverns were quiet. Tonight the whole town was holding its breath like a compositor holding a line of type — carefully, levelly, knowing that one wrong tap and everything spills.

They were going to shoot Tomas Hartwell at dawn.

* * *

I went first to the shop.

Elias was asleep in the back room, which surprised me less than it should have. The soldiers had come through the print shop during the fighting — Brandt had told me later, in his flat, apologetic way, that he'd been assigned to the inventory and had inventoried almost nothing worth mentioning — but the presses stood untouched under their dust sheets, and the type cases were exactly where I had left them, false bottoms and all, empty now of everything except lead and lies. Someone had left a pot of tea on the stove, gone cold. Ilsa's doing? No. Ilsa was in the prison on the hill, awaiting her own trial. Corvin was dead. The tea must have been Elias himself, some flicker of routine surfacing through the fog of him, an old man making tea for a daughter who wasn't there.

I sat down beside his bed and did not wake him. His breathing had the ragged rhythm of a press running slightly out of true — serviceable, but not for long. His hands lay on the blanket, and even in sleep they were curled slightly, fingers bent around ghosts of sorts and quads, the way a printer's hands do after forty years. I had inherited those hands. Looking at them, I understood something I would spend the rest of my life trying to say properly: that we do not choose our inheritances, we only choose what we set them to.

On the table beside the bed lay a newspaper, three days old, folded open to a small item on the fourth page. *One Tomas Hartwell, printer, convicted of sedition and bearing false testimony against Magistrate Voss, is sentenced to death by firing squad. Sentence to be carried out at dawn.* Below it, in the same column, an advertisement for cough syrup.

That was how history got printed about other people: between the cough syrup and the shipping notices. I had believed that once. I had built my whole careful life on that belief. It had taken a feverish stranger at my loading door, fourteen drowned strikers, and one berth on one boat to teach me otherwise, and the tuition had been everything.

I touched the newspaper item once, lightly, the way you touch a fresh impression to check the ink has taken.

Then I took out Tomas's last letter and read it again by candlelight, though I knew every loop and crossbar by heart.

* * *

He had written it the night before his surrender, and given it to the prison chaplain — or to a man dressed as a prison chaplain; knowing the committee, probably the latter — with instructions that it reach me wherever I was. It reached me in Freiburg, in the house of the sympathetic printers who had taken in the plates, on the morning I was preparing to come home anyway. I had read it standing up. I read it sitting down now, and then a third time lying awake beside my sleeping father, and it was different each time, which is the miracle of letters: they change because you change, like looking into moving water.

*Dear August,*

*I know what you did. Don't deny it, don't apologize, and above all don't cry about it, because if you cry about it I will be able to picture you crying and I would rather spend my last week picturing the look on your face when you were twelve and dropped a full case of 12-point Baskerville down the cellar stairs and swore so fluently that Father said you had a future in the trade.*

*You sent me a letter in my own hand telling me to stay and testify. It was very good. The g's were nearly perfect. You always did have a criminal eye for other people's handwriting — I remember you copying Father's signature onto our school excuses so convincingly that he signed one himself years later without noticing. I want you to know that I read it four times before I understood, and when I understood, I laughed until the man guarding me asked what was funny, and I said: my sister has finally gone into the family business.*

*Here is the thing you must believe, because you will be tempted not to: this was my idea too. Not yours alone. Ilsa argued for the boat. Ilsa shouted about it. But I sat in that cellar for two years being a name on a roster instead of a man in a room, and I am tired of being ink. Voss killed fourteen people at Millbridge Ford and the only thing standing between him and a comfortable retirement is my mouth. I can buy justice with the one coin I have left. Nobody should be allowed to make that purchase cheaply, and nobody should be prevented from making it either. You gave me the boat and I am giving it back. We are even. Do not write to argue — I know you. You are already composing the argument. Put it away. Set it in type someday instead, if you must.*

*Tell Father I fixed the clock. Tell him the escapement wanted cleaning, that's all; it was never broken, only dirty, which is the condition of most things that get called broken.*

*Your loving brother,*
*Tomas*

*I have asked them to let the bells ring afterward. Fourteen times. Once for each of them. If the town refuses, ring them yourself. You know where Father keeps the key.*

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater.

I stood in the square with perhaps two hundred others — more than the authorities expected, fewer than the town contained, the difference being fear, which keeps excellent hours. The soldiers had cordoned off the far end by the old granary wall, and the crowd stood at a respectful distance, the way crowds do, close enough to witness and far enough to pretend they hadn't. There were millworkers whose fathers had drowned at Millbridge Ford. There were students with bandaged heads from the barricades. There was Frau Ostermann from the bakery, who had once chased me out of her kitchen for stealing rolls and who now stood with her hat off, holding it against her chest like something wounded. There were also, I noticed, three men from the taverns, the ones who had explained so loudly that the uprising had always been a mistake. They stood at the edges and kept their hats on and looked at their boots. Grief embarrasses cowards. It was the only comfort available that morning and I took it.

Elias had insisted on coming.

Getting him there had taken an hour of negotiation worthy of a treaty congress. He woke confused — he often did now, waking into whichever decade his mind happened to be occupying — and for a while he was certain that it was 1842 and that Tomas was late for supper. Then, in one of those terrible lucid hours that seemed to arrive on their own schedule, he had gone very quiet, looked at me, and said: "They're going to shoot my son today."

"Yes, Papa."

"And you're going to let me stand there and see it?"

"If you want."

He had put on his good coat, the one with the ink-burn on the cuff, and buttoned it wrong, and I had rebuttoned it right, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing else that was true enough to say.

Now he stood beside me in the gray light, holding my arm, and his mind was flickering like bad type — I could feel it in his grip, moments of pressure and release. "Cold morning," he said. "Good printing weather. Damp air keeps the paper from cracking." And then, a minute later: "Where is the boy? He'll miss—" and stopped himself, because even the fog remembered.

"He knows," I said. "He's not missing anything."

The door of the granary opened at first light and they brought him out.

I had braced myself for a broken man. What came through the door was upright, unhurried, and — I swear this, because it mattered enormously to me then and matters still — clean-shaven, with his collar straightened and his cuffs turned neatly back. Someone had seen to it. Perhaps the chaplain. Perhaps a guard who had a brother of his own. Tomas walked to the wall the way he used to walk to the case when he was showing off a difficult justification, with that slight lift of the chin that meant: watch this, it's going to come out even.

He found us in the crowd. Of course he did; he'd known exactly where we'd stand. He didn't wave. He didn't smile, quite. He looked at Elias first and inclined his head the smallest degree — *good morning, Father, fine printing weather* — and then his eyes came to me, and held, and in them was no accusation at all. Only the look he used to give me across the composing stone when one of us caught the other's mistake: acknowledgment, amusement, and love, filed together under a single glance.

His lips moved. I read them the way I read everything, letter by letter. Two words.

*Set it.*

And then they tied the blindfold, and the order was given, and the thing was done with the efficiency governments reserve for the things they least want witnessed, and my brother became a body, and then a flag-draped formality, and then a sentence in a newspaper between the cough syrup and the shipping notices.

Elias made no sound. He simply gripped my arm with a strength I hadn't known was in him, and stood very straight through all fourteen seconds of it — I counted; counting is what printers do instead of screaming — and afterward he said, clearly, in the voice he used for correcting apprentices:

"Fourteen. Mark it down. Fourteen."

* * *

The bells began at noon.

I don't know who rang the first peal. I suspect the sexton of Saint Aldric's, a soft-spoken man named Pfeil who had lost a nephew at Millbridge Ford and who had spent two years ringing the ordinary services with what I now understood to be extraordinary restraint. At noon he pulled the rope, and the great bell spoke once, and the sound rolled out over the roofs and down into the streets and into the river, and every person in the market square stopped where they stood.

Once. For Brenner, the foreman, who had organized the strike.

Twice. For his wife Marta, who had stood beside him.

Three times. Fourteen times. The names I knew by then better than my own alphabet — I had set them in type, I had packed them in a crate disguised as coffin linings, I had carried them across a border sewn into the lining of a coat. Each stroke of the bell fell on the square like a full stop, and the crowd did not disperse between them, did not murmur, did not move. Two hundred people and then four hundred and then more, drawn out of shops and doorways by the sound, standing in the cold with their hats off, learning the count.

The soldiers did not stop it.

That is the detail I want recorded, whoever reads this, whenever this is read: they did not stop it. An officer started toward the church tower and then stopped, and stood with his hand on his sword hilt, and did nothing. Perhaps he lacked orders. Perhaps he understood what every printer understands: that you cannot confiscate a bell. That sound, once rung, is already distributed. That there were women on the hill road and farmers in the valley who would hear fourteen strokes at noon and ask someone why, and someone would tell them, and the telling would be a printing, though no press ran at all.

Beside me, Elias counted aloud. His voice cracked on seven and steadied by ten. When the fourteenth stroke faded, he reached over and straightened my collar, an absent fatherly gesture, and said:

"Your brother would have liked that. He always said a bell was just a strike note with good distribution." A pause, and the fog rolled gently back in. "Is the boy coming to supper? The clock says—"

"The clock's stopped, Papa," I said. "We'll wind it."

* * *

They buried him — buried *them*, the town quietly added the thirteen others to the ceremony without asking anyone's permission — on the hill behind the church, in ground that had been refused to suicides and was apparently willing to make an exception for sedition. Ilsa Reiner attended in chains, escorted by two guards who had the decency to look at the sky throughout. Her trial was set for the following month; Voss, triumphant, restored, decorated, had insisted on it, wanting the conspirators tried publicly and thoroughly, never guessing — or perhaps, in his vanity, never caring — that a courtroom is only another kind of press, and that testimony, once given under oath before witnesses, cannot be recalled from circulation any more than a bell can be unrung.

Tomas's grave marker was a plank, because stones cost money and the government certainly wasn't paying. I knelt and did the thing printers do instead of praying: I checked the alignment. The plank was crooked by perhaps two degrees. I straightened it, tamped the earth at its base with the heel of my hand, and stepped back, and it pleased me in a way I couldn't explain and didn't try to, that it stood plumb.

Elias stood at the graveside for a long time after the others had gone. The light was failing. Finally he said, conversationally, to the plank or to me or to the decade his mind was visiting:

"He mended the clock, you know. Years ago. Everyone said replace it. He said, no, it's not broken, it's only dirty." He shook his head slowly. "Most things aren't broken. They're only dirty. Somebody ought to write that down."

"Somebody will," I said.

* * *

That night I climbed the stairs to the shop and lit every lamp.

I pulled the dust sheets off both presses and oiled them, though they needed no oiling — the gesture was for me, not them. I opened the type cases one by one, all forty of them, and ran my thumb along the rows of steel and lead, greeting them like old colleagues returned from a war. In the bottom drawer of the stone counter, under a false bottom that had hidden conspiracy plates through an inspection and a raid, I found what Brandt had left there weeks ago: a single sheet of paper, unsigned. On it, in his cramped clerk's hand, were written the fourteen names of Millbridge Ford, with ages, trades, and next of kin, transcribed from the coroner's suppressed report — the report he had been ordered to destroy and had copied instead, and carried, and waited, all this time, for someone who could give it wings.

A minor man. A small disobedience. It was going to be enough. Between Brandt's list and Tomas's sworn testimony, which Ilsa had smuggled out of her cell in the hem of a chapel veil and which now lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, I had everything. Every fact. Every name. The whole account of Millbridge Ford, verified, witnessed, sworn.

What I did not have was a face for it. Any pamphlet could carry the words; any pamphlet's words could be denied, dismissed, attributed to agitators. Words float loose unless you sink them in something people trust. The government had its seal. The church had its cross. The truth had — nothing. A truth without a mark is a letter without a postmark; anyone can claim it, anyone can disown it.

I sat down at the graver's bench near the window, where the light would come in the morning, and I took up a blank punch of annealed steel, and I turned it in my fingers for a long time.

I was not a type-cutter. I was a compositor, a pressman's daughter, a forger of g's — but I had watched Herr Lindqvist cut punches in Freiburg, and I had asked questions, and he had let me hold the graver, and I had the one qualification that cannot be taught, which is that I could not think of anything else to do with my grief except make it into something with serifs.

I struck the first cut before dawn: a stem, straight and unflinching, the way he had stood at the wall.

Outside, the town slept. Inside, the lamps burned, and the clock on the wall — Tomas's clock, cleaned and wound, keeping perfect time — ticked steadily behind me like a patient editor, and the steel took the edge of the graver with a small bright whisper that sounded, if you leaned into it, almost like a name beginning.

I worked until my eyes failed, slept two hours, and woke with the design fully arrived in my head, complete, waiting for my hand the way copy waits for the case.

It would need a lowercase g, of course. Looped. Crossed t's, generous ones.

I knew exactly whose hand to copy.
