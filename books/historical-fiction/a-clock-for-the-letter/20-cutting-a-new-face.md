## 20. Cutting a New Face

The first snow came the week after the bells, and with it a silence in the shop that August had never learned to love. She had imagined, all her life, that silence was the natural state of a print shop between editions — the press breathing, the type sleeping in its cases, the whole room waiting like an orchestra before the conductor's downbeat. But this silence was different. This was the silence after.

Fourteen bells had rung at dawn on the day they shot him, and the town had counted every one. Nobody had planned it; that was the strange and holy thing about it. The sexton had simply kept pulling the rope past the hour, and the town below had stood still and counted aloud under its breath, and by the ninth bell even the soldiers on the square had stopped pretending not to listen. Fourteen. One for each soul the river had taken at Millbridge Ford, and one more for the man who had finally said their names out loud where the tribunal could not pretend not to hear.

August had stood in that crowd holding his last real letter — the one written in his true hand, unforced, unforged, the one that said *You always were the better forger, so I will say plainly what I mean: I am not afraid, and I am proud to be your brother* — until her fingers went numb around the paper. Then she had walked back to the shop, unlocked the loading door, lit the lamp over the composing stone, and begun to draw letters.

That was in October. It was snowing now, and she was still drawing them.

* * *

The idea had come to her, as ideas do, sideways — while she was doing something else entirely. She had been sorting the damaged type, the battered faces that no foundry would take back: the cracked serifs, the letters worn smooth as river stones from a thousand impressions. And she had held one up to the lamp, an old lowercase *g* with its loop worn nearly away, and thought: *Tomas wrote his g's exactly like this. Open at the bottom. Never could close a loop, even in his copybook. Master Brenner used to rap his knuckles for it.*

And then she had sat down very slowly on the composing stool, because a thought had arrived that was so large it required sitting.

A face. A new face. Cut by her own hand, letter by letter, punch by punch — a typeface that did not exist anywhere in the world, that would exist only because she made it exist. She would cut it in Tomas's honor, and every page ever printed with it would carry his hand in it, his open-looped g's and his tall impatient t's, forever. Not a monument of stone that weathered. Not a name on a plaque that schoolboys walked past. Letters. Working letters. The most ordinary, most indestructible thing she knew how to make.

Mourning, she decided, was a kind of composition problem. You could not set grief in someone else's type. You had to cast your own.

* * *

The steel, at least, was easy to come by. Corvin was dead and Ilsa was in exile in Switzerland — August received one letter a season, unsigned, postmarked Basel, containing nothing but a pressed flower and occasionally the word *still here* — but the conspiracy's network of sympathizers had not entirely dissolved. A chandler who owed Ilsa his life sold August files at cost. A farrier who owed Corvin considerably more than his life supplied her with steel blanks "for making awls," which is what she told anyone who asked, including her father, including herself on difficult days.

She had never cut a punch before. She had set type ten thousand hours, yes; she knew the anatomy of a letter the way a midwife knows a body — the stem and the bowl, the ear and the tail, the counters where the light lives. But knowing a letter and carving one into hardened steel, mirror-reversed, at a size small enough that a mistake smaller than an eyelash ruined the whole character — that was another education altogether.

Her first attempt at a capital T looked, she had to admit, like a gallows with a hat. Her second looked like a gallows without a hat, which was somehow worse. She threw both across the shop, then retrieved them from behind the ink barrel, because waste was waste and even failures could teach you where the file should not go.

By Advent she could cut a letter that would strike clean. By Epiphany she could cut one that was, she allowed, looking critically at the proof pulled onto soft paper, actually beautiful.

* * *

The g came last, because the g mattered most.

She had saved it the way you save the best bite on your plate, and also the way you save the thing you are afraid of. Every other character in the face she designed fresh — a sturdy roman with just enough warmth in its curves that Brandt, when he saw the proofs, said it looked like handwriting that had gone to university — but the g she copied faithfully from Tomas's letters, the open-bottomed loop, the unclosable circle, the flaw that Master Brenner had rapped knuckles for and that August now intended to immortalize in steel.

She worked on it four evenings. On the fourth evening, she held the finished punch under the lamp and turned it, and the little loop caught the light, open at the bottom, imperfect, alive, and August put her forehead down on the composing stone and wept for the first time since the bells.

It was a good cry, as her mother used to say. The kind that leaves the room tidier than it found it.

* * *

Elias declined through the winter the way a candle declines — gently, with occasional gutterings, giving off light the whole time.

Some mornings he was entirely himself. He would come down at seven, inspect her proofs, grumble that the spacing on her new face was "generous to the point of charity," and settle into his chair by the stove with the paper, reading items aloud in the voice he had used when she was small: "'Grain prices steady,' August. Steady! In my day grain prices had the decency to be dramatic."

Other mornings were different mornings.

"August," he said one gray Tuesday, appearing at her elbow while she filed a serif, "have you seen your brother? He was meant to help me distribute."

Her file stopped mid-stroke. She had learned — it had taken weeks, and there had been one terrible afternoon involving a broken punch and words she regretted — that correction did not help. Correction confused him, frightened him, sent him into the fog deeper. What helped was the truth, or the nearest navigable channel to it.

"He's delivering papers," she said carefully. "He asked me to tell you not to wait supper."

"Did he." Elias nodded, satisfied, and patted her shoulder with a hand that had once been heavy enough to work a press by itself and was now light as a bird landing. "Good lad. Terrible handwriting, though. You'd think a printer's son—" He shook his head fondly and wandered back toward the stove. "His g's. Always open at the bottom. Like a door left ajar."

August stared at the punch in her hand — the actual g, the steel g, the door left ajar — and laughed until she had to sit down, and then laughed a bit more because crying and laughing had gotten so mixed up in her that winter that she had mostly given up telling them apart.

Another time, near Candlemas, she came downstairs to find him standing at her workbench, holding the tray where she kept the finished punches in oiled cloth, examining them one by one against the window light with the absorbed frown of the master craftsman he had been forty years ago.

"These are fine work," he said. "Whose hand is this?"

"Mine," August said.

"No." He turned a capital H in the light, tilting his head. "I mean whose *hand*. The model. You didn't invent these curves out of nothing, girl. Letters have parents." He squinted at the serif. "This one has a familiar chin."

"It's based on family correspondence," August said, which was true.

"Hm." He set the punch down with the exaggerated care of a man who no longer fully trusted his own fingers, and looked at her for a long moment with eyes that were clear as glass and seeing, perhaps, some other winter entirely. "You know," he said, "when the notice came about Tomas — the execution notice — I printed it myself. They brought me the copy and I set it and locked the chase and ran two hundred impressions, and my hands did it, and I have thought every day since about whether my hands could have refused."

August stood absolutely still.

"I don't remember if they could have," Elias said. His voice was level, almost conversational, which made it worse. "That's the trouble with memory going. You can't interrogate it anymore. It won't answer questions. It only makes announcements." He folded the cloth back over the punches, tucking each one in like a child. "But whoever taught you to cut steel taught you well, and I hope — I do hope — I had something to do with that."

"You did everything," August said. "Everything, Papa."

"Well." He cleared his throat and turned toward the stove with great dignity. "Someone in this family must be able to close a loop."

* * *

Brandt came on Thursdays.

He had returned to town in November, hatless, coatless of insignia, carrying a single valise and the expression of a man arriving to collect a debt he believed he owed. He had presented himself at the shop door and said, "I can set type, sweep floors, or turn myself in to whatever remains of the magistrate's office, whichever is most useful," and August, after regarding him for a long moment, had handed him a broom.

He never spoke of the night he burned his report. He never spoke of Voss, except once, in February, when the papers carried word that Voss had been quietly reassigned rather than publicly disgraced, promoted sideways into some provincial bureaucracy where massacres became administrative errors. Brandt read the item, set down the paper, and said, "They've moved him somewhere the names don't follow," and then swept the floor with unusual thoroughness.

"They'll move the names too, eventually," August said. "Names travel badly in official documents. That's why they need to travel in print."

Brandt looked at the rows of punches gleaming in their tray — nearly half the alphabet by then, each character cut twice, once in steel for the punch, once in copper for the matrix — and something moved across his careful face.

"You're going to print it," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going to print it properly," August said. "In a face no one's ever seen, from a shop no one suspects, with fourteen names set so beautifully that no reader will ever be able to claim they skimmed past them."

"And when Voss's successors come asking—"

"Then they'll come asking about a typeface named for a dead man," August said, "and I will look at them the way my father looks at grain prices, and I will be perfectly baffled."

Brandt smiled — a rare event, still slightly rusty from disuse — and picked up his broom, and Thursday resumed.

* * *

By the thaw she had the whole alphabet, plus figures, plus the punctuation she considered non-negotiable — the em dash, which she cut with particular relish, having spent her life watching men use it badly — and enough matrices cast to compose a full page. The new face had no name yet, officially. In her own head, and in the little ledger where she recorded each punch's dimensions and quirks, she called it simply *Hartwell Memorial*, though she suspected that if she ever had to say it aloud to a customer she would blush.

The casting was the ceremony. She had imagined it would be routine — molten metal, mold, done — but the first time a fresh *T* slid out of the matrix, still warm, bearing Tomas's tall impatient crossbar, she held it in her palm and felt the way you feel when a fire takes hold after a long damp evening. Here was a letter that had existed nowhere in the world eight months ago. Now it could be multiplied without limit. Now it could outlive the magistrate and the tribunal and the empire, probably, if she had anything to say about it, outlive the language.

She set a trial line that night:

*TOMAS HARTWELL, PRINTER'S SON, WITNESS.*

Pulled a proof. Looked at it a long time.

Then she unlocked the case where she kept his last real letter, laid it beside the proof, and compared hands — the living hand and the resurrected one, the original and the edition. The g matched. The t crossed high and impatient. Even the spacing had his particular generosity, his habit of leaving air between words as if the words needed room to breathe.

"There," she said to the empty shop, to the press, to the stove, to the man who wasn't there and was now everywhere the face would ever go. "Now you're legible."

From the chair by the stove, without opening his eyes, Elias said, "Who are you talking to, Tomas?"

August closed her eyes. Opened them.

"Just talking to myself, Papa."

"Mm." He shifted under his blanket. "You always did have his voice. Isn't that strange. Two children, and one voice between you." A pause long enough that she thought he'd drifted off. Then, softly, in a tone she couldn't quite parse — lucid, or not, there was no longer any reliable instrument for measuring — he added: "He'd have liked that line you just set. About being a witness. He'd have liked it very much."

Her heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. "Which line, Papa?"

"The one on the stone." His eyes stayed closed. "I may be old, girl. I'm not blind."

* * *

She worked late after that, not because there was work — the alphabet was done, the matrices cast, the type distributed into cases that Brandt had fitted with the false bottoms they knew so well how to make — but because a thing had assembled itself in her mind while she filed and carved and listened to her father call her by her brother's name, and it would not stay unassembled any longer.

The testimony existed. She had carried it out of the burning cellar; she had carried it across borders in her mind if not her person; the plates were wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floor of the type cabinet, fourteen pages plus Tomas's sworn statement, waiting for a year in which printing them did not mean dying for them. The year had turned. Voss was far away and the names were gathering strength. Ilsa's pressed flowers said *still here*, which meant the network breathed.

And now she had a face for it. Not any face — *the* face. The account of Millbridge Ford, printed in letters cut from grief and filed into shape by hand, in a dead man's open-looped g's, in type that had never been registered with any inspector because it had never existed until the winter after the bells.

She took a sheet of proof paper and composed the title page, slowly, one letter at a time, savoring each pick from the case like a woman choosing words in a language only two people had ever spoken, one of whom was dead and one of whom was asleep by the stove.

*A TRUE ACCOUNT OF MILLBRIDGE FORD.*

Below it, smaller, in the same face: *Set and printed in a typeface cut in memory of T.H., who witnessed.*

She pulled the proof and hung it to dry on the line above the stone, and stood back, and looked at it, and felt the future shift its weight in the chair across the room like a patient customer who has finally decided what he wants printed.

Brandt would need to be told. There were sympathetic shops downriver, and beyond them Basel, and beyond Basel the world. Fourteen names, fourteen hundred copies, maybe more. It would be dangerous — less dangerous than a year ago, but danger, she had learned, did not retire; it merely changed clothes.

August banked the stove, hung up her apron, and paused at the foot of the stairs, looking back at the drying proof catching the last lamplight — the first page in the world ever set in that face, and very soon, she promised it silently, not the last.

Upstairs, faintly, she heard her father's clock tick, steady as a pulse, keeping time for all of them.
