## 21. The Edition That Outlived Them

The last letter of the last line of the last page went into its chase at four o'clock on a February morning, and August Hartwell did not cry, because her hands were full.

She had learned that about grief and about type both: you cannot tremble while holding a composing stick, not if you mean to keep your lines square. The stick demanded steadiness the way the river demanded the bridge — take it or go around, but you will not be excused from crossing. So she locked the forme, tightened the quoins, and only then allowed herself to stand there in the cold grey light leaking through the shop windows, looking at what she had made.

Fourteen names. Set twice each — once in the running account, once alone at the foot of the final page, in a column, like mourners at a graveside who had finally been permitted to stand in their proper order.

*Anna Reider. Josef Brandl. The widow Cermak.* She would not need to read them. She had set them so many times that winter that they lived somewhere behind her sternum now, arranged in their own little case, sorted by hand and by heart.

"You're leaning on the stone," said a voice from the loading door. "You'll crease the sheet, leaning on the stone."

August turned. Brandt stood in the doorway with snow in his hair and a sack over his shoulder, looking precisely as he always had — the nervous inspector grown into a nervous exile, thinner at the crown and grayer at the temples, as though conscience had been billing him interest for six years.

"You're supposed to knock," she said.

"At a print shop? I've read what happens to people who knock on print shops." He came in, stamped his boots, and set down the sack, which clinked in the way sacks of metal clink, a sound August loved more than most music. "I heard you were cutting a new face. I heard it in Frankfurt, if you can believe it. A printer there — Kessler, fat man, laughs like a cart going downhill — he showed me a specimen sheet and said, *this came out of your town*, and I looked at the g's and I thought, well."

"Well," August agreed.

"The looping descenders. Very distinctive." He hesitated, then said, quieter: "They look like his hand."

"They do," said August. "That was the idea."

* * *

The typeface had taken her the better part of two winters, and she had loved every hour of it in the way one loves a long illness that turns out to have been teaching you something. Cutting punches was her father's old craft, the deep craft beneath the daily craft, and Elias — who could no longer reliably find Tuesday — still had the hands for it. Some mornings she would come downstairs to find him at her bench, turning a graver over and over, frowning at it like a man meeting an old friend in the wrong town.

"Who cut this?" he asked her once, holding up a finished punch of a lowercase *a*.

"You did, Papa. Last week."

"Did I?" He examined it with genuine admiration. "Steady work. Whoever he is, he should do more of this."

"He means to," August said, and kissed his head, and went to sort type so he wouldn't see her face.

Now the face was done — all forty-odd sorts of it, roman and italic, the serifs slightly soft the way handwriting goes soft when a person is writing fast because he has something terrible to say and wants it said before courage leaves. She had named it Hartwell Antiqua on the specimen sheets, but everyone in the trade already called it Tomas, because she had told Kessler why, and printers talk the way rivers run: downhill, endlessly, and reaching places you'd never guess.

And now, on this February morning, with Brandt shaking snow off his coat like an enormous dog, the forme was locked and ready, and the account of Millbridge Ford — fourteen names, one witness, one magistrate's order given on a Tuesday in the rain — was about to become not memory but paper, which is the only form of memory that can be handed to a stranger.

"Shall we?" said Brandt.

"We shall," said August.

* * *

The press ran until noon, and then it ran past noon, and Elias sat on his stool by the stove the whole time, folding sheets with the solemnity of a man wrapping gifts. His folding was perfect. Folding, apparently, lived in the fingers, in some country of him that the forgetting had never found a map of. He could not tell you what year it was, but he could fold two hundred sheets into quarters without one crooked crease, and he did, humming, occasionally addressing the stack as "the boy's homework."

"Papa," August said, at the top of the second hour, "do you know what we're printing?"

"Truth," said Elias promptly.

She blinked. "Yes. How—"

"It's always truth," he said, mildly scandalized. "What else does anyone print? Advertisements?" He folded another sheet. "Your mother used to say our house smelled of ink and lies, but she meant the government notices. Those were lies. This—" he patted the stack "—this smells different. You can smell the difference. Can't you?"

August lifted a fresh sheet to her face. Ink, oil, paper, the ghost of the stove. And underneath, perhaps, the difference he meant, which was probably nothing at all and entirely everything.

"Yes," she said. "I can."

"Good." He folded on. Then, after a moment, in the small voice he used when he was standing at the very edge of a lucid hour: "Is it the boy? Is it about Tomas?"

August's throat did something complicated. "It's about Millbridge Ford."

Elias nodded slowly, seven times, as though confirming an appointment. "Then it's late," he said. "But it's here. That's the main thing with truth. It's always late, and it must always arrive." He looked up at her, and his eyes were clear as they had not been in months. "You printed it well?"

"In the new face."

"Tomas's face," he said, and folded the next sheet with especial care, smoothing it flat with both palms like a man tucking in a child. "Good. Good boy."

He meant the typeface. Or he meant Tomas. Or he meant her; he had taken lately, on bad days, to calling her by her brother's name, and she no longer corrected him, because being called Tomas by her father was not entirely wrong and not entirely painful, and there were worse inheritances than a dead brother's name in a living mouth.

* * *

Brandt proved a good hand. Better, August admitted privately, than she liked — quick at the case, careful at the press, with the particular diligence of a man who had spent years doing paperwork for the wrong side and wanted, arithmetically, to balance the ledger.

They worked mostly in silence, which suited them both, but around mid-afternoon he paused over the galley of victims' names and said, "I registered these presses. Did you know that? Six years ago, Voss's list. Your father's shop was first. I wrote it in my own hand."

"I know," said August. "I watched you do it."

"And then I didn't report the false bottoms in your type cases, and then I burned my report, and then I walked out of my own life." He shook his head at the galley. "People think betrayal is one act. It isn't. It's stationery. It's a whole career of small papers. And then one day the opposite is true too — one small paper, burned, and you're a different man, and it took you thirty-nine years to get there."

"How old are you now?"

"Forty-five."

"Six years well spent, then," said August, and handed him another ream.

He laughed — a rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't swung in a while. "Kessler told me you'd put me in the book. As a character."

"He's in everything," August said. "There's a fat laughing printer in it. It's barely disguised. Don't flatter yourself; the inspector who burns his own report is far too noble to be you."

"That's a relief," said Brandt. "The noble ones always die in books."

"Not in mine," said August. "In mine the guilty go free for years and the good die at dawn and the truth arrives late, smelling of ink." She pulled the lever. The press spoke. "But it arrives."

* * *

Distribution was the part that required imagination, and imagination was supplied chiefly by the network Kessler had stitched together across three borders — printers, mostly, plus one saddler, two bargemen, and a woman in a customs house whose name appeared on no list anywhere and whom August pictured, without evidence, as enormous and unkillable.

The plan was simple in the way of things that have to survive contact with stupid men. Five hundred copies, sewn into false bottoms of grain wagons, hidden in hymnals (the account of Millbridge Ford tucked between psalms, where no official would look out of sheer theological terror), wrapped as wallpaper samples, and — August's favorite — bound inside forty copies of Voss's successor's own annual civic report, so that any official shelving the town's proud record of drainage improvements would unknowingly shelve fourteen murders directly beside them.

"There's poetry in that," Brandt said, gluing endpapers.

"There's spite in that," said August. "Poetry is what spite calls itself when it grows up."

The first wagon left on Thursday. The second on Saturday, driven by a bargeman who whistled the whole route, badly. By the following month, August knew from Kessler's letters — which arrived written in the margins of invoices, prices standing in for sentences — that copies had surfaced in Frankfurt, in Basel, in a reading room in Leipzig where students reportedly copied the fourteen names onto walls until the authorities painted over them and the students painted them again, and the painting war continued, which is a kind of immortality, if a damp one.

Voss's successors — Voss himself had retired on a pension to a town upriver, where he was said to play cards badly and tip poorly — could suppress a print shop. They could smash a cellar press; August still dreamed sometimes of the sound that had made, the crunch of lead and wood, Corvin's blood on the stairs, Ilsa's hands cuffed behind her as they marched her out. They could seize a wagon, ban a pamphlet, fine a printer into ruin. What they could not do was reach into every hymn in every pew in every village within two hundred miles and pull out page 63.

"You've made it boring," Brandt observed, with satisfaction. "That's the trick, isn't it? Make the truth so ordinary-looking, so distributed, so much a part of the furniture, that destroying it becomes more trouble than tolerating it."

"My brother died so that a document could hide among drainage reports," said August. "I've decided to find that dignified."

"It is," Brandt said simply. "It is dignified. Truth doesn't need trumpets, August. It needs ten thousand quiet copies."

* * *

On Sunday, Elias wandered.

Not far — he never went far anymore, just out the loading door and down toward the river, following some errand his mind had assigned him. August found him at the rail by the ferry steps, holding a folded newspaper he'd taken from the shop, offering it hopefully to passersby.

"Excuse me," he was saying to a fishwife. "Could you give this to Tomas? He'll want the shipping news. He follows the shipping news. He's expecting it before supper."

The fishwife looked at August with the particular pity townspeople extend to families with a decline in them. August took her father's arm.

"He's not back yet, Papa," she said. It was the story they had settled on, she and the whole town, by unspoken agreement: *not back yet*. Not dead, not executed, not named in the book and carved in the type. Just delayed, like a boat in weather. It was a lie, and it was also, in the edition she had just printed, the closest thing to true: Tomas was not gone. He was in the g's. He was in the loop of every descender on every page in every hymnal from here to Basel. Delayed indefinitely, in lead.

Elias let himself be led home. Halfway up the lane he stopped and said, "You printed the shipping news well, August. The boy will like his name in it."

His name was not in it. But fourteen others were, in his own letters' shape, and August decided that between a father's failing mind and a brother's fixed type, some message might yet get through to whoever was keeping the great ledger.

"Yes," she said. "I set it myself."

"He'd be proud," said Elias. "Both of you. All of you." He frowned, working at something. "How many of you are there again?"

"Two," August said. "Just two, Papa."

"Two," he repeated, satisfied, and walked on, and did not ask again, because the question had already slipped back under whatever water his memories swam in now. But at the shop door he paused and touched the frame the way he had touched it every day for forty years, checking the hinge, the way a captain touches the wheel, and August thought: *there*. There is the man who printed the coerced death notice and hated himself quietly for thirty years, and who taught his daughter every letter of the alphabet that she has now aimed like a weapon. Inherited guilt. Inherited craft. Same parcel, different ends.

* * *

The final copies came off the press in early March. August kept one aside — the first off the evening run, pulled slightly crooked, imperfect, hers — and wrote on its flyleaf in pencil, then crossed it out and wrote nothing, because some dedications don't fit on flyleaves. She shelved it in the case beside the shop clock, which kept its perfect time, having been repaired by a boy with clever hands some twenty years ago and never once wound wrong since.

Brandt stayed through March, then April. He slept in the loft, ate like a horse, and turned out to be, in peacetime, exactly what a small print shop needed: a man trained to notice everything and now free to notice it for the right side. He reorganized her distribution lists. He negotiated paper prices down by sheer bureaucratic stubbornness. He taught the neighbor boy to sweep the shop properly, a skill previously unknown in the western provinces.

And one evening in April, standing at the case, setting the last of a job lot of wedding invitations in the Tomas face — the newlyweds had specifically requested it, which August chose to interpret as the town voting, quietly, on what it remembered — Brandt said:

"You know they'll ask you to teach."

"Who will?"

"Printers. Apprentices. Kessler's already telling people you'll take them. The face needs hands, August. Type doesn't cut its own descendants." He slid a line of *Millbridge* into the stick, admiring it. "You can't keep all this in one pair of wrists."

"I can try."

"You tried," he said, gesturing at five hundred copies in a hymnal near Basel. "It worked. Now it needs to outlive you, which is, I'm told, the entire point of you people."

August was preparing her rebuttal — she had a good one, involving wrist injuries and the price of bread — when the bell above the shop door rang, and a girl came in.

She was maybe eleven, freckled, with ink already on one sleeve for reasons that would become apparent later and were, at present, a mystery even to herself. She stood in the middle of the floor, took in the cases, the press, the hanging proofs, the clock, and Elias asleep by the stove, and delivered her speech in one breath:

"Good day. My name is Marta Cermak and my grandmother says you printed her mother's name in a book and she wants to know if you take apprentices because I can read and I'm not afraid of ink and also what is that machine."

Silence. The clock ticked, perfectly.

Brandt looked at August. August looked at the girl — at the name, first, *Cermak*, one of the fourteen, returned in a small loud body that was not afraid of ink — and felt the future arrive the way type arrives at the case: heavy, cold, and absolutely certain of its place.

"What machine?" said August.
