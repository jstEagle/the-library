## 22. A Clock for the Letter

The girl who asked the question was called Marta, and she had ink on her nose again, which meant she'd been leaning over the stone with her whole face instead of just her hands. August did not point this out. There had been a time — forty years and more of apprentices now — when she would have scolded. She had learned that the ones who lean in too close are usually the ones who end up understanding the work.

"Frau Hartwell," Marta said, holding up a proof sheet, "why is this typeface called Hartwell? You made it yourself, didn't you? So why name it after a dead man?"

The shop went quiet in that particular way it did when the younger apprentices sensed a story. The two older boys at the composing stone kept working, but their hands slowed. Old Brenner, who came in three mornings a week to mind the press and drink August's coffee, did not even pretend to keep pulling proofs. He set down his brayer and folded his arms.

August finished locking the chase she was working on before she answered. This was not stalling. Well. It was partly stalling. But it was also a lesson, though she never announced her lessons as such: *the form comes first, then the talk.* A printer who abandoned a locked form mid-sentence deserved whatever chaos followed.

"There are two Hartwells in that drawer," she said at last, nodding toward the case nearest the window, where the light fell best for setting type. "Can you tell them apart?"

Marta looked. The apprentices all looked. To them it was one face, one family of letters — the tall, clear, slightly narrow characters August cut through the winter after everything, when her hands needed grief-work and her heart could not bear idle hours.

"No," Marta admitted.

"The difference is in the g." August took out a loupe, an old jeweler's glass she kept more for ceremony than necessity now, and handed it over. "Look at the lower bowl. One of them loops open, like a hand letting go of something. The other closes tight, like a fist."

Marta bent over the proof sheet, turning it in the light. "This one's open."

"That's mine. I cut those. Closed fist is Tomas."

* * *

She wound the clock first.

She always wound it before telling anything about Tomas, though she had never said so aloud and would have denied it under oath. The clock stood in the corner by the stairs, a plain walnut case that Elias had bought secondhand the year August was born, from a man who swore it had come off a riverboat and could not say which river. For most of August's childhood it had run badly — gaining a quarter hour a week, losing it back in some sulking fit, striking eleven when it meant twelve. Her father had cleaned it, adjusted it, threatened it in two languages. It ignored him with the patience of machinery.

Tomas had fixed it the summer he turned fifteen. He took the movement entirely apart on the kitchen table, laid the wheels out on a cloth like surgery, and put it back together over two days while Elias hovered and August, ten years old, sat on the floor handing him things he hadn't asked for. Afterward it kept perfect time. It had kept perfect time ever since — through the uprising, through Voss's tribunal and its aftermath, through Elias's long gentle dimming, through four changes of government whose names August made a point of forgetting, through fire in the neighboring building that they had fought with wet blankets and cursing, through every winter since.

"You wind it every day?" Marta had asked once, months ago.

"Every morning before anything else," August had said. "Before coffee."

"Why?"

"Because if it stops," August had said, "I would have to be the one to start it again, and I don't know how. My brother did."

Which was true, and also not the whole truth, which was the condition of nearly everything August had ever said about Tomas.

* * *

She got the key from the hook behind the door where it had hung for fifty years, opened the case, and fitted the crank to the winding arbor. Seven turns. Never more. Tomas had told her — she had been ten, hanging off his elbow — *seven turns, August, and never let anyone rush you. A thing you do for someone every day should be done slowly enough to think of them.*

She thought of him. She always did. Some mornings it was only a flicker, a name passing through like a customer through a shop. Some mornings it was the whole of it: the dock at dawn, the letter in his hand, his eyes finding hers across thirty feet of gray water while the boat pulled away, and the almost-nothing that moved in his face — not accusation, not forgiveness exactly, but recognition, the look of a man reading his own handwriting and knowing perfectly well he had never written it.

Seven turns. She closed the case.

"Sit down, all of you," she said. "Proofs can wait. Brenner, pour yourself more coffee, you'll want it, you've heard half this story and pretended you haven't."

"I haven't heard any of it," Brenner said, lying comfortably, and poured.

* * *

"You have to understand what the town was like," August began, settling onto her stool — the same stool, actually, though re-caned twice, the one she had sat on as a girl sorting type while her father muttered at galley proofs. "In 1848. Before. We were a printing family, which sounds grander than it was. Newspapers, handbills, wedding announcements, bills of lading. My father, Elias — your ears may have caught my calling him by name in my sleep, Marta, no need to look alarmed — my father believed deeply in two things: accurate punctuation and staying out of it."

"Out of what?" asked Jakob, the taller of the boys.

"Everything. History. Politics. The century." August smiled. "He used to say that revolutions were ink for other papers. That somewhere there were men whose job it was to make history, and our job was to spell their names correctly afterward. I believed him for twenty years. It was a comfortable belief. Like a good chair — you don't notice how much of you it holds until someone takes it away."

"And then?" Marta. Always Marta asking *and then*, leaning forward, ink-nosed, hungry.

"And then a man collapsed at our loading door with a fever and a satchel full of pamphlets the government wanted burned, and among his papers were letters in my brother's handwriting. My brother had been executed two years earlier. I had read the notice. My father had printed it — though that's another thread of the story, and we'll pull it gently or we'll be here until supper."

She gave them the shape of it, because she could not give them all of it — nobody had all of it, that had been the trouble from the beginning. The conspiracy that faked the execution to protect the sole witness to a massacre. Fourteen strikers at Millbridge Ford, drowned on Magistrate Voss's order when their march crossed the water. Her brother alive and dead at once, hiding inside his own obituary. The cellar press. Ilsa Reiner, who ran conspirators the way Elias ran a shop — with inventory lists and strict rules about cleanliness. Corvin, feverish, brave, hopeless with spelling; August had corrected his proofs even in the worst weeks, and he had accepted correction with the humility of a man who knew he'd die mid-sentence anyway. He did. He covered the retreat when the troops came back, and the last thing he handed August was a page with three misspellings, and she had loved him for them ever since.

"And the boat," Marta said softly. She had heard fragments before. Everyone in the shop had. It was the town's story by now too — the bells, the fourteen strokes, the trial. But August noticed she still said *the boat*, the way you'd say *the war*, meaning the private part of it.

"Yes," August said. "The boat. One berth. My brother, whose testimony could hang a magistrate, or my father, who couldn't remember what day it was but was — " her voice did something she hadn't authorized — "was the man who taught me that a misplaced comma could ruin a life, and who once walked three miles in the rain because he thought I'd be cold waiting at the ferry. The cause needed Tomas. I needed my father. And Ilsa needed me to need Tomas, and I looked her in the eye and lied to her, which is the bravest thing I've ever done, and I include the smuggling."

"What did you do?" Jakob asked, though he clearly knew.

"I forged my brother's handwriting," August said. "One last time. A letter ordering himself to stay. And I told my father the berth was his alone, arranged by friends of the cause, no discussion necessary — because with Elias, by then, if you presented a thing confidently enough, it became the past, and he trusted the past absolutely. He boarded believing my brother had ordered him to go. My brother stood on the dock holding a letter in his own forged hand and understood in about four seconds exactly what I had done, and we looked at each other across the water, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing you could put into words that wouldn't diminish it."

She paused. Brenner poured her coffee she hadn't asked for. She drank it.

"He testified anyway," she went on. "At Ilsa's trial. Named Voss in open session, fourteen names, every detail, calm as a man reading shipping news. They convicted Voss. They shot Tomas at dawn a week later, and the town rang the church bells fourteen times, once for each striker, and I stood in the crowd holding the last real letter he ever wrote me, and I want you both to understand — " she pointed the empty cup at Jakob and Marta in turn — "that the bells rang *because* of what happened on that dock. His death was not separate from my choice. It was made possible by it. That is the arithmetic of love sometimes. Nobody teaches it because nobody wants to learn it."

* * *

Elias lived six more years after the crossing. He arrived abroad confused, delighted, convinced at intervals that he was on holiday. August wrote him that Tomas had gone ahead to arrange lodgings in the interior, which eased him wonderfully; he spent happy weeks composing questions for Tomas upon his arrival. Toward the end, his memory thinned further, and he began calling August by her brother's name — *Tomas, hand me the blue pencil, Tomas, has the boy eaten* — and she answered to it without hesitation. It did not feel like erasure. It felt like being handed something precious and fragile across a crowded room.

She brought him home when he was dying, because he had asked once, lucidly, to hear the river. He died in the spring with the window open. At the funeral, the bell-ringer, unprompted, added fourteen strokes at the end of the tolling, and August understood that the town had decided to keep count of its debts forever.

* * *

"Now," August said, "the typeface."

She rose, unlocked the flat cabinet under the window — the good lock, the one Brandt had installed when he came back after the amnesty, gray at the temples, carrying his old inspection ledger and asking, in the humblest voice she'd ever heard from a government man, whether printers took apprentices past fifty. He had worked here eleven years since, and retired to two streets away, and still came by Tuesdays to argue about kerning. But that was his chapter, not hers.

She lifted out the steel punch — the master punch, the first character she had ever cut entire, and the one from which the rest of the alphabet had seemed to grow almost without her permission, the way a word grows from a first letter. She laid it on white paper where the light could catch it.

"When I decided to print the true account of Millbridge Ford — the whole testimony, all fourteen names, the dates, the orders given — I had a problem. Every typeface in this shop had printed lies for Voss at one time or another. My father's beautiful old romans had set the false death notice. I wanted type that had never lied. Type born clean. So I cut a new face, letter by letter, through one whole winter. And I made one decision that no founder would approve of and every printer should: I based each letter on my brother's hand."

"Hold up the proof again," she told Marta. "The closed-fist g. See it now? He wrote like that since he was a boy — clenched, stubborn, holding on. I didn't design a memorial. I copied him. Every capital T leans forward slightly, as if late for something, because he was always late for something. The serifs on the lowercase l are heavier than they should be, because his l's were heavy; he pressed too hard; he wrote as though the paper might dispute him."

Marta was looking at the proof sheet with an expression August remembered wearing at ten years old, sitting on the floor, handing tools to a boy who hadn't asked for them.

"So when people ask why it's named for a dead man," August said, "I tell them it isn't. It's named for a living one. He says something every time this shop prints. Every poster, every ballad sheet, every child's primer, every correction of every lie any magistrate tries to tell — it goes out in his voice, in his stubborn leaning-forward letters. He has been speaking continuously for—" she calculated, purely for theater; she knew to the year— "forty-one years. Longer than he was alive. That is what type is for, if you want my opinion, which nobody at the founders' guild does. It is a way of making a person outlive their allotted span by lending them your hands."

* * *

The lesson broke up the way lessons should — gradually, and into work. Jakob went back to distributing type, carefully, one letter home at a time, the way August had drummed into him. Brenner claimed the afternoon proofs and began humming. Marta lingered.

"Frau Hartwell. May I ask one more thing?"

"You may ask eleven."

"Why do you wind the clock every morning? Truly?"

August considered giving the practical answer — the escapement, the mainspring, habit, oil. She had given it before, to interviewers from the historical society, who had written it down faithfully and missed the point entirely, which was fine; the point was not for them.

"Because," she said, "my brother repaired that clock the summer he was fifteen, and it has kept perfect time for fifty-three years, through every kind of weather this century has produced. Somewhere in that walnut case is a small piece of his attention, still working. Winding it is the only appointment I have ever kept with him. Seven turns, no more, done slowly enough to think of him." She took the key from the hook and held it out. "Tomorrow morning, you'll do it. I'll show you where the arbor is, and the rule about seven turns, and you will think of him, because you now know enough to do it properly. And the morning after that, you'll do it again, and one day — not soon, I intend to be difficult for many years yet — but one day the clock will be yours, and the story will be yours, and you'll tell some ink-nosed apprentice why the typeface leans forward."

Marta took the key as though it were made of glass.

That evening, after the others had gone, August stayed to clean the press herself — old habit; the apprentices cleaned it adequately, but there were thoughts one could only think with a rag in hand and the smell of solvent rising. Through the window the street lamps came on one by one. Down at the river a boat horn sounded, ordinary, going nowhere tragic. On the stone counter lay tomorrow's forme, locked and waiting: the fourth printing of the Millbridge account, in Hartwell type, fourteen names standing in their lines like men at attention, none of them drowned anywhere but in the past, where she had anchored them with lead and ink and her brother's unbroken fist of a g.

The clock struck seven, precisely, sweetly, right on time.

August hung the winding key on its hook, checked it twice, and reached for the light.
