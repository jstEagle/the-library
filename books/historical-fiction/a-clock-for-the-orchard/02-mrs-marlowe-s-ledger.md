## 2. Mrs. Marlowe's Ledger

Edith Marlowe did not knock before entering rooms she owned, and she owned most of the rooms in this town one way or another—through money, through marriage into money, or through the particular currency of a woman who had once told a Member of Parliament to his face that he was afraid of his own mother. So when the door of my little room above the carriage house swung open at nine o'clock on a Tuesday morning, I didn't startle. I put down my pen and waited.

"You've read the post," she said. It wasn't a question. Reading the post was my first duty of every day, and I had done it, all forty-one items of it, sorting the correspondence into three piles: hers to answer, mine to draft, and the small stack of letters from women who could not read them themselves and would come by the kitchen door on Thursday to hear what their sisters and sons and husbands had written from the front.

"Read it, sorted it, drafted three replies you'll want to see."

"The ones for the *Clarion*?"

"And one for Mrs. Prewitt in Harbury, about her boy."

"Good." She stood there in the doorway with her hands folded, which was how she stood when she was about to ask for something she knew she shouldn't have to ask for. Edith Marlowe did not like asking. She preferred telling, and when telling failed—which it rarely did—she preferred waiting, because she had learned that silence made most people fill it.

I had been her driver and her reader for two years by then. I knew her silences the way farmers know weather. This one meant rain.

"There's a thing I need you to carry," she said.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I was, in the spring of 1917, because what happened afterward depended so much on what I had been before. I was nobody. That was not an insult; it was a profession. A driver is furniture that moves. A woman who reads letters aloud in a town where half the adults can't read them is a voice without a face—you hear the words, you thank the words, you don't look twice at the mouth they come out of. I had gray eyes that people forgot and brown hair I kept in a plait tight enough to last all day, and I wore coats the color of puddles. When shopkeepers described me later—and they would describe me, eventually, under oath—they would struggle. Medium height. Ordinary. The woman? Oh, the woman who drives for Mrs. Marlowe. I couldn't say.

That was the design. Some of the design was Edith's. Most of it, though—the careful not-being-looked-at—I had built myself, plank by plank, back when being looked at had cost me things I don't write down even now.

"I'm not a member of anything," I said, when she told me what she wanted me to carry. "You've been careful about that. Everyone has. I drive. I read. I don't know where the letters go after they leave your desk, and I don't ask."

"That was true two years ago," Edith said. "It stopped being true around the time you started correcting my routes."

I had corrected her routes exactly once. The Millbrook road had a new sentry post, and sending Nell Ashby through with pamphlets sewn into a coat lining was asking for trouble; I'd suggested the canal towpath instead, adding twenty minutes and subtracting a search. I hadn't thought of it as belonging to anything. I'd thought of it as keeping a girl out of a cell.

"That was driving," I said. "A driver who knows the roads is worth two who don't."

"It was membership." She crossed to the window. My room had one, overlooking the orchard, and beyond the orchard wall the gatehouse with its clock, its hands frozen at ten past four since the frost cracked the mechanism in January. "Oscar. Sit down."

Nobody called me Oscar except her. To everyone else I was Miss Whitlock, or Whitlock, or nothing at all—a nod, a coin passed over a counter. The name in her mouth always sounded slightly surprised, as if she'd found it in a drawer she'd forgotten she owned.

I sat.

She took the ledger out of her coat.

* * *

You will have heard about the ledger by now. Everyone has. There are people alive today because of it and people dead who would have lived without it, and the inquiry spent eleven days arguing about whether it was evidence of sedition or evidence of loyalty, as if those were the only two containers a thing could fit into. But in April of 1917 it was just a book in Edith Marlowe's hands, roughly the size of a hymnal, bound in green cloth gone soft at the corners, and I looked at it the way you look at an unexploded shell.

"What is it?"

"You know what it is."

I knew some of it. I knew that the orchard network moved more than fruit: pamphlets out of Birmingham, letters between branches of the Women's Social and Political Union that no longer trusted any post office, money wrapped in wax paper inside wheels of cheese. I knew that Thursday evenings at the kitchen door were sometimes about more than letters from the front. I knew there were safehouses in three towns whose names I could point to on a map but not, until that morning, connect to faces.

What I hadn't known was the scale. She opened the ledger on my table and turned pages, and the names went on and on. Not just the loud ones—the women whose arrests made the papers—but quiet ones. Schoolmistresses. Farmers' wives. A girl of nineteen in Harbury who ran messages on her bicycle because no constable would stop a girl delivering eggs. Next to each name: what she carried, where, when, whom she could shelter, whom she must never be seen with. Routes drawn in pencil and re-inked when they changed. Codes explained in margins in Edith's upright, unhurried hand.

There were a hundred and forty-three names. I counted without meaning to. Counting is another thing I do without meaning to.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the request yet."

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no."

Edith closed the ledger but kept her hand flat on the cover, as if it might get up and walk. "Tell me why."

"Because knowing is dangerous, and I am useful precisely because I don't know anything. You built it that way. You told me yourself, my first week: *Miss Whitlock sees nothing, hears nothing, and is therefore invisible to everyone including God.*"

"I was wrong about the second part and we both knew it at the time."

"Then find someone else."

"With what qualifications?" She pulled the other chair to the table and sat, and for the first time since I'd known her she looked, briefly, her age—fifty-one years and a decade of fighting packed behind the eyes. "The women in this book trust each other because they've met each other. They can't carry the whole of it. If Agnes Teller is taken, everything Agnes knows goes into a cell with her. Same for every name in here. But you—" she tapped the cover once, sharply— "you already carry half the network in your head and nobody has ever thought to arrest you for it."

"That's not—"

"The Prewitt letter. Last month. You noticed the date was wrong before I did, that she'd written the twelfth when she meant the second, and you held the reply for ten days so the error wouldn't expose her handwriting to the Harbury postmistress. You didn't tell me. You just did it." She leaned forward. "And the canal route. And the time you took the long road past Wetherby because you'd seen a motorcar parked outside the clerk's office twice in a week and didn't like the look of the men smoking beside it. Don't tell me what you know and don't know, Oscar. I've watched you know things for two years."

I looked at the ledger. Green cloth, soft corners, a hundred and forty-three lives in pencil.

"If anyone finds that book," I said, "it isn't a book anymore. It's a list for a firing squad."

"Yes."

"And you want me to carry it. In the carriage. In my coat. Through towns full of soldiers."

"I want you to carry it in your head," Edith said, "and keep the book somewhere no one would think to look. Those are different requests and I'm making both."

* * *

We argued for another hour. I want that on the record too, because the story that got told afterward—that I was brave, that I volunteered—is a lie with better posture than the truth. The truth is that I refused four times, and each refusal was reasonable, and she dismantled each one the way she dismantled MPs, politely and without hurrying.

Why me? Because I was invisible. Why not keep it that way? Because invisibility was already doing work; it might as well do more. What if I were caught? Then I'd be searched and found to be carrying nothing but letters from anxious mothers, which was true, and questioned and found to know nothing, which would be the first lie of my life. What if she were taken? Then someone had to hold the web, or a hundred and forty-three women would discover they'd been spinning alone.

It was that last argument that got me, and I resented it. She saved it for last deliberately. She was a strategist down to her bones.

"Invisibility is a skill," she said, in the quiet after. "Not an identity. I know you've used it as armor—I'm not criticizing, God knows a person needs armor—but armor that never comes off stops being protection and starts being a wall. You can put the skill down, Oscar. Pick it up again tomorrow. That's what makes it a skill instead of a grave."

"You make it sound simple."

"It isn't simple. It's necessary. Those are different words and I've spent fifty-one years learning the difference."

Outside my window the orchard stood in its April white, blossom coming loose in the wind and drifting across the grass like a slow snowfall of the wrong season. The gatehouse clock said ten past four, same as it had said since January. I remember thinking that a stopped clock was the safest clock in the world—it couldn't testify against anyone—and I remember thinking, immediately after, that a stopped clock was also useless, and that I'd have to decide which kind of person I intended to be.

"All right," I said.

Edith didn't smile. She wasn't a smiler in moments that mattered; her smiles were for company. What she did was exhale, a long breath as if she'd been holding it since March, and push the ledger across the table to me.

"Memorize it. All of it. Names first, then routes, then houses, then the codes. Take it in whatever order your mind likes, but take all of it. When you can recite it back to me without the book, we'll decide where the book itself should live."

"How long?"

"You'll know when you're done. People always do." She rose, straightened her coat, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "One more thing. There's a courier coming up from Harbury this week—a new girl, good, fast, doesn't know how good. You'll meet her on the Millbrook run, probably. Her name is Ashby."

"Nell Ashby. I know the name. She's in the book."

"She's in the book," Edith agreed, "which means that from this morning forward, she is partly yours. As you are partly mine. That's what the book is, underneath everything else—it's a record of debts. Who owes whom their safety." She looked at me for a moment longer than was comfortable. "I chose you because you're careful. Be careful differently now."

The door closed behind her. I sat at my table with the ledger in front of me and the orchard blowing white outside, and I opened it to the first page, where the first name was written in ink faded almost to brown.

*Agnes Teller, Millbrook. Bread deliveries, Tuesdays.*

I began to memorize.
