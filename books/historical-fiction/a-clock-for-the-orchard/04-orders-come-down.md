## 4. Orders Come Down

The notice went up on the market cross on a Tuesday morning, and by noon the whole town had read it and no one would say so aloud.

Oscar heard about it from the baker's boy, who came to the Marlowe house at a dead run with flour still on his sleeves. She was in the kitchen, mending the strap of her delivery satchel, when he burst through the back door without knocking.

"Soldiers," he said. "Requisition. The whole town's to be taken for billeting. They've posted it at the cross."

"Who posted it?"

"Two men in uniform. Not ours—regulars. There's an officer coming Friday." The boy swallowed. "My mum says they're taking the big houses first."

Oscar set down the strap. Her hands were very still in her lap, which was a trick she'd learned young: stillness as a kind of armor. "Thank you, Tommy. Go home the long way, past the mill, so it doesn't look like you came here."

He went. She sat a moment longer, listening to the kitchen—the tick of the mantel clock, the drip of the tap Edith kept saying she'd have seen to—and understood that the weather had turned.

She found Mrs. Marlowe in the study, at the window that faced the orchard. Edith Marlowe did not startle easily, but Oscar watched something move through her face like water under ice when she repeated what the boy had said. It was gone in a second, smoothed over, and then Edith was only herself again: upright, gray-haired, hands folded on the windowsill as if she were posing for a portrait of composure.

"Billeting," she said. "Of course. We're on the road between two depots; it was always a matter of time."

"They said sedition too," Oscar said. "Tommy didn't read all of it, but the word was there. Seizure of materials."

Edith turned from the window. For a moment she looked older than her years, and then younger than them, and Oscar could not decide which frightened her more.

"Well," Edith said. "Then we know what we're about."

* * *

Captain Alistair Renshaw read his orders twice on the train and once more standing on the platform at Marleigh Halt, because he was a man who believed that instructions, like people, revealed more on the third reading.

The mandate was straightforward. Requisition the town of Marleigh and its surrounding farms for use as a supply depot and rest station. Establish order. And—paragraph four, typed in the same gray ink as everything else—"identify and seize written materials of a seditious or morale-subversive character, particularly materials pertaining to organized opposition to conscription and the war effort."

There had been intelligence. Someone in London had a file, and the file had names, and the names pointed here: to a village of orchards and a dissenting congregation and a woman named Marlowe whose letters had been quoted, with relish, in two pacifist pamphlets seized in Manchester.

Renshaw was thirty-nine years old, neat, unglamorous, with a face built for committees rather than battlefields—a fact that had cost him a front-line command and bought him this one. He did not think of himself as cruel. He thought of himself as thorough. In his experience, most trouble grew from things left unswept: a corner not searched, a name not followed, a loose thread left dangling out of laziness or sentiment. He intended to sweep.

He surveyed the town from the halt platform with the unhurried eye of a man inventorying property. Stone cottages. A church with a squat tower. Orchards running up the valley in ordered rows like soldiers themselves, though he suspected the comparison would not flatter either party. Smoke rising from chimneys. People in doorways pretending not to watch him, which meant everyone was watching him.

A good town, he thought. Orderly. Which meant it could be made more orderly.

His adjutant, Lieutenant Prewitt, consulted a clipboard. "The Marlowe house is the large one past the church, sir. Best position for the officers' mess."

"Yes," Renshaw said. "We'll begin there."

* * *

By Thursday evening the Marlowe kitchen held six women and smelled of tea nobody drank.

Oscar knew all of them. That was the trouble with a small network in a small town: you couldn't be a stranger among friends, and every one of these faces was a name she carried now, had been carrying since the day Edith put the ledger in her hands. Miss Ada Fenwick from the schoolhouse. The Cooper sisters, who ran the dairy route and therefore ran half the county's gossip without ever meaning to. Widow Hartree, seventy-one, who hid leaflets inside hymnals and swore God approved. Others whose names Oscar would not write down even in her own head if she could help it.

Edith stood at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood.

"The requisition takes effect Friday morning," she said. "They will search houses. They will take papers. Some of you are thinking of what's in your drawers right now, and I don't blame you, but hear me: panic burns more than fire does. We do this calmly and we do it completely."

She looked around the table, and each woman looked back, and Oscar, sitting by the stove with her tea untouched, felt the room hold its breath together.

"Miss Fenwick, the school records—anything with marginalia goes tonight. The Coopers, your correspondence. Mrs. Hartree—" a ghost of a smile crossed Edith's face— "I believe you've had practice."

"Hymnals burn slow," said Widow Hartree serenely. "Good paper stock."

"And mine?" asked the youngest of them, a girl called Ruth Pargeter who couldn't have been twenty, whose voice cracked on the question.

"Yours comes here," Edith said. "Everything comes here, and then it leaves here. Oscar will see to the distribution."

Every head turned toward Oscar. She had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of not being looked at, and it never stopped astonishing her how quickly that art failed her in this kitchen.

"What distribution?" Oscar asked quietly. It wasn't defiance. It was the question everyone else was too frightened to ask.

Edith met her eyes across the table, and Oscar saw that her employer had already decided, days ago perhaps, and was only now informing the rest of them. That was Edith's way: courtesy as a wrapper around iron.

"You'll take what can't be burned to the places we agreed," Edith said. "And the ledger goes where we discussed."

The mantel clock ticked. Outside, the orchard moved in the wind, a sound like the whole valley exhaling.

"It's not enough time," Oscar said.

"It never is," said Edith. "That is rather the nature of the work."

* * *

Renshaw arrived Friday under a sky the color of dishwater, and the rain began within the hour, as if the town itself had decided on its posture: cold, damp, unyielding.

He established his headquarters in the vestry of the parish church, having decided that the Marlowe house would serve better as a symbol seized later than a base occupied now. First impressions mattered. He wanted the town to wake Saturday morning and find soldiers at the crossroads, soldiers at the mill bridge, soldiers walking the lanes with clipboards and requisition schedules—and he wanted them to find those soldiers polite. Fear was useful, but fear managed poorly curdled into resentment, and resentment organized itself. He had read enough intercepted correspondence to know that organization was precisely what Marleigh did.

By midmorning he had the billeting schedule drafted and the first searches assigned. He gave explicit instructions: nothing torn, nothing trampled, nothing broken that did not need breaking. Seized papers were to be catalogued, boxed, and brought to him personally. He was not interested in bonfires. Bonfires destroyed evidence and created martyrs in equal measure. He wanted to *read* what these people wrote.

At eleven o'clock, standing in the churchyard under his umbrella while the rain needled down, he watched the townsfolk pass along the lane to market. Farmers. Shopkeepers. Children. An old woman with a hymnal-shaped parcel under her coat who crossed the street when she saw him, unnecessarily, since he had no interest whatsoever in hymnals.

And a young woman driving a mail cart.

She passed within ten feet of him. Plain dress, plain cap, plain face doing its best to be plainer than God had made it. She nodded to him—actually nodded, the way one nods at a lamppost, acknowledging its existence without granting it significance—and drove on. He noted the cart, the route, the efficiency of her passage. Mail carts saw everything. Mail carriers heard everything. Half the intelligence files in London were built on postmasters and the other half on their clerks.

"Prewitt," he said.

"Sir."

"The postal service here. Who runs it? Deliveries, collections."

"There's a sub-office on the high street, sir. One clerk. Deliveries go by cart—there's a woman does the rounds, I'm told. Locals trust her with their letters, apparently. Can't all write, so she reads aloud for them."

Renshaw watched the cart diminish up the lane, rain silvering behind it.

"She reads aloud," he repeated.

"For the illiterate ones, sir. Seems well-liked."

"Mm." He filed it away—not as suspicion, suspicion required grounds, and he was too disciplined to indulge in its absence—but as a question. Questions, unlike suspicions, could be answered. "Find out her name."

Prewitt wrote it down. "Whitlock, sir. Oscar Whitlock."

"Whitlock," Renshaw said, tasting it. "Well. Everyone has a function in a town like this. Let's learn hers."

* * *

Saturday night, after the last delivery, Oscar sat alone in the gatehouse at the edge of the orchard with a lamp turned low and the ledger open on her knees.

She had spent three days moving paper. Widow Hartree's leaflets were in the church wall behind a loose stone, wrapped in oilcloth. The school records had gone into a bee skep, of all places, Miss Fenwick swearing the swarm wouldn't mind. The Cooper sisters' correspondence was ash in their own grate, burned page by page with the kettle on, so the smoke would look ordinary. Ruth Pargeter had wept over her burning letters and been comforted by Widow Hartree, who observed that paper was the cheapest thing any of them would lose this year.

And the ledger—names, routes, safehouses, sums, dates going back two years—lay in Oscar's lap while outside the rain kept falling and somewhere down the valley soldiers slept in requisitioned beds.

She had memorized it already. She'd done it without deciding to, the way she memorized everything: the letters she read aloud, the addresses, the sums owed and forgiven, the small confidences townsfolk entrusted to a woman they'd decided was furniture. Two hundred and seventeen names. Fourteen safehouses. Nine routes. She could recite the network the way other people recited psalms, and she supposed, privately, that it amounted to the same act of faith.

So why did her hands shake holding the book?

Because memory could be dismissed, she thought. Memory could be called invention, malice, a woman's fancy. Paper testified. Paper had weight in a way a voice did not—at least it did until someone decided to burn it, and Captain Renshaw, by all accounts, was not a burning sort of man. He was worse. He was a reading sort.

Oscar closed the ledger and pressed her palms against its cover, feeling the grain of the leather, the swell of the pages. Somewhere in the dark, the gatehouse clock ticked above her, the old works her father had kept oiled and she had kept oiled after him, faithful as a heartbeat.

"All right," she said aloud, to the clock, to the ledger, to no one. "All right."

She took off the clock's face plate.

* * *

Sunday, Renshaw received his first catalogue of seized materials and found it thin.

Forty-one items. Sermons of a nonconformist persuasion. A bundle of Quaker tracts. Letters from soldiers at the front, which he returned unread to their families as a gesture—he was not without gestures—that cost him nothing and purchased goodwill cheaply. Two pamphlets, months old, water-damaged, quoting a Mrs. E. Marlowe of Marleigh on the subject of conscientious objection.

No correspondence of Marlowe's own. No lists. No minutes of meetings that the Manchester file insisted had occurred.

"Either they burned everything before Friday," Prewitt offered, "or—"

"Or there was nothing here to begin with," Renshaw finished. "Which the file says otherwise." He stood at the vestry window, watching the rain finally slacken over the wet orchards. "People who burn everything burn carelessly, Lieutenant. Ash in grates, scraps in gardens, scorched corners. My grandmother burned letters for sixty years and I found every last one of them behind a drawer panel." He turned. "These people are careful. Careful means organized, and organized means a system, and a system means a place where the important things live."

"The Marlowe house is scheduled for search Tuesday, sir."

"I know what's scheduled." Renshaw buttoned his coat. "Search it anyway tomorrow. Early. Before anyone can pass a warning through the dairy route or the schoolyard or whatever clever little arteries they use. And send for the postal woman—Whitlock. If half the town's letters pass through her hands, I want to know what passes through her head."

He paused at the door, looking back at the boxes of seized paper stacked neatly against the vestry wall, forty-one items of almost perfect innocence, and felt the particular unease of a man who has swept a floor and found it suspiciously clean.

"Nobody hides nothing," he said, mostly to himself. "They hide something."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Over the orchards, in the clearing sky, the evening star came out hard and bright, and down in the valley the gatehouse clock struck eight, its voice carrying further than anyone in Marleigh quite admitted.
