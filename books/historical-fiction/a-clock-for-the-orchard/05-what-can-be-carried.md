## 5. What Can Be Carried

The warning came by laundry.

Mrs. Petch's line on Monday held her dead husband's shirts hung inside out, every one of them, collars showing like a row of pale open mouths. I had read enough letters in that town to know a signal when it was hung where the whole lane could see it and nobody could be accused of anything but careless washing. Inside out meant: *they are coming sooner than we thought.*

I was carrying a basket of mending that contained no mending at all—only two shirts of my own turned the wrong way round—and by noon I had walked past four houses whose lines told me the same thing in four variations. Mrs. Petch. The Alderton sisters, who had hung their tablecloth sideways so it drooped like a flag at half-mast. Widow Crane, who had simply taken everything in, which meant *burn what you cannot carry.* And at the end of Cooper's Lane, on Edith Marlowe's own line, a single pillowcase pinned by its closed end, hanging like a sack.

That one I had taught her myself, three years ago, back when the network was six women and a borrowed typewriter and I still believed we were too small to matter to anyone with power. A pillowcase hung shut means: *bring me everything. Tonight.*

I did not go straight to the Marlowe house. That was the first rule and the hardest one, always—that you do not run, because running is a kind of confession the whole town can read. So I finished my rounds. I delivered a letter to the Hendersons from their boy in France, who was alive as of the eleventh and did not know if he would be alive by the time the letter arrived, and I read it aloud twice because Mrs. Henderson asked, and I memorized it without deciding to, the way I memorized everything now. I delivered a circular about seed potatoes that was not about seed potatoes. I let old Mr. Fenn press a shilling into my hand for reading his daughter's letter even though reading letters for him was something I'd have done free, and I understood that the shilling was how he kept it from being charity, and that this mattered to him more than the shilling.

All the while the thought sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: *hours. We have hours, maybe.*

My whole life fit into a room above the cobbler's shop. I had built it that way on purpose. A valise packed and kept packed under the bed—two dresses, a coat, needles, thread, the photograph of my mother that was less a face than a suggestion of one. My wages in a tin. Six books. When you have been nobody since the day you were born, you learn to measure your life in things that can be carried, and you learn the strange comfort of it: nothing to lose is its own kind of armor. For twenty-six years I had worn that armor so long I had forgotten it was armor. I had thought it was skin.

Now the stone in my chest said otherwise. Now there were names in my head that could hang women, and paper in a house on Cooper's Lane that could hang them faster.

* * *

The Marlowe kitchen smelled of coal smoke and burnt sugar. Edith had been baking—she baked when she was frightened, a fact she would have denied under oath—and there were eleven small cakes on a rack that no one would eat, cooling toward stone while she stood at the table with the ledger open in front of her and her hands flat on either side of it as if she were holding the table down in a wind.

Nell Ashby was already there.

She stood by the window with her bicycle cap in her hands, turning it, turning it, and when she saw me her chin came up in that way of hers, half challenge, half apology, and something in my chest unclenched before I told it to. She had ridden over from Harrowgate through the afternoon fog with word folded into her boot sole—a notice, official, stamped, real. Requisition of the town. Arrival of military authority within the week; seizure of materials prejudicial to the war effort upon arrival. Prejudicial. As if papers could wound.

"They'll come here first," Nell said. Not asking. She had been to this kitchen enough times now to know the shape of the map in Edith's head, which put the Marlowe house at the center of everything and therefore at the front of every danger.

"First or last," Edith said. "It doesn't matter which. It matters only whether they find something." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were doing the thing I had never gotten used to—the thing where she looked at you and you felt *counted*, tallied, entered into some private column of hers marked *useful.* "Oscar. You've heard."

"The lines are out. Everyone knows by supper."

"Then we work tonight." She turned the ledger toward me and I made myself look at it, that plain school exercise book, cover gone soft at the corners, that held more dangerous sentences than any book in England. Names in her small upright hand. Routes written as shopping lists. Safehouses named as relatives—*Aunt Vera, Harrowgate; Cousin Bell, two counties east*—because Edith understood, as I understood, that the safest lie is the dullest one. "It has to leave this house tonight and it can't be found tomorrow. Not hidden badly. Not found. Do you understand the difference?"

"I understand."

"It can't stay with any of us," she went on, and her voice was level, teacherly, the voice she used at meetings when the men came to jeer. "They'll search the houses of everyone known to associate with me. They know who associates with me; they've had someone counting for months, I'd stake the house on it." Her mouth tightened. "So it goes somewhere they will walk past a hundred times and never see. Somewhere dull. Somewhere that belongs to no one."

"Or somewhere that belongs to the army already," Nell said quietly.

We both looked at her. She put her cap back on, tugging it down over her ears, and I watched color climb her neck and watched her refuse to be embarrassed by it.

"Think," she said. "They requisition the town. They take the houses they need. What do they trust? Their own billets. Their own stores. Nobody searches what's already theirs." She shrugged, the shrug of a woman offering a thing she wasn't sure was worth offering. "My father repaired the gatehouse clock at the orchard road. Army'll want the gatehouse—it sits where both roads meet. They'll post a sentry there before they post one anywhere else."

"And hide the ledger behind the sentry," I said.

"Behind the clock," said Nell. "Nobody opens a clock. Clocks are furniture. Furniture is nobody's business."

There was a silence of perhaps three seconds in which I understood that I loved her. It arrived with no ceremony whatsoever, between one sentence and the next, the way a match arrives: here, then light. I filed it away with everything else I carried, in the place where things wait to become useful later, and I said, "The housing's deep enough. Father Whitlock's almanac used to sit inside the case at our church and no one knew for nine years."

Edith was already moving. She took the ledger off the table and held it a moment against her chest—not sentimentally, I don't think, but the way you hold a door shut—and then she held it out to me.

"You'll do it," she said. "Not Nell; her face is known at the crossing since spring. Not me; if I'm seen leaving my own house tonight with anything in my hands we're finished before they arrive. You." She waited until I took the book. Its weight surprised me every time, though it couldn't have been two pounds. Paper always weighs more than it says it does. "You're the invisible one, Oscar. You've spent your whole life being passed over. Tonight that's worth more than courage."

"Invisibility is a skill," I said, "not an identity." Her words from June, given back to her, and something crossed her face—satisfaction, or grief, or both.

"Go after the lamplighter," she said. "And Oscar—" I stopped at the door. She was looking at the eleven ruined cakes as if she couldn't remember baking them. "If it comes to choosing between the book and yourself, choose yourself. It's only names. They're in your head now anyway. That's the real vault. The paper is just a copy."

But she was wrong and we both knew it, because heads can be opened too, with time and methods that didn't bear thinking about while the smell of burnt sugar was still in the room, and a copy in ink is testimony that doesn't break. I took the ledger out into the dark without answering her.

* * *

The town scattered its papers the way a flushed covey scatters, all of us flying low and in different directions, and I saw pieces of it as I went. At the Aldertons' a chimney breathed hard for an hour, giving up years of minutes and membership lists to the wind. Widow Crane carried a oilcloth bundle down to the mill race and came back empty-handed, and neither of us looked at the other, which was its own kind of embrace. In the churchyard I passed young Tom Ellery, home on leave with his arm in a sling, digging—so he would say if asked—a grave for the sexton's dog, and the soil he'd moved said otherwise, and I blessed him silently and kept walking. Every household had chosen its method. Burn, sink, bury, carry. The whole town performing innocence at once, badly, beautifully.

I had chosen carry. The valise under my bed went into the mill race after all—I wasn't sentimental about my own six books—and what remained I wore: the ledger flat against my stomach under my coat, bound there with a scarf, so that I walked through the lamplit streets with the network of forty-one women pressed warm against my skin like a second heartbeat.

The lamplighter was working Cooper's Lane as the last light failed. I gave him ten minutes. Then I went out the Marlowes' garden gate and along the hedgerow to the orchard road, and the whole way I recited names in my head, Marlowe's rule from the first day I'd taken the ledger on: *know it so well the loss of the paper is a nuisance, not a death.* Forty-one names, and I said them like a rosary. Ada Petch. Ruth Alderton, Sarah Alderton. Constance Crane. Vera Linney, Harrowgate. On down the list, and my feet found the ruts and the darkness without my eyes because some part of me had always known these roads better than I admitted even to myself.

The gatehouse stood black against the last grey of the sky, its clock face a paler square above the door, hands stopped at twenty past seven since March when old Ashby's repair finally gave out entirely. The orchard around it slept, rows of apple trees pruned and waiting, and the smell was cold sap and wet grass, and I stood a moment in the shadow of the hedge letting the quiet tell me whether it was honest quiet or occupied quiet.

Honest. For now.

The clock case was oak, older than anyone living, and the door swung on hinges that complained once before I remembered to lift the door slightly as I opened it—Ashby's trick, Nell's father's trick, the knowledge of families passing hand to hand like contraband. Inside: weights, pendulum, the great dusty works, gears fat as moons. And below them, where the case deepened toward the floor, a cavity behind the false back panel that repairmen use for tools. I had brought a candle stub and matches and my hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake. Fear had passed through shaking hours ago and come out the other side as something harder. Purpose, Edith would call it. I was beginning to think it was closer to anger wearing purpose's coat.

I wrapped the ledger in the oilskin I'd carried for the purpose and slid it into the cavity, flat, snug, invisible unless you dismantled the clock to its bones. Then a hesitation I hadn't planned: my fingers rested on the oilskin a moment longer, and I thought, absurdly, *goodnight,* the way you'd speak to something living. Forty-one women sleeping in oak and dust. Sleep well. Wake loud, when it's time.

I closed the case. Lifted the door so the hinge stayed silent. Wound nothing—the clock was dead and dead was safer—and stepped back into the orchard dark, and that was when the fear came back, properly, all at once, because the work was done and done work leaves you nothing to hold.

They would post a sentry here. Nell had seen truly: the gatehouse was exactly the sort of place soldiers seize, two roads meeting, a wall, a roof. Which meant I had hidden the most dangerous book in the county inside a building about to fill with the very men hunting it—which was either brilliant or insane and would remain undecided until it was decided for us. Behind a sentry. Behind a clock. Behind the assumption that no one hides treasure in the conqueror's own pocket.

I got back over the hedge and down the lane with my heart going like the pendulum the clock no longer had.

At the corner by the well I nearly walked into Nell. She had her bicycle up on its stand in the lee of the wall, waiting, and she didn't ask *did you do it*, she asked, "Steady?"

"Steady," I said.

"Good." She hesitated, and in the dark her hand found my sleeve—just the sleeve, two seconds, then gone—but the touch ran up my arm like current. "I'm away before first light. If they stop me at the crossing I'm a girl visiting an aunt. Aunt Vera," she added, and I almost laughed, almost, and clamped it down because laughter carries at night like a shout.

"Come back safe," I said, which was not the coded phrase, and was not nothing either.

"Always am." She wheeled the bicycle around, paused. "Oscar. Whatever happens when they come—steady hands. He'll be watching for flinches. Men like that read people the way you read letters."

"How do you know what men like that—"

"Because I've been stopped at checkpoints since April," she said, "and I'm still riding." And she pushed off into the dark, and the sound of the chain faded, and I stood alone by the well with my empty hands.

Empty hands. That was the strangest part. I had carried that ledger against my body for three hours and my body still expected the weight of it, kept bracing for a burden that was no longer there, and I understood suddenly that this was the whole education, the whole trade, the entire secret art of it: you learn to hold a thing so completely that holding it changes nothing about how you walk, how you stand, how you meet a soldier's eye. You learn to be full and appear empty. Edith called it a skill. Standing by the well with the night coming down cold, I finally believed her.

I went home to the room above the cobbler's with its bare bed and its unpacked valise-shaped absence under it, and I slept four hours without dreaming, and woke before dawn to a sound I had never heard in Cooper's Lane before: boots. Many boots, in step, on cobbles, coming up the London road.

Grey light through the window. Down the lane, the first column of them, rifles slung, breath steaming, an officer at the front checking something against a map of our town drawn by someone who had never lived in it. Requisition day. They had come a week early.

I dressed slowly. I braided my hair. I put on my plainest coat and my most forgettable face, and I thought of the ledger asleep in the oak, forty names strong—forty-one, I corrected myself, counting Edith, counting Nell, counting, for the first time in twenty-six years, myself.

Then I opened my door and went out to meet them.
