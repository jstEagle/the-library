## 6. The Requisition

Captain Edward Renshaw arrived in Marleigh at half past six in the morning, before the fog had lifted off the orchards, and he counted the chimneys smoking as the lorry rolled down the high street. Fourteen. A small town. Small towns were either easy or impossible, and he had learned in three years of this work that the difference was not size but silence. Chattering towns gave themselves away in a week. Silent towns kept things.

Marleigh was silent.

He stepped down from the lorry, adjusted the strap of his case, and stood a moment in the road letting the cold get into him properly. The men unloaded behind him—twelve military police, two clerks, a sergeant named Doyle who had done this work in Cardiff and knew to keep his voice low. Renshaw had read the file twice on the train down. *Edith Marlowe. Widow. Independent means. Correspondence intercepted under Defence of the Realm Act 39(2). Suspected coordination of anti-conscription agitation through rural distribution networks.* The word *network* had been underlined by someone at the war office who wanted it to mean more than it did. Renshaw did not yet know whether it did. That was why he had come himself instead of sending a captain of lesser patience.

The requisition order went up on the notice board outside the George and Dragon at seven o'clock sharp, nailed there by Corporal Finch while three soldiers stood beneath it looking bored and dangerous, which was the correct number for the effect. By seven-fifteen the high street had filled with townsfolk reading it in that particular stillness Renshaw had come to think of as the first silence—the one before people decided what they were allowed to feel.

He watched from the doorway of the inn with tea he did not drink.

The order was straightforward enough. The army took the schoolhouse for a garrison. It took the mill yard for transport storage. It took, in the language of the document, *such premises as may be required for the efficient administration of military operations*, which meant whatever Renshaw decided it meant, and he intended it to mean the Marlowe house on Orchard Lane, with its wide hall and its long windows facing the water meadows. Not because he needed the rooms. Because he needed the woman out of them.

You did not search a network from its edges inward. You searched it from its center outward, and you made the center watch while you did it.

* * *

At nine he presented himself at the Marlowe house with Doyle and four men, and Mrs. Marlowe received them in her front hall as if they had come about drainage.

"Captain." She was perhaps fifty-five, upright, wearing grey wool and no jewelry except a plain brooch. Her handshake was dry and brief. "You'll want the drawing room, I expect. It's where I do my correspondence, so I imagine that's where you'll want to look."

Renshaw allowed himself one moment of professional appreciation. She had skipped the outrage entirely—skipped *what is the meaning of this* and *I shall write to my MP*—and gone straight to cooperation, which was either innocence or discipline, and discipline showed itself in exactly the same way.

"You're very quick, Mrs. Marlowe."

"I'm very old, Captain. It looks similar from the outside."

The house smelled of woodsmoke and apples. There were books everywhere—not hidden, displayed, the way innocent people display books—and a piano with sheet music fanned across it, Debussy, someone's careful fingering penciled into the margins. He noted the pencil marks. He noted everything; noting things was the whole of his trade.

The search began at nine-twenty. Doyle took the ground floor, methodical as a man mowing a lawn: skirting boards, undersides of drawers, the backs of pictures, the dust lines where something had stood and been removed. Two privates went up with instructions for the bedrooms and the linen press. Renshaw himself sat in the drawing room with Mrs. Marlowe and let her make conversation, because the conversation was the search too.

"You'll be billeting men here," she said. "How many?"

"That depends."

"On whether you find anything." She folded her hands. "Captain, I'll save us both some hours. I write letters. I have written letters against conscription since 1916, and I signed my name to every one of them, and I'd sign them again. If that's your sedition, there it is. But if you've come for something larger—if you've been sent down here because London believes a widow with a typewriter runs a conspiracy—then you've been misinformed, and you'll find nothing, and the men you billet in my home will have been wasted on you."

"And if I don't believe you?"

"Then you'll search beautifully and go away empty, and we shall both have performed our parts."

She said it without heat. That was what unsettled the junior officers, he knew—they mistook her calm for arrogance and got rough with it, and roughness with a woman like Edith Marlowe was like punching water. Renshaw had interrogated U-boat crews and conscientious objectors and once, memorably, a bishop, and he had learned that calm came from two sources: a clear conscience, or a rehearsed position. His job was the sorting.

Upstairs, a drawer slammed. Boots crossed a ceiling.

In the hall, he saw her for the first time.

* * *

She was standing by the coat stand with a stack of folded linens in her arms—youngish, mid-twenties maybe, brown hair pinned back without ceremony, a face you would pass on a street eleven times before remembering it once. The maid, or the companion, or whatever Marleigh called such women. She had come in from outside; her boots were wet at the toes and there was a red mark on her cheekbone where the cold had caught her.

Doyle stopped her. "Name?"

"Oscar Whitlock." Low voice, level. "I live in. I do for Mrs. Marlowe."

"Do for her how?"

"Everything that needs doing."

Doyle looked at Renshaw, and Renshaw looked at the woman, and the woman looked back with an expression of polite, faintly bored attention—the expression of someone waiting for a bus. It was well done. It was possibly too well done. Most servants in a searched house showed something: fear, or the vicious satisfaction of watching authority paw through their employer's drawers, or at minimum the animal anxiety of employment threatened. This one showed nothing at all, and nothing, worn that smoothly, was a garment tailored to fit.

"Set those down," Renshaw said. "Open each one for me."

She set the linens on the hall table without hurry and unfolded them one by one—sheets, pillowcases, a tablecloth—shaking each out gently, and there was nothing sewn into any of them, and he had known there would be nothing, and she had known he had known, and the whole exchange was conducted like a duet both parties had rehearsed separately.

Her hands interested him. They were steady. Not clenched-steady, white-knuckled over fear—he had seen plenty of that this week—but steady the way a surgeon's are, or a pickpocket's, hands that had practiced being observed. When she refolded the sheets her corners matched exactly. Servants' hands did many things well, but exact corners under the eye of a searching officer was a specific skill, and it belonged to people who had trained themselves to perform normalcy on demand.

"What do you read to her?" he asked.

"Sir?"

"She's going blind. Or nearly. The file says her correspondence has increased while her eyesight has failed. Someone reads to her. Letters, documents." He watched her face. "Is that you?"

Something moved behind her eyes—not alarm, exactly. Recalculation. "I read the newspaper sometimes. And letters from her sister in Canada."

"Read anything else?"

"If it's put in front of me, Captain, I read it. I don't sort the post, I only carry it up."

It was a perfect answer and delivered like a bored recitation, and Renshaw smiled at her—one of his genuine smiles, the ones that disarmed—and she returned it politely, a servant's smile to an officer's face, and neither of them believed a syllable of the transaction.

"Whitlock," he said. "Unusual Christian name."

"It's a family name, sir. My mother's doing."

"Where were you this morning between six and eight?"

"Feeding hens and fetching milk from Tanner's farm, same as every morning. Mr. Tanner will tell you, and his boy, and probably the boy's dog if you ask nicely."

There it was again—that flicker of wit kept carefully small, a flame turned down to a pilot light. He filed her then, right there in the hall of the Marlowe house, filed her under a heading he reserved for a rare category of person: *question*. Not suspect. Questions were better than suspects. A suspect you could arrest, and an arrested person stopped telling you things. A question sat quietly inside an active situation and generated answers for months.

"Thank you, Miss Whitlock. You may finish the linens."

She finished the linens. He went back to the drawing room.

* * *

By noon the search had produced: forty-one letters of anti-conscription opinion, all signed, all already known to the intercept office; a pamphlet entitled *The Cost in Men*, printed in Leeds, two copies; a subscription card for a pacifist weekly; and one locked writing desk in the study that yielded to Doyle's crowbar and contained household accounts, seed catalogues, and the dead husband's campaign medals.

No ledger. No lists of names. No routes, no code words, no evidence of organization beyond one stubborn woman's signature.

Renshaw sat in the study among the spilled contents of the desk and thought about the shape of the absence. Innocence had a texture, and this wasn't quite it. Guilty people hid things shallowly or deeply, but rarely *cleanly*—there was always a residue, a receipt, a name on the back of an envelope, a second inkwell, a burn mark in a grate where paper had died in haste. The grate in this study held three-month-old ash from ordinary coal. The wastepaper basket held a grocer's bill and a church circular. Either Edith Marlowe was exactly what she appeared—a loud, lonely woman whose pen outran her influence—or everything that mattered had been removed from this house before the army thought to want it.

And removal required warning. And warning required a source. And sources in a town this size were like nerves in a tooth—one touch and the whole jaw lit up.

He sent Doyle to the post office with a warrant. He sent Finch to the railway siding to inspect the freight book. He sat alone in the study for ten minutes with his notebook open, writing down not facts but timings: when the requisition order had been drafted, who in Whitehall had handled it, how many days between draft and arrival, and therefore how large the window was in which a warned person might act. The window was five days. Five days was enough to move papers to the moon, if the papers were light and the courier was invisible.

Then he went back downstairs, and found Miss Whitlock polishing the hall mirror, and watched her for a full minute through the drawing room door before she turned around.

She didn't startle. He noted that too. Most people felt a stare the way skin feels weather; she registered his presence only when she chose to turn, which meant either remarkable composure or practice at being watched. Both were interesting. Neither was proof of anything, and he was a patient man, and patience was the entire arsenal of his profession.

"Miss Whitlock. Does Mrs. Marlowe keep a diary?"

"No, sir."

"How do you know?"

"Because I dust the furniture, sir, and diaries are heavy things to hide from a duster."

Again the little flame. Again the perfect servant's cadence carrying a perfectly insubordinate sentiment. He almost liked her, which was a warning sign he had learned to heed; the people you liked were the ones built to be liked.

"The house will be garrisoned," he told her. "Eight men, effective tomorrow. Mrs. Marlowe keeps her rooms and the drawing room; the rest is the army's. You'll remain as staff. You'll continue to carry mail—supervised now, the postal arrangements have changed. And you'll understand that everything in this town passes through my office eventually. Everything always does."

"Yes, Captain."

"Do you have any questions?"

She considered him with those mild eyes, folding her duster over her hand with those surgical, unhurried fingers, and said, "Only whether the men will want feeding at the same hour as the household or separately. It affects the bread."

It affected the bread. He wrote the phrase down later that night because he suspected he would want it again—it was exactly the kind of sentence that seemed like nothing and later turned out to have been a door.

* * *

That evening he walked the town alone, collarless, in a civilian coat that fooled nobody, and let Marleigh look at him and decide he was harmless enough to ignore. He heard the public bar of the George go quiet when he passed and resume a half-tone lower. He saw the baker's boy run an errand with unusual urgency and noted the address. He stood at the orchard gate at dusk and watched a woman he did not know ride a bicycle along the lane from the direction of two farms whose names were in his file, and she rang her bell cheerfully at him as she passed, and he touched his hat.

Small towns did not defeat him by hiding information. Information always surfaced. Small towns defeated him by burying it under so much ordinary life that digging cost more than the prize was worth—and his job, in the end, was arithmetic. He would open the post. He would count the loaves. He would learn who bought lamp oil in quantities beyond their lamps, who traveled to neighboring towns on errands that bore no fruit, whose griefs and debts and grudges could be converted, at standard rates, into testimony.

Somewhere in Marleigh was a list of names. Lists wanted to become evidence; it was their nature, given time and pressure. He had time. He intended to supply the pressure.

Back at the inn he wrote up the day in his private file, the one no clerk transcribed. Under *MRS. MARLOWE* he wrote: *Cooperative beyond reason. Center holds. Search elsewhere.* Under *TOWN*: *Silent. Silence is organized.* And then, on a fresh page, because questions deserved pages of their own:

*WHITLOCK, O. — domestic, Marlowe house. Hands steady under search. Answers polished. Humor controlled. Reads aloud (per file inference). Present during removal window? Probable. Watch. Do not approach. Approaching tells her she matters.*

He blotted the page and sat looking out at the darkened high street, at the curfew emptiness, at the one lit window up on Orchard Lane where a widow sat in a drawing room being brave at a piano, and he thought about the linens, and the exact corners, and the phrase *it affects the bread*, and he understood that he had spent today finding nothing in a house where something certainly was, and that the something had been carried out of it—perhaps in a laundry basket, perhaps under a wet shawl, perhaps inside the head of a woman who folded sheets like a surgeon and looked at captains like weather.

He blew out the candle. Tomorrow the searches would begin outward, farm by farm, crossing by crossing, and somewhere a courier with steady hands would have to move again, and movement, sooner or later, was visible.

Patience, he told himself. The ledger would come to him. Paper always wanted, in the end, to be an archive.

He was wrong about that, though it would take him most of a year to learn how.
