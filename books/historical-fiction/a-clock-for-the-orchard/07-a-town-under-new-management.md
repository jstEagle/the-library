## 7. A Town Under New Management

The first thing the army did was change the clock.

Not stop it—they weren't barbarians, Captain Renshaw's men would have you believe—but regulate it. Two corporals came to the market square with a ladder and a canvas bag of tools and set the town clock to Greenwich time, as if we'd been living by some foreign sun before they arrived. Old Fenwick stood watching them with his arms folded and said, too loudly, that the clock had kept this town honest for ninety years without their help. He was fined a shilling for loitering. After that, people watched the soldiers work in silence, which is its own kind of language.

I learned the new rules the way I learned everything: by carrying paper.

The occupation issued its orders as printed notices, pasted to the post office door each morning, and because I read faster than most, I became the one who told people what their lives now contained. Curfew at eight. Assembly of more than five persons prohibited. All correspondence subject to inspection. All public meetings forbidden. The word *forbidden* appeared so often it stopped looking like a word and started looking like a wall.

"Read it again," said Widow Prue, holding her basket against her chest like a shield.

"All correspondence subject to inspection," I said.

She nodded slowly. "Then mind what you say when you hand things over."

That was the whole of it, really. That was the new management in a sentence. Mind what you say. Mind what you carry. Mind what your face does when a soldier reads your mother's handwriting over your shoulder.

I had spent my whole life minding. It should have been easier for me than for anyone.

* * *

They billeted six men at the Marlowe house.

Edith received them in the front parlor with tea and the particular smile she reserved for opponents she intended to outlive. The soldiers were awkward guests—young, mostly, farm boys from Yorkshire and Kent who looked at her portrait of her father as if it might report on them. Only Corporal Dunn, the eldest, met her eyes with anything like comprehension, and I watched Edith mark him the way she marked everyone: a small nod, filed away.

I slept in the gatehouse still, but now there was sentry duty at both ends of the orchard lane, and my comings and goings were logged in a book that Corporal Bell kept with the diligence of a man who believed the war could be won through neat handwriting.

*Name.*

*Oscar Whitlock.*

*Business.*

*Post.*

Every morning, six days a week. *Post.* And every morning he wrote it down as if it were something worth knowing, and every morning I thought: if only you knew what business actually meant, boy, you'd need a second book.

Because here is what the army never understood about a town like ours: mail does not stop. People still love each other, still owe each other money, still die and leave things to be divided. The letters kept coming, thinner now, censored twice—once by the field offices, once by our own postmaster Hobbes, who stamped everything with rubber-tired obedience. And the townsfolk still needed them read aloud, because half of Orchard Vale had never been taught their letters and the other half had lost their spectacles to economy.

So I read less and remembered more.

It happened almost without my deciding it. A letter would come for Mrs. Aldous from her son at the Front, and I would open it under the soldier's eye, scan it, and read aloud only the weather and the food. But the rest—the line about his sergeant being transferred, the mention of a man from the next village shot for desertion—I carried out of the room in my head, and delivered later, in the churchyard or across a fence, in a voice pitched for one listener.

"You've changed," Nell said to me once, at the crossing where our routes overlapped. We were allowed ten seconds' conversation under the sentry's gaze; we used four.

"How?"

"You used to read like the words belonged to the page." She adjusted the strap of her satchel, eyes forward. "Now you read like the words belong to you."

I didn't answer. There wasn't time, and anyway the truth would have taken longer than ten seconds: that the page had become dangerous, that ink could be seized, that the only safe place left in Orchard Vale for a sentence was behind my ribs.

* * *

The informers came out within three weeks, the way mushrooms come out after rain.

You always know who they are afterward, though you can't prove it. The Hensley girl reported the vicar for a sermon she found defeatist; he was warned, and the sermon that followed was about Noah, which everyone understood to be about endurance, and which no one could call sedition. Mr. Cutler reported his own brother-in-law for hoarding flour. Mrs. Brand reported a conversation she claimed to have overheard at the well—a conversation, several women agreed privately, that had never taken place, since none of them had said it.

The strange thing was not that people informed. Fear does that; fear has always done that, in every occupied town since occupation was invented. The strange thing was how quickly informing began to feel like civic duty, how the army didn't have to compel it, only arrange conditions and let ordinary loneliness do the rest.

Edith saw it before any of us.

"They think they're watching the town," she said to me in the kitchen, quietly, while Corporal Dunn's boots crossed the ceiling above. "But a network of informers is itself a network. Routes. Names. Payments. Everything we built, they're building too, only theirs runs on fear instead of trust." She stirred her tea. "Fear moves faster. Trust lasts longer. We must simply be sure to last longest."

"And if we don't?"

She looked at me then, and there was nothing soft in it. "Then it will have been worth attempting."

I want to record that I took comfort from her certainty. What I actually felt was colder: the understanding that she had already assigned herself to the column of acceptable losses, and might be assigning the rest of us there too, name by name, in the ledger I carried in my skull.

* * *

Supervised delivery was its own education in performance.

A private named Sallow rode shotgun on my route Tuesdays and Fridays, a round-shouldered lad who read every envelope upside down and once confiscated a birthday card because it was "coded"—it had a picture of a horseshoe on it. With Sallow I performed illiteracy's opposite: I read everything fully aloud, slowly, in a flat voice, leaving nothing between the lines, until he grew bored and looked at the hedgerows instead.

With Corporal Bell at the checkpoint I performed efficiency: forms completed, names spelled correctly, nothing to see.

And with Renshaw—who passed me twice on the lane that month, once on foot and once in an army lorry, always slowing slightly—I performed nothing at all. I simply went on. That was the hardest act. His eyes had a way of settling on a person like a hand checking a pocket, and I could feel him taking inventory: the steady hands, the unremarkable face, the driver who had been in the room when the house was searched and whose pulse hadn't jumped.

He hadn't spoken to me yet. I understood, in the way one understands weather, that he was saving it.

Meanwhile the town learned its new arithmetic. Eight o'clock, shutters. Five persons, disperse. A letter from your son, opened by strangers before it reached you. Mrs. Aldous stopped asking me to read the censored portions even privately—"What I don't know can't be reported on me," she said, and hated herself for saying it, and said it anyway. That was the occupation's true harvest: not the arrests, which were few, but the thousand small self-edits, the sentences swallowed, the greetings cut short in the lane because someone might be counting.

Against which, small defiance. Fenwick wound the market clock two minutes fast every night and let the army correct it every week, a duel nobody acknowledged. The schoolmistress taught the children a folk song with verses old enough that no regulation could find the sedition in them. And the women of the network, scattered and supervised, kept meeting in ways that were not meetings: at washing lines, at funerals, in the queue outside Hobbes's office, passing words wrapped inside complaints about the price of bread.

I collected it all. That was my work now, though no order had ever assigned it. Every whispered name, every safehouse that opened or closed, every hour a soldier's patrol shifted north—I laid each fact down somewhere the requisition couldn't reach, and the weight of it changed me in ways I didn't examine. You cannot hold that much remembering and stay the person you were. Some nights I recited the ledger silently from Z to A, just to feel the whole architecture of it stand up inside me, name upon name upon name, and I would lie in the dark of the gatehouse thinking: I am the most dangerous document in this county, and I look like nothing at all.

* * *

Nell came through on a wet Thursday, under escort like everyone, and we got eleven seconds at the crossing because the sentry was arguing with a farmer about a lost pig.

"The printer's been asking after us," she said, eyes on the mud.

"Which printer?"

"The kind who keeps his type in another county." Then, softer, almost lost in rain: "You're keeping well?"

"Every word of it," I said.

She almost smiled. The sentry turned; she was gone.

Eleven seconds. I stood in the wet road and understood that those eleven seconds had been the entire substance of my week, and that this was either love or conspiracy, and that I had stopped believing there was a difference worth naming. Somewhere behind me the market clock ticked two minutes fast, and ahead of me the lane bent toward the gatehouse, toward the clock housing where a hollow waited, empty now these many weeks, and toward whatever came next.

I found out sooner than I wanted to. Because when I reached the post office that afternoon, late, Hobbes was waiting at the counter with a letter in his hand—not mail, nothing with a stamp—and Captain Renshaw's lorry stood outside in the street, engine running, and Hobbes wouldn't meet my eye.

"Miss Whitlock," he said. "The captain would like a word."
