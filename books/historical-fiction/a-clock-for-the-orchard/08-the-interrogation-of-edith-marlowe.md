## 8. The Interrogation of Edith Marlowe

They had taken the good chair from the parlour.

Edith Marlowe noticed this before she noticed anything else—the captain's chair, the one with her mother's needlework on the backrest, now standing behind a trestle table in the requisitioned schoolhouse as though furniture could be promoted like men. She had been told to sit opposite it, in a hard schoolroom chair meant for eleven-year-olds, and she had sat, folding her hands in her lap the way she did at committee meetings when some alderman was explaining her own cause to her incorrectly.

No charge had been named. No arrest had been declared. A corporal had simply appeared at her door that morning and said, *Ma'am. The captain would like a word,* and what could she say to that except that the captain was welcome, and would he take tea?

He had not taken tea.

Captain Renshaw sat down across from her and arranged three objects on the table with the deliberation of a man laying out surgical instruments: a notebook, a pencil sharpened to an aggressive point, and a copy of her own letterhead—the cream paper with the small printed orchard, which they must have found in her desk drawer along with forty unopened envelopes.

"Mrs. Marlowe," he said. "Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't aware I'd been given a choice," she said pleasantly. "But I'm glad of the walk. The morning was fine."

"It was." He opened the notebook. He was younger than his rank suggested—thirty-five perhaps, with the kind of tidy, patient face that made you understand how armies worked, because armies were not built by shouting men but by tidy patient ones. "You've been writing a great many letters."

"I write a great many letters. I've written letters since I was nine years old. My first one was to my cousin Ada, complaining about a governess. I stand by every word."

The pencil made no note. He watched her instead, the way a man watches water for signs of rising.

"You correspond with women in seventeen counties," he said. "We have samples."

"Then you know my handwriting is deteriorating. I blame the ink available these days."

"Mrs. Marlowe." He leaned back, and his voice softened into something almost kind, which she recognized immediately as the most dangerous register a man in his position owned. "You are sixty-one years old. You are a widow. Your son is at the Front and writes home with pride about his mother. Do you know what I would like to do? I would like to leave this town having never troubled you again."

"How generous."

"It isn't generosity. It's economy." He tapped the letterhead once, lightly. "There is a war on. Every hour I spend on a harmless old woman is an hour I don't spend on the actual enemy. Convince me you're harmless, and we're both done by lunch."

And there it was: the offer, the frame, the trap laid out flat in the sun so she could admire its workmanship. *Convince me you're harmless.* As if harmlessness were a thing she could prove, and proving it wouldn't be the first lie.

Edith unfolded her hands and refolded them.

"Captain," she said, "I will tell you exactly what I do, and you may make of it what you like. I write to women whose husbands and sons are away. I ask after their health. I tell them where they can find milk when the shops run short, and which farmers will sell eggs honestly, and how to apply for the separation allowance when the army loses their paperwork—and the army does lose their paperwork, Captain, with remarkable consistency. I convene a knitting circle. We produce socks. If sedition can be knitted, I have not yet discovered the pattern."

"That's a very complete answer."

"I'm a very complete woman."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the acknowledgment that one had been attempted. He wrote one line in the notebook. Then:

"The knitting circle. Where does it meet?"

"Wherever there's room. Last week it was Mrs. Pargeter's kitchen. The week before, the chapel vestry. The week before that, my orchard, because it was warm and the apples were falling and someone had to be under them."

"Who attends?"

"Women who need socks."

"Names, Mrs. Marlowe."

"Captain," she said gently, as if correcting a child's arithmetic, "if you imagine I am going to hand you a list of the women of this town so that your soldiers can knock on their doors and frighten them in their own kitchens, then you have mistaken me for someone with a great deal less sense than I have. And I think, from your file-keeping, that you don't mistake people easily. So let us not waste each other's mornings pretending."

The pencil stopped.

For a moment the schoolroom was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the boarded windows, a lorry changed gear on the high street—a sound that had not existed here in August and was now as ordinary as rooks. Renshaw studied her, and she studied him back, and between them the good chair stood like a chaperone at a bad dance.

"You understand," he said at last, without heat, "that I am required to conclude from that answer that you have something to conceal."

"I understand that you are required to write something in the notebook, and that whatever you write will be truer than you intend. Yes. Write that I have something to conceal. Everyone has. The vicar conceals his drinking. The butcher conceals his prices. Concealment is the English religion; we merely practice different congregations."

He wrote it down. She watched him do it and felt—not fear, exactly. Fear was for later, at night, in the dark of her own house with the clock ticking. Here in the daylight she felt the older thing, the thing that had carried her through twenty years of platforms and hecklers and thrown fruit: the clean pleasure of refusing to be smaller than she was.

"Your driver," Renshaw said.

The change of direction was well done. She kept her face still, but inside, a hairline crack of attention ran through her, quick as ice shifting.

"What about my driver?"

"Whitlock. Reads letters aloud at the market, I'm told. For the ones who can't read." He turned the notebook toward himself, as if consulting it, though she suspected he consulted nothing. "An educated person doing illiterate work. That interests me."

"Oscar reads to people because half this county can't read, Captain, and the other half is ashamed to admit it. If that interests you, your war has gone very quiet indeed."

"And where does Oscar go, of an evening?"

Home, she wanted to say, to a cold room and a thin supper and the ledger hidden—no. Not hidden. Kept. There was a difference and the difference was everything, and she would not teach this man the word *ledger* by so much as a syllable's carelessness.

"Oscar goes where I send Oscar," Edith said. "Which is nowhere, now, since you've requisitioned the roads. You've made my driver the most idle person in Kent. I hope you're proud."

He closed the notebook. It was done with such neatness—squarely, corners aligned—that she understood the closing itself was a message: *we are not finished; I am only pausing.*

"Thank you for your time, Mrs. Marlowe. You'll be asked to come again."

"Will I? Without charge, without warrant, without so much as a polite lie about my arrestable qualities?"

"Yes," he said, and stood, and even held the door for her, and that—God help her—that was almost the worst of it. Courtesy laid over coercion like a clean cloth over a stain. "Mind the step."

She minded the step.

* * *

Outside, the light hit her like a hand. October light, low and gold, lying along the schoolyard wall where the paint was flaking, and against the wall, hands clasped, hat straight, waiting with the stillness of a fence post, stood Oscar.

Edith had told her not to come. She had said it plainly at breakfast, in front of the toast: *You will not stand outside a military interrogation like a wife at a courtroom door.* And here Oscar was, because Oscar heard instructions the way stone hears rain.

"You're early," Edith said.

"I was always coming," Oscar said. Which was not the same thing as admitting anything, and Edith loved her a little for the distinction.

They walked. Not fast—they had learned to walk at the speed of the unremarkable—but steadily, past the bakery where the queue now contained two soldiers and no laughter, past the noticeboard with its fresh requisition orders pinned over the parish fete poster like a lid nailed onto a box. Oscar said nothing until the corner of Church Lane put a wall between them and the high street.

"Well?" Oscar asked.

"He wants names. Naturally."

"And you gave him—"

"Socks. I gave him socks, and a knitting circle, and my opinion of army paperwork." Edith allowed herself, briefly, the smile she'd been holding in the schoolroom like a coin under her tongue. "He wrote it all down. He's thorough. That's the trouble with him—he isn't stupid, and he isn't cruel, or rather he doesn't need to be, which is worse, because cruelty you can name and fight. Thoroughness just arrives one day and owns the town."

Oscar walked three paces in silence. Then, quietly, watching the road rather than her:

"He asked about me."

Of course he had. Of course. Edith felt the crack of attention in her own chest widen into something colder. "Yes."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you read to people who can't read. That you're idle now, thanks to his roads." She stopped, and turned, and took Oscar by both shoulders—an old habit, standing a head shorter, looking up. "Listen to me. He is building a picture out of small facts. Every fact you give him cheaply is a brushstroke in it. So from today you give him nothing—not your routes, not your evenings, not the name of the publican who leaves the back gate open. Nothing. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Oscar said, and her voice was steady, and her eyes were not entirely, and Edith thought: *she knows already what it costs. Good. Better she learns it from me than from him.*

They walked on. At the gate to the orchard Edith paused, one hand on the latch, looking up at her own apple trees gone ragged with unpicked fruit because the pickers were at the Front. Somewhere in that orchard, in the gatehouse, in walls she trusted, was the truest account of what she and a hundred women had built while the country told them to be quiet and grateful.

"Edith," Oscar said—she used the name rarely, and it landed each time like a key turning. "If he comes for you properly. Arrests you. Takes you somewhere we can't follow."

"Then you will carry on."

"That's not what I was going to say."

"It is what you should say, and quickly, while we're both being brave in daylight." Edith pushed the gate open. The hinge groaned; nothing else in the world made a sound. "Whatever happens to me, the record survives. That was always the arrangement. I am sixty-one, Oscar. I have been arranging things since before your parents met. Let me arrange this last one."

Oscar stood in the gateway with the light behind her, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Then she came through the gate and closed it behind her, and helped her, wordless, up the path—two women walking through falling apples toward a house that soldiers had already searched once and would search again, better, next time.

* * *

That evening Edith sat in her parlour—in the good chair, returned to its place by a corporal with an apology so perfunctory it barely qualified as words—and listened to the clock, and thought about courtesy.

It was courtesy that undid her, in the end, that first night. Not the questions. The questions had been weather; she had been rained on by better men in worse halls. But the way Renshaw had held the door. The way he had thanked her for her time while making it clear her time was now his to request indefinitely. The way the whole apparatus of the state had descended on her town wearing its politeness like gloves, so that when it gripped you, you could not even say where the skin touched.

She had spent twenty years fighting men who opposed her openly. Openness was a gift; you could strike openness. But this man did not oppose her. He *processed* her. In his notebook she was not a woman but a discrepancy, and discrepancies were corrected, not defeated.

And underneath the anger, quieter and more corrosive, sat the other thing—the knowledge she could not shake, that while she sat giving him socks and sarcasm, he had been asking after Oscar. Asking lightly. Filing her away. Her invisible girl, her careful reader-of-letters, the one person in the county who knew every route and every safehouse and every name—

Every name.

Edith set down her tea untasted.

Because here was the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the spring, since the night she had put the ledger into Oscar's hands and watched those long careful fingers learn its weight. One ledger in a gatehouse clock. One mind in a town full of soldiers. And herself summoned, repeatedly, obligingly, to a schoolroom where a thorough man asked gentle questions about her driver.

They didn't need to find the ledger. They only needed to find her, and wait, and let the interrogations do what interrogations do—which was not extract secrets but *locate* them, narrowing, narrowing, the way a man narrows a field before he sets the dogs loose.

She had thought she was protecting the network by putting it in the safest place she knew.

She had put it in the one place Renshaw was already looking.

Edith rose, went to the window, and looked out across the dark garden toward the orchard, where a single lamp burned in the gatehouse window—Oscar, awake, working, keeping. The lamp looked very small and very far away, and around it the night lay over the trees like a hand.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *Tomorrow I move it again. Tomorrow I tell her everything.*

But the requisition orders had gone up on the noticeboard that afternoon, and one of them, new since morning, concerned the gatehouse specifically: *structure to be inspected for billeting suitability, Thursday.*

Thursday was two days off.

Renshaw would not wait two days.
