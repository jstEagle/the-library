## 9. Two Roads Out of Town

*Oscar*

The list came to me folded inside a funeral notice, which Edith Marlowe would have appreciated if she'd been free to appreciate anything. *Mrs. Albert Greaves regrets to announce the passing of her husband's cousin, a service to be held Thursday.* The paper was thin as onion skin, and written on the back in pencil so faint it might have been accident were eleven names not arranged in two careful columns.

Eleven women willing to swear, before any tribunal that would listen, to what had been done in this town since the requisition. Eleven names I could recite already, because that was my work now—my only work. To carry what couldn't be carried.

The printer was a man named Aldous Wren in Fenmarsh, two counties east, who had once set type for suffrage pamphlets and had not yet been arrested for it. A letter from him had reached us through three hands, each hand dirtier than the last, and the message was simple: *Send me your witnesses' statements and I will set them in type and store them where no fire of yours or theirs will reach them. Come yourself if you can. Paper travels badly; people travel worse.*

Two counties. Two checkpoints between here and there, at least—the one at Harrow Bridge where the military police checked papers against lists, and the random ones they threw up like snares wherever a road narrowed.

"You can't go alone," Nell said.

We were in the scullery of the Hollis farmhouse, which had become our post office, our meeting room, and occasionally my bed, though never when Nell was in it. She stood with her back to the door out of habit now, watching the window glass reflect the yard behind her.

"I've gone everywhere alone for twenty years," I said.

"And how did that serve you? You were invisible until they needed someone to blame." She said it without cruelty, which was her gift. Nell Ashby could say a true thing the way other people handed you a cup of tea—as if it were nothing, as if it cost nothing, as if you weren't meant to examine it too closely. "Besides, a woman traveling alone reads as suspicious. Two women visiting a sick aunt read as nothing at all."

"Is that what we are? Sisters?"

"We're whatever the road requires us to be." She took the funeral notice from my hand, read it upside down in three seconds—I watched her do it, that quick flick of comprehension—and gave it back. "Thursday. Market cart goes as far as the crossroads. After that we walk."

"After that we lie," I corrected. "And after we lie, we hope."

She almost smiled. "That's every journey ever taken. Get some sleep. We leave before light."

I did not sleep. I lay in the narrow cot above the scullery and recited the eleven names in order, then in reverse, then by parish, the way a child counts sheep except my sheep were living women whose lives depended on my memory staying warm. When the first grey came through the shutters, Nell was already downstairs with bread and hard cheese wrapped in a cloth, and something in her face told me she hadn't slept either.

*Nell*

I had crossed into occupied territory twice before this, so I knew the grammar of it: the lowered voice, the unremarkable clothes, the way you rehearse your lie until it bores you. What I hadn't known was Oscar Whitlock. I'd heard of her—every courier in three towns had heard of Mrs. Marlowe's reader, the quiet one who carried whole conversations in her head—but hearing of a person and walking beside them at four in the morning are different educations entirely.

She walked like water finding its level. No wasted motion, no glance lingering anywhere a glance shouldn't linger, and yet I caught her taking everything in: the sentry patterns, which farmhouses showed lamplight, which dogs barked at soldiers and which had learned silence. She'd been invisible so long she'd made an art of it, and watching art being made is its own kind of intimacy.

At the crossroads the market cart waited, driven by Tom Hollis's brother-in-law, a broad silent man who smelled of turnips and asked no questions, which in 1917 was the highest form of hospitality.

"Two sisters," he said loudly, for the benefit of no one, "off to see their aunt in Fenmarsh. Poor soul's got the rheumatism."

"Poor soul," I agreed, and climbed up beside Oscar into the smell of straw and axle grease.

The sun rose as we rolled east, and I learned something else about her: she talked in a cart when she thought no one could hear. Not much. But she pointed out a heron standing in a ditch with the concentration of a woman naming an old friend, and she said, apropos of hedgerows, that her father had been a gardener before he died, and that she measured every year of her life by what fruit was ripe when things happened. "Plums," she said, "when the army came. It seems obscene now, eating plums that week."

"What week?" I asked, though I knew. Everyone knew.

"The week they took her." She didn't say Edith's name. She didn't have to. In this county, *her* had become singular.

I wanted to put my hand over hers on the cart bench. I noticed the wanting the way you notice weather coming—pressure behind the ears, a change in the air. I had wanted things before, quietly, and buried them under the road like everything else I couldn't afford. But sitting beside Oscar Whitlock with eleven names folded against her ribs, I felt the burial ground shifting.

"Oscar," I said.

"Don't," she said gently, eyes forward. "Not here. Not where the hedges have ears."

So we rode the rest of the way in silence that was, I decided, its own kind of speech.

*Oscar*

Harrow Bridge checkpoint appeared out of the heat haze like something built overnight by giants with poor planning—a sawhorse barricade, a canvas awning against the sun, six men in uniform and a sergeant with a ledger of his own. I understood ledgers now. I understood what they were for and what they cost.

"Sisters," Nell said before the sergeant could ask, holding out both our papers. Hers identified her as Ellen Ashby, seamstress, of Malden Cross. Mine as O. Whitlock, companion. Both documents were excellent forgeries; I tried not to think about whose skill that reflected, or what it said about our times that forgery had become a neighborly trade, like jam-making.

"Aunt in Fenmarsh," the sergeant read. He was perhaps fifty, sunburnt, tired in the way of men who had been told their whole war would be glorious and had instead been issued a clipboard. "Rheumatism?"

"Dreadful," Nell said, with such sincere sympathy that I nearly believed in the aunt myself.

He looked at us. Here is what I know about being looked at: most people flinch somewhere—the mouth, the shoulders, the breath. I had spent a lifetime teaching myself not to, and Nell, I realized, had the same schooling. We stood in the sun and let ourselves be ordinary, two women slightly wilted, mildly inconvenienced, thinking about lunch.

"Open the basket," the sergeant said.

Nell opened it. Bread, cheese, a jar of pickle, a folded shawl. And beneath the shawl's false bottom—God bless whoever had sewn it—nothing at all, because the list wasn't in the basket. The list was in my left boot, wrapped in oilcloth, flat against my ankle where I'd convinced myself I could feel it burning like a coal.

The sergeant prodded the bread, wrinkled his nose at the pickle, and waved us through. It was only forty yards past the barricade that my knees informed me what they thought of all this, and I had to ask Nell to stop so I could pretend to fix my bootlace while my hands shook.

"You felt it," she said quietly. Not a question. "When he looked at you."

"I felt the bootlace wanting to be tied."

"Liar." But she smiled when she said it, and helped me up, and her hand stayed in mine a half-second longer than the road required.

*Nell*

The second checkpoint found us at dusk, on a lane so narrow the hedges scraped the cart wheels, and it found us badly. There was no time to rehearse, no amiable sergeant—just a lantern swinging up out of nowhere and a young lieutenant with a voice still cracking, ordering us down off a hay wagon we'd paid a farmer to ride, and asking questions in the wrong order.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Visiting our aunt."

"Where is your aunt?"

"Fenmarsh."

"That's not what I asked."

It was exactly what he'd asked, but saying so seemed unwise. Beside me I felt Oscar go very still—not frightened-still, but the stillness of a clock deciding whether to run fast or slow. The lieutenant was nervous. Nervous boys with rifles are more dangerous than confident sergeants with clipboards, and everyone on that lane knew it, including the boy.

"Names," he demanded.

"Ellen Ashby. This is my sister Olive."

"Whitlock," Oscar said, calm as milk. "Our aunt is expecting us. She'll wonder what kept us, Lieutenant, if you keep us much longer, and she writes letters. She writes a great many letters."

Was it a threat? It was nothing. It was an old woman with a pen. But the boy's ears went red, because in an occupied country every old woman's pen is a loaded thing, and he waved us on with a stiffness that was trying very hard to be authority.

We walked the last three miles because the farmer wouldn't take the cart past the mill, and by then it was full dark, and by then we were both running on bread and adrenaline and the particular giddiness of people who have cheated death twice before supper. Fenmarsh showed itself as a single lamp and a sign for the Wheatsheaf, and beyond it, down a lane, a cottage with a press visible through the window like a sleeping animal.

Aldous Wren turned out to be seventy years old with ink to his elbows and no interest whatsoever in our journey. "Statements," he said, when Oscar produced the list from her boot. "You have them?"

"In our heads. All eleven. Written testimony to follow by safer channels, but he wanted—" she glanced at me, "—the names confirmed in person. So there's no mistake in the setting."

Wren grunted, lit a lamp, and pulled paper toward him like a man pulling on boots. "Then recite."

And Oscar recited. Names, parishes, dates, what each woman had seen and would swear to—the searchings, the seizures, Edith Marlowe taken from her own kitchen without charge, the safehouse burned with a family's winter stores inside it. Her voice never rose and never shook. Wren wrote in a hand like copperplate, faster than seemed possible, and I stood at the window keeping watch on the lane and listened to a woman turn memory into evidence, name by name, and understood that I was watching the war lose something it would never get back.

*Oscar*

Wren worked past midnight. He fed us cold mutton and ink-scented tea and offered his floor, but there was a question hanging over the cottage—where next, when tomorrow came, whether we traveled back together or apart, whether what had happened on that road stayed on that road—and neither Wren nor his press had anything to do with it.

"There's a barn behind the cottage," Nell said. "Hay's clean enough. I don't want to sleep under a roof tonight. Roofs feel temporary lately."

So we climbed through the loft door into hay that smelled of summer, which felt like a forgery itself, given the season. We lay down a careful arm's length apart, and the dark came down around us, and I listened to the distant tick of Wren's press through the wall—no, not his press; the cottage clock, keeping honest time the way clocks do regardless of who owns the hours.

"Olive," Nell said.

"That's not my name."

"It is tonight." A pause, and hay rustling as she turned toward me. "Oscar. On the bridge. When your hands shook—you let me see them shake. You didn't hide it."

"You'd have known anyway. You always know."

"Yes," she said simply. "I do."

I should tell you what I knew by then. That Nell Ashby had crossed into occupied territory five times that I knew of and probably more. That she sewed a false bottom into a basket with stitches fine enough to shame a professional, and lied to a sergeant with a sincerity I could have learned from, and kept watch at windows with a patience I could not. That when she said *don't, not here,* on the cart, I had obeyed, and the obeying had cost me something I couldn't itemize.

"I've been nobody for a long time," I said to the rafters. "It was safer that way. Being nobody means there's nothing to find. Nothing to burn. Nothing to—" I stopped.

"To love," she finished.

The word sat in the hay between us, enormous and small at once, the way true words are.

"Nell Ashby," I said, "you are the least nobody person I have ever met."

"Is that a confession?"

"It's the only kind I make anymore. Everything else gets recited for the record." I turned toward her. In the dark I could find her by breath and warmth, and I did. "But yes. If you're asking. Yes."

Her hand found mine in the hay, and hers was steady, and for once mine was too.

* * *

*Nell*

Morning arrived with Wren banging on the barn door and news that rearranged the world: the checkpoint at Harrow Bridge had been doubled overnight. Someone had reported two women heading east. Someone always reported; occupation ran on reportage the way mills ran on water.

"They're stopping carts going west," Wren said, pressing a parcel into Oscar's hands—printed proof sheets, the first fruits of the night's labor, hidden inside a hymnal. "Carriages less so. Dr. Pym's trap goes back at ten. He owes me for a job of printing. You'll be his nieces. He has nieces. He can't remember how many."

Dr. Pym proved to be deaf, cheerful, and constitutionally incapable of answering a direct question, which made him perfect cover. At Harrow Bridge the young lieutenant peered into the trap, asked his questions, received answers about cataracts and church pews and the price of oats, and waved us through looking vaguely concussed.

It was only when the bridge was a mile behind us that Oscar exhaled, and only when the town's rooftops rose ahead of us that she spoke again.

"They'll come for the press eventually," she said. "Wren knows. He said he'd print three copies and bury two."

"Then the record survives even if he doesn't." I watched her hold the hymnal against her chest like a heartbeat. "You did it. Eleven names, set in type, where no fire reaches them."

"For now." She was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice I hadn't heard from her before—smaller, and truer—"When they took Edith, I memorized her face. Deliberately. In case I was the only one left who'd seen it. I've been doing that all my life, Nell. Memorizing things instead of keeping them. Because keeping things gets them taken away."

"And last night?"

"Last night I kept something anyway." She looked at me straight on, in daylight, in front of God and Dr. Pym's deaf ears, and I felt the ground move. "I'm telling you so it's on the record."

The trap rattled on toward town, toward curfews and billeted soldiers and a house where Edith Marlowe's chair sat empty. I held Oscar's hand where the driver couldn't see, and I thought: whatever comes next, whatever the informers and the checkpoints and Captain Renshaw's patient hunters bring—we had made a thing together that fire hadn't touched.

Neither of us knew yet how soon we'd need it. Neither of us knew that back home, a safehouse was already marked, and a friend of ours was already being followed, and the word *traitor* was already loose among us, wearing a familiar face.
