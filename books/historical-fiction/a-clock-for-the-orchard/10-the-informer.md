## 10. The Informer

The fire was visible from the crossing, and everyone in town stopped to watch it, which meant everyone in town would remember where they were standing when the Hartfield cottage went up.

I stood with them. I let my mouth fall open at the right intervals. I said *God help them* to nobody in particular, because that was what people said, and being invisible had always been a matter of saying what people said.

Inside me there was a ledger of a different kind. Hartfield cottage: two rooms, a widow named Agnes Pell who could not read but could recite the names of forty women who had signed their marks on her kitchen table. Agnes, who kept a tin of shortbread for couriers and called all of them "duck" no matter how old they were.

Agnes, who had gotten out. That much I learned by noon, counting faces at the relief queue like a farmer counting sheep. She wasn't among them, but neither was she among the ones Renshaw's men marched down the high street with blankets over their heads. She had simply vanished, which in an occupied town was its own kind of news.

The one they took was Tom Ellery. Sixteen years old, a bicycle courier with a cracked front tooth he was vain about, a boy who whistled when he thought no one was listening and blushed when you caught him at it. They took him at the corner of Mill Lane at half past six in the morning, before the fire had even finished eating itself, as though the arrest and the burning were two hands of the same body moving together.

That was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. The fire and the arrest, moving together. Which meant someone had told them both things—where the papers were, and where the boy would be riding—and told them at the same hour, in the same breath.

Someone had talked.

* * *

I did my rounds under supervision the way I'd done them for three weeks now. Private Denny walked six paces behind me with his rifle slung and his boredom showing, and I carried the ordinary mail—the ration notices, the letters from mothers to sons at the front—and I did not read any of them aloud, because reading aloud was now done only in the presence of a soldier, and nobody wanted their private griefs performed for an audience of khaki.

So I remembered instead. That was the work now. I walked past houses and the houses spoke to me. Number four Church Row: Margaret Ellison, blind in one eye, could hide three grown women in her cellar and had done it twice during the requisition census. The bakehouse off Cooper's Yard: the ovens stayed hot past midnight twice a week, and what looked like flour deliveries were sometimes women arriving from towns whose names I kept in my head like coins sewn into a hem.

And every house I passed, I asked the same question: *which of you burned?*

Not which of you is brave enough—that question answered itself daily, in small stubborn ways, a window box planted in regimental colors just slightly wrong, a hymn sung a beat too slow when the patrol passed. The question was which of you is frightened enough. Because bravery was common as dirt in this town. Fear was the rarer commodity, and fear was what Renshaw bought.

Clerk Hobbes kept the municipal office, such as it remained under requisition. He was a gray man made grayer by khaki, his office now a records annexe, his own desk pushed against the wall while soldiers' clerks used the long tables. He doffed his cap to me in the street the way he had done for twenty years. His son wore a lieutenant's uniform in France and Hobbes wore the son's photograph in his breast pocket like a talisman, and showed it to anyone who stood still long enough.

It was Hobbes who processed the billeting lists. It was Hobbes who knew which households had been assessed, who paid what, whose property had been flagged. And it was Hobbes who had access to every scrap of paper that moved through the municipal office—including, three weeks ago, a requisition schedule that Edith Marlowe's network had needed badly, and that had reached us through a channel I had never been permitted to know.

A channel. Singular. One thread connecting the clerk's office to the orchard. If that thread had frayed—if someone had pulled on it from either end—

I was doing it again. I caught myself outside the bakery, staring through the window at Mrs. Cooper's flour-dusted hands, wondering if the tremor in them was guilt or age or nothing at all. This was what informers did to you even before you found them: they got inside your seeing. Every face became a document to be read, and I had spent my whole life reading documents, and I was good at it, and that was suddenly the most terrible thing about me.

* * *

Nell came to me at dusk by way of the churchyard, which was the agreed route, though agreement counted for less than it had. She slipped into the shadow of the yew where I was pretending to read a headstone, and her face in the last light was tight as a drumhead.

"You heard about Tom."

"Everyone heard about Tom."

"He hasn't said anything." She said it fast, like getting it out quickly hurt less. "Denny talks too much when he's tired. Tom's given them nothing. Not a name."

"A boy of sixteen holding out." My voice came out flat. "They'll make him sing eventually. Everyone sings."

"Then we get him out before he does."

"How?" I turned on her, and the word came out harder than I intended, and harder than she deserved. "How, Nell? Tell me the plan. Walk into the yard behind the drill hall where they're holding him and carry him out over your shoulder?"

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about Nell Ashby; she never flinched, she just recalibrated, her eyes going somewhere internal and coming back with something new. "You're scared," she said.

"I'm *thinking.*"

"You're scared, and it's making you cruel, and I've seen you be a lot of things but I've never seen you be cruel." She stepped closer. Under the yew it was nearly dark now, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Oscar. Someone gave them Hartfield. Someone gave them Tom's route. Do you understand what that means? It means they know the network has branches. It means they're pulling the branch ends to find the trunk."

"And the trunk is Mrs. Marlowe."

"The trunk is the *ledger,*" Nell said. "Edith can hang. Names can't. You keep saying you're only the driver, only the reader, only the nobody. Fine. Be nobody. But be nobody who's planning."

I looked at her then—really looked, close enough to see the chapped skin at the corner of her mouth, the exhaustion under her eyes that matched my own—and I felt the thing I had been refusing to feel since the crossing, since the coded phrases neither of us admitted to knowing, rise up in me like water finding a crack. And I hated it, because caring about Nell Ashby meant giving the fear a second address.

"If they took you," I said, "I don't know what I'd give them."

"That's why you won't let them take me." She almost smiled. Almost. "Now walk me out of this churchyard, Miss Whitlock, before the sexton decides we're mourning somebody."

* * *

The found family assembled itself carefully over the next two days, in pieces, never all at once. Margaret Ellison brought word from Agnes Pell—alive, hidden in a hay barn eight miles off, furious, demanding news of Tom. Mrs. Cooper sent flour that weighed wrong in the sack, and inside the misweight was a note in pencil so faint it might have been accident: *Who else knew the Hartfield address?*

That was the question, wasn't it. Who else knew.

I sat up with the list in my head. Not on paper—never on paper anymore—but in my skull, laid out like a diagram. Agnes Pell, obviously. Tom Ellery, whose route included Hartfield. Edith. Me. And four others: the channel to the clerk's office, whoever they were; Mrs. Cooper, who provisioned; Margaret, who sheltered stragglers; and Nell, who had run documents to Hartfield twice in October.

Six people. Seven if you counted the unknown channel as two.

I lay awake doing what I despised. Weighing people. Agnes had lost everything; would she trade names to stop losing more? Tom was sixteen and alone in a cell; the bravest boy in the county was still a boy. Mrs. Cooper had grandchildren in the town, under the soldiers' boots, right there, every day. Margaret had already been warned once. Nell—

Nell I dismissed immediately, and then was disgusted with myself for needing to dismiss her immediately, for the fact that love required an alibi.

Which left the channel. Which left the clerk's office. Which left Hobbes, doffing his cap, wearing his son's photograph like a shield, processing the paperwork of the occupation with gray diligence. A man who believed in order, in procedure, in the proper filing of things. A man whose son was at the front, whose letters came through army channels, whose safety—or the rumor of his safety—could be placed on a scale by any captain with a scale.

I didn't want it to be Hobbes. That was the trouble. Wanting it not to be him was exactly the sort of softness an informer fed on.

* * *

On the third day after the fire, I was summoned—not arrested, merely *summoned*, which was Renshaw's preferred instrument, a summons delivered with a polite knock by Private Denny, who couldn't meet my eye.

I expected Renshaw. Instead I got Lieutenant Pryce, the intelligence officer, a young man with a careful mustache and a folder, who sat across from me in the requisitioned schoolhouse and did not offer me a chair until I'd been standing a full minute.

"Miss Whitlock. You read letters for the townspeople."

"Read them. Before the restrictions."

"You're literate. Unusual in a woman of your—" he consulted the folder, "—station."

"It's not unusual. It's just unrecorded."

He smiled at that without warmth and turned a page. "Thomas Ellery has been cooperative."

The floor did not move but I felt it move. "He's sixteen."

"He confirmed the existence of a distribution point in the Hartfield area. Naturally we already knew that; the premises burned." Pryce looked up, mild as milk. "He was unable to name others. Perhaps you can help him. An illiterate boy, a literate woman—it stands to reason she'd have been useful to you."

"To *me.*"

"These networks use drivers. Readers. People who pass unnoticed." He closed the folder. "You have steady hands, Miss Whitlock. I noticed it at the searches. Most people's hands go about performing their alarm. Yours don't. Captain Renshaw remarked on it as well."

I let my shoulders round. Let my gaze drift to the middle distance, the look of a woman who reads other people's letters and keeps her own thoughts small. "I drive a motorcar for Mrs. Marlowe, sir. Her hips are bad. I don't know anything about networks, but I know her joints ache before rain."

Pryce studied me for a long moment, and I understood that the performance worked or didn't work on a knife's edge, and that he had filed something away regardless. "You may go."

I went, at an ordinary pace, past the drill hall yard, where a bicycle leaned against the wall with a cracked front tire that no one would ever repair, and I did not look at it longer than a stranger would.

* * *

That night the family met—one branch of it—in Margaret Ellison's cellar, among the preserves, by one candle, because Margaret said the Germans hadn't got her down here and neither would the British. Edith Marlowe sat with her bad hips on a crate of damsons and received our reports the way a general receives weather.

"They're peeling us," she said, when I'd finished. "Hartfield first, now Tom. They take an edge, wait, take another edge. They're not hunting information, they're hunting *shape*. They want to know what we look like as a whole." She rubbed her hands together; the rheumatism had settled into them this autumn. "So we change shape."

"Tom first," Nell said. "Every day he holds out is borrowed."

"Tom is being held because someone drew them a map," Edith said, and her eyes came around the cellar, unhurried, resting on each of us the way her eyes rested on everything, weighing and loving in the same motion. "I won't insult this room by pretending we haven't each thought it. So let's say it plainly: there is a leak, and it is close, and until it is found, every route, every safehouse, every name is at risk. Including Oscar's."

"Including mine," I said. "Yes."

"You carry the most," Edith said quietly. "You know that."

"I know it."

Mrs. Cooper's hands were folded; her knuckles were enormous. "The Hartfield address," she said. "Who knew it besides us?"

Silence. The candle guttered and steadied.

"The channel," Margaret said. "Whoever feeds us from the municipal side. They'd have known the requisition maps. Hartfield was flagged in September."

"The channel comes through me," Edith said. "And the channel is a person who believes they are serving decency, or I would never have accepted them. Which means they are someone with a conscience. And a person with a conscience, pressed correctly, becomes a person with a confession." She paused. "Consciences leak in both directions."

I thought of Hobbes's cap coming off his gray head. Of the photograph in the breast pocket. Of a son at the front and a father at a desk, and a captain who owned scales.

"There's one more thing," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, level and far away. "Pryce told me Tom talked. Confirmed the Hartfield distribution point. But Renshaw's men burned Hartfield the same morning they took him—before any interrogation could have mattered. They didn't need Tom's confirmation. They already knew." I looked around the candlelight. "Either Pryce lied to me about Tom—which costs them nothing—or the arrest came *after* the knowledge, and Tom was taken to cover the source. Taken to look like the leak."

"Why would they do that?" Mrs. Cooper whispered.

"Because a real informer needs protecting," Nell said slowly. "And a boy who can't name anybody is the perfect informer-shaped hole to fill."

Edith Marlowe was very still. Then: "Or Oscar is wrong, and Tom broke faster than any of us wants to believe, and we are building comfort out of arithmetic." She sighed, and for one moment she looked every one of her fifty-eight years and the weight of every woman she'd ever asked to be brave. "We proceed as if both are true. Nobody moves anything. Nobody carries anything. For one week the network does nothing but exist. And Oscar—"

"Yes."

"Find me the leak. Gently if we can. But find them."

* * *

I walked home past the municipal office. There was a light in the back window—late even for an annexe—and through the frosted glass, a shape bent over a desk. Gray coat, gray head, working while the soldiers slept, the way he had worked for twenty years, serving whatever government put paper in front of him.

I stood across the lane in the dark and watched him, and I hated myself for watching, and I watched anyway.

Then the shape straightened, and stretched, and did a thing I will remember until I die. The man at the desk took a photograph frame from his breast pocket—the son, the uniform, the pride—and set it face-down on the blotter before returning to his papers.

Face-down. As if he couldn't work under those eyes.

An innocent man doesn't turn his son's portrait down. Or a guilty one does. I had spent three weeks learning that everything proved both ways, that paranoia was a hall of mirrors where every reflection accused its neighbor, and that this was perhaps the true purpose of informers—not the names they sold, but the sight they stole, until a town could no longer look at itself without suspicion.

I turned for home.

Behind me, the light in the clerk's window went out, and in the darkness where it had been, another light appeared—a match flame, low, cupped, moving toward the back door of the office where the requisition files were kept, at an hour when no honest clerk had any business unlocking anything.

And I realized, with a cold that started in my spine and spread outward, that I had been asking the wrong question entirely. Not *who talked.* But *who was talking still*—and to whom—and whether the man I had suspected all along was not selling us to Renshaw at all, but trying, in his gray and cowardly and human way, to warn me.
