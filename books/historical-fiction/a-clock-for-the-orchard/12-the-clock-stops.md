## 12. The Clock Stops

I felt it before I heard it. That's the strange thing about knowing a town the way I knew Marlowe's Crossing—not its map, though I carried that too, but its pulse. The gatehouse clock had ticked in my chest for six weeks like a second heart, and on the morning the soldiers came down the orchard lane with a crowbar and a canvas sack, that second heart stopped.

Nell woke me. Her hand on my shoulder in the dark of the loft above the cooper's yard, no lamp lit because lamps were noticed now.

"Oscar." Just my name. She'd learned to say it so it meant everything else.

Below us, boots on cobbles—four pairs, maybe five, moving without hurry. Hurry was for men who feared resistance. These men moved like the outcome had been arranged in advance, which, given Captain Renshaw's habits, it almost certainly had.

We went to the window. Frost furred the lower pane; I breathed a hole in it and looked through. The lane ran silver under a waning moon, and down it came Lieutenant Prosser with three men and, behind them, walking separately the way you'd walk a dog you didn't fully trust, came the town clerk. Hobbes. His breath smoked. He kept his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the ground, and I thought: *there*. There is the shape of what a man becomes when he trades his neighbors for his son's safety. Not monstrous. Small. Folded inward like a letter no one will claim.

"They're going to the gatehouse," Nell said.

"Yes."

"The clock."

"Yes."

I had known this morning would come since the night I'd stood in the gatehouse with a screwdriver and a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, with the ledger open on my knees for the last time before it went into the clock housing. I had read it then not as reading but as planting—every name pressed into me, Mrs. Fenwick of Harrow Lane and her sister-in-law in Birmingham, the two railway clerks who moved leaflets in luggage, the farmer's wife outside Aylesbury who fed couriers and asked nothing, the doctor who wrote letters certifying men unfit whom he'd never examined, eleven routes, four safehouses, thirty-one names, some dead already by fever or shellfire and therefore beyond Renshaw's reach but not beyond the record's. I had read it until I could close my eyes and walk its pages in the dark. Then I'd closed the housing, wound the mechanism, and set the pendulum swinging, and I remember thinking that this was what Edith had meant all along: *invisibility is a skill.* The clock could keep the time. I would keep the rest.

And then, three weeks ago, in the deep hours after a checkpoint near Bicester where a corporal had looked too long at our parcels, I had gone back and taken it out again. Because steady hands are only steady if they never have to be, and I did not trust luck, and I did not trust Hobbes, whose son wore a uniform and whose eyes had begun doing arithmetic whenever I passed the clerk's office. I moved the ledger to a place I have never written down and will never say aloud, and I left the clock housing empty except for dust and the smell of oil, and I wound the clock as always, and I told no one. Not even Nell. Especially not Nell—the fewer who knew, the safer she'd be, and I was learning that love is mostly a series of decisions about what not to say.

Now I watched them walk toward an empty box and told myself to feel safe.

What I felt instead was cold.

* * *

They took the clock apart right there on the gatehouse floor. We saw none of it directly—we couldn't get within sight of the orchard lane without being seen ourselves—but Nell had a gift for finding ears, and by ten o'clock we had the whole performance relayed to us in fragments: the cooper's boy had watched from the top pasture; the postman had been stopped at the crossing and held there "for his own convenience," which put him exactly where he could see; someone's aunt had sent word via a basket of eggs. This was how the occupied town spoke now, in baskets and detours, an entire language built out of what the soldiers couldn't be bothered to confiscate.

Prosser's men unscrewed the face first. Then the housing panels, laid out on the flagstones in order, careful as surgeons—they'd been told to be careful, which meant Renshaw expected to find something and wanted it intact. They lifted out the pendulum. They reached into the cavity where the weights hung.

Empty.

The story came to us four times that morning and each version agreed on the essential fact: the lieutenant stood over that open clock for a long minute, then sent a man running back to headquarters, and then—this detail I made the boy repeat twice—he began to laugh. Not loudly. A short exhale through the nose, the laugh of a man who has just realized the size of the thing he is actually hunting.

By noon Renshaw himself was at the gatehouse. I know because I made myself go and look. This is a discipline I recommend to anyone who intends to survive being hunted: watch your hunter. Learn his gait, his appetites, the direction his attention travels. He stood in the gatehouse doorway with his hands clasped behind him, studying the dismantled clock the way he studied everything, as if it were a document he intended to edit. Then he turned and looked down the lane, past the cottages, past the church tower, at the town itself, and I watched him arrive at the understanding that had been waiting for him six weeks: the ledger was not hidden in the town.

The ledger *was* the town. It was walking around in a driver's head, wearing a cap, carrying mail under supervision.

He couldn't know that, of course. But he knew something had happened, because the empty housing told him: whoever hid it had come back for it, quietly, while under his occupation, and he had never seen. He filed his questions the way I filed names. I felt his attention pass over the lane like a searchlight and I stood still inside it and thought about wallpaper.

Then he gave an order I didn't need the boy's relay to interpret, because half the town heard it: the motorcar to Marlowe House. And the soldiers went up the drive, and knocked, and brought Edith Marlowe out between them without letting her stop for her coat.

She didn't struggle. Of course she didn't. Struggle is for people the cameras might miss, and Edith had spent twenty years making sure cameras were pointed at her. She paused on the step to look back at the house—one breath, no more—and then she walked to the car with her spine doing the work her coat couldn't, and she got in, and the car pulled away past me where I stood at the corner pretending to wait for the postman, and her eyes found mine through the window with terrible accuracy, and she did not signal anything at all. That was the signal. Nothing. *Carry on.*

* * *

Nell found me afterward sitting in the coop loft with my back against the wall and my hands useless in my lap, and I want to be honest here, because the record deserves honesty even when it costs: I sat with triumph and guilt in the same chest and could not tell them apart by touch.

Triumph: the ledger was safe. Thirty-one names slept in a place no soldier would find them, and Renshaw had raided a box full of nothing, and every hour that passed without discovery proved again that invisibility works, that the skill can be practiced under fire, that I had done the one thing I was built for.

Guilt: they had taken Edith because the box was empty. She was in a cell in the requisitioned schoolhouse tonight because I had been clever. Every step of the logic that protected the network had led, with perfect mechanical certainty, to her door, and the mechanism I'd wound was ticking away in her chest now instead of mine.

"She'd tell you not to think that way," Nell said. She'd fetched bread from somewhere; she tore it and handed me my share without asking whether I wanted any. With Nell, food wasn't a question, it was a position.

"She'd tell me not to think at all. Think later, she'd say. Work now."

"She'd be right."

"I moved it, Nell. Weeks ago. I didn't tell you, I didn't tell anyone, and it worked, it worked perfectly, and now—" My voice did something I hated, cracked in the middle like a girl's, and I pressed the bread against my mouth to give my hands an errand.

"Now she's arrested," Nell finished. "Yes." She sat down next to me, shoulder to shoulder, and let the silence do some of the talking, which is one of the many languages she speaks better than I do. After a while she said, "You want to know what I think? I think if you'd left it in the clock, we'd be sitting here feeling exactly the same, only worse, because they'd have the names too. Both things were always going to happen. The empty clock just decided which thing."

That is Nell entirely. She can hold a hard truth flat on her palm like a coal and not flinch.

"There'll be leverage," I said. "That's why they took her. Not evidence—leverage. Renshaw doesn't have a case, he has a hostage."

"Then he'll make an offer."

"He already made one." I hadn't told her that either. Chapter and verse: the interview room, the tea I didn't drink, the voice pitched low and reasonable—I can give you immunity, I can have her released, all it costs is the names, and think what mercy that is, Miss Whitlock, sparing her the tribunal, sparing yourself. "I refused. But he'll make it again, and next time he'll have her in the building when he does. He'll let me hear her through a wall. He's patient enough for that."

Nell was quiet a moment. "Can you refuse it through a wall?"

I thought about lying to her, because lying is also invisibility, and then I remembered what we'd admitted to each other in a hayloft with a witness list dry between us, and I told the truth. "I don't know. Ask me again when the wall is real."

She nodded slowly. Then she said the thing that reorganized the whole day, the way she does, plainly, as if reporting weather: "Then we don't wait for his offer. We move tonight. You, me, the list of who matters most—if they take you tomorrow, someone else has to carry the names. Two of us memorizing halves. And we start getting word to the ones nearest the coast about slowing down, going quiet, till this blows over or doesn't."

"It won't blow over. He won't stop now. An empty clock isn't a defeat for a man like that, it's an insult."

"Good," said Nell. "Angry men make mistakes. Patient men don't. I'd rather he were angry."

Down in the yard the cooper's boy was singing something tuneless and innocent, and somewhere across the rooftops the church bell marked four o'clock, and I thought of the gatehouse clock standing open and unwound in the orchard lane, its heart removed, keeping nothing. Let it stand. I had the time now. I had all of it—thirty-one names, eleven routes, four doors that opened in the dark. And tomorrow, or the day after, Captain Renshaw would send for me again, and this time he would arrange for me to hear a familiar voice through a wall, and he would believe, because his whole life had taught him to believe it, that everyone can be bought if you find the right currency.

He was about to learn what I'd learned in the loft with the bread in my hand: that some of us were never for sale because nobody ever offered us anything we wanted.

What he'd offer instead—he'd find it soon enough—was Edith's freedom. And I had just discovered, sitting there in the dark with Nell's shoulder warm against mine, that I did not know yet what I would say.

But I knew what Edith would say. She'd say it through the wall, if she had to:

*Forget me. Save the record.*

And I knew, with the same terrible clarity, that I was going to refuse her.
