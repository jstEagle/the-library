## 13. Letters Never Delivered

The county gaol sat on the far side of the river, close enough to town that you could walk it in twenty minutes and far enough that the walk gave you time to change your mind. I changed mine twice on the first Tuesday, once at the bridge and once at the ironmonger's where the road bent, and both times I kept walking anyway, because that was the thing about me that Edith Marlowe had seen before I had: I could be frightened and useful in the same breath.

Visiting hours were Wednesday and Saturday, two until four. You signed a book at the gate. Your name, your business, your relation to the prisoner. *Relation*, the form said, as if there were a word for what I was to her. I wrote *secretary* the first week. By the third week the sergeant at the desk had stopped reading it, and I could have written anything.

They took my bag. They looked through the letters I'd brought — three from women in the network, scrubbed clean of anything that mattered, all weather and recipes and the price of coal. Then they walked me down a corridor that smelled of carbolic and cabbage to a room with a table bolted to the floor and two chairs on opposite sides of it. A wardress stood by the wall with her hands folded. A second door stayed open behind my chair so that everything we said would travel out of it and be heard.

Edith came in without being brought, or so it seemed; she walked like a woman entering her own drawing room, and only the pallor under her skin told you what four weeks inside had cost. Her hair was coming loose at the temples. Her collar was clean. She had pressed someone — a wardress, I later learned, a girl of nineteen who adored her — into laundering for her, because Edith Marlowe would not be seen rumpled even for the purposes of sympathy.

"Oscar," she said, warm as ever, and sat.

"Mrs. Marlowe."

"Don't be absurd. Sit."

I sat. The chair was cold. Between us the table was scarred with initials, generations of them, carved by hands that had needed to leave proof they existed.

"How is your mother's hip?" she asked, loudly enough for the doorway.

"Better. She walks to the corner now without the stick."

"And the Hendersons' boy — home safe?"

"Home. He doesn't say much."

"No. They don't." Her eyes did the rest of it, quick and bright, running over my face the way she used to run them over a letter before handing it back — checking for what I hadn't said. "You've been eating. Good. Nell?"

"Nell's well. She sends—" I stopped. Nell sent nothing, because sending anything was dangerous, because Nell Ashby's name was already in too many mouths. "She asks after you."

"She's clever not to write." Edith folded her hands on the table. Under the fold, where only I could see, her thumb moved once across her knuckles — the old gesture, the one she used when counting heads at a meeting, making sure everyone who came was counted going home. "Oscar. We have perhaps ten minutes before they grow bored of listening. Let me spend them properly."

"You always spend them properly."

"I'm going to spend these ones telling you to stop coming."

The room went very still around me. The wardress shifted by the wall. Somewhere down the corridor a door clanged and settled.

"That's not—" I began.

"It is." Her voice stayed level, pitched for me and no louder. "Listen to me. Every Wednesday you cross that bridge, you sign their book with your own hand, and every Wednesday you make yourself a person of interest. Renshaw has a file on this town thinner than he'd like. You're thickening it for him. You, with your steady hands and your excellent memory, are walking into his office once a week and letting him take your measure."

"He can take whatever measure he likes."

"He can take the whole record, Oscar." And there it was, underneath the drawing-room voice: urgency, worn smooth from being carried. "You know what you are now. There is no ledger anymore. There is no paper anywhere in the world with those names on it except inside your skull, and you come here, through a checkpoint, past his men, to sit across a bolted table from a woman in custody, and you think that costs nothing? It costs everything. It risks everything. Not me — me, they'll hold until the war embarrasses them and then release with an apology nobody will print. But the names. The routes. Mrs. Aldous in the mill cottage. The Fenwick sisters and their cellar. The man at the railway siding who looks the other way. If you are taken, all of it is taken with you."

"I won't be taken."

"Nobody plans to be taken. That is the first thing power understands about us and the last thing we understand about it." She leaned forward a fraction. In the good light of her parlour, two years ago, over tea she'd poured for a girl who read letters for the illiterate half of an orchard town, she had leaned forward just that way and said: *You see everything, don't you. That's rarer than you think, and worth more than you fear.* I had thought she meant it kindly. She had meant it as recruitment.

"Then give the names away," she said. "That's what memory is for. Scatter them. Tell no one person everything. Put Mrs. Aldous in one head and the Fenwicks in another and let no single arrest be a catastrophe."

"They wouldn't all keep quiet."

"Some would break. Yes. Better some than all."

"Edith." I had never called her that to her face. It landed between us on the scarred table like a coin spun and settling. "I have carried things for you for three years. Letters. Money. Names. I have memorized forty-one women and eleven houses and six routes out of this county, and I have done it gladly, and I am not going to hand pieces of it to people who might crack under a lamp in a police station. I'll carry it myself or it doesn't travel."

"That is loyalty," she said softly. "Loyalty is a virtue the way a flood is water. It depends entirely on where it goes."

"Then judge my geography."

For a moment — just a moment — the composure cracked and something young looked out of her face, something tired and grateful and afraid, and I understood that she had rehearsed this speech in her cell for four weeks and had known, probably from the first rehearsal, that it would fail.

"The record must survive," she said. "Promise me that much. Not me. The record. If it comes to choosing—"

"It won't."

"It may. War is long and men are patient. If it comes to choosing between an old woman in a cell and forty-one names that deserve a future, you choose the names. Say it."

"I won't say it."

"Oscar—"

"Because it's a false choice," I said, and my voice came out lower and harder than I intended, loud enough that the wardress by the wall turned her head. I brought it back down. "You want me to promise in advance to abandon you so that you can feel efficient about your own sacrifice. I decline. I have watched you refuse false choices for three years — you taught all of us that a question can be rigged, that *when did you stop* is not a question but a trap. This is a trap. *Choose the record or choose me.* No. Both. I will save the names and I will get you out, and if you tell me that's greed, then let me be greedy."

The silence held. Down the corridor, keys rang against each other like dull bells.

Edith looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I had never seen her do in any meeting, any march, any confrontation with magistrates or husbands or soldiers: she laughed. Quietly, carefully, behind her hand — but really laughed, her eyes creasing, her shoulders shaking once.

"Oh, you are *magnificent*," she breathed. "Renshaw sits in his office thinking he holds the stubborn one in this affair. He has no idea."

"He has several ideas."

"He has categories and you fit none of them. That's our advantage. Spend it well." The laugh faded into something steadier. She glanced toward the doorway, measuring the minutes the way she measured everything. "Very well. Both. But hear my conditions, since you've outvoted me. One: you don't come every week. Fortnightly. Make them miss you; make your absences boring. Two: you bring nothing written, ever, not even laundry lists — I mean it, Oscar, they photograph laundry lists in this war. Three—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, and her eyes fixed on mine with the full weight of the woman who had built a network out of teacups and sewing circles and the assumption that no one suspects a lady, "—three: when the historians come, and they will come, and they will be men with grants and deadlines and a version of events already drafted in London, you make certain that what they find is true. Whatever else burns. Whatever else is lost. Including me. The story survives the storyteller or it was never a story, only gossip."

"That's four conditions."

"The fourth is the one that matters."

* * *

I walked back across the bridge with the river grey below me and the wind pushing at my shoulders, and I did my arithmetic the way she'd taught me without teaching me: what do I carry, what can be taken, what remains if everything is taken.

What remained was forty-one names. Eleven houses. Six routes. Dates, amounts, the phrasing of codes, the way Mrs. Aldous signed herself *Auntie* in letters to her sister in Coventry and how *auntie* meant *send help*. The cellar door in the Fenwick house that stuck unless you lifted while you pushed. All of it filed behind my eyes in a dark that no warrant reached.

And what remained, too, though I had not put it in any ledger because it was never evidence of anything but myself: Nell Ashby's face in the hayloft two counties over, lit by a shuttered lantern, saying my name like a phrase she'd decided to learn properly. The weight of her arm across my shoulder in the dark. The way we had agreed, without agreeing, that neither of us would ever again pretend not to know the code.

Love against duty, Edith had framed it. She liked clean oppositions; they made fine speeches. But standing mid-bridge with the water moving under me I understood that she had it backwards, or sideways, or inside-out: love wasn't the opposing army. Love was the reason the duty was bearable. Nell guarding the door while I wrote. Edith's thumb moving across her knuckles, counting everyone home. I was not choosing between them. I was carrying them both, the way I carried everything, and the carrying was the whole of me.

At the far end of the bridge a figure waited by the milestone, collar up, hands in pockets, pretending to admire the river. My heart did its foolish lift before my caution caught up.

"You're supposed to be in two towns away," I said when I reached her.

"So are you," Nell said. "Fortune favours the badly supervised." She fell into step beside me, matching my pace without looking at me, which was its own kind of intimacy. "Well? How is she?"

"Unbowed. Annoying. Magnificent." I kept my eyes forward. A soldier passed us heading into town and we were two women walking, nothing more. "She ordered me to forget her and save the record. I refused. She laughed at me. I think I won."

"You never win with her. You survive her, and she lets you believe it was winning." Nell's mouth curved. Then it didn't. "Oscar. There's something else. Why I came."

The wind came off the water and I felt it go through my coat and into the place where fear lived now, permanently, the way a splinter lives under skin.

"At the records office," she said quietly. "The night I covered for you — I didn't see everything from the yard. But yesterday I went back along the lane, daylight, harmless, a woman calling on her aunt. There's a car comes twice a week, army staff car, and the officer who gets out isn't garrison. Different uniform. Different manner. He carries document cases in and lighter cases out." She paused. "And Oscar — he carries a leather folio with a crest on it I didn't know, so today I asked someone who reads those things. It's not a regimental crest."

"What is it?"

"Historical section," Nell said. "War Office. Whoever he is, he's not collecting evidence." Her hand found mine briefly, in the shadow of the parapet, and gripped hard. "He's writing the account."

I stood on the bridge with the river running under us toward the sea, carrying forty-one names and one refusal, and understood that we had very little time.

"They're drafting the story already," I said.

"Someone is," said Nell. "So we had better find out exactly what it says — before they finish it."
