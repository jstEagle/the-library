## 14. The Historian's Draft

Mr. Ambrose Fenn had been at the War Office for eleven years and had learned that history was not written; it was *edited*. The distinction had cost him some sleep in his first winter and none since.

His office was on the third floor, a room with a north window that never warmed, and it contained everything he required: a desk, two lamps, a tin of pencils sharpened to needles, and four crates of confiscated papers that the Ministry had decided were no longer evidence but raw material. Evidence went to court-martial files and gathered dust until someone burned it. Raw material went to men like Fenn, who shaped it into the account the country would keep.

The account had a working title: *Domestic Order and the War Effort, 1914–1918: A Preliminary Survey.* It would run to perhaps three hundred pages. It would be read by ministers, by dons, by the authors of schoolbooks who would boil it down further still, until what remained was a paragraph in a classroom in 1960, and then nothing at all. Fenn understood this arithmetic. He was, in his way, a craftsman of forgetting, and he took a modest professional pride in the finish of his work.

He began each morning with the same ritual. He read a document once. He made his notes. He returned the document to its crate and did not look at it again, because a historian's error was not in lying—lying left fingerprints—but in dwelling. You dwelt on a thing, you gave it weight, and weight showed through the prose like a bone through thin cloth. The trick was lightness. The trick was to handle every fact as if it were the same size.

The crate marked MARLOWE, E.—CIVIL SEDITION, HOME COUNTIES had arrived in March with a requisition order stapled to its lid. Inside: correspondence seized from the Marlowe house, mostly innocent—a great deal about fruit trees, jam, and the price of sugar—and a set of interrogation summaries dictated by Captain Renshaw's office, which were thorough in the way of a man writing for an audience above him.

Fenn read them with his usual detachment. Then he came to the fourth folder and stopped.

It was a transcription fragment, three pages only, headed INTERCEPTED CORRESPONDENCE — PARTIAL — COURIER NETWORK ("ORCHARD"). Somebody—Renshaw's clerk, presumably—had copied out fragments of letters before the originals were segregated. And scattered through the fragments, in code so thin it was nearly conversation, were names. Not Edith Marlowe; her name would have been useless anyway, too prominent to be anything but a figurehead. These were other names. Women. Dozens of them, implied rather than stated: *the widow at the crossing will take the northern parcels now*; *ask for Mrs. Hale when the apples come in*; *tell her sister the clock is slow this month.*

Fenn sat back. He did not dwell. He made himself not dwell. But the arithmetic of his craft had changed overnight, because these were not the papers of one eccentric gentlewoman with opinions. This was a network. A distribution system. A supply chain of paper and conscience running through half the county under the cover of orchards and errands, and it had been run, by every indication in the file, entirely by women.

He turned back to Renshaw's summaries. Here was Marlowe, questioned without charge, giving nothing but courtesy. Here was a note in the captain's hand about a driver—"the Marlowe woman's girl, steady, watchful, worth monitoring"—with no name attached, which was sloppy, or deliberate, or both. Here was an inventory of seized materials, and here, appended later in different ink, a discrepancy: items listed as destroyed whose destruction certificates could not be located. Fenn noted the discrepancy in pencil, very lightly, and then, after a moment, erased it. Not his department. Not his war.

By noon he had drafted the section. It came to eleven hundred words. He had done it many times before, and the machinery ran smooth: *Isolated agitators, drawing on metropolitan agitation, engaged in leaflet distribution of negligible strategic effect; local authorities responded proportionately; the matter was contained.*

Contained. Isolated. Negligible. Three words, and forty women became a weather event.

There was a difficulty, though, and the difficulty had a shape he recognized. The fragments named people. If he wrote the network out entirely—if he claimed there had been nothing at all—the fragments in the archive would contradict him for any researcher diligent enough to pull the crate. The solution was not silence. The solution was misdirection. He would acknowledge the "orchard circle" as a minor curiosity, a handful of cranks around one enthusiastic hostess, their activities "largely social, with a fringe of pamphleteering." He would give the paragraph a small, dismissive warmth, almost affectionate. Readers trusted a historian who could smile at the harmless.

He was pleased with the phrase *largely social* when he wrote it. It had exactly the right weight, which is to say none.

At four o'clock, Colonel Havers stopped by, as he did on Thursdays, and looked over Fenn's shoulder without apology.

"The Marlowe business," Havers said. "How are we handling it?"

"Largely social," Fenn said, and tapped the paragraph.

Havers grunted approval. "Renshaw wanted it burned outright."

"Burning leaves a gap," Fenn said. "A gap invites questions. A dull answer closes them." He did not say the rest, which was that burning also created martyrs, and martyrs had a way of acquiring names, and names were the one commodity Fenn's whole profession existed to control. Better to drown the story than to fight it. Better a footnote than a flame.

"Keep me informed," Havers said, and left, and Fenn returned to his proofs, and if his hand paused—for half a second, no longer—over the line where Mrs. Hale's name would have gone, he attributed it to the cold in the room. The window faced north. It never warmed.

He ordered the proof set in galley by Friday. Twenty copies. Distribution list attached. One copy, he noted with mild irritation, had already been requested by a captain in the field—Renshaw again, wanting to see how his campaign would read to posterity. Vanity. Fenn approved the request. Vanity was easy to predict, and predictable men made good margins on a page.

The galley proofs went out Monday morning. By Wednesday, copy number twelve was on a train north, folded inside a newspaper, in the possession of a compositor's sister who owed somebody in the orchard a favor that had nothing to do with apples.

Fenn never knew this. That was the design. But somewhere between London and the Home Counties, his eleven hundred careful words—his *isolated*, his *negligible*, his *largely social*—were traveling toward the one person alive who could weigh them against the truth and find them wanting, name by name by name.

* * *

Oscar read the proof twice, standing in the cold pantry with the door wedged shut and Nell keeping watch at the scullery window, because Edith was still in custody and the house still wasn't safe, and some things had to be read even when reading them felt like pressing a bruise.

Nell had brought it wrapped in butcher's paper inside a delivery of tinned milk, and she'd said, before Oscar unfolded it, "You'll want to sit down." Oscar hadn't sat down. She'd stood at the shelf with the pages in her hands while the ice block sweated beside her elbow, and she'd read.

*Isolated agitators.*

*Largely social.*

*Negligible strategic effect.*

She read the names that weren't there. That was the thing—she kept waiting for grief, and instead she got inventory. Mrs. Hale at the crossing. The Widow Prewitt and her northern parcels. Sister Cobb, who'd carried leaflets in her bread baskets for eighteen months and never once been stopped, because nobody stops a woman with bread. Miss Ada Finch, who'd taught half the town their letters and could copy any hand she saw. The Fenwick sisters. Old Marta at the mill gate. Nell's own aunt, though Nell had never said so aloud, and didn't now. Forty-one names in Oscar's head, and the page in her hands said *a handful of cranks*, and the page would win, because the page had a printing office behind it and a distribution list and time—years, decades, all the time in the world—while Oscar had memory, which everyone knew decayed, and no press, and a mistress in a cell.

She handed the pages back and found her hands were perfectly steady, and understood, with a clarity that felt less like feeling than like temperature, why Renshaw had wanted the ledger burned. She had thought it was about arrest, about charge sheets, about covering the army's conduct. It was simpler and vaster than that. It was about this. About who got to say *isolated*. About whether, thirty years on, a schoolgirl would learn that the orchard had grown nothing but fruit.

"They're not even lying badly," she said. "That's what frightens me. It reads well. Anyone would believe it. I believe it, a little, and I *know*."

"You know forty-one names," Nell said quietly.

"Forty-three." Oscar heard herself correct the number and something shifted in her chest—not warmth, exactly. Cold, clean purpose, the kind she'd only ever felt before in the seconds after hiding the ledger in the clock housing, when fear finished hardening into task. "I've been carrying them like parcels. Reading them over at night like letters for people who can't read. But parcels get delivered and forgotten. Letters get answered." She looked at the galley proof, at the neat ministerial type, the reasonable tone, the smile in it. "This isn't evidence anymore, Nell. It never was. The ledger was always about the story. Whoever writes first writes true. That's the whole law of it."

"Then we write," Nell said. "We've got paper. We've got your hand."

"It's not enough to write it down in a kitchen and hide it in another clock." Oscar was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the cold pantry smelling of salt and ice. "Hidden truth is just a rumor with better manners. It has to go where this goes"—she tapped the proof—"into the transcript, into the survey, into the paragraph they'll teach. Ahead of it. Before the revision sets like plaster. There'll be an inquiry after the war—there always is, Edith says so, said so months ago. Public. Minuted. Official record." She stopped pacing. "They think I'm nobody. The captain filed me away as a question and lost the question. Nobody is exactly what I need to be until the day I stand up in front of the inquiry and am not."

Nell watched her for a long moment. In the dim of the pantry her face was half shadow, and Oscar thought—not for the first time, but with new weight—that whatever Nell was about to say would be the truest thing either of them had said in weeks.

"Then you'd be visible," Nell said. "Permanently. To everyone. Renshaw, the Ministry, every man who ever preferred the quiet version. For the rest of your life."

"Yes."

"And you'd do it anyway."

"I memorized forty-three names," Oscar said, "because I thought carrying them invisibly was the safest thing for everyone. I was wrong, and the proof of how wrong is in your pocket." She held out her hand. "Give it back. I want to read it once more. I want to know exactly what we're up against, word for word, so that when my turn comes to write, I leave nothing out and put nothing in that isn't mine to put."

Nell passed the pages over, and their fingers touched on the transfer, and neither of them hurried it.

"One more thing," Nell said as Oscar smoothed the proof flat on the shelf. "Copy twelve came to me through my cousin at the print works. There was a note tucked with it. Unsigned."

"What did it say?"

"'Count the copies. One is missing from the record already.'" Nell let that sit in the cold air. "Someone over there knows what they've done. Someone over there is watching to see if anyone notices."

Oscar looked down at the galley proof—at *negligible*, at *contained*, at the small warm dismissiveness of *largely social*—and felt the last of her hesitation go the way the ledger had gone into the clock: swallowed whole, converted to purpose.

"Then someone over there should watch closely," she said. "Because I've noticed. And I remember everything."

She folded the proof along its creases, precise as a letter, and slid it into the false bottom of the flour bin where the network had once hidden pamphlets, and upstairs the gatehouse clock struck six across the orchard, steady as a pulse, keeping time over the place where the true record slept.
