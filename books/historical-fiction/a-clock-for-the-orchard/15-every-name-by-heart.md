## 15. Every Name, By Heart

I began with the letter M because it was the letter that had cost us the most.

Marlowe, Edith — no, Edith was not a name to be recorded. Edith was the hand holding the pen. I crossed her out before the ink dried. She would have scolded me for it. *The record is not a monument, Oscar. It is a tool. Put in only what a stranger would need to finish the work we started.*

So: Marlowe, E., organizer, arrested 14 March 1918 on pretext of correspondence with enemy aliens, charges fabricated by Capt. Renshaw, evidence: none. Held at — I had to stop and press my palms against my eyes until the dark went red. Held at the county barracks, converted granary, east wing. That much I knew from the visiting-room sergeant's clipboard, which he'd angled toward me once, carelessly, the way men angle things toward women they've decided are furniture.

Merritt, Agnes. Safehouse: the mill house at Harrow Ford, third door past the chapel, blue gate. Route carried: medical pamphlets north, then cash south, twice monthly since spring of '17. Taken 2 March when the safehouse burned. Status unknown.

*Status unknown.* The phrase sat on the page like a nail driven partway in, waiting for the hammer. I wrote it anyway. A record that left out the uncertain wasn't a record. It was an obituary.

Nell came in on the hour, the way she'd promised she would, and set a cup of chicory on the table without asking if I wanted it. I didn't look up. Looking up meant breaking the line of concentration, and the line was all I had; it ran from my memory through my wrist onto the paper, and if it snapped I might not find it again before dark.

"Page?" she asked.

"Four."

"How many names left?"

"Forty-one that I'm sure of. Nine I'll swear to but can't date."

"Then you're a quarter done and it's barely noon." She turned my cup a quarter-turn so the handle faced my hand, a small tyranny of care, and went back to the front room.

I heard the others arrive through the wall the way you hear weather: boots on the stair, the murmur of greeting kept low out of habit though there was no soldier within a mile of us now. Mrs. Pyle from the bakery, who had never been in the ledger herself but had baked bread into every parcel the network moved for two years. Tom Ellery, whose lorry had carried our crates under burlap sacks of turnips and whose son had died at Passchendaele wearing a uniform he'd been conscripted into by the same government his mother's bread had helped resist. Old Dr. Kessler, who had signed false medical certificates for eleven women so they could travel without questions, and who now stood in my doorway with his hat in his hands like a schoolboy called before the headmistress.

"We thought," he said, "you might want witnesses."

"I want dates," I said. "I want spellings. I want someone to check me against themselves, because memory is a liar that believes its own lies."

Mrs. Pyle laughed, one short bark. "That's why we're here, girl. None of us trusts ourselves alone anymore."

I cleared the table of everything but paper, ink, and the lamp, and I began again from A.

* * *

It is strange work, transcribing yourself. Every name I wrote came out of me like a stitch pulled from a hem — a small release and then a hole where something used to be held. By evening of the first day I had thirty-one pages and a headache that sat behind my right eye like a coin pressed to the bone.

Ablewhite, Ruth. Ashcombe, Vera. Barrow, Helen, neé Fitch, married name used on railway tickets because hers drew less attention. Bellamy, Constance — Constance who had taught half the town's children their letters and had never once asked that her own name be spoken aloud in connection with anything.

"You're doing Bellamy wrong," Nell said from the doorway. It was near midnight. Everyone else had gone home through back gardens, out of habit older than the armistice. "Two Ls. She showed me her marriage lines once, made a joke about how her husband spelled his own name wrong on them and nobody ever corrected him because correcting men isn't done."

I looked down. One L. I fixed it, and my hand shook doing it, because Constance Bellamy was in the granary too — taken the same week as Edith, released two days after, walking home with a limp she hadn't had before and refusing, flatly, to say what had caused it. Some names come off the page easy. Some come off bleeding.

"You know her," I said. Not a question.

"I carried with her twice." Nell came and sat across from me, elbows on the table, chin on her fists. "Before your time on the northern route. She hummed when she walked. Hymn tunes, always the wrong words. I keep thinking—" She stopped.

"Keep thinking what?"

"That if you write enough of them down, they stop being ours. They become facts." Her voice did something complicated on the word *ours*. "And facts don't need anyone to love them."

I put the pen down. In the three weeks since the records office — since the night the originals were swapped for my forgeries, since the night Nell took a truncheon across the shoulders and ribs covering my escape, since the night I ran four miles through frozen mud with a satchel of worthless paper while she stayed behind — I had not once let myself think about the alternative shape of that night. The one where she didn't walk out of it. The one where the last thing between us was a hayloft and a sentence neither of us had finished saying since.

"They need both," I said. "Facts and someone to love them. That's the whole trick. Renshaw has facts without love, and look what he built them into." I turned the page. "Tell me about Constance's routes. Start from the beginning. If I write what you saw and I write what I saw, the truth will be somewhere in the overlap."

She talked. I wrote. The lamp burned low and I fed it, and outside the window the orchard stood bare and patient in the moonlight, waiting for spring like everything else in England.

* * *

We worked nine days. Word travels fast in a small town even when everyone pretends it doesn't, and by the fourth day people were leaving things on the doorstep: a remembered date on the back of a grocery list, a route sketched in pencil on butcher's paper, an envelope with no name containing a photograph of a woman I'd only known as *the tall one from Carlisle*, whom I could now, thanks to the photograph, give a face and finally a name — Hartley, Winifred — because her daughter had written on the back, in a child's careful hand, *my mama, taken away for talking*.

Tom Ellery brought me a map of the whole northern network drawn from memory, and we argued about it for an hour over the kitchen table, two people pulling at the same thread from opposite ends until the knot came loose and lay flat and true. His version had the Harrow Ford crossing a mile downstream from mine. We sent Mrs. Pyle's grandson — fourteen, quick, invisible in the way boys that age are — to walk the bank and count the bridges. He came back muddy and triumphant. Tom was wrong by exactly a mile. I wrote my version. Then I wrote Tom's correction in the margin, because a margin note saying *Ellery disputes; ground verified by F. Hobbes' boy, 11 April* was worth more than clean pages. Clean pages are for novels. This was going to be read by committees.

On the seventh day I reached the letter W and stopped being able to write.

Wren, Polly. Nineteen. Messenger between the orchard houses, hired originally to scare crows — that was the story, and it was even mostly true; she had carried notes tucked inside the rags she used for the job. She was the friend who was taken when the informer talked. She had been in the network four months. She had shown me once how to whistle crows down, two notes, low then lower, and she laughed at me for a full minute when all I got was something that sounded like a dying kettle.

I knew what happened to Polly. Everyone in the room knew what happened to Polly, because Tom had found out through a cousin in the transport corps: transferred to a hospital ship, dysentery, dead at Portsmouth, buried in a pauper's plot with a number instead of a stone. Nineteen years old. Scared crows.

My pen hovered over the page and nothing came out of it. Not ink, not words. My hand simply declined.

Nell didn't touch me. She had learned that about me in the hayloft and after — that hands on my shoulders when I'm stuck make the sticking worse. She just pulled her chair around beside mine and said, "Say it out loud first. It's easier going down on paper if it's already been in the air."

"Polly Wren," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable like ice on a puddle. "Dead. Number instead of a stone." And then, because the record deserved more than that and so did she: "She whistled better than anyone I ever met. Two notes. Low then lower."

I wrote it. All of it. The dates, the routes, the crow-rags, the whistle, the number instead of a stone. Grief doesn't get smaller. But it turns out it can be spent, like currency, and this was the only shop that took it.

* * *

On the ninth evening, Mrs. Pyle brought out a bottle of something prewar and cloudy, and we marked the completion of the first draft the way our side had learned to mark victories: quietly, with the curtains drawn, each of us aware that a draft was not a record and a record was not justice and justice, whatever it turned out to be, was still months of committees away. The inquiry had been announced in the papers that week — a public commission into "irregularities of military administration in requisitioned districts," which was official language for *we are going to ask Captain Renshaw to explain himself, gently, in front of witnesses we have chosen*.

Edith was still inside. That was the arithmetic underneath everything. Eleven days of writing, forty-one names sure and nine probable, a map verified by a fourteen-year-old's muddy boots — and Edith Marlowe still slept in a converted granary because the commission hadn't convened yet and Renshaw's resignation hadn't happened yet and mercy in this country moved at the speed of paperwork.

I read the pages aloud that night, start to finish, because Edith had drilled it into me: *a record unspoken is a rumor*. The family sat around my kitchen table — Nell, Mrs. Pyle, Tom, Dr. Kessler, the Hobbes boy sitting on the stairs where he'd been told to stay and listening anyway — and I read for two hours, and when I got to the end I said the closing line I'd written last, the one I'd argued with myself over for a full day:

*"The following account was written from memory by Oscar Whitlock, driver and reader to Mrs. Edith Marlowe, and checked against the independent recollections of those named herein. Where memories disagreed, both versions are given. Where a woman could not be fully identified, she is listed by description rather than omitted. No name in this book was left out for lack of proof. Names were left out only when the war had taken even the proof."*

Silence, after. The good kind. Then old Dr. Kessler took off his glasses, polished them on his scarf, and put them back on, and said, "Read the Wren entry again."

I did. When I finished, he said, "Add the dysentery was contracted at the depot before embarkation, not aboard the ship. The cousin in the transport corps has it wrong. I treated her myself, the week before they moved her. She had it already and hid it so they wouldn't hold her back." He paused, and his voice went thin. "She hid it so they wouldn't delay her run. She thought the run mattered more."

I corrected it. My hand was steady. Nine days of writing had taught my hand things my heart was slower to learn.

Nell walked the doctor home. Through the window I watched them go down the garden path, her hand light between his shoulder blades, and I understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand things at the edge of exhaustion: this was the work now. Not the smuggling, not the clocks and checkpoints and forged ledgers. This. Sitting still long enough to let other people's memory lean against yours until the structure holds.

The knock came at half past ten, when the house should have been empty except for me.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried. Not Nell's rhythm. Not anyone's rhythm from the family. I blew out the lamp before I remembered there was no army within thirty miles anymore, before I remembered that the war was over, that fear was supposed to be discontinued like ration books and blackout curtains.

Through the window I saw the shape of a man on my step, civilian coat, collar up. He knocked again, and then he said my name — my actual name, Oscar Whitlock, the name almost nobody in this town had ever had reason to use — and my blood went cold in a way it hadn't even gone in the records office with the truncheons swinging.

Because there were only two kinds of people who knew that name and had reason to say it at my door after dark.

One of them was in prison.

The other kept a private notebook now, and had lately begun to wonder what it was for.
