## 17. Night at the Records Office

*Oscar*

The forgeries were finished on a Tuesday and we went in on a Thursday, because Nell said Thursday was the night the sentries drank, and Nell had spent six weeks making it her business to know what sentries did after dark.

We told no one. Not Edith—Edith was behind wire by then, and even if she hadn't been, she would have ordered us not to go, which is why we didn't tell her. Not the others in the network; a thing known by four people is a thing known by the army within a day. We told Hobbes, who had given us the schedule in a shaking hand: *burning day is the twenty-ninth, they've requisitioned the incinerator at the tannery, everything from the records office cellar goes into it at dawn.* And then we folded that paper up small and burned it ourselves over the kitchen stove, and watched the words curl, and I thought: this is how it ends for every true thing now. It ends as smoke unless someone carries it.

I carried the forgeries in a false-bottomed bread basket, wrapped in oilcloth like a loaf. Nell carried the tools: a pry bar cut down and wrapped in sacking, a jar of lamp black, two of Mrs. Prewitt's meat pasties because she insisted no one does honest work hungry. The real ledger—the one Renshaw's men had taken from Edith's house in October, the one with sixty-one names in it—I had memorized down to the ink blots. What we were stealing back was paper. But paper was the whole war, as far as I could see it. Men burned paper and called it peace. Men signed paper and called it law. If we swapped ash for ash and kept the truth in our heads and our hands, then Renshaw's fire would be a ceremony performed for an empty church.

"You're doing the face," Nell said. We were walking the long way round, along the millrace, where the dark was thickest and the ice at the edges made a sound like someone whispering through their teeth.

"What face?"

"The one where you're composing your own obituary."

"I'm composing yours," I said. "Yours is shorter. *Died as she lived, eating.*"

She laughed, low, and bumped my shoulder with hers, and I felt it all the way down. That was the trouble with Nell Ashby. She made the terror into something you could carry, because she handed you her half of it and walked close.

The records office had been the corn exchange before the war, which meant it had the one flaw in its architecture that made the whole plan possible: a coal chute at the rear, angled and rusted, disused since the army decided coal was a luxury for occupied towns. Hobbes said the grate at the bottom hadn't been locked since summer because the key was lost and nobody cared. Nobody caring was the only country we were still citizens of.

We waited by the tanner's wall across the lane while a patrol went by—two soldiers, young, one of them singing very quietly to himself, some music-hall thing about a girl who waited. His friend told him to shut up. They didn't shut up. Their voices faded toward the square, and Nell counted to a hundred with her lips moving, and then we crossed.

The chute grate came away easier than it should have, shrieking once on its hinge, and we both froze in the dark like rabbits that have read enough books to know how it goes for rabbits. Nothing moved. Somewhere inside the building a clock ticked—of course it did; the whole town ran on clocks, and I had hidden one myself once, and here I was breaking into a building to the rhythm of another—and after a while the ticking was just the night again and we went down the chute, one after the other, into the cellar that smelled of dust and mildew and the ghost of corn.

* * *

*Nell*

Here is what nobody tells you about being frightened: it makes you precise. My hands, which shook when I signed my own name to nothing more dangerous than a laundry list, went steady as a surgeon's the moment my boots hit that cellar floor. I'd noticed it before—at the checkpoint outside Harrowgate, holding the witness list inside my coat while a corporal read my face like a page he couldn't make out. Fear strips off everything decorative and leaves the working parts.

Oscar lit no lamp. She didn't need one; she'd been in that cellar before, in daylight, under escort, delivering requisition forms, and her memory was the eighth wonder of our small world. She took my sleeve and steered me between crate shapes and past a stack of chairs from the exchange hall, to the far wall where the army kept its confiscated papers in three filing cabinets and a tea chest, guarded by a padlock worth about ninepence.

The pry bar made less noise than I expected. The lock made more. When it hit the floor I picked it up and put it in my pocket, because I have a superstition about leaving pieces of yourself behind, and that night my pocket held a padlock and later it would hold worse, but I'm ahead of myself.

"Which ones?" I whispered.

"Second cabinet. Blue folders." Her voice was barely breath. "They're marked *M*. Everything marked M."

We worked fast, by touch, passing folders hand to hand. Oscar opened each one just enough to feel the shape of it—the handwriting, the weight of pages—because she alone knew which papers were the network's and which were ordinary confiscations, farm deeds and ration complaints, the boring sediment of occupation. Every blue folder that was ours she set in the basket; every replacement forgery she'd written herself, aged with tea and handled until the paper lost its shop-newness, went into the gap. She worked like a woman defusing something. She never hurried. I hurried for both of us, checking the door at the top of the cellar stairs every forty breaths, listening for boots.

Halfway through, she stopped. Held a folder open against her chest in the dark, though neither of us could see a word of it.

"This one's Edith's hand," she said. "This is the original route map. The one from before October." A pause. "Nell, they kept her handwriting in a filing cabinet for four months like it was stock."

"It is stock to them."

"Yes." I heard her swallow. "That's why we're here."

We finished the swap. Sixteen folders out, sixteen folders in, the forgeries arranged with the same dog-eared carelessness so the shelf would look undisturbed to any eye less obsessed than Renshaw's. Oscar closed the cabinet. I rehung the broken hasp as best I could and wired it to look locked. And that should have been the whole of it—in, out, home, dawn burning a lie, victory by breakfast.

Then the door at the top of the cellar stairs opened.

* * *

*Oscar*

Light came down the stairwell in a hard yellow bar, and with it a voice, bored and young: "Who's down there? Who's there, I said?"

Nell's hand found my wrist and gripped it, and I understood the arithmetic instantly and completely. Two ways out. The stairs, lit, occupied, fatal. Or the chute, across the cellar, in the dark, requiring perhaps ten seconds we did not have if the soldier came down.

There was no time to discuss it, and anyway we had discussed it, weeks ago, in the abstract way people discuss drowning. Nell pushed the basket into my arms. All sixteen folders, the whole recovered record, the names of sixty-one women and the roads between them. Then she stepped out of the dark into the edge of the light, deliberately, the way an actor steps into a spotlight, and said in her loudest careless voice:

"Only me. Clerk sent me down for the ration returns. Didn't know His Majesty's army needed a guard on its own paperwork."

It was so good, so easy, so exactly the kind of cheek that disarms rather than alarms, that for one full second I believed it would work. I heard the soldier laugh—nervous, flattered—and I was already moving, basket hugged to my chest, sliding along the wall toward the chute with my heart going like a bird in a box.

Then the second voice came. From the doorway above. Older, unhurried, and known to me in a way that turned my blood to well water.

"Well," said Captain Renshaw. "Now that's interesting."

He had come for the ledger himself. Of course he had. Burning day was coming and Renshaw trusted no one else's eyes. He had come down his own cellar steps at eleven at night with a lantern, and found a courier from a town forty miles away standing in his records office telling jokes to a private.

"Miss Ashby, isn't it," he said. "From Harrowgate way. What a long way from home you are."

"Necessary journey, sir," Nell said, and I could hear it—the wobble underneath the brass, hairline, invisible unless you loved her, and I loved her, so I heard it like a crack across a lake.

"Put the light on her," Renshaw said mildly. "Search her. Mind the stairs, Private, there's a man missing two teeth somewhere who'll tell you cellars are bad luck."

I reached the chute. I got the basket up over the lip, hooked it on the rusted edge, and then I made the mistake that I will answer for as long as I live: I looked back.

* * *

*Nell*

Don't look back. That's the rule, and she broke it, and God forgive me but I loved her for it, because looking back means the person in your arms matters more than the skin you're in, and that's the only religion I ever kept.

What she saw: me turning out my pockets for a private whose hands were shaking, and Renshaw coming down the last steps with the lantern held high, patient as winter. What she saw next happened fast. The private grabbed my arm. I am not large but I am quick, and quick was all I had, so I used it wrong—I kicked the lantern instead of running for the chute, because if I ran for the chute they'd see her, and the lantern went over and the oil caught on the sacking I'd dropped and for about four seconds the cellar was bright orange chaos and everyone was shouting and nobody could see anything, least of all which way anyone had gone.

In those four seconds Oscar went up the chute. I know because I heard the basket scrape, that specific rusty note, and then nothing, which meant she was clear or she was holding her breath, and either way my work was the same: keep their eyes on me.

Renshaw didn't shout. That was the frightening part. In the flare and gutter of the burning sacking he stood still and said, "Hold her," in the tone of a man closing a ledger, and the private held me, and a second soldier clattered down the stairs, and the first blow landed on my shoulder while my arms were pinned, and the second caught my mouth.

I want to say I was brave. I wasn't. I cried out, and then I bit through my lip trying not to cry out again, and I thought about the basket going up the chute and I thought about Oscar's face at the checkpoint in October, and I thought, absurdly, about the pasties, still in my other pocket, crushed to crumbs. Mostly I thought: *count*. Because counting keeps you in your body and out of the panic, and I counted the blows the way I'd counted sentries, seven, eight, the boot catching my ribs, nine, and somewhere in there Renshaw's voice saying, quietly, over the top of it, "Enough. Enough. She's no use to me concussed. Miss Ashby. You'll tell me who you were meeting. Take your time. We have until dawn, and then"—a small pause, the sound of a man consulting a pocket watch—"we have a fire, and everything gets simpler."

They dragged me up the stairs. On the landing, through swelling eyes, I saw the cellar door swing shut on the little fire dying itself out on the wet floor, and I thought about Oscar climbing out into the frozen lane with the whole record in a bread basket, and I laughed, which cost me a tooth, and was worth it.

* * *

*Oscar*

I lay flat in the frozen weeds behind the tanner's wall with the basket under me and blood singing in my ears, and I listened to the muffled sounds of the cellar through stone, and every sound was Nell and none of it was mine to stop.

That is the sentence I have tried to make peace with for years afterward: *none of it was mine to stop.* I could have gone back down. I have played that cellar a thousand times in the dark hours, and in the version where I go back, we are both taken, both beaten, the basket is found, the record burns twice—once as paper and once as us. In no version do I rescue her. There is no version. Some nights I hate the mathematics of it. Most nights I understand that loving someone sometimes means carrying what they bought for you at the price they chose, and honoring the purchase by walking.

I stayed down until the soldiers brought her out. I watched through a knot-hole in the tanner's fence as they loaded her into a lorry—not gently, but not cruelly either; Renshaw had called them off, and there was something in the set of his shoulders I couldn't read then and can read now: a man who had expected to find a conspiracy and had found instead two women and a bread basket, and didn't yet know which was more dangerous.

The lorry pulled away toward the barracks. Its taillight shrank down the road like a spark going up a chimney in reverse, and I lay in the weeds with sixty-one women's names pressed against my ribs, and the cold came up through the ground and into me and I let it, because pain was a form of staying, and staying near her, even a quarter mile near, was all I had.

Then I got up. Because the record doesn't walk itself, and dawn comes whether you are ready or not, and somewhere across town a man named Hobbes knew the schedule and a tannery had an incinerator and at first light Captain Renshaw intended to burn what we had already stolen.

I ran the lanes with the basket, gate to hedge, hedge to wall, the way I'd run mail for five years, invisible by design, and I reached the safehouse with the sky still black and my breath tearing, and Mrs. Prewitt opened the door before I knocked—she'd been awake, they'd all been awake—and she saw my face and knew.

"They've got her," I said. "And tomorrow morning they burn the cellar."

"And what's in the basket?" she asked.

"The truth," I said. "All of it. And now we have to decide, before sunrise, whether we let him burn a lie—or whether we go get Nell back while his fire is busy believing it."
