## 19. Armistice

The bells started before the light did.

I woke in the loft above the printer's shop with Nell's elbow in my ribs and the whole town ringing like a struck anvil, and for one confused second I thought it was a raid—four years of habit dying harder than that. Then I heard the shouting, the bottles, someone playing a concertina badly enough to be a public menace, and I understood.

They've stopped it. It's over.

Nell was already at the window, hair down, looking out at the high street where lamps were being lit at four in the morning by hands too excited to hold a match steady. "Oscar." Her voice was strange. "Oscar, come and see."

I came and saw. Bonfires already going on the green. Soldiers pouring out of the billets like water from a kicked bucket, some of them weeping into their collars, some of them singing, none of them sure what they were supposed to feel about having survived. A woman in the street below had brought out a tray of something and was handing cups to strangers. The war had ended the way a fever breaks—all at once, in the night, leaving everyone sweating and astonished and alive.

"It's over," I said, to hear it said.

"It's over," Nell agreed.

We stood there holding each other's sleeves, not quite celebrating, because we were two people who knew better than most what *over* meant and what it didn't. Over didn't mean Edith Marlowe out of custody. Over didn't mean the ledger safe. Over didn't mean Renshaw gone from our lives like a bad season. Over only meant the guns, and even that had been announced before it happened, so that even peace arrived as a rumor first.

But downstairs, Mr. Prewitt the printer was already pulling his shutter, and he called up through the floor: "Girls! They're saying the town's to be released! The requisition order's rescinded as of noon!"

* * *

The occupation dissolved the way frost does—not dramatically, not all at once, but patch by patch until you looked up and couldn't find where it had been.

By eight o'clock the soldiers were forming columns in the market square, sullen and unformed, being counted by sergeants who kept losing track. By nine, Captain Renshaw's headquarters—the old corn exchange—had its shutters half-closed, and clerks were carrying boxes to a lorry with the particular energy of men who want very much to not be the last one out. By ten, Mrs. Denby had taken down her flag and put up a different flag, and was telling anyone who'd listen that she had never approved of the occupation, not for one minute, not ever, and wasn't it a shame about the Hendersons' boy.

That last bit landed strangely. Private Henderson had spent eleven months billeted in our town writing letters home for other soldiers because he had the best hand in his company, and he'd been killed in September, and nobody had quite known how to grieve a man who was both occupier and boy from two villages over. Now the town didn't have to decide. The armistice decided it for them: everything before November eleventh belonged to the war, and the war was over, and who wanted to think about it?

I walked the high street at noon with the true ledger against my body, wrapped in oilcloth under my coat, as I had every day for weeks, and watched the machinery of the occupation pack itself away. It was obscene how fast it went. Six months of curfews and checkpoints and neighbors lowered to informers, and it all folded up in a morning like a market stall. A corporal was chalking numbers on crates. Two military policemen were taking down the notices from the board outside the exchange, peeling them off in sheets, and the wall beneath was a palimpsest of regulations—NO ASSEMBLY, NO CORRESPONDENCE WITH THE ENEMY, NO SPREAD OF REPORTS LIKELY TO CAUSE ALARM—and behind all of it, older layers, the ghost-edges of posters I remembered: recruitment rallies, war loans, Edith Marlowe's own meetings advertised in her own bold hand before such meetings became crimes.

A soldier caught me watching. Young fellow, cheeks still soft. "Done now, miss," he said, with a grin that wanted forgiving. "All finished."

"Yes," I said. "All finished."

He meant the war. I held the ledger tighter and meant something else entirely, and neither of us said what we meant, which is more or less how the whole business had run from start to finish.

* * *

Edith Marlowe came home at four o'clock in the afternoon, on foot, without escort, carrying her own small case.

Nell and I met her at the gate. I had rehearsed things to say. I had rehearsed them for weeks, standing in visiting rooms across a table scarred with initials, while Edith told me about prison food with the air of a woman reviewing a disappointing hotel. None of the rehearsals survived contact.

She was thinner. That was the shock of it—I had seen her weekly and hadn't let myself see it, the way you don't see a candle burning down when you're watching the flame. The bones of her face had come forward. Her hands, always quick, moved slowly, and when she set down her case to unlatch the gate, she had to steady herself on the post for a moment, and she let me see her do it, which cost her more than the steadying did, I think.

"Well," she said, looking past us at her house. "They've let the garden go entirely."

"Mrs. Hobbes kept an eye on it," Nell said. "Roughly."

"Roughly," Edith agreed, and went up the path, and stood a moment among the ruined roses with her face turned toward the orchard, and I understood she was doing arithmetic—counting what the months had cost, naming what could not be replanted—and then she turned around and she was Mrs. Marlowe again, spine first.

"You'll both come in," she said. "There is a great deal to do and I find I've lost my taste for standing about."

Inside, she sat at her own kitchen table for the first time in five months, and put her hands flat on the wood, and looked at us with those clear gray eyes that had frightened magistrates and comforted frightened women with the same expression.

"Now," she said. "Tell me what state the record is in."

Not *how are you*. Not *what did it cost*. The record first, because the record was how she asked after everything else; the record was the only vocabulary she had for love, and God help me, I understood her completely.

"The originals are safe," I said. "The copies are safe. Everything Renshaw burned was forged, and he doesn't know it yet—or he does know it and can't prove it, which is worse for him." I took the oilcloth bundle from my coat and laid it on the table between us. "And this is mine. Every name. Every route. Every date. Written from memory, checked against what we recovered. It's complete."

Edith looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then she looked at me, and there was something in her face I had never seen there before—not approval, which I'd had often enough, but something rawer, less composed.

"They told me you'd forgotten me," she said quietly. "In there. They liked to tell prisoners things like that. That the network had scattered, that no one was keeping the record, that I'd be released into a world that had decided none of it ever happened." She touched the oilcloth once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "I didn't believe them. But it is another thing altogether to be right."

"You should rest," Nell said, in the gentle voice she used for people she liked, which was a short list, and shorter still among the living.

"I shall rest when this is in a government archive with three copies and a catalog number," Edith said, "and not before."

* * *

The town celebrated all evening and well into the dark, and I went out among them because Nell made me, because she said I'd forgotten there was anything to celebrate, which stung mostly because it was true.

The green was a sea of fire and fiddle music. Men from the works had rolled a piano out of the Legion hall and someone was murdering popular songs on it to general delight. Old Mr. Cadwallader, who had buried two sons and not smiled since 1916, was dancing with both daughters of the Postmaster, badly, laughing. The bellringers rang until their hands gave out and other hands replaced them. Down by the gatehouse, somebody had gotten the orchard clock's face lit with a hurricane lamp so the whole crowd could watch it strike seven, and when it did, they cheered the clock. Cheered a clock. Four years of measuring time by telegrams and casualty lists, and here they were cheering a machine for doing nothing but ticking, for letting time pass gently, the old way, the way it used to before.

Nell bought me toffee from a stall and we ate it leaning against the war memorial that wasn't a war memorial yet, just a plinth with a tarpaulin, waiting to learn what names would go on it.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what."

"Standing in the middle of a party conducting an audit." She bumped my shoulder with hers. "Go on, then. Say it. What's wrong with tonight."

Nothing was wrong with tonight. That was what was wrong with tonight. The guns had stopped and the town had survived and Edith was home eating toast in her own kitchen, and the ledger was whole, and Nell's split lip from the records office had healed to a silver thread, and by any honest accounting I was the luckiest woman in three counties. And underneath the celebration, cold as a coin at the bottom of a well, sat the thing I could not say out loud in a crowd:

So what happens now? Who reads it?

A ledger in a drawer is paper. Names recited to a kitchen are gossip. We had saved the truth entire, at the cost of a safehouse burned and Tom Weller taken and Nell beaten nearly senseless on a stairwell, and the truth sat in oilcloth against my ribs, and the men who ran the country after the war would be the same men, or worse ones, who had run it during, and they had historians on staff. I had seen the historian's draft. I knew exactly how the story went when other people wrote it: the war won, the home front loyal, the agitators regrettable and marginal and, where convenient, imaginary. Forty-one women. Forty-one names I carried in my skull like coins in a sock. And nowhere, in any official account, a line for any of them.

"Oscar," Nell said. "You're a thousand miles off."

"Two counties," I said. "At most."

She studied my face in the firelight. She had gotten good at reading me—months of checkpoints and haylofts will do that—and whatever she found there made her take my arm properly, and steer me out of the crowd, up the lane, to where the noise fell away and the orchard began and the gatehouse clock ticked in the dark like a patient heart.

"Say it," she said.

"Everyone keeps asking what we'll do now the war's over," I said. "Nobody's asked what becomes of the record. Edith says archives. Archives take years, Nell. Archives take committees. Meanwhile there'll be books written—there already are books being written—and in those books, none of this happened. The requisition happened, maybe, a paragraph, administrative detail. The women—" I stopped. The firelight was visible over the rooftops, orange, festive. "Tom Weller is in a cell and the official account will say he was a common criminal. Mrs. Okafor's house burned and it will say accidental fire. You were beaten half-dead and if it appears at all it will appear as a burglary."

"So we write ours first."

"We wrote ours first. We have ours first. Paper in a drawer, Nell. That's not the same as it being *true* in the way that counts. True in the record. True where the men look."

Nell was quiet a moment. Above us the stars were out, indifferent, the same stars that had watched the checkpoint at Marley Ford and the night run to the printer's and every frightened mile between.

"There's talk," she said finally. "Prewitt heard it through his London trade people. That there'll be inquiries. After wars there always are—they inquire into how it was run, who profited, who was mistreated. Public. With witnesses sworn." She turned to look at me. "With transcripts, Oscar. Official ones. Printed ones. The kind the men have to read because they're bound and shelved and can't be unwritten."

Hope is a humiliating thing. I had kept it strangled for months, practical, and here it stood up inside me like a fool at a funeral, and I hated how fast my heart went.

"If there's an inquiry," I said slowly, "into the administration of the home front. Into the requisition. Into the treatment of civilians under military authority—"

"Then somebody will have to testify," Nell said. "About sedition charges against women running a soup kitchen network. About letters seized. About ledgers burned. About a captain who tortured a town for six months and got a commendation." She shrugged, elaborately casual, watching me sideways. "Somebody with an unusually good memory."

I stood there in the dark with the clock ticking and the town roaring happily behind us, and I felt the ledger against my ribs not as a burden for the first time in longer than I could remember, but as an argument. As evidence. As a thing built for exactly this.

"They'll destroy him," I said. "Or he'll destroy himself first. Either way he won't come quietly."

"No," Nell agreed. "He won't."

"And Edith shouldn't testify—she's known, she's discredited by association, they'll say she's vindictive. It has to be someone they never saw coming. Someone invisible." I heard my own voice do something strange on that last word, and Nell heard it too, and grinned.

"Somebody like that," she said. "If only we knew one."

Overhead, the clock gathered itself and struck the quarter hour into the empty dark, unhurried, certain, keeping time whether anyone listened or not. Behind us the fires burned and the town sang, and ahead of us somewhere in London a committee was being drafted and a historian was sharpening his eraser.

"Nell," I said. "When the summons comes—"

"When it comes," she said, taking my hand, "we'll be ready."

It was not quite a promise. It was better than a promise. It was a plan, and I had learned from Mrs. Marlowe that a plan is just hope with its boots on, and mine were already laced.

Three days later, a letter arrived at the printer's shop, franked by His Majesty's Government, requesting the attendance of persons with knowledge of events in this district before a Commission of Inquiry sitting in London in the new year.

Prewitt read it twice and handed it to me without a word, and his hands were shaking, and so, when I held it, were mine.
