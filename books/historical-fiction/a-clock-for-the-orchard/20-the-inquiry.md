## 20. The Inquiry

They held it in the county assize hall, the same room where Oscar Whitlock had once stood accused of stealing a loaf of bread at fourteen years old, though the record of that had been expunged and no one living remembered it but her. She remembered everything now. That was rather the point.

The room had been dressed for gravity. Bunting left over from Armistice hung limp above the judge's bench, and someone had arranged potted palms along the walls in an attempt at dignity that only made the space feel like a railway waiting room. Rows of benches filled with townsfolk, journalists from three counties, two men in war-office grey who sat together and wrote nothing, which told Oscar they were there to listen for something worth not writing down.

Edith Marlowe sat in the second row, hands folded, hat pinned at her best angle. Diminished, the doctors said. Her hands trembled when she thought no one watched. But she had dressed as if for a committee meeting, because that was how you told the world you expected to be taken seriously, and Oscar loved her for it with a fierceness that startled her.

Nell sat on her other side, close enough that their shoulders touched. Nell's ribs still ached from the records office; Oscar could feel the flinch she'd learned to hide whenever she shifted on the bench.

"Whatever happens," Nell murmured, "you've rehearsed this."

"I've rehearsed being arrested after it," Oscar said. "That part I know by heart too."

"Oscar." Nell's hand found hers, brief, warm, gone again before anyone could remark on it. "Say the names."

* * *

Captain Renshaw testified first.

He had chosen his uniform carefully—pressed, ribboned, but not grandly so. A man aware of how he would look beside the bunting. He answered the chairman's questions with the unhurried clarity of a officer giving evidence about troop movements: dates, orders received, procedures followed. Every word was true. That was his genius, Oscar realized, listening. He had never needed to lie. He had simply decided which truths deserved to exist.

Asked whether materials had been seized from private homes, he confirmed it, citing Defence of the Realm regulations with the section numbers ready. Asked whether any persons had been detained without charge, he said that certain individuals had been *held for interview*, a phrase he repeated twice until it sounded like something out of a manual. Asked whether Mrs. Edith Marlowe of Marlowe Orchard had been among them, he paused—a small pause, expertly measured—and said that Mrs. Marlowe had been treated throughout with every consideration her age and sex required.

Her age and sex required. In the second row, Edith's spine did not move. Her hands, folded in her lap, went very still, the stillness of a woman holding herself to her chair by will alone.

Asked whether documents pertaining to the anti-conscription network had been destroyed, Renshaw said: "Certain papers were disposed of according to schedule, sir, once their evidentiary value was exhausted."

Evidentiary value exhausted. Oscar wrote the phrase in the margin of her notebook, because she had learned from novelists and from Captain Renshaw both that language was a weapon and you should always capture the enemy's ammunition.

Then came the half-truths, delivered like full ones. He had not burned anything, precisely; he had authorized disposal. He had not threatened anyone; he had *invited cooperation*. He had never heard of a ledger kept at Marlowe Orchard recording names, routes, safehouses—"If such a document existed, sir, it would have been my duty to preserve it"—and the room believed him, or wanted to, because he was clean-shaven and steady-voiced and the alternative to believing him was admitting what the last eighteen months had actually been.

The chairman asked if he wished to add anything.

Renshaw turned then—not to the bench, but to the room, letting his gaze travel the rows as though looking for friends. It found Oscar and stopped. One heartbeat. Two. A man greeting an old question.

"Only that history is often written hastily," he said, "in the first grief of peace. I trust this inquiry will prefer accuracy to sentiment."

He stepped down and walked past the press bench without looking at anyone, and Oscar understood suddenly, completely, that he was not defending himself. He was defending the record. He was testifying on behalf of the version of events that was already being typed in London, and his careful voice was simply the public rehearsal of it.

Which meant that whatever she did next would be done against a finished draft.

* * *

They called her after lunch.

"Oscar Whitlock," the clerk read out, and she rose, and the name sounded strange spoken aloud in this room, as strange as it ever did, because she had spent thirty-one years making sure nobody needed to say it.

She took the stand. She gave her name, her age, her occupation—*secretary and companion to Mrs. E. Marlowe*, which was true and had always been true and had never once been the whole of it. She declined the chair offered and stood, because her mother had taught her that standing made people listen with their spines.

The counsel for the inquiry, a tired man named Prosser who had been polite to her in the corridor and would be polite while dismantling her if instructed, began gently.

"You were employed at Marlowe Orchard during the requisition."

"I was."

"And you are familiar with the documents seized—or alleged to have been seized—from the household?"

"I am familiar with one document in particular."

A stir, small, like wind finding a gap in a window. The two men in grey stopped not-writing.

Prosser glanced at his papers. "Captain Renshaw has testified that no such document was recovered."

"Captain Renshaw is correct," Oscar said. "It was never recovered. It was moved. Repeatedly. And when moving it became more dangerous than keeping it, I committed it to memory and let him burn a copy we had prepared for exactly that purpose."

Silence now, real silence, the kind that presses on the ears. Somewhere behind her a journalist uncapped a pen with unnecessary noise.

"You are telling this inquiry," Prosser said carefully, "that you can produce the contents of a document that no longer exists."

"No, sir. I'm telling this inquiry that the document exists in its most secure form." She looked at the chairman directly, an older man with a face like a closed ledger himself. "I have it by heart."

Someone laughed—one short syllable from the back—and then stopped laughing, because she did not smile.

"The ledger recorded the orchard network," she said. "Forty-one women across five parishes. Names, routes, meeting places, the houses that sheltered couriers moving information the government called sedition and I call the truth. Most of those women cannot read or write. Their letters were written for them by others, or by me, and their words traveled from hand to hand for eighteen months through a town under military occupation. Captain Renshaw disposed of the paper. He could not dispose of me, though he tried several methods, which I will detail if asked."

"Miss Whitlock—"

"You may call me Mrs., if it simplifies the record. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that I am going to recite the names now, all forty-one, and I ask that they be entered into the transcript exactly as I say them, spelling included, because some of these women have never once seen their own names printed anywhere, and it seems right that the first place should be the official record of their country."

The chairman leaned forward. "Miss—Mrs. Whitlock. This inquiry has procedures regarding—"

"Yes, sir. Procedures." She said it the way Renshaw said *evidentiary value*. "I have listened to procedures all morning. I'll only take a few minutes. I've been carrying these names for over a year, and I promise you they weigh less than what you're carrying if you refuse to hear them."

And she began.

"Agnes Tiller, of Tiller's Farm, Mill Lane parish—T-I-L-L-E-R. Ruth Ellery, weaver, Cross Street, known on the routes as *the wren*. Hetty Sorrell and her daughter May, who ran messages between the mill and the chapel and were seventeen and fifteen when they started and neither of them ever caught..."

She did not hurry. That was the discipline of it, the craft of it—the same skill she had used reading letters aloud to men who could not read, the slowing down, the giving each word its full weight. She spelled the hard names. She gave the parish, the trade, the route number where she knew it. Somewhere around the eleventh name the shuffling in the benches stopped. Around the twentieth, the journalists stopped writing and started listening, because there is a difference between notes taken for a story and a thing heard.

"...Dora Pask, who hid leaflets in her loaves and lost her oven license for it. Widow Crane, sixty-eight, who said to tell you, if it ever came to this, that she was too old to be frightened and had enjoyed the scandal immensely. Lottie Hobbes—" and here her voice did not change at all, though she saw the town clerk's son in his uniform at the back of the room, saw him go white—"Lottie Hobbes, who carried the northern route and was taken in March of this year and held nine days without charge, and whose father sits behind you, gentlemen, and has not looked up since I began."

Clerk Hobbes did not look up. But he did not leave either. That was its own testimony.

"...Vera Ashby of Fenwick, cousin to Nell Ashby, who sits beside Mrs. Marlowe and who was beaten nearly to death preserving this record, and who is not ashamed to be named here, and neither am I. And Edith Marlowe, of Marlowe Orchard, who built the network, who taught half this county that a woman's opinion is not a rumor but a fact, and who is sitting in the second row in her good hat, and who told me once that invisibility is a skill, not an identity, and I have come today to give the skill back."

Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one.

She stopped. The room stayed quiet a moment longer, the way a congregation does, and then the sound came back into it—pens, breath, a woman in the third row crying without embarrassment.

The chairman removed his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. When he spoke, his voice had lost its procedural polish.

"Mrs. Whitlock. Are these names... verifiable?"

"They are verifiable against the seized correspondence stored at the county records office, third floor, east annex, crates eleven through nineteen—which Captain Renshaw scheduled for destruction four days from now, believing the ledger burned meant the proof burned with it. I have the schedule. I have witnesses. And I have," she added, taking the folded pages from her coat, the transcription she and Nell had written out in fair hand over six weeks of nights, "a copy. Entered into evidence, please, before anyone's evidentiary value gets exhausted."

She handed it down. The clerk took it as though it might ignite.

At the back of the room, very quietly, Captain Renshaw put on his cap and left. He did not run. Men like him never ran. But Oscar watched the door close behind him and understood that she had just seen a man discover, publicly, in real time, that the story had escaped his custody.

The chairman cleared his throat. "This inquiry will adjourn until tomorrow morning. Mrs. Whitlock—you will be available for further questioning."

"Sir," Oscar said, gathering her notebook, "I have been available my entire life. Nobody thought to ask."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold and cold. Nell was waiting on the steps, and beside her Edith, and beside Edith a knot of women Oscar recognized from the ledger itself—Agnes Tiller in her market coat, Dora Pask still smelling faintly of flour, Widow Crane looking genuinely disappointed there hadn't been more scandal.

Edith took Oscar's arm. Her grip was stronger than the doctors promised.

"Forty-one," she said. "You said all forty-one."

"All forty-one. Spelling included."

Edith was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone she reserved for committee resolutions: "Tomorrow they'll try to make it about you. Your character, your motives, your—" a small wave of the trembling hand, "—your associations. They always do. Be ready."

"I've been ready for years, Mrs. Marlowe."

"Edith," she corrected, as she had corrected a hundred times.

They came down the steps together, and at the bottom a reporter stepped into their path—young, ink-stained, breathless, notebook already open.

"Mrs. Whitlock—miss—where did you learn all those names? How does a person carry a thing like that?"

Oscar considered the question. Behind the young man, the assize hall windows glowed with late light, and somewhere inside it the clerk was entering forty-one names into the official transcript, ahead of the historians, ahead of Renshaw's friends in grey, ahead of everyone.

"Practice," she said. "I read aloud to people who couldn't read. You learn what a name weighs when somebody trusts you to carry it."

The reporter scribbled fast. Then, without looking up: "One more. There's talk the war office has already got a draft account of all this. Finished. Ready to print. That the inquiry's testimony won't matter because the book's already written."

Nell's hand found Oscar's. Edith's grip tightened on her arm.

"Then they'd better print quickly," Oscar said, smiling at the young man with all her teeth. "Because I've got another forty-one names, and the minutes of every meeting, and a list of every order Captain Renshaw signed—and I haven't even stood up yet."

The reporter grinned, and wrote that down too, and somewhere across the square, in a first-floor window of the county records office, a light was burning late—crates eleven through nineteen stacked beneath it, four days remaining, and someone standing watch who was not on anyone's schedule.
