## 1. The Question Too Many

The ink froze faster than I could warm it.

I kept a dish of coals beneath the drafting table, and still the black went sluggish in the pen, gathering at the nib like a thing reluctant to leave the bottle. Six years of winter mornings in the workshop had taught me every trick — breathe on the plate, hold the pen against your wrist to keep it supple, work in short strokes before the line could thicken — but December of 1746 seemed determined to outlast my cunning. The cold came up through the flagstones and down through the roof slates, and it sat in the room like an unpaid debt.

I bent over the border plate and drew the River Wend one more time.

Not the river as the crown's cartographers had drawn it, fat and convenient, swinging east to swallow three villages that had never owed it anything. My master's river. The true course he'd walked in hip-deep water with his instruments wrapped in oilcloth and his teeth chattering through his beard, cursing the marsh and loving it too. Elias Hartwell had died for that line, though I did not yet know it, that last morning in October when I laid out the plate and saw what he had done.

Now I was finishing it. The final border plates, eleven of twelve complete, each one a small heresy rendered in iron-gall ink on copper so fine you'd need a jeweler's loupe to see the difference from the crown version — unless you knew where to look, and someone, somewhere, clearly knew where to look, because Elias was dead and men in garrison coats had begun asking after his records.

"Ansel." Mrs. Quill's voice, low, from the doorway between the workshop and the kitchen. "She's come early."

My pen stopped mid-stroke. A bead of ink swelled at the nib and I touched it away with blotting paper, careful as a surgeon.

"Early," I said. "Census takers aren't due till Thursday."

"She's due now, by the look of her. Standing at our gate with her book under her arm like a psalter." Mrs. Quill wiped her hands on her apron, which she only did when frightened, though nothing about her face admitted fear. Thirty years keeping house in other women's homes before she ran from the last of them — no one asked her why and she never said, but she flinched at magistrates' letters and she could smell officialdom the way horses smell weather. "You want me to answer the door?"

"No." I set down the pen. Under the table, where my skirts — my breeches, rather; six years of breeches, bound flat and buttoned high, until I sometimes forgot they were a costume — pressed against the table leg, I made myself take one slow breath. "No, Mrs. Quill. I'm the apprentice. It should be me."

"And if she asks questions?"

"I'm very good at answers."

That much was true, and it was the whole of my life: I was good at answers because I had been composing them since I was eleven years old, when my father put me in my dead brother's coat and told me that girls couldn't inherit apprenticeships, but Ansel could. Ansel Hartwell. I had worn the name so long it fit better than my own skin. The girl I had been existed now only in a locked box at the back of my mind, next to the memory of my mother's hands, and I did not open the box. Not even for myself.

Mrs. Quill held my gaze a moment longer, then nodded once and withdrew to the kitchen, where the kettle was already singing its small domestic lie: all is well here, all is ordinary, come in, sit down, have tea.

* * *

Verity Cole did not look like a census taker.

She looked like a schoolmistress who had been disappointed by her pupils and had drawn conclusions about humanity generally. She stood in the doorway shaking sleet from a gray traveling cloak, of middling height, perhaps five-and-twenty, with brown hair scraped into a knot so severe it seemed an act of discipline rather than fashion. She carried herself very straight, the way people do who have been taught that stooping invites dismissal. Her eyes were the trouble. They were pale gray and entirely unromantic, and they moved over the workshop the way my master's calipers moved over a drawing — measuring, checking, finding the error.

"You'll be the apprentice," she said. Not a question. She consulted a leather folio. "Ansel Hartwell. Apprentice to Elias Hartwell, surveyor and mapmaker, deceased this past October."

"That's correct, madam."

"Deceased how?"

"The marsh fever." The lie came smooth as varnish. "He surveyed the Wendlow marshes through September and took the ague. It carried him off inside the week."

"Mm." She wrote something. Her handwriting, glimpsed upside down, was small and merciless. "And you continued the workshop alone?"

"With the household's help. We're engravers and printers as well as surveyors. Tam does the plates." I nodded toward the corner bench, where Tam looked up from his burnisher and gave the census woman his open, unguarded smile. He was forty and had the face of a boy and the arms of a smith, and he had not heard a word we said. He heard everything else, though. He read lips across a room, read moods through walls, read me more truly than anyone alive. Now his eyes narrowed slightly, reading the set of my shoulders, and he turned back to his work with the deliberate calm of a man choosing not to look.

"And the housekeeper?"

"Mrs. Quill. And the widow Hartwell, my master's mother, upstairs. She's eighty-one and sleeps most of the day."

"Four souls." She wrote it. "Then let us proceed, Mr. Hartwell."

*Mr.* The word landed in my chest the way it always did, familiar as a key turning. I showed her the ledger of births and households as best I could reconstruct it — Elias had never married, no children, the workshop registered in his name since 1731. She checked entries against her folio. She asked the parish for confirmation of the death, noted that the burial record gave *marsh fever* in the curate's own hand. All routine. All survivable. I felt the dread in me loosen by degrees, the way a knot eases when you stop pulling at it.

She accepted tea from Mrs. Quill. She complimented the fire. She even glanced at the framed map of the county road on the wall and said, without inflection, "Fine work."

And then, closing her folio, rising, gathering her cloak — all the gestures of departure, every signal that the interview was over and the machine of government satisfied — she paused at the door and turned back.

"One question more, Mr. Hartwell. It's a formality. The commission requires verification of household continuity across the war years." Her voice remained perfectly level, perfectly bored. "Elias Hartwell had sons, I believe? The registry of 1740 lists two boys in this household. Apprentices."

The coal dish ticked as the fire shifted.

There had never been two boys. There had been one boy — me — entered in the 1740 register twice, once under my name and once under the name of a cousin who had died of smallpox in 1739 and whose existence Elias had borrowed, with a wink and a forged entry, to pad the workshop's labor allocation. It was a fraud worth perhaps two shillings a year in tax relief. It was also, if anyone pulled the thread, a fraud that invited closer looking, and closer looking was the one thing I could not survive.

"Elias had one son," I heard myself say. "Josiah. He was apprenticed alongside me until the fever took him — the same fever, the same autumn. You'll find his burial in the parish book. My master never recovered from it. He went back to the marshes partly to be rid of his grief, I think."

Every word was prepared. I had prepared them years ago, rehearsed them in the dark, because a lie you build once and never maintain is a lie that collapses. Josiah's grave was real. The curate's hand was real. Even the grief was real — Elias had grieved, though not for the boy the world believed in.

Verity Cole listened to all of it with her head tilted slightly, the way one listens to a clock, counting.

"Yes," she said at last. "So I see."

She settled her cloak. She thanked Mrs. Quill for the tea. At the door she paused again — and this time the pause had weight, had mass, and I felt it press against the room like a change in the weather.

"Your master's maps are known in London," she said. "His border work especially. It would be a loss to the crown if the workshop's plates were scattered or sold after his death. I trust they remain... intact?"

"They remain where he left them," I said. "Under lock."

"Naturally." Something moved behind the gray eyes — not suspicion exactly, or not only. Recognition, maybe. The look of one performer watching another and wondering how long the play has been running. "Good day, Mr. Hartwell."

The door closed. Her footsteps crunched down the frozen lane and faded.

For a long moment none of us moved. Then Tam crossed the room and put his hand flat against the small of my back — his way of asking *are you hurt* — and I realized my knees were shaking.

* * *

That night I could not sleep. I climbed to the loft above the workshop with a candle and unlocked the cabinet where the atlas lay in its oiled canvas, and I told myself I was only checking the plates against damp. That was even true. But my hands went where my dread pointed them, past the finished plates, to the half-bound folio itself, to the spine where the stitching had always seemed to me slightly wrong — too tight, too deliberate, the work of a man who sewed bindings for thirty years and did not make mistakes.

I cut the thread.

Beneath the false backing, sewn flat against the boards, was a sealed packet of oilskin. Inside the oilskin, a single sheet of survey paper, folded in quarters, and a scrap of writing in Elias's cramped hand, dated the third of October — three days before he died:

*They know the border is wrong and they mean it to stay wrong. If I die before the atlas is done, the truth dies in these marshes with me. Whoever finds this — finish it. Trust the benchmarks, not the men.*

The sheet inside was the Wendlow border, drawn in his final hand, and it was not the border on any map I had ever seen, crown or otherwise.

I sat in the freezing loft with my candle guttering and understood, slowly, the way ice understands the river: the work I loved was not a map. It was testimony. And I was holding it.
