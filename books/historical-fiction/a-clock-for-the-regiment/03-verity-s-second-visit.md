## 3. Verity's Second Visit

Verity Cole had verified four hundred and eleven households since the frost came, and she could count on one hand the ones that had told her the truth.

It was not that people lied to census takers, exactly. They lied to the crown, to the tax rolls, to the militia lists — and a census taker was merely the crown's hand at the door, easy to resent and easier to deceive. But most lies were small and lazy. A grandmother struck from the record because her pension would cost the parish. An extra lodger unmentioned. A trade softened from *smuggler* to *cooper*. Verity had learned to hear lies the way a farrier hears lameness in a horse's walk: not by looking for it, but by noticing the absence of sound where a step should fall.

The Hartwell workshop rang with absence.

She had thought about it for nine days. Nine days of ledgers and lamp oil and the particular misery of February roads, and still the workshop kept surfacing in her mind at odd hours — while she ate, while she copied names into the fair book, while she lay awake in the inn at Crossford listening to the wind worry the shutters. *How many sons did Elias Hartwell have?* She had asked that question in thirty households where a master's family was in question, and it had never once mattered. At the Hartwell door it had landed like a stone dropped down a well, and Ansel Hartwell — if that was his name; the register said so, but registers said many things — had answered too quickly and then not quickly enough, filling the silence after with talk of ink and drying times, as though a man's sons could be counted the way one counts plates.

And there was the other thing. The thing she had been sent for.

Verity rode out of Crossford before noon with her commission papers in her satchel and a second document beneath them that no commission authorized: a copy of Elias Hartwell's death registry entry, pulled from the parish book under pretense of routine verification. *Died of marsh fever, attended by none.* The dates troubled her. Hartwell had returned from the border survey on the fourth of October. The registry recorded his death on the fifth. Marsh fever took days to kill a man, usually a week or more of worsening — it did not strike a surveyor down between one sunset and the next, unless he had carried it already, burning, through his final days in the field. And if he had been that ill, why had he gone into the marsh at all? Why had no one fetched a physician? Why did the garrison's requisition logs show a rider dispatched from the fort to Hartwell's last camp *two days before* the surveyor was reported missing?

She had asked herself these questions in the dark of the inn and then asked them again in daylight, when they seemed less like the fancies of a tired woman and more like the shape of something real. Her superiors in the Survey Office believed the matter simple: Hartwell had drawn things he should not have, the drawings had passed into hands they should not have reached, and Verity Cole was to go into the border country, verify the household, catalogue what remained of his papers, and report anything irregular directly to Major Hesse at the garrison. *Irregular.* The word had a clean bureaucratic ring to it. What it meant, she understood now, was *anything that might reach an inquiry.*

They had not sent her to find the truth. They had sent her to bury it.

She told herself, riding the frozen lane toward the workshop, that she was doing only her duty. She told it to herself the way one tells a story twice to see if it holds.

* * *

The workshop smelled the same as it had nine days past — hide glue and lampblack, wet paper and wood smoke — but today the smell was layered with others: onions, bay, the sweetness of dough rising somewhere near the back. The household was preparing, as every household was preparing, for the verification. A thin woman with grey-shot hair and quick hands — the housekeeper, Mrs. Quill, according to the ledger, though the name sat oddly on her, like a coat borrowed from a smaller sister — met Verity at the door and took her wet cloak without being asked.

"Miss Cole. We hoped the roads would keep you longer."

"I'm sturdier than the roads, Mrs. Quill."

"So are most things, worse luck." The woman's smile was genuine and guarded at once, a rare and useful combination, and she led Verity through to the long workroom where the apprentices worked at sloped benches beneath windows glazed with diamond panes.

There was only the one apprentice. He sat with his back half to the door, bent over a copperplate, and did not rise until Mrs. Quill said his name — *Ansel* — with a slight emphasis that made the boy's shoulders tighten before he turned.

"Master Cole." He rose and bowed, and Verity performed the arithmetic she had performed nine days ago and set aside: the boy was seventeen at most, fine-boned beneath the ink-stained apron, and he stood the way people stand who have practiced standing differently. His voice was low and even. His hands, when he gestured to a chair, were steady. Everything about him was correct, and everything about him was effort.

"Your household has been entered in the fair book," Verity said, unfolding her papers on the bench he cleared for her. "I'm required to verify entries against persons present. Names, ages, trades."

"You've come all this way twice for five names."

"The crown is thorough."

"The crown," said Mrs. Quill, setting down the tea tray, "has never once mended a fence in this parish."

"Quill." The apprentice's voice was soft, a warning.

But Verity found she liked the housekeeper for it, and she let the moment breathe before she opened the fair book. "Elias Hartwell, master of the household, deceased the fifth of October. Widow, Margaret Hartwell, aged sixty-one. Housekeeper, Bridget Quill, aged forty-four. Engraver, Tam Norry, aged thirty—" She paused. "Deaf, the parish note says. Can he confirm his own entry?"

"He confirms it by nodding when I say his name," said Ansel. "He's in the cellar, cutting a plate. Shall I fetch him?"

"In a moment." Verity let her finger rest on the page. "The entry that interests the office is the apprentice. Ansel Hartwell, aged nineteen, apprenticed to Elias Hartwell, mapmaker, six years past. Kin?"

The silence was very slightly too long. Not empty — measured. She could feel the boy deciding, could almost see the scales behind his eyes.

"No kin," he said. "My master took me on when I was thirteen. From the foundling rolls."

"Foundling rolls," Verity repeated, and wrote nothing, which was its own kind of lie, and she knew it. "Then you'll forgive the question, but the office will ask it whether I do or not: how many sons did Elias Hartwell have?"

The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere below the floorboards, faintly, came the rhythmic scrape of a burin on copper.

"One," said Ansel Hartwell. "One son. Died in infancy, before my time."

"And yet," Verity said, lifting her eyes to his, "the parish register of the neighbouring parish — the one your master's own family kept their books in — records no son born to Elias Hartwell at all. Only a daughter."

She had planned the sentence on the ride and rehearsed it through three frozen fields, and now that she had said it she watched it land. It did not land the way the question had landed nine days ago, like a stone down a well. It landed the way a hand lands on a shoulder. The boy went very still, and Mrs. Quill set down the teapot with a click, and Verity understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that she had just done something irrevocable — that she had stepped off the road her commission travelled and onto another road entirely, unpaved, unmapped.

"That would be an error in the parish books," Ansel said quietly. "Books err."

"They do." Verity closed the fair book. "Often at the crown's convenience."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then the widow, Margaret Hartwell, appeared in the doorway — Verity had not heard her come, and wondered if anyone ever did — and looked at the three of them standing around the tea like conspirators around a body.

"You'd best drink that before it stews," the old woman said. "And you'd best say what you came to say, miss, because we've all got work, and lying takes it out of a person worse than labour does."

* * *

So Verity said it.

She waited until the tea was poured and the widow had seated herself by the stove with the air of a woman settling in for a story she had already paid for, and she chose her words the way she had been taught to choose them in the Survey Office: precisely, and with an eye to deniability.

"My commission covers two matters. The first is the verification you've just endured. The second is not written in any order I can show you." She drew the registry copy from her satchel and laid it flat on the bench between them. "Elias Hartwell died on the fifth of October. The garrison at Marren Fort dispatched a rider to his survey camp on the second. Your master's death is recorded as fever. I have seen marsh fever. It does not kill in a day unless it has been killing for a week — and a man dying for a week does not run benchmarks in standing water. Someone knew where he was before anyone admits having known. Someone reached him. And the office that employs me has shown no interest whatsoever in asking who."

Ansel's face had gone white to the lips, but his eyes — her eyes, Verity corrected herself, whoever's eyes they were — stayed on hers without flinching. "You're saying the garrison killed him."

"I'm saying the garrison wanted something he had, and that whatever questions should have followed his death were stopped before they started. I'm saying that when I reported the discrepancy in the dates to my superiors, I received within the week a private instruction: catalogue the Hartwell effects, seal anything relating to the border survey, and forward it all to Major Hesse. Not to the Office. To Hesse. And I was instructed to certify, in writing, that nothing of cartographic significance remained in this workshop." She held the old woman's gaze, then the apprentice's. "I have not certified it. I am telling you instead, which is likely the stupidest thing I have done in eleven years of crown service, and I would take it kindly if someone in this room told me why I shouldn't have."

The fire popped. Down in the cellar the burin had gone silent — Tam Norry, listening after all, or simply resting his tools.

"Why?" It was Ansel who spoke, barely above a whisper. "Why tell us? You could seal everything. You could take the atlas and be done."

"Because I read the atlas," Verity said.

Nine days ago, cataloguing, she had opened the great folio that lay on the master's bench under its dust cloth, and she had seen the border. Not the border of the crown's map, the one hanging in every schoolroom and garrison hall, the tidy line that ran east of the marshes and put the ford at Marren comfortably inside the king's territory. Hartwell's border ran west. West of the marsh, west of the ford, drawn in his own meticulous hand, benchmarked and triangulated and dated — dated three days before he died — and it moved the ford, and everything the ford commanded, out of the garrison's jurisdiction entirely.

A man could be killed for an ink line. Men had been.

"Elias Hartwell mapped the truth," Verity said, "and the truth contradicts a map the garrison has spent four years and a great deal of money defending. If his version reaches an inquiry, commanders fall. So they sent me — a woman with a reputation for exactness and no family worth blackmailing — to gather it all quietly and lose it quietly, and I sat in my inn two nights ago with my pen dipped and my certificate blank, and I found I could not write the word *nothing*." She looked down at her own hands, ink-stained at the right fingertips, the same as any mapmaker's. "I have been performing a role I did not choose since I was twenty-two years old. Exact Miss Cole, reliable Miss Cole, the Office's good instrument. I am tired of being well-used by men who burn what they cannot file."

The widow made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly suppressed. "Six years I've watched a girl pretend to be a boy in her dead husband's workshop," said Margaret Hartwell, "and now the crown sends a woman pretending to be obedient, and you sit at my table wondering why none of us trust each other." She raised her cup. "Drink your tea, Miss Cole. You're among colleagues."

Verity felt the words go through her like cold water. She turned to the apprentice, who had not moved, whose secret now stood naked between them and who seemed, strangely, less afraid than relieved — as though the pretending had weighed more than the exposure.

"You knew," Ansel said slowly. "When you asked about sons. You knew before you came back."

"I suspected. Now I know. And knowing it, I am dangerous to you — and to myself, because Hesse expects my certificate by month's end, and when he doesn't get it, or gets the wrong one, he will send someone who doesn't ask questions over tea."

"Then help us," Ansel said. It was not quite a plea. It was closer to an offer, extended the way one extends a hand across a survey line. "There are things in the atlas you haven't seen. Things my master sealed inside the binding. If what you say is true, then neither of us can finish our parts alone. I need time and someone watching the road. You need evidence that won't burn."

Outside, the light was failing early as winter light does, blue shadows pooling in the lane. Verity rose, gathered her papers, and paused at the door with her cloak half-fastened.

"I'll come again Thursday," she said. "Officially, to complete the verification. Bring out the folio. All of it." She hesitated, and then, because it seemed suddenly important: "My father wanted me to keep his shop's books. I wanted to draw coastlines. He said the crown didn't suffer women chart-makers, and he was right, and I took the next thing the crown offered instead." She fastened the clasp. "I'd rather like to see a coastline drawn properly, Master Hartwell."

She was three miles down the lane before she let herself think the name plainly, and when she did, it settled something in her that had been unsettled for eleven years.

Behind her, in the darkening workshop, Ansel Hartwell stood a long while at the window, watching the census taker's lantern shrink up the road — and then turned to Mrs. Quill with a face gone pale as fresh paper.

"She asked about Thursday," Ansel said. "Quill — the garrison's letter said their man comes for the survey records Friday."
