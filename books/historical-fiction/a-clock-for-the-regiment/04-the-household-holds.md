## 4. The Household Holds

The knock came at dusk, and it was the wrong kind of knock.

Mrs. Quill had a taxonomy of knocks, delivered to Ansel once as instruction and never repeated since: the tradesman's double rap, apologetic; the neighbor's shuffle-and-tap; the census woman's precise, measured triple, which they'd already endured twice that winter. This one was a fist. Four blows, evenly spaced, with the patience of a man who knew the door would open because it always opened for him.

Ansel set down her pen mid-stroke. The border plate beneath it — the western marshes, half-inked, Elias's true line creeping toward the paper's edge — drank the wet black in silence.

"Through the cellar," she said quietly. "Take the plates."

Tam was already moving. He'd heard the knock through his feet, or through some sense older than hearing; he crossed the workshop gathering tools with both hands the way other men gathered weapons, and when Mrs. Quill pulled up the trapdoor behind the drying rack he went down into the dark without a word, the folio of finished plates strapped to his chest like a child.

Mrs. Quill smoothed her apron. "Housekeeper answers the door," she murmured. "You're the apprentice. Boys are stupid when they're frightened. Be stupid."

The fist fell again. Mrs. Quill opened the door on the second blow, and the lantern-light brought in a man made mostly of red wool: garrison coat, garrison boots, a face like a slammed drawer. Behind him on the lane stood two soldiers who were pretending to admire the frozen gutter.

"Captain Ross," the man said, not to Mrs. Quill but past her, into the house, as though the house owed him an answer. "Survey records. All of them. The crown requires copies for the new ordnance map."

"The master's dead," Mrs. Quill said, with the flat sorrow of a woman who had buried husbands and knew how to spend grief like coin. "God rest him."

"Dead men's papers aren't dead." He stepped inside without invitation, stamping snow. His eyes went where eyes always went first: the workbench, the press, the racks of drying sheets. Then to Ansel, standing too still beside the ink-stained bench.

"You'd be the apprentice."

"Yes, sir." Pitch it low. Let the fear show — boys were allowed fear, expected it even. It was competence that got boys looked at twice.

"Name?"

"Hartwell, sir. Ansel Hartwell."

Something moved behind his eyes — not recognition, but the placing of a stone in a wall he was building somewhere out of sight. "Elias Hartwell's boy."

"His apprentice, sir. He took my name on."

"How Christian of him." Ross pulled off one glove, then the other, finger by finger, taking his time about it. "The garrison is compiling a rival survey. Your master's field books would spare us six months' work. I'll have them tonight."

Ansel felt the floor tilt under her, very slightly, the way ice shifts before it decides. The field books existed. They also contained notations no legitimate surveyor should have been making — soundings in the marsh that weren't soundings, bearings to places that appeared on no crown chart, measurements Elias had taken at night by lantern-light and never explained. And beneath all of it, in every hand that had drawn those pages, the small terrible fact that the atlas's borders disagreed with the crown's.

"The field books are in disorder, sir," she said. "My master worked in cipher for his own notes. It'll take time to copy anything legible."

"Then you'll work fast." Ross smiled without warmth, a man performing a smile from memory. "I'll send for them Friday. Copies, mind — the originals stay under crown seal." He let that sit, then added, almost kindly, which was worse: "It would go badly for a household like this one if the crown's maps proved better than its mapmaker's."

He left the smell of wet wool and horse behind him. Through the window Ansel watched the soldiers fall in behind him, and watched their breath hang in the lane like small ghosts refusing to move on.

* * *

"He knows," Mrs. Quill said, for the third time, though saying it changed nothing. She was pacing the kitchen, which smelled of tallow and yesterday's onions, while Ansel sat with her hands flat on the table as if pinning something down. "That look. Men like that don't come for copies. They come to see what flinches."

"Then we don't flinch." Ansel heard her own voice and was briefly surprised by it — steady, dry, Elias's voice more than hers. "We give him exactly what he asked for. A complete set of copies. Ordinary ones."

"Ordinary."

"Field books with nothing in them worth hanging anyone for. Soundings that are only soundings. Bearings to nowhere interesting. Six months of dull, honest, useless work." She was already seeing it, page by page, the way she saw plates before she cut them. "He wants the atlas. So I'll build him a twin with no soul in it."

Mrs. Quill stopped pacing. "How long does a thing like that take?"

Ansel did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Forty pages minimum, matched to the originals' dates, watermarks, blots, Elias's cramped leftward slant aging across three years of marsh seasons. In fair conditions, a month.

"We have until Friday."

"That's not possible."

"It's possible," Tam said.

They both turned. He stood in the kitchen doorway with the folio still strapped to his chest and snow melting in his hair, and he signed as he spoke, his hands doing half the sentence: *I've watched his hand for nine years. I can cut any hand you can draw.*

"You can't forge a man's whole life in four days," Ansel said.

*No,* Tam signed, and his mouth curved — not quite a smile, more the baring of teeth that engravers do when shown an impossible plate. *But you don't have to forge it all. You forge the pages he'll read first and the pages he'll check against. The middle pages only need to be boring enough that nobody loves them.*

Ansel stared at him. Nine years she had thought of Tam as the hands of the workshop — the finest burin in the county, attached to a quiet man who heard the world through wood and metal. She had never once thought of him as its strategist.

"We'd need the original watermarks," she said slowly, thinking aloud now. "The garrison will compare paper."

*Paper we have.* He tapped the folio against his chest. *Sheets your master bought and never used. Dated stock. We print the copies on the same year's paper and age the ink over the stove.*

Mrs. Quill had gone still by the hearth, and when she spoke her voice had lost its protest. "I'll need to know when the cooking fires burn hot," she said. "A woman baking through a cold snap heats ovens day and night. Nobody wonders at it." She untied her apron with the air of a general accepting a commission. "And I'll bake enough bread to feed the garrison itself, so the flour goes missing and no one counts it."

Ansel looked between them — the deaf engraver rolling up his sleeves, the runaway housekeeper planning alibis in flour — and understood, with a sensation like a stitch being pulled tight, that neither of them had asked the question. Not *what's in these books*, not *why does the garrison want them*, not *are we criminals now*. They had simply stepped to the wall beside her, the way men step to a breach.

"You know I can't tell you everything," she said. "And I don't know all of it myself."

*Didn't ask,* Tam signed, untying the folio. *Your master fed us eight years. Whatever killed him, we eat at his table still.*

* * *

They worked four nights, and the nights blurred into a single long lamplit country.

By day the workshop performed itself: Ansel visible at the front bench grinding ink, sweeping, being a dull and frightened boy for any passing soldier's benefit, while Tam slept in snatches and carved at midnight. By night the real work happened behind shutters. Tam cut Elias's hand in copper — not copied but *rebuilt*, stroke by stroke, until the old man's slant ran off his burin like water he'd drunk all his life. Ansel composed the content: plausible soundings, honest distances, marsh bearings trimmed of anything that pointed toward the truth. The forgery could not be empty — emptiness invited suspicion — so she salted it with the kind of error Elias had actually made in his first season, misjudged tides, a benchmark misplaced and corrected in a later hand. Flaws were the surest proof of honesty.

On the third night, near dawn, her pen slowed over a page and she caught herself weeping without drama, the tears simply falling onto the blotting paper, because she was writing Elias alive again — his impatience, his second thoughts, his habit of annotating the weather as though it owed him money — and he was alive in her hand and dead in the ground, murdered, she now believed, for the very lines she was carefully leaving out.

Tam put a mug of something hot by her elbow and didn't look at her, which was his way of looking at her.

*Friday,* he signed. *Then we burn the copper.*

"No," Ansel said. Her voice cracked and she steadied it. "We keep the plates. Bury them with the others."

He studied her a long moment. Then he nodded once, and she understood that he had just agreed to share a grave's worth of risk without asking why, and that this was simply what Tam did.

Mrs. Quill ran the household above them like a clockwork of alibis. The widow — old Mistress Hartwell, who sat by the parlor window in her shawl and said little and missed less — descended to the kitchen twice during those days to demand, loudly within earshot of the lane, why the house smelled of ink and bread and secrets. "Because it always has, you old bat," Mrs. Quill answered, equally loudly, and steered her back upstairs. On Thursday a neighbor came asking after the smell of baking at strange hours, and left with two loaves and a story about a wake for the late master, God rest him, poor soul, the household eating its grief.

By Thursday midnight the duplicate set lay stacked and bound in twine: forty-one pages, three years deep, smelling faintly of the stove. Ansel fanned through them one last time by candlelight. Elias's hand looked back at her from every sheet, tired and exact and telling nothing. She had spent four days impersonating a dead man so thoroughly that part of her feared she'd forgotten her own script.

Tam pressed the last copper plate into wax paper and tucked it beneath the loose hearthstone in the cellar, beside the folio of true plates, and gave the stone a pat like closing a coffin lid.

They slept two hours. Dawn came gray. Friday came.

At ten o'clock the fist fell again — four blows, patient — and Mrs. Quill opened the door to Captain Ross and the smell of wet wool, and Ansel carried the bound set to him herself, a boy staggering under the weight of dull honest labor, apologizing for the smudges, for the disorder, for the lateness of everything.

Ross took his time. He untied the twine. He turned pages with fingers that had clearly handled evidence before, and Ansel stood in the terrible stillness of a forged document's first reader, watching his eyes travel down columns of invented soundings, and kept her breathing shallow and her face stupid.

Somewhere around the twentieth page he paused. His thumb rested on the margin, on a small annotation in Elias's hand — *tide wrong, remeasure* — and Ansel felt the whole constructed world of the last four days balance on that thumb.

Ross grunted. He closed the set, retied the twine himself, and tucked it under his arm.

"Adequate," he said. Then, at the door, with the air of a man remembering an errand: "The census woman will be round again next week. Final verification." He settled his collar against the cold. "She asks sharper questions than I do. Mind you answer them the same way twice."

The door shut. Down the lane, the soldiers' footfalls crunched away into the frost.

Mrs. Quill let out a breath she'd held since Tuesday. Tam signed something swift and profane and joyful. But Ansel stayed where she was, staring at the closed door, because Ross had said *again* — and Verity Cole had told her, at the second visit, that she wouldn't need to come a third time.

Through the window, far down the white lane, a small figure in a gray traveling cloak had just turned the corner by the church wall, and was walking toward the workshop with a satchel and a purposeful stride.

Not next week. Today.
