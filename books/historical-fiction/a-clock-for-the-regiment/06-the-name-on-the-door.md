## 6. The Name on the Door

The warrant went up on the garrison board on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the fishwife was reciting it in the market square as though it were scripture.

"*Item: a warrant issued for the apprehension of the Hartwell apprentice, wanted for questioning in matters pertaining to crown surveys, unauthorized cartography, and correspondence with persons unknown.*"

Ansel heard it secondhand, from Mrs. Quill, who came back from buying salt with her cheeks mottled red and the basket clutched against her chest like a shield. She set it down hard on the worktable, scattering dried thyme across the plates Ansel had been cleaning.

"They've named you," she said. "Not by name — worse than that. They've named your *position*. There's only one Hartwell apprentice in this parish, girl. Every soul in town knows it."

"Every soul in town also believes that apprentice is a boy of nineteen." Ansel kept her voice level. She wiped thyme from the copper plate with her thumb. "A warrant for *the apprentice* is still a warrant for a boy. So long as I don't give them reason to look twice."

"And how long is that? How long do you walk past soldiers who are being *paid* to look twice?"

Tam signed something from the corner, quick and sharp, his big hands cutting the air. Ansel read it without thinking, the way she read weather: *They took Denny Marsh's boy this morning. Asked him about the workshop. Let him go at noon.*

"Denny's boy knows nothing," she said aloud, for Mrs. Quill's benefit. "But they're asking. That's new."

"It's not new, it's *finished*," Mrs. Quill said. "This is what comes before, Ansel. I've seen it. First they question the neighbors, then they come with papers, then they come with lanterns. You cannot be here when they come."

Outside, the wind had turned, dragging the smell of the marsh inland — cold rot and rain coming. The light through the workshop windows was the grey of pewter. On the wall above the drying racks hung Elias Hartwell's last finished map, the one of the northern coast that had made his reputation, and beneath it, in the long drawer Ansel had locked every night for six years, lay the atlas that would unmake everything if it were ever found.

She thought about the folio hidden in its binding. The border drawn wrong. Drawn *true*. Three days before he died.

"I'm not leaving," she said.

* * *

Mrs. Quill did not shout. That was the terrible thing about her; she had been a housekeeper too long and a runaway longer, and shouting wasted breath that arguments might use. She sat down across the worktable, folded her hands, and dismantled Ansel piece by piece with the patience of a woman unstitching a hem.

"You'll finish the atlas, you say. To what end? You'll ink a few more plates, sew the quires, and then what? Put it under your arm and walk to London? A girl dressed as a boy carrying the single most dangerous document in the borderlands, into the teeth of the garrison that wants it burned?"

"I'll take it where Elias meant it to go."

"Elias is dead."

"He died for it," Ansel said, and her voice cracked at last, which she hated. "He came back from the marsh with fever and a secret eating him alive, and he gave it to *me*, not to the crown, not to the church, not to anyone else. He drew me into it when he taught me to hold a burin. Everything since then — the binding, the plates, the six years of being someone I'm not — it was all in service of finishing what he started. If I run now, if I burn it or bury it or leave it half-done in a ditch, then all of it was for nothing. The lying was for nothing."

"The lying could *end*," Mrs. Quill said softly. "That's my point, child. Walk out tonight as yourself. A dress, a bonnet, a different parish. There are houses that take in women alone. You could be Ansel — truly Ansel — within the week, and no one the wiser, and no one hanged."

Ansel looked down at her hands. They were ink-stained to the second knuckle, the nails cut short, the right forefinger calloused in a way no farm girl's finger ever calloused. Six years ago she had bound them flat every morning before her master woke, terrified he'd see the tell. Now they were simply her hands, the hands of a mapmaker, and she could not imagine them holding anything but a pen.

"You think being Ansel is a name I can put on like a bonnet," she said. "You think it's waiting for me somewhere south, if I just stop pretending. But Mrs. Quill — there is nothing to *be* Ansel except this work. I gave up everything else. I don't know how to be a girl in a strange parish. I know how to hold a line true to three points of the compass. This atlas is the only honest thing I have ever made. It is the only place where I exist at all."

"That isn't identity, girl, that's stubbornness."

"It's loyalty," Ansel said. "To him. To Tam. To you. The atlas proves what the garrison did — what they covered up, what Elias found and they killed him for finding. If I abandon it, I abandon everyone who ever told the truth and got buried for it. Including him. Including me."

Silence, except the wind working at the shutters.

Mrs. Quill unfolded her hands. Refolded them. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quieter and harder, the voice of a woman remembering her own door she'd fled through twenty years ago.

"When I ran from my husband, I left everything. Silver, letters, my mother's ring. I kept one thing." She touched the small brass key she wore at her throat; Ansel had never once seen it off her neck. "A key to a house that burned down years before. Do you know why?"

Ansel shook her head.

"Because a person needs one thing that says *this was mine, and I was real*. One proof against all the years of running." Her eyes went to the locked drawer. "I understand better than you know, child. But a key opens a door. Your atlas is a door with the garrison standing behind it. Weigh that."

"I have weighed it every night for six weeks."

"Then God help us both," Mrs. Quill said, and stood, and began — because she was Mrs. Quill, and the world could be ending and she would still be useful — to bank the fire and lay out supper things, as though the argument were over.

It was not over. Both of them knew it was not over.

* * *

It happened two days later, at dusk, while Ansel was grinding pigment and the first fat drops of rain were starting against the windows.

The knock was wrong. She knew every knock the household used — Tam's double rap, Mrs. Quill's three-and-a-pause — and this was none of them: heavy, official, spaced like a man reading a form. She had time to blow out the grinding lamp, slide the drawer key into her boot, and pull her cap low before the door opened on a sergeant and two privates, water streaming off their hats.

"Hartwell apprentice?" the sergeant said, holding his paper up against the wet.

"Present," Ansel said, and pitched her voice down, the old trick, the thousandth time she'd done it.

"We're posting inquiry notices to all survey workshops. You'll confirm receipt." He did not wait for confirmation; he was looking past her, into the workshop, at the racks of plates and the great press and the locked drawer, and Ansel watched his eyes do the arithmetic of value. "Where's the deaf mute?"

Her blood went cold. "At the chandlery, fetching wax."

"He was at the chandlery an hour past. My men saw him come toward this street." The sergeant stepped inside without invitation, boots printing wet crescents on the floorboards. "Tam, isn't it? Big fellow. Signs with his hands."

Behind the partition that screened the stair down to the cellar, Ansel heard — so faintly only she could have caught it — the creak of a single boards-shift. Tam had heard the voices' vibration through the floor, or seen the lanternlight swing wrong, and he had moved, and moving was the worst thing he could do.

"He goes where he likes," Ansel said. "He doesn't answer to me."

"Everything in this house answers to somebody." The sergeant drifted along the plate racks, one gloved finger trailing over the copper like a customs man appraising cargo. "Word of advice, apprentice. Warrants have a way of growing names. Today it's *the apprentice*. Tomorrow it's *the apprentice and associates*. You keep irregular company — a runaway below stairs, a mute engraver, an old woman nobody can account for. A man starts to wonder what a household like that is hiding."

He turned. For one long moment he looked straight at the partition. Rain drummed the roof. Below the floor, Ansel knew, Tam stood in the dark among the cider barrels with his lantern unlit, hands open at his sides, utterly silent — a man who had spent his whole life being quiet in a loud world, and had never needed it so badly.

Then the partition behind which Tam hid creaked — a soft settling, wood contracting in the damp — and the sergeant's head came around like a dog's.

"What's down there?"

"Cider and rot," Ansel said. "You're welcome to fall through the stairs and check."

Something in her flatness stopped him, or the rain did, or the fact that a warrant naming *the apprentice* did not yet authorize a search of cellars. He grunted, posted his notice on the door with a nail and two hammer-blows that rang through the whole house like a bell, and left.

They stood listening until the hoofbeats faded. Then Ansel crossed to the partition and pulled it aside, and Tam came blinking up into the lamplight, pale under his stubble, and she seized his sleeve and made him sit down before his legs gave.

His hands moved, slow with shock: *He smelled me. Like a hound.*

"He guessed," Ansel said. "Guessing isn't finding. But it will be." She looked at the notice nailed to their door, black letters gleaming with rain: NOTICE OF INQUIRY — ALL PERSONS ASSOCIATED. *All persons associated.* Not the apprentice alone anymore. The household entire.

Mrs. Quill appeared in the kitchen doorway with a candle, and the three of them stood in a triangle around the truth none of them had ever spoken aloud: that they were harboring a fugitive girl, forbidden maps, and each other, and that the garrison had begun closing its hand.

Tam lifted his hands one last time that night. He signed slowly, deliberately, making sure she read every word:

*We do not hide you. We keep you. There is a difference.*

Ansel felt her throat close. She nodded, and signed back — clumsily, with her ink-stained fingers, the way she'd learned from him a decade ago in this same room — *I know.*

She went down to the worktable after the house slept, unlocked the drawer, and unwrapped the atlas. By candlelight she pinned the next plate — the central marches, the hardest of them all, where Elias's true border diverged most fatally from the crown's lie — and dipped her pen.

Somewhere out in the rain, hooves were already moving. She did not hear them yet. But the atlas lay open under her hands like a heartbeat, and she bent to the work, because it was the only prayer she knew.
