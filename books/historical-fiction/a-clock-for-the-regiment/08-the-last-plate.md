## 8. The Last Plate

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Ansel finally lifted the burin.

Below her, in the cellar, Tam was hammering — softly, patiently, the way he did everything — fitting the false panel over his hiding place while Mrs. Quill directed him in the sign language they had invented between themselves, a vocabulary of fingers and tilted hands that Ansel had never been permitted to learn fully. It belonged to them. She had her own secrets; the household had learned long ago to let each person keep what they must.

Above her, through the floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of a workshop pretending to be an ordinary workshop: Mrs. Quill moving crockery, the widow's cough, the clock in the front room ticking with its usual stubborn regularity. Elias had bought that clock thirty years ago from a peddler who swore it kept better time than Parliament. *A clock for the regiment,* he used to say, tapping its case when Ansel was slow with her letters. *It marches whether you follow or not.*

She followed it now. The tick had become the measure of her hand.

The plate lay before her on the pressing table — the ninth and final plate of the atlas, copper as warm as living skin where she had just finished warming it over the flame. On its face, drawn in reverse so it would print true, ran the border of the northern marsh country: not the line the Crown had published forty years past, thick and confident as a general's signature, but the line Elias Hartwell had walked himself, benchmark by benchmark, in the last summer of his life. A different line. A truer one. The difference between them was no wider than a hayrick in most places — and in one place, a finger of fenland holding six farms and a chapel, it was the width of a hanging.

Three days before he died, Elias had redrawn that border. And three days after he died, they had carried him home feet-first with mud dried in his hair and a fever the apothecary called marsh sickness, and Ansel had believed it, because believing was easier than looking at the bruises on his wrists.

She set the burin's point against the copper and began.

There was no artistry left to summon; the artistry was in the waiting. Six weeks she had let the drawing sit under cloths while soldiers rode the lane and Verity Cole's visits hung over the house like weather. Six weeks she had told herself the plate wasn't ready, the ink wasn't right, her hand wasn't steady. All lies, and she had known them for lies, because the truth was simpler and more terrible: as long as the last plate was unfinished, the atlas was still only a suspicion. Finished, it became a testimony. And testimony could be answered with rope.

But the warrant was posted on the church door now. *The Hartwell apprentice.* Four words, and every one of them a noose. The garrison wanted the apprentice and the survey records, and they would come for both at week's end — sooner, if Captain Merrick's patience wore through, and everything about Merrick suggested it wore quickly.

So: the burin, and the copper, and the clock ticking.

She cut the line of the true border the way Elias had taught her to cut — *let the tool do the remembering, girl; your hand forgets, the steel doesn't* — and as the groove opened, silver-bright against the dark metal, she found she was not thinking of borders at all. She was thinking of the first time she had held a graver, aged nine, her sleeves rolled and her hair stuffed up under Elias's dead son's cap, and how he had watched her make the first stroke and said nothing at all. She had waited days for him to send her away. He had never mentioned it again. That silence had been the longest kindness of her life, and she understood it only now: he had decided in that moment that whatever she was, she would be it here, in this room, at this table, and the world could come and argue with his corpse if it objected.

It had come. It had argued. His corpse was not available for comment, so the atlas would speak instead.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the north window, and with it Mrs. Quill, carrying bread and a look like a woman walking to her own funeral.

"You haven't slept."

"I slept." This was false. "How is Tam?"

"Settled. Hating it." Mrs. Quill set the bread down and stood over the plate, arms folded into her apron. She was sixty if she was a day, with forearms like a laundress and a face that had stopped pretending to be pleasant sometime around the death of King George's queen. "The widow's packing the china. I told her to pack nothing that matters. China doesn't matter."

"What matters?"

"That you're done by noon." Mrs. Quill's eyes went to the plate, then to the folio lying open beside it — the sealed papers Ansel had cut from the atlas binding, Elias's hand cramped and feverish across the top: *Surveyed contrary to instruction. The line is wrong. They know it is wrong.* "And that you sign it."

Ansel's hand stilled on the burin.

"I've signed every plate," she said. "*E. Hartwell & Apprentice.* As always."

"That's not signing. That's hiding behind a dead man's coat-tails." Mrs. Quill pulled the stool around and sat, which she almost never did, because sitting meant staying, and staying meant the conversation would happen whether anyone wanted it or not. "Girl. Listen to me. Tomorrow there may be no workshop, no plates, no us. Tonight there is this table, and you, and ink. If the work is worth dying for — and I've watched you decide it is, six times over these past weeks — then put your name on it. Not his. Yours. Let whoever reads it next know that a living hand made this line, and whose hand it was."

"They'll hunt me harder if—"

"They'll hunt you regardless. The warrant says *apprentice*, not *boy*. Merrick isn't stupid, whatever else he is. Sooner or later someone tells him what you are, and likely for coin." Mrs. Quill took up the pen — Ansel's good pen, the one with the cracked ferrule Elias had mended twice — and laid it across the plate's margin like a challenge. "I ran from a husband for eleven years before I ran here. You know what I regret? Not the running. That I spent eleven years answering to a name that was never mine. Don't do the same with your own life, child. There's no percentage in it."

Ansel looked at the copper. The true border ran across it, bright-cut and exact, every bend and benchmark faithful to Elias's final survey. In the lower corner, the cartouche waited, empty of everything but the title: *ATLAS OF THE NORTHERN MARCHES, Being a True Delineation.*

Being a true delineation.

She picked up the pen. Her hand, which had not shaken once all night, shook now — one long tremor, starting at the wrist and running out through her fingertips like the last vibration of a struck bell.

Then it passed, and she wrote:

*Ansel Hartwell.*

Not *the apprentice*. Not *A. Hartwell, his mark*. Her name, whole, the name her mother had given her before there was any reason to hide it, in a fair round hand that any guild examiner would have praised. Below it, smaller, because honesty demanded the whole ledger: *late apprentice to Elias Hartwell, and his heir in this work.*

Mrs. Quill read it upside down, which was how she read everything, and something happened to her face that on a softer woman might have been weeping.

"Well," she said, voice like gravel in honey. "About time."

* * *

They packed the atlas into Elias's old survey case — oiled leather, brass corners, built to survive rivers and being dropped from horses, which in its career it had done both. The widow wrapped each loose plate in flannel. Mrs. Quill wedged the sealed folio along the spine's channel where the binding hid it, exactly where Ansel had found it, because some hiding places were made well the first time.

Tam came up from the cellar to help, which nobody had sanctioned and nobody forbade. He moved among them quick and silent, lashing the case's straps with a sailor's knots — he had been pressed onto a merchantman at fourteen, the only chapter of his life he could not be persuaded to sign about — and when the case was closed he set his broad palm flat upon the leather and looked at Ansel with a question in his face.

"Someone has to carry it to Aldous Wren," Ansel said. "The clerk at the surveyor-general's office. The one honest man left in the whole rotten apparatus — if Verity Cole judged right, and I think she did."

Tam pointed at her, then at himself, then made the gesture they all knew: *who goes?*

"Me. I go." She said it before Mrs. Quill could open her mouth. "The warrant names me. If any of you are caught with the atlas, it damns you worse. If I'm caught, I'm caught for my own name at last, and that's—" she searched for the word and found it, unexpectedly, easily, "—that's honest."

Mrs. Quill muttered something about honesty being cold comfort in a cell, but she didn't argue, and Ansel understood that the argument had been lost somewhere in the night, in the scratch of a pen on copper's margin, in a name written plain.

Through the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, the clock struck ten.

And then it struck eleven. And then twelve, half a beat too soon, the way it did when the mechanism wanted cleaning — and Ansel stood very still in the middle of the ruined, packed, half-blind workshop that had been her whole world since she was nine years old, and listened to it keep time without her.

"We should go over the plan again," the widow said nervously. "The back gate, the drainage path, the causeway—"

The knock came then.

Not the garrison's knock — that would be boots and iron, a battering rhythm. This was two raps, light, spaced, deliberate, in a pattern Ansel knew because she had heard it twice before on this door: the census taker's knock, Verity Cole's knock, the knock of a woman who had taught herself to announce herself precisely so she would never be mistaken for anyone else.

Mrs. Quill's hand went to the poker. Tam stepped in front of the survey case like a wall deciding to be a wall.

Ansel crossed the room and lifted the latch.

Verity Cole stood in the doorway spattered to the knees with mud, her cloak steaming, her official satchel gone from her shoulder and her riding crop gone from her hand and her commission — Ansel saw it now — gone, she realized with a chill, from where it always sat in its oiled folder inside her coat. Her face was grey with exhaustion and set like a gravestone.

"They moved it up," Verity said. "The raid. Merrick's column left the garrison at dusk." She looked past Ansel's shoulder at the packed cases, the bare walls, the little household standing frozen among their own ruins, and then back at Ansel's face. "You have perhaps an hour. Less, if the lanterns I saw on the south lane belong to whom I think they belong."

As if summoned, a light bloomed far down the lane — one lantern, then another, swaying with the particular rhythm of men on horseback carrying more than lamps.

Tam blew out the candle.
