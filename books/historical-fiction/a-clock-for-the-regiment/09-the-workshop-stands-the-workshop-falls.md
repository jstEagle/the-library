## 9. The Workshop Stands, the Workshop Falls

*Verity*

The lanterns came up the lane at half past eleven, and Verity counted them the way she had once counted errors in a ledger: coldly, precisely, with her heart nowhere in the arithmetic. Eight lanterns. Two sergeants. A lieutenant she did not know, riding a horse too fine for the garrison's allowance, which meant the commander had sent his own man, which meant there would be no pretending this was routine.

She met them at the workshop door with a candle in one hand and a leather folio under her arm, and she made her voice into the thing it had been trained to be for nine years — flat, bored, faintly offended.

"You'll want to announce yourselves properly," she said. "This is a crown census district. I am the enumerator of record."

The lieutenant looked at her the way men look at furniture. "Warrant names the Hartwell apprentice and all papers of survey. Step aside."

"By whose order?" She opened the folio against her forearm as if consulting it, though she could have recited every forged line Ansel had inked at two in the morning: the commission from the Surveyor-General's office, the instruction that *all materials pertaining to the disputed border be conveyed under escort to the clerk of inquiry in Ashford, no copies to be made, no seizure to precede conveyance*. It was beautiful forgery. It was also, if anyone checked the seals closely enough, hanging rope.

"Colonel Merrick's."

"Then Colonel Merrick will be interested," Verity said, holding out the folio, "to learn that his warrant contradicts a superior instruction dated four days prior. You may read it by lantern-light if you like. I'd rather you didn't bring fire near the premises; the ink is not yet fully set on some of these plates, and I am personally accountable for every sheet."

That word — *accountable* — worked on officers the way a hand works on a horse's neck. The lieutenant took the folio. He read. His lantern guttered in the wind off the marsh, and behind the shuttered windows above her head Verity could hear, very faintly, a floorboard settle. Tam, going down into the cellar with the press tools. Mrs. Quill following with the strongbox.

Somewhere beyond the dark gardens, already a quarter mile gone, a girl was running with an atlas against her chest.

"The clerk of inquiry is a clerical matter," the lieutenant said at last.

"So is a warrant," said Verity. "Yours has the wrong year on it, incidentally. But then, haste does that."

She had not known until she said it whether the year was wrong. She saw him see it. She saw him decide, in the space of one breath, that he would rather not have noticed — because men who serve Colonel Merrick have learned what questions cost. He folded the folio shut.

"We'll search regardless."

"Of course you will." She stepped back and held the door wide, candle high, lighting their way into the house where three people she loved were hiding or fleeing or already gone. "Mind the drying racks. Ruin a plate and I'll enter it in my report, and my reports go to Ashford now. Everything goes to Ashford now."

It was a bluff built on a forgery built on a truth, and it would hold for perhaps twenty minutes. She intended those twenty minutes to be enough.

*Ansel*

The marsh at night was a country Ansel knew only by memory and mathematics, which proved to be enough. Elias had taught her its grammar: the benchmarks he'd cut into stone and oak, each chiseled mark a sentence saying *you are here, and here is true even when everything else lies*. In daylight she had needed instruments. Tonight she needed only her hands, finding the cross-cut in the black poplar's root, the drilled hole in the bridge abutment, reading the dark country like a page she herself had inked.

The atlas rode inside her coat, bound tight in oilcloth, beating against her ribs like a second heart. Every step was sacrifice made ordinary: behind her the workshop, the only roof she had loved, burning in prospect if not yet in fact; ahead of her twelve miles of drowned ground with soldiers somewhere between. She had chosen the marsh over the road because Elias had chosen the marsh before her, and because the horses could not follow where the benchmarks led.

The rain began at the third benchmark, a low stone half-sunk in sedge, and she allowed herself thirty seconds under an alder to work her fingers back to life. In those thirty seconds she heard them — hooves on the firmer ground to the east, several horses, moving the way she herself would move if she were hunting someone who must reach Ashford by morning.

They knew. Not everything, but enough. Someone had read the forged orders more honestly than the lieutenant had.

Ansel left the alder and went into the flooded meadow where the benchmark stood on the one safe causeway of packed clay, a path no wider than a coffin, invisible unless you trusted the stone. Her boots broke the skin of the water. Cold climbed her legs. Twice the clay gave under her and she caught herself, arms out, the atlas a weight she would not drop even to save her own balance — and that was the thought that frightened her, arriving plain and uninvited: *the book first, yourself second.* Elias had died holding exactly that thought. She could feel its whole shape now, the way you feel a fever coming on.

The fourth benchmark was the one he had buried, the one she and Verity had dug out of the mud together in the rain while trust was still possible between them. She passed it without stopping, but her hand brushed the top of the stone as she went, the way you touch a grave, or a promise.

The horses turned onto the causeway behind her. She heard the first rider swear as the marsh took a hoof to the fetlock, heard the sergeant's voice — she knew the sergeant's voice now, she had heard it through the workshop floor a hundred times in her dreams — ordering two men to dismount and spread along the dike. Flanking her. Cutting the long mile between the fourth stone and the fifth, where the ground rose toward the turnpike and Ashford beyond.

She ran. The water fought her at every stride. And at the fifth benchmark, where Elias's chisel had cut the mark crooked because his hand was already failing him, she found what she had prayed she would find and had not dared expect: light. A fen-man's punt tied in the reeds, pole laid across the thwarts, placed there years ago by a man who mapped drowning-country because he loved the people who lived in it, and maintained since by whoever inherited his habit of mercy.

Ansel got the atlas above the waterline first. Then herself. Then pushed off into the black channel as the lanterns reached the stone.

A shot went over her head. Another struck the water flat, skipping. She lay low in the punt, poles deep, and let the marsh do what marshes do for those who read them — swallow, conceal, keep.

Near loss came anyway, near the end, where the channel braided and she chose wrong and grounded on a sandbar in sight of the turnpike's distant lamps. She got out and hauled, water to her waist, atlas held overhead like something rescued from a flood — which, she thought wildly, it was — and the sand sucked at her boots and the far bank might as well have been the moon. She thought of Verity stalling eight lanterns alone. She thought of Tam in the cellar listening to boots on his floorboards. She hauled.

Her feet found gravel. Then road. Then running.

*Clerk Hobbes of the Surveyor-General's office kept a lamp burning in his window at Ashford, which was either sentiment or signal, and Ansel had bet her life it was signal.*

*Verity*

They burned the workshop at dawn, officially by accident — a knocked lantern, a dry rafter, the sort of fire that happens when fires are wanted. Verity stood in the lane with her hands cuffed before her, arrested on suspicion of forging crown documents, and watched the roof go. She had bought Ansel four hours and it had been enough, and knowing that made watching the flames almost bearable. Almost.

Mrs. Quill walked past the soldiers with a market basket on her arm, gray-haired, unremarkable, and did not look at Verity at all, which was the correct performance of grief. Tam had been led out of the cellar an hour earlier, deaf to every shouted question, and had signed, with perfect courteous incomprehension, that he understood nothing. They scattered like a hand of cards. The house lost. The family intact.

The lieutenant approached Verity as the east wall fell inward, throwing sparks.

"The clerk at Ashford says he never issued your instruction," he said.

"He will," said Verity. "Give him a day."

He studied her, uncertain whether he'd been threatened or promised. She looked past him at the fire, and found — to her own surprise — that she was smiling. Somewhere south of here, soaked and exhausted and more herself than she had ever been permitted to be, Ansel Hartwell was carrying the truth at a dead run. Everything Verity had spent nine years filing away in triplicate was finally, at last, going where it should.

"Take me wherever you're taking me," she told the lieutenant pleasantly. "But do write down that I asked you to check the date on your warrant."

* * *

Hobbes opened his door before Ansel finished knocking, as though he had been standing behind it all night, and perhaps he had; his lamp was still lit, his ink still wet, and his eyes went straight to the oilcloth bundle in her arms the way a starving man's eyes go to bread.

"Miss Hartwell," he said. Not apprentice. Not the boy. Miss Hartwell.

"You know my name."

"Elias wrote it in the margin of every plate he loved," said the old clerk, stepping back to let her in. "I wondered when you'd come to claim it. Come in, child, and bar the door — you've brought me the most dangerous map in England, and we have until morning to make it evidence before they make it ashore."
