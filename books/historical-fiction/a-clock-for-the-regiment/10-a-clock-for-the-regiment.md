## 10. A Clock for the Regiment

The inquiry chamber smelled of tallow, wet wool, and old paper — the last of these Ansel knew better than anyone alive. They had set the atlas on a table at the front of the room like a relic, under the eye of two clerks and a surveyor-general who kept touching his spectacles as though the thing might blink. Elias Hartwell's final work, bound in calfskin that still bore a scorch along one corner from a workshop that no longer existed.

Ansel sat three rows back in a borrowed coat, hands folded so no one would see them shake. She had signed her name on the title plate in full daylight: *Ansel Hartwell*. The clerk had read it aloud when the atlas was entered into evidence, and the room had not laughed, had not gasped, had simply written it down. That was all she had wanted for six years and it took less time than sharpening a pen.

Verity sat at the counsel table in civilian grey. Her commission was gone; she had burned it herself, page by page, in the grate of a roadside inn the night she rode to warn them. Now she was neither crown officer nor fugitive but something stranger — a witness whose testimony the inquiry could not do without and did not quite know how to name.

The surveyor-general's chief clerk, Mr. Aldous Renner — the one incorruptible man Ansel had found in a city of purchased men — opened the folio to the plate that mattered. The true border. The marsh line drawn by Elias Hartwell's hand three days before he died, with the benchmark coordinates that proved the garrison's version had been moved four hundred yards to swallow a ford, a ferry toll, and a smuggling road the commander had been taxing privately for a decade.

"Observe," Renner said, "the date."

A murmur went through the benches. Colonel Ashgrove sat opposite, resplendent, his lawyers arranged around him like chess pieces. He had smiled when Ansel entered. He was not smiling now.

Verity spoke for an hour. She laid out the census irregularities, the orders from her superiors to bury the folio, the warrant naming *the Hartwell apprentice* — a name that had never belonged to any single person, which had been its whole purpose. She spoke plainly, without ornament, and Ansel watched the room turn toward her the way rooms turn toward fire.

When it was done, Ashgrove rose to object and found that the men behind him had already begun shifting their papers, their faces already composing themselves into the expressions of men who had never supported him at all. Power, Ansel thought, was a map drawn in ink that dried the color of whatever won.

They stripped his commission before the candles were relit after the recess.

* * *

She went back to the ruin once more before leaving the city. There was nothing left of the workshop but the chimney and the low stone cellar where Tam had hidden, blackened beams lying like fallen rafters of a ship. Someone had chalked a crude clock face on the garden wall — soldiers' humor, a joke about a regiment that ran on time until the day its time ran out. Rain had half-washed it away.

Mrs. Quill met her there, shawled against the wind, carrying nothing but a carpet bag and the particular posture of a woman who has decided where she is going next.

"The print shop on Chandler's Row has a room above it," Mrs. Quill said. "Two windows. Light enough for drawing, if you draw."

"I draw," Ansel said.

"Then it's settled." Mrs. Quill looked at the scorched stones a moment longer than necessary. Elias's widow had kept this household together for twenty years on stubbornness and small economies, and she surveyed its ashes now the way she'd surveyed a burned loaf — with irritation rather than grief, because grief was a luxury that let the bread go unbaked twice. "He'd have hated this," she said. "Elias. He'd have hated that they burned paper."

"He'd have hated more that we saved none."

"We saved the only paper that mattered." She took Ansel's arm, and they walked out through the broken gate together, past the chalked clock, into a street already busy with people who had never heard of the atlas and would live their whole lives without needing to. That, too, felt like something worth mapping.

Tam was waiting at Chandler's Row. He had found work with the print shop inside a week — good engravers were rare, and deafness troubled printers not at all in a trade conducted in gestures over noise. He showed Ansel the press like a proud cat presenting a kill, then showed her the room above: bare floorboards, a hearth, two windows full of grey spring light. On the sill he placed a gift he had been cutting secretly for weeks, she realized later — a brass rule, six inches, with her initials filed into the end. Not *A.H.* Both names, spelled out entire. *ANSEL HARTWELL*, letter by letter, in an engraver's finest hand.

She began the new map that evening. Not a border. A river, this time — the whole watershed of the northern country, from source to marsh, drawn honestly, commissioned by no one, owed to no one. She ruled the first lines herself, with her own brass, under her own name, apprenticed to nobody living or dead.

Her hand was steady. That surprised her most.

* * *

Verity came in May, on an evening when the river map had grown to fill two sheets and the smell of ink had made the rented room feel like home instead of exile.

Ansel heard the knock — two raps, precise, official even now — and knew the fist before she opened the door. Verity Cole stood on the landing with a trunk at her feet and rain in her hair and the expression of a woman who had rehearsed several speeches on the walk over and abandoned all of them.

"They've posted me nowhere," she said. "I resigned. Formally. It seemed polite, given I'd already burned everything else." She glanced past Ansel's shoulder at the sheets pinned to the wall, at Tam's head appearing cautiously from the printing floor below, at Mrs. Quill emerging from the pantry with the speed of a woman who could feed any number of unexpected guests within ten minutes' notice. "There's a position. Renner offered it — verifying surveys against the record. Fieldwork. Months alone in bad country." A pause. "I thought perhaps the country needn't be crossed alone."

Below them, Tam stamped twice on the boards — the household's signal, agreed long ago, that meant *come in, you're letting the cold in.* He had never heard a word Verity Cole had spoken and had judged her entirely by her hands: how she held them, how they waited, how they helped carry without being asked. His verdict had come early and would not be revisited.

"You understand," Ansel said, "that we ask questions here. Too many questions. It ruined me once."

"I'm counting on it." Verity stepped over the threshold and set down her trunk, and the room rearranged itself around her the way rooms had learned to do for every stray this household had ever taken in — Mrs. Quill already clearing a shelf, Tam already pointing at the trunk and miming *heavy — where — bed or desk?*

"Desk," Verity guessed, and guessed right.

Later, when the tea was poured and the rain had settled into the steady drumming that meant it intended to stay the night, Verity stood at the window beside the new map and studied the river Ansel was building out of nothing but honesty and light.

"It'll take years," she said. "The whole watershed. You'll be middle-aged before the last sheet is proofed."

"Yes," Ansel said, and found that the sentence tasted like a promise rather than a sentence.

Verity nodded slowly. Then she drew a chair to the table, took the spare pen Mrs. Quill had set out without being told to, pulled the third unmarked sheet toward her own side, and asked, "Where does the river begin?"

Ansel dipped her pen, watched the ink gather dark and certain at the nib, and reached across the years — past the marsh, past the burning, past the boy she had been and the woman she had finally been permitted to say aloud — to the farthest blue place on the map she carried always behind her eyes.

"North," she said. "It begins in the north. Everything does."

And beneath the two windows, above the soft percussion of the press beginning its night's work, the first true line went down.
