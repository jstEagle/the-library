## 2. An Apprentice of Small Mercies

The tallow candles were the first thing Vane ever taught me to hate.

"Wax is for churches," he said, sliding a stub of tallow across the workbench with two fingers, the way a pawnbroker slides a coin. "Wax burns clean, and clean light makes careless hands. You'll work by dirty light or you won't work at all."

I was eleven years old and I had stolen his pencil case.

That was how it began—not with kindness, not with a door held open, but with theft. I had followed him out of the stationer's on Corvin Street, where he'd bought six pencils and argued about the grade of the graphite for twenty minutes, and I had lifted the case from his coat while he counted his change. I was quick in those days. Quick and hollow, the way winter makes a boy quick and hollow. I had the case half a street away when a hand closed on my collar like a closing book.

He didn't call for a constable. He didn't even raise his voice. He turned me around, looked at me the way he later taught me to look at an old map—with suspicion, then patience, then something like appetite—and said, "You went for the pencils. Not the purse. Not the watch."

I said nothing. Silence was free and I could afford it.

"Most of them go for the watch," he said. "You went for the tools." He took the pencil case back, opened it, inspected the contents as though I might have damaged them out of spite. Then he closed it and said the sentence that ruined my life, though it took me years to understand it as ruin: "Come inside. It's cold, and I have work that wastes my eyesight."

* * *

Three months since we buried him, and I still work by tallow.

The workshop at night is a different country from the workshop by day. By day it is simply a room above a shuttered map-seller's shop on a street nobody walks twice: benches under the north windows for the even light, the great flat press in the back room under its canvas shroud, the smell of linseed and lamp-black and the particular sour sweetness of hide glue that gets into your clothes and never leaves, so that even now, when I catch it on my own cuffs, I think of him. By night the room contracts. The shadows take the shelves. The globe in the corner—he owned three globes, one of which was wrong on purpose, though he never told clients which—becomes a hunched figure keeping watch by the stove.

I ink coastlines by candlelight because daylight through these windows can be read from across the street, and a man inking coastlines at a dead mapmaker's bench is a man asking to be noticed. So I wait for dark. I work small. I keep the shutters lapped like folded hands.

The coastline I am working tonight belongs to a harbor that officially does not exist.

I should say plainly what I am doing, because there will come a time—I understand this now, though I did not then—when plainness will matter more than safety. Aurelius Vane was commissioned, before his death, to produce an atlas. The commission papers name it as *A General Survey of Northern Coastal Settlements*, which is true the way a coffin lid is true: it covers everything and tells you nothing. In truth it is a single city, mapped street by street, ward by ward, drawn with a precision no official survey has ever attempted, of a place that was struck from the registries fourteen years ago. A city I am forbidden to name, even in the privacy of my own skull. I draw its streets and do not say its name aloud. I have not said it aloud since he died. Some part of me believes the walls repeat things.

The atlas must be finished by spring. That, too, I must state plainly, because it is the rope I am walking and the reason I walk it. The commission—the money, such as remains of it, and more importantly the legal instrument that lets the work exist at all—voids at the vernal equinox. Unfinished by spring, it becomes what the ministry would call an irregular document and what a magistrate would call evidence. Finished by spring, delivered under seal to the commission's trustee, it becomes merely eccentric: an old man's obsession, posthumously published, too strange to hang anyone for.

That is the theory. I hold it the way a drowning man holds a plank he has not tested.

* * *

Here is what Vane taught me, in the order he taught it.

First, projection. "Every map lies," he told me my first week, spinning the deliberately wrong globe with one finger. "The world is round and paper is flat, so every map is a decision about which lie to tell. Your only sin would be to lie without knowing it." He showed me Mercator's honest distortion, the poles swollen fat with ambition, Greenland wearing the clothes of a continent. He showed me how a projection could flatter an empire or diminish it, could make a border look natural or look drawn by a drunk. "Governments choose their projections the way they choose their historians," he said. "Remember that. Write it somewhere in yourself where the rain can't get at it."

Second, silence. This he taught less by instruction than by demonstration. Clients came to the shop—naval men, land speculators, once a minister's secretary with soft hands and hard eyes—and Vane answered every question with exactly as much truth as the question deserved and not a drop more. He had a manner of listening that made people believe they had been answered when they had merely been received. After they left he would sometimes stand at the window watching them go down the street. "A map shows what is there," he said once, without turning around. "A mapmaker shows what he is shown. The difference between the two is the whole of our trade, Dominic, and the whole of our danger."

Third, and last, over four years and mostly by accident, he taught me that I belonged somewhere. He never said this. Vane did not say warm things; he said precise things, and occasionally the precision itself was warm, the way a well-cut key is warm in a lock. When I drafted my first full county map at thirteen and he examined it for an hour in silence and then said only, "You may sign it," I understood that I had been given something larger than praise. My name, in his hand's tradition, in the lower margin, in the small italics that meant *assistant*. It was the first time in my life my name had appeared anywhere except a parish register and a constable's list.

I learned to sleep indoors again. That took longest. For the first month I slept on the floor beside the stove even though there was a cot, and he never remarked on it, and one morning the floor beside the stove had a mattress on it, and neither of us ever spoke of that either. Small mercies. He was a man of small mercies, precisely measured, like everything else he measured.

* * *

Tonight the tallow gutters and I re-ink the breakwater at the mouth of the northern harbor and think about the day I understood that the atlas was not a survey.

It was two years ago. He sent me to fetch a plate from the locked cabinet—the second cabinet, the one he opened himself, never trusting me with the key—and while he bent over the press I saw, spread beneath a blotter on his desk, the corner of a map of streets with names I didn't know. Names in no language I could place. And in the margin, in his hand, a note so small I nearly missed it: *counted from testimony, not survey.*

Testimony. I did not know the word's weight then. I know it now, or I am learning it. A survey is done with chains and theodolites and the permission of the government whose ground it measures. Testimony is done with ears. It is what you map when the ground itself has been made illegal.

I asked nothing. That was the third lesson holding: silence. But he saw me see it. I remember his hand coming down over the blotter's corner, flat and deliberate, the way you'd quiet a startled animal. Neither of us mentioned it, then or after. The map stayed beneath the blotter. The atlas grew, year by year, in the locked cabinet, and I grew with it, and neither of us named what we were making.

Now I name it nightly, silently, to myself, as I work. What I am finishing is not a survey. It is a record. And records of the kind it is are not finished by apprentices; they are finished by accomplices.

* * *

There is a difficulty in the work I should note, because it pressed on me tonight like a thumb on a bruise.

Vane drew the western quarter himself—all of it, every courtyard and culvert—and his hand is unmistakable, a line that begins confident and ends slightly dry, as if the pen were running out of certainty at the same rate as the man. My lines are younger. Straighter. Where my work meets his on a single sheet, any competent examiner would see at a glance that two hands made it and that one of them is absent. There is no way to counterfeit him. I tried, early on, out of some instinct I didn't examine—practiced his terminals, his little hesitations—for a week in the evenings, and produced nothing but an insult to us both. He caught me at it once. I expected anger. He only looked at the failed copies for a long moment and said, "No. Don't learn to be me. Learn to be legible. One of us is enough."

One of us is enough. I did not understand it then. Tonight, inking his harbor against my own headlands, I understood it completely and wished I didn't: the atlas will show, forever, the seam where the dead man's line ends and the living man's begins. There is no hiding it. There is only finishing it.

Spring is nine weeks off. I have counted. I count everything now—candles remaining, coal remaining, coins remaining in the commission's dwindling disbursements, weeks remaining. An apprentice of small mercies learns arithmetic young.

* * *

Near midnight I heard steps in the street below and put out the candle by habit, cupping the flame dead between finger and thumb the way he taught me, and stood in the dark listening.

Footsteps on a deserted street mean nothing nine times in ten. A drunkard finding his way home. A watchman on his rounds. I stood among the shadowed cabinets and waited, and the steps passed, slowed fractionally—I am almost sure they slowed, though wanting a thing makes a man invent it—and went on, and faded toward the river.

Nine times in ten, nothing. I lit the candle again and told myself I was the tenth time being careful rather than the tenth time being afraid.

But standing there in the dark with my thumb still stinging, I thought about how Vane had taken me in. Not kindness—appetite, I had called it, and it was true, but it was more than that. He had seen a boy steal his pencils and recognized something: a person with no name worth stealing, no history worth tracing, no connections worth interrogating. He had bought silence when he bought me. He had bought, whether he planned it or not, exactly the kind of apprentice you would want if you were mapping a city that did not exist and needed someone who could vanish without being missed.

Perhaps I flatter myself. Perhaps he was merely cold-hearted and I was merely cold. We were, either of us, useful to the other, and that is a kind of love in a workshop.

Or perhaps—this thought came late, by the stub-end of the candle, and it did not leave—he had been preparing me. All those lessons. Projection, silence, legibility. Perhaps the atlas was always meant to survive him, and I was the mechanism of its survival, purchased off the street for the price of a mattress and taught for eight years toward this one spring.

If so, he had left no instructions. That was the thing I kept circling, the splinter in the mind: no letter, no sealed packet, no *in the event of my death*. Vane, who planned everything, who bought pencils by grade and coal by weight, had died—insofar as I could verify that he had died at all—leaving nothing but an unfinished atlas, a voiding commission, and an apprentice who knew just enough to hang.

I snuffed the candle at last and stood a moment in the full dark, listening to the stove tick as it cooled.

And that was when I heard, quite distinctly, a knock at the street door below. Three knocks. Paused. Then a fourth—soft, deliberate, nothing like the fist of a watchman or the drum of a creditor.

Nobody knows I am here. Nobody has knocked on that door since the funeral.

I did not move. Below, after a long minute, came the sound of the knocker being lifted again.
