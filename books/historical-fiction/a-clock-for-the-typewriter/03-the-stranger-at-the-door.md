## 3. The Stranger at the Door

I had been walking for eleven days when I found the workshop.

Not eleven days of continuous walking—there had been trains, a ferry, two nights in stations where the lamps burned low and men in grey coats read newspapers without turning the pages—but eleven days since I had left Bremen with a leather satchel against my ribs and a letter inside it that should not have existed. Eleven days is a long time to carry a question. By the end of it, the question had weight, and shape, and a temperature of its own. It pressed against me like a second heart.

The city was called—well, the name doesn't matter now. A river town, grey-bricked, gull-haunted, the kind of place where commerce happens in whispers and everyone's coat is one season out of fashion. The directions in the letter were precise in the way that only a mapmaker's directions can be: *past the cooper's yard with the iron hoop gate, along the wall where the mortar has been patched twice, third door, blue paint gone chalky, no sign.*

There was no sign. There was the chalky blue door, and above it a single window so grimed with salt air that I could not tell whether anyone watched from behind it. The cooper's yard was exactly as described. The patched mortar was exactly as described. Which meant the man who wrote the letter knew this street intimately, had walked it hundreds of times, had noticed how the light fell on a gate that most people passed without seeing.

Aurelius Vane had been dead for three months.

That was the arithmetic I could not make come out even. Three months in the ground, in the parish churchyard at Edgeholt, under a stone I had stood beside while rain dripped off my nose and a vicar who never met him said words about dust. I had paid for that stone myself—or rather, Vane's money had paid for it, money he had left in an envelope with instructions so detailed they included the engraver's name. Dead men do not leave instructions for their own headstones. Dead men do not leave anything, that is the whole meaning of the word.

And yet eleven days after his burial, a letter had arrived at a boarding house in Bremen in his hand.

I stood across the street from the blue door for a quarter of an hour, because I had learned patience the way other people learn prayers—repetitively, and without entirely believing. My trade was carrying things. For six years I had carried sealed packets for Aurelius Vane: to Rotterdam, to Danzig, to a farmhouse in the Jura whose occupants spoke French with a German accent and never once asked what was in the oilcloth. I did not open packets. I did not ask. I observed, I remembered routes, I changed trains when a man in a grey coat looked at my bag too long. Vane had chosen me, I think, because I understood that the contents of a parcel are none of the courier's business, and because I had learned early that curiosity is a luxury, and expensive.

But he had also written to me, over those years, perhaps forty letters. Instructions, mostly. Schedules. Occasionally something almost warm—a line about the weather in his city, a joke about a landlady, once a pressed flower between the pages with no explanation at all. I knew his handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall. The narrow ascenders. The way his *r* trailed off like a man leaving a room mid-sentence. The peculiar flat-topped *e*, as if the letter had been clipped by some small bureaucratic scissors.

The letter in my satchel was his hand. Every letter of it.

Dated eleven days after we buried him.

*If you are reading this, I have stopped writing,* it began, which was a strange thing for a living hand to write, and then: *Go to the workshop. Third door past the cooper's yard. Tell the boy nothing until you are inside. His name is Dominic Montrose. He will not want to believe you. Make him.* And at the bottom, in the space where a signature would go, instead of a name: a small drawn symbol, a circle bisected by a line that bent slightly at the crossing, like a clock face with one hand fallen.

I had seen that symbol before. It sealed every packet he ever sent.

So: the door. I crossed the street.

My knock produced nothing at first. Then footsteps—not hurrying, measured—and the sound of a bolt, and the door opened the width of a forearm, and a young man looked out at me.

He was younger than I expected. Nineteen, twenty, maybe twenty-one; it was hard to tell with the thin ones, the ones the streets had made before someone else remade them. Dark hair cut badly, ink-stained fingers, a shirt buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the room behind him. His eyes went over me the way a customs officer goes over luggage: travel coat, dusty hem, satchel, the particular posture of a woman who has slept sitting up. He catalogued all of it and found me wanting.

"The workshop's closed," he said. "Estate matters. Come back through a solicitor."

"I'm not here about the estate."

"Everyone's here about the estate."

"Are they," I said. "Do they carry letters?"

Something moved in his face—not fear exactly, but the shadow of it, passing over quickly like a bird across a window. "Letters."

"From Aurelius Vane." I kept my voice level. This was part of the craft too: you do not say the dangerous thing loudly. "Eleven of them. One for each day, if you're counting, and I am, because I've spent eleven days counting." I let that sit. "The last is dated the ninth of March."

I watched the date land. He didn't answer it. Instead he said, "Vane died in January. I buried him. I carried the coffin. I can show you the receipt from the stonecutter." It came out fast and flat, rehearsed—the speech of a man who had been saying it to himself for months, not to convince others but to hold his own world together.

"May I come in," I said, "or would you prefer to discuss a dead man's correspondence on the doorstep?"

For a moment I thought he would shut the door. I saw his hand tighten on its edge, white-knuckled, a boy deciding whether to bolt or fight. Then something else won—something I would come to know well in the months after, that stubborn, dangerous hunger of his, the appetite for truth that would eventually cost both of us more than either of us could then imagine. He stepped back and pulled the door wide.

*

The workshop was dim and smelled of linseed oil and cold metal and, underneath it, something older—the dry mineral smell of paper that has been waiting a long time. I stood just inside the door while he lit lamps, and I let myself look.

Every surface was given over to the work. A great table dominated the room, scarred and wax-dark, strewn with pens in ranks like soldiers, burnishers, a magnifying glass on a brass arm. Along one wall, drawers with brass label-plates, all the labels removed—someone had unscrewed them carefully; the screw holes were still bright. A printing press sat under canvas in the corner like a piece of furniture in a dead relative's house, covered but not discarded. And on the walls, maps. Dozens of them. Coastlines I half recognized, mountain ranges rendered in fine hatching, cities drawn in section and elevation and plan.

I looked at those maps longer than I should have. Something about them bothered me before I understood why, and then I did understand: there were gaps. Cities omitted. Whole provinces left blank where any schoolroom map would have names. It was as if someone had taken the known world and subtracted from it, deliberately, precisely.

"You're staring," the young man said.

"Your maps have holes in them."

"They're exactly as they should be." He said it too quickly, and I filed that away. He set a lamp on the worktable and turned to face me, arms folded, and I recognized the stance—he had built himself into a fortress and I was standing outside the walls. "Now. You'll show me these letters."

"Not yet." I unshouldered my satchel anyway, held it in both hands. "First you'll tell me who you are."

"Dominic Montrose. Apprentice to—" He stopped. The title had become a kind of wound; I watched him step around it. "I was Vane's apprentice. Four years."

"He never mentioned you."

That landed harder than I intended. He flinched—small, but I was watching for small things. "He wasn't a man who mentioned people," he said finally. "Or places. Or anything. You'd know that, if you knew him."

"I carried for him six years. I never once saw his face by daylight."

It was true, and hearing it said aloud made it stranger than it had ever seemed in the doing. Six years of meetings in doorways and railway refreshment rooms, always evening, always a hooded lamp or a fog or a crowd. He had paid well and spoken little and never once asked about my life, which I had appreciated, right up until the moment I began needing him to have asked.

Dominic was studying me now with a different attention. Not the customs-officer look. Something rawer. "You really didn't know him," he said slowly. "At all."

"No one knew him. That was the design."

"He designed everything." There was bitterness in it, old bitterness, and grief underneath the bitterness like water under ice. He crossed to the worktable, pulled out a chair with more force than the gesture required. "Sit down. Show me the letter."

I sat. I unbuckled the satchel. And here is the thing I must confess, because everything afterward grew out of it: I hesitated. Not because I distrusted him. Because the moment I handed the letters over, they stopped being mine alone—stopped being a private madness I could explain to myself as fatigue, or forgery, or grief playing tricks—and became a fact shared between two people. Facts shared between two people have consequences. I had spent my whole adult life making sure nothing I carried could be traced back to me.

But I had not walked eleven days to keep a madman's mail.

I put the packet on the table between us. "Top one," I said. "Read the date first."

He picked it up. Even before he turned it over I could see him reading the hand—his eyes moving the way eyes move when they recognize something, the small unconscious nods of familiarity. His thumb traced the direction of an address. Then he flipped the envelope, saw the postmark, and turned the letter toward the lamp to read the date written inside.

I watched his face do something I have only otherwise seen on people receiving news of a death.

"That's impossible," he said. Quietly. Without drama. The way you state a measurement.

"Read it."

"I can see the date."

"Then read the letter."

His hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady—apprentice's hands, trained hands, hands that had learned to be steady while everything around them shook. He broke the seal. Inside, three pages in that narrow trailing hand. I knew the contents; I had read mine eleven times on the ferry. But I watched him read as though watching my own vertigo performed by another person.

"'Dominic,'" he read aloud, his voice going strange, formal, as if quoting scripture. "'By the time this reaches the boy, the workshop will be unsafe. He will not leave, because he is stubborn in the way I taught him to be, and it will be my fault, and I am sorry for it in advance, which is worth nothing, I know.'" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked up at me. "This is him. This is—" He held the page closer to the lamp, angled it, and I realized he was examining the pen pressure, the ink flow, the micro-habits of a hand he had watched for four years. "The *k*. He crossed his *k*s late, after the next word, you can see the drag where he came back—"

"I wouldn't know. I only know what it looks like."

"It looks like him. It *is* him." He set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Who are you? Truly. Who sent you?"

"A dead man sent me, apparently." I heard my own voice crack slightly on the word *dead*, and hated it. "My name is Ilse Brandt. I'm a courier. Have been since I was nineteen. Aurelius Vane was my employer for six years, and he paid me punctually, and he never told me anything, and in January he died, and I went to his funeral because someone should, and eleven days later this came." I spread my hands. "So you tell me, apprentice. You knew him face to face. Is it his hand?"

He didn't answer at once. He had picked up the second letter, and the third, laying them side by side, comparing—I understood then that he wasn't reading them anymore, he was conducting an examination, forensically, because the examination was easier than the conclusion. I gave him time. Outside, a cart went past, and somewhere down the street the cooper's hammer started up its patient rhythm.

"There's a mistake he makes," Dominic said at last, not looking up. "In his *m*s. When he writes quickly, the middle stroke drops—it happened in his marginalia, in his field notebooks, everywhere. We used to laugh about it. Well. I laughed. He pretended to be annoyed." His finger hovered over the page without touching it. "It's here. All three letters. Same error, same position, end of a rushed line." He looked up at me and his fortress had cracked clean through; what looked out was a boy standing in ruins. "Nobody knows about the *m*s. Nobody alive. If someone forged this, they studied him closer than I did, and I studied him closer than God."

"Then he isn't dead."

"His heart stopped. Dr. Larch certified it. I identified the body, Ilse." My name in his mouth, first time, casual as a stone tossed in a well. "I *saw* him. Old men die. It's the most ordinary thing in the world."

"Old men don't write letters from inside it."

He pushed back from the table and stood, and paced the length of the workshop, and I let him pace, because I remembered what pacing was for. The lamplight swung his shadow back and forth across the blank-drawer wall. Twice he stopped in front of the covered press as though it might advise him.

"He said the workshop would be unsafe," he said finally, his back to me. "Why? Unsafe how? From whom?" He turned. "What did he send you, besides dates and instructions? What did he *do*, Ilse? What was all the carrying for?"

"Sealed packets. I never opened one."

"Six years and you never once—"

"Curiosity is expensive," I said. "I told myself that for six years. Now I'm paying the bill, so let's skip the sermon."

Something almost like a laugh escaped him—one breath, quickly suppressed, the first human sound he'd made. He sat back down across from me and put both hands flat on the table, and I saw him make a decision, saw it happen in the set of his shoulders, the lifting of his chin. Whatever he was about to show me, he had shown no one. He had been keeping it alone for three months, and keeping things alone corrodes a person; I knew that too.

"If Vane is alive," he said slowly, "then everything I've spent a winter believing is wrong. The funeral. The grave. The—" He stopped himself on whatever word came next. "But if Vane is alive, and he wrote that this workshop is unsafe, then he knew something in January that I don't know now. And there's only one thing in this room anyone would want badly enough to kill for." He rose again, went to the far wall, and knelt at the lowest of the unlabeled drawers.

"What is it?" I asked. "What's the one thing?"

He drew out a key, turned to me, and for the first time since the door opened, he smiled—and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man about to stop being certain about the world.

"Come and see," he said. "But first close the shutters. Both of them. And after this, Ilse, neither of us answers that door without asking who's knocking twice."

* * *

Behind the false panel lay a portfolio bound in grey cloth, and inside the portfolio, the beginning of the thing that would burn through both our lives like a fuse: maps of a city with no name, drawn in a hand I knew, dated—some of them—after January.

Dominic spread the first sheet under the lamp, and neither of us spoke for a long moment.

Because the city on the paper was complete. Every street. Every square. And in the top corner, in that narrow, trailing, impossible hand, a note that we would spend the rest of the spring learning to read:

*They will tell you this place never existed. Hold the proof in your hands and remember: paper outlives power.*
