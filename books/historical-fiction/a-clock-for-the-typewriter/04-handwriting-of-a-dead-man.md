## 4. Handwriting of a Dead Man

I did not sleep.

That is not a dramatic flourish; it is a tradesman's observation. I sat at Vane's desk with the lamp trimmed low and the seven letters spread before me like a hand of cards I did not know how to play, and I compared them against the only other examples of Aurelius Vane's hand I possessed—which is to say, against eleven years of his marginalia, because the man had never once in his life read a document without annotating it. He wrote on invoices, on delivery slips, on the backs of proofs he'd rejected, in the margins of atlases so valuable I still feel a sympathetic pain in my chest when I remember what he did to them. *Check this sound depth*, in the corner of a chart of the northern coast. *Wrong by half a degree*, beside a mountain range some government surveyor had drawn with more confidence than accuracy. *Ask the boy*, written on a bill from the paper merchant, which I took for years afterward as a kind of benediction.

His hand was distinctive the way a face is distinctive—not any single feature but the whole arrangement of them. A narrow, vertical script that leaned slightly left, against the grain of every copybook rule. A crossed *t* with a flat, impatient stroke that trailed off at the right end as though the thought behind it had already moved on. The descenders of his *g* and *y* curled back under themselves like hooks. And the capital letters—he built his capitals out of small straight strokes, drafted rather than written, so that even his signature looked like something that had been plotted on a grid. He used to say that a mapmaker's hand should be indistinguishable from his work: no sentiment, no flourish, nothing personal left exposed. *The line is a promise, boy. Make it carefully or don't make it.*

So I knew his hand the way you know a voice through a wall. And the letters the stranger had carried across the city and up my stairs were in that hand.

Not similar. Not a good imitation. That hand.

* * *

Let me be precise about the method, because precision was all I had left at three in the morning, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a spar that is plainly too small for him.

First: ink. I held each letter to the lamp and then under the lens—the big brass one Vane had bought secondhand off an optician going blind himself, he claimed, and had laughed about for years. The ink on the letters was iron-gall, the same mixture we mixed ourselves in the back room, vinegar and gall and a measure of gum arabic that Vane adjusted seasonally because humidity changed how it sat on paper. It had gone brown at the edges of the strokes the way our ink went brown. Commercial inks stayed black or faded blue. This was ours, or something made to the same recipe, aged convincingly.

Second: pressure. You can read a pen's pressure the way you read a contour line—the light strokes are the plateaus, the heavy ones the escarpments. Vane bore down hardest at the start of a stroke and lifted through its end, always, even when tired, even when his hands had begun to shake two winters ago after the bad fever. The letters showed exactly that pattern. More than that: in two places—in the address on the third envelope and in the date of the fifth letter—I found the tremor. Small, controlled, unmistakable. A forger fakes strength or fakes age, but rarely both in the same paragraph, and almost never with such economy.

Third: speed. His writing sped up mid-word and slowed at word-endings, because he thought in phrases rather than letters. I laid the fifth letter beside a page of his marginalia from 1904—a critique of Mercator's distortion at high latitudes, four dense lines of it—and matched the rhythm stroke for stroke. The *t*-crosses trailed identically. The hooks of the *g*s curled to identical depths. Whoever wrote these letters had not studied samples of Vane's writing. They had absorbed the motion of it, the physics, the particular fatigue of a wrist that had drawn sixty thousand miles of imaginary coastline.

By four o'clock I had stopped looking for evidence of forgery, because there wasn't any, and had started looking for something worse: evidence of authenticity.

I found it. That is the whole trouble. I found it.

* * *

Here is the thing only two people alive ever knew, and one of them was buried in Harrowgate cemetery under a stone that said AURELIUS VANE, 1839–1911, BELOVED CARTOGRAPHER, REST IN MEASUREMENT—which last phrase had been my doing, over the mason's objections, because grief makes you sentimental about jokes the dead man would have enjoyed.

In the winter of 1899, working late on a commission of coastal charts, Vane made an error. Not a real error—an error of habit. In the middle of a longitude figure he wrote the digit *7* with the continental form, crossbar through the stem, the way they write it in Karsthal.

I want to be clear about what I am saying and what I am not saying. At the time I didn't know why he'd done it. I teased him—cautiously, the way you tease a master—and he went very still, the way he went still whenever anything touched the locked drawer at the back of the flat-file where he kept work he never spoke of. Then he corrected the figure, and he said, in a voice I had never heard him use before and have never heard since: *You will not mention this to anyone. Ever. Not in jest.*

And I didn't. For twelve years I didn't. Because I understood, without being told twice, that I had seen something pass across the surface of his hand from somewhere underneath it—from whatever country, whatever life, he had been before he was Aurelius Vane of Thimble Lane, purveyor of charts to the merchant houses and teacher of one unsatisfactory apprentice.

The seventh letter—the one Ilse had said was the last, dated the twenty-ninth of March, eleven days after we put him in the ground—included, halfway down the second page, among instructions about oiling the press and keeping the flat-files away from the damp wall, a single stray figure. A longitude reference. And the *7* in that figure was crossed.

The continental form. The error of habit. The error he had forbidden me, on penalty of nothing he needed to name, ever to mention.

Either Aurelius Vane had written that letter eleven days after his funeral—or someone knew that about him. Someone who had known him long enough, and closely enough, and in whatever other country his hand originally came from, to inherit even his mistakes.

* * *

I got up at some point and walked the length of the workshop, which takes nine steps, and walked back, which takes nine more, and did this enough times that I could have surveyed the route. The stove had gone cold. Grey light was starting at the top of the shutters. Somewhere below, a cart went past toward the market, and the horse did that thing horses do, clearing its throat like a bureaucrat about to deliver bad news.

Consider the possibilities, I told myself. I had learned enumeration from him too; it is half of all drafting. Possibility the first: Vane lived. He had faked his death, arranged the funeral—I remembered the coffin's strange lightness, and hated myself for not wondering then, and wondered whether I had chosen not to wonder—and written these letters from wherever he was hiding, using a courier he trusted, leaving me here like a fool guarding an empty nest.

Possibility the second: Vane was dead, truly dead, and these letters were the work of someone who knew him intimately. Intimately enough to reproduce a tremor and a lifetime of motor habit. Which meant someone who had known him before Karsthal was erased. Which meant the letters were bait, or a key, or a trap dressed in a dead man's clothing.

Possibility the third, which arrived around dawn and sat down across from me like an uninvited guest: the two possibilities were not alternatives. Both could be true at once. Vane could live, and the letters could still be bait—written by Vane, yes, but written *for* someone else's eyes, with me positioned precisely where a lure needs its fish.

I did not like that third possibility. I noticed, distantly, that my hands were cold, and that I had been holding the seventh letter hard enough to crease it.

* * *

What frightened me most was not the handwriting. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is the one luxury the dead leave us in quantity.

What frightened me was the content.

Because the letters were mundane. That was the horror of them. Read them cold and they were the correspondence of any aging craftsman traveling on family business: complaints about inns, instructions about the press, a passage about the price of calfskin that ran four sentences and was wrong about the price, deliberately wrong, in the way Vane was sometimes deliberately wrong in his margins to see if I was checking his figures. I had checked. Of course I had checked. The calfskin figure was wrong by exactly the factor he'd used once before, in 1903, as a test. Another message inside the message. Another lock that opened to my key alone.

He was writing to me. Through her, whoever she was—but to me. Eleven days dead and writing to me, in his own hand, with tests embedded in the text like seeds in a fruit, and none of the letters said a single word about the atlas, or the commission, or Karsthal, or the thing in the locked drawer, or any reason whatsoever for a living man to arrange his own funeral.

Which meant the letters weren't about the letters. They were about the fact of their arrival. They were a signal. *The courier comes; open the door.* He was telling me, from wherever he was, that the door must be opened—that this woman was to be admitted, trusted, given whatever she asked for.

Or someone wanted me to believe exactly that.

I sat back down at the desk and did what I do when the world stops making sense: I worked. I took up the pen and went back to the coastline I had been inking when she knocked, the eastern seawall of the city with no name, and I drew for perhaps ten minutes before I understood what my own hand was doing.

I had drawn the seawall wrong. Wrong by half a degree, in the old phrase. And I sat there staring at my own deliberate error, my heart going hard and slow, and realized I had reproduced Vane's test-fudge, his margin-trick, his little dishonesty of craft—unconsciously, the way you catch a cold. Twelve years of apprenticeship and the man's habits had gotten so far under my skin that even my fear of him came out through my fingers wearing his handwriting.

I struck the line. Redrew it true. Sat in the grey light with ink on my hands and the seven letters squared in front of me like documents awaiting a verdict that only the grave could issue.

Either way—either answer—I had already lost something. If Vane lived, then the man whose funeral I had wept at had let me grieve, had planned it, had spent my sorrow like currency he'd counted in advance. If Vane was dead, then someone out there owned him so completely that they could wear his hand like a glove, and had come to my door wearing it, smiling.

There was no version in which I was still merely an apprentice finishing his master's work. The world had come to Thimble Lane after all, the history I'd always assumed happened elsewhere, to other people—it had knocked, and I had opened the door, and now it was sitting at my desk reading over my shoulder.

* * *

She was asleep on the settee in the front room, if you could call it sleep. She lay with her boots on, her travel bag buckled and dragged under one arm like a shield, and her breathing had that shallow, rationed quality of someone who has trained herself to wake at a floorboard's complaint. When I stood in the doorway she opened her eyes without any transition at all, no surfacing, just open—grey eyes, steady, taking my measure before I'd said a word.

"You've been at them all night," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I've been at them all night."

"And?"

"And either Master Vane is alive, or the person who wrote those letters knows things about him that only a ghost or a very old friend could know." I kept my voice level. I was surprised how level it was. "The handwriting is perfect. The tremor is perfect. There's a figure in the seventh letter—" I stopped. Some part of me refused, even now, even to this stranger, to give up the *7*. Old habit. Vane's habit. Silence as inheritance.

"A figure," she repeated.

"A detail. It doesn't matter."

"It matters," she said, sitting up, and there was something in her voice then—not urgency exactly. Certainty. The tone of a person who has carried something a long way and has finally set it down in the right room. "It matters because I can tell you who wrote them."

"Then tell me."

"I can tell you who wrote them," Ilse said, pushing her hair back from her face, "because I know exactly when he wrote them, and where he was standing, and what he said to me while he sealed the envelopes." She looked at the seven letters lying on the desk in the grey morning light, and then back at me. "I watched your dead master seal those letters with my own eyes, Mr. Montrose. Fourteen months ago."

"Fourteen months," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "But the dates—"

"The dates are his," she said. "Everything in them is his. But he wrote them fourteen months before he died, Mr. Montrose. He wrote them knowing he was going to die. Or—" and here she paused, and I saw the first crack in her certainty, a hairline fissure running through all that steadiness, "—knowing he was going to *stop*. There's a difference. He taught me the difference once. And there's something else you need to hear before you decide what to do with any of this."

The stove was cold, the light was grey, and somewhere down the lane the city was waking up around a workshop that held a dead man's atlas, a dead man's letters, and two people who had no idea yet how many living men were already walking toward it.

"He wasn't only your master," Ilse said. "He was my employer. For eleven years. And you need to know what he paid me to carry."
