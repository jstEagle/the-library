## 5. What the Commission Paid For

The workshop smelled of tallow and old paper, and the boy who lived in it looked at me the way people look at a debt collector.

I understood. I had been looked at that way before, in doorways from Danzig to Trieste, by men who recognized on sight that I carried something they'd rather not receive. It's a specific expression—guilt dressed up as suspicion. Dominic Montrose wore it well. He was maybe twenty-two, all elbows and ink stains, with the pallor of someone who had made an arrangement with darkness long ago and stopped noticing.

He hadn't asked me in. He was standing in the doorway of the shop on Wehrlein Lane with the bolt half-drawn, deciding. Behind him I could see the press, the plan chests, the great sloped drafting table with its lamp still burning at midday—a lamp lit for no one, which told me more about him than anything he might have said.

"You said you have letters," he said.

"Eleven of them." I let my satchel slide off my shoulder and held it against my hip like a shield, which is what it was. "All in his hand. All signed."

"He's dead."

"Yes," I said. "That's what I came to discuss."

* * *

Let me tell you how it began, because he would want the whole ledger if he wanted any of it, and because I had spent nine years keeping this account and never once been asked to read it aloud.

I was nineteen and working the parcel counter at a freight house in Bremen when a small, neat old man with a mapmaker's stoop asked me whether I could keep a secret for money. Not those words exactly. He asked whether I could carry sealed packets between cities without ever learning—or claiming to learn—what was inside them. He asked it the way other men ask the time. As though the answer were obvious and only the price needed negotiating.

His name then was Herr Falk. Later it was Herr Brandt, then Herr Weiss, then, in the last years, when we had grown careless with each other out of simple familiarity, Aurelius Vane. The names changed; the handwriting never did. That was the joke I didn't yet understand—that the man could shed identities like coats but couldn't disguise his own pen. Even folded into brown paper and waxed shut, his letters were as recognizable as a mother's face.

The rules were simple, and he recited them the first day like a catechism:

One. Never open a packet.

Two. Never carry two packets from the same sender in the same month.

Three. If anyone asks, you are carrying invoices for a stationer's concern. Memorize the name of the stationer. Invent nothing else; invented details are the first thing interrogators smell.

Four. If I stop writing—his phrase, always, *if I stop writing*, as if death were merely a lapse in correspondence—you will know what to do.

I didn't know what to do. He never told me, not in nine years. I thought it was one of those things old men say to feel consequential, the way generals describe wars that will never come. I nodded along. I took his money. Thirty crowns a delivery at first, rising over the years until I could have lived on two deliveries a season, and there were always more deliveries. Vienna to Prague. Prague to Krakau. Once, memorably, Marseille, where the recipient was a printer with flour-white forearms who wept openly at the sight of Vane's seal before burning the letter unopened in his stove.

I never opened a packet. I want that entered in the record, whatever record survives. Nine years, perhaps two hundred packets, and I never once broke the wax. Not out of virtue—I am not sure I believe in virtue—but out of a colder thing: the understanding that whatever was inside those envelopes had already cost lives, or would, and that ignorance was the only armor anyone had ever offered me.

There were nights, though. There were nights in third-class carriages with the lamp swinging and my thumb resting on the edge of a seal, feeling the wax soften under body heat, and I would think: *one look*. One look, and I would finally know what all this was for—the coded schedules, the changed names, the recipients who answered doors with chains drawn. One look, and the mystery that paid my bread would resolve into something I could judge.

I always took my thumb away. Whether that was discipline or cowardice, I've had nine years to decide, and I still don't know.

* * *

Dominic let me in eventually, because the alternative was discussing the letters on the doorstep, and even a boy raised to silence knows that some conversations shouldn't be had within earshot of a street.

He cleared a space at the drafting table with the automatic tidiness of an apprentice—scales here, pens cased there—and I laid the eleven letters out in order, dated, as he immediately turned them face-up to check. Of course he checked. I'd have thought less of him if he hadn't.

"Where did you get these?"

"From him. Over nine years. I was his courier." I said it flatly, watching his face do three things at once: disbelieve, recalculate, and then—the third thing, the one that mattered—believe, because some detail of it fit something he'd already suspected. Apprentices of secrecy recognize fellow apprentices. "Every few weeks. Sometimes months between. Sealed packets, delivered across most of Central Europe. I never opened them."

"Never?"

"Never."

"And the last one?" His finger had already found the final envelope, the one with the date I knew would undo him. He saw the date. I watched him see it. Eleven days after the funeral he had attended, after the coffin he had presumably helped carry, after three months of inking coastlines alone in a dead man's shop.

"That one came six weeks ago," I said. "Postmarked Hradec. It's short. You can read it—it isn't sealed."

It was the only letter he'd ever sent me unsealed, which should have frightened me more than it did. I unfolded it now, because he seemed unable to touch it, and read aloud, because some sentences need air:

*Miss Brandt—if you are reading this, I have stopped writing. Come to the workshop on Wehrlein Lane, in the fourth city of your itinerary, and ask for my apprentice. Tell him nothing of the route. Tell him only that the commission must be paid.*

*You will forgive an old man for giving you instructions so late. I intended to prepare you better. I intended to prepare everyone better. Intention is the coin of cowards; I have always been rich in it.*

*A.V.*

Silence took the room. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on bad springs, and the windowpanes murmured.

"The fourth city of my itinerary," Dominic said slowly. "He numbered them? He gave you a list of cities and numbered them?"

"He gave me routes. Cities in sequence. This was always the fourth." I had checked that itinerary a hundred times over the years, wondering why Wehrlein Lane sat at position four rather than first, why the man I worked for had ranked his own hiding place behind Prague, behind Krakau, behind a border town whose name I'll take to my grave. I think now he ranked them by danger. Fourth place meant: *come here last, come here only when everything else has failed.* It meant this shop was the vault, not the door.

"Tell him nothing of the route," Dominic repeated. His jaw was tight. "He's protecting you from me. Or me from you."

"He protects everyone from everyone. It's the only way he knows how to love people."

I hadn't meant to say that. It came out with the terrible ease of truth spoken in a cold room, and I watched it land on the boy like a dropped weight. He turned away and busied himself with the letters, arranging them by date with a curator's precision, though his hands weren't steady.

"These are dated wrong," he said finally. "Look. March, April—those are before. Then June. He died in May. I stood at his grave in May. I paid for the stone." A pause, and then, quieter, the sentence underneath the sentence: "I chose the stone."

"I know."

"You don't know anything." He said it without heat, almost gently, the way you correct a child. "You carried envelopes. I carried him. I found him, Miss—" He stopped. "What is your actual name? Since Brandt is apparently a coat he bought you."

"Ilse," I said. "The rest stays mine for now. You of all people should respect that."

Something moved through him at that—not anger. Recognition, unwelcome. He looked down at the June letter again, the posthumous one, and I saw him do the arithmetic that was cracking his world into new shapes: either his master had written this before dying, knowing the date would fall after—which required a kind of foresight that resembles fraud—or the man in the ground under the stone Dominic paid for had never been in the ground at all.

"There's a third possibility," I said, because I had done this arithmetic myself, on trains, for six weeks. "Someone else wrote it. Someone who knows his hand perfectly."

"Nobody knows his hand that perfectly."

"Somebody does," I said. "Somebody spent thirty years making maps beside him. Or—" and here was the thought I had fed on in dark compartments, "—somebody spent thirty years forging documents for a living, and taught the trade to no one we know of. Dead men write excellent letters, Mr. Montrose. They have no distractions."

* * *

We argued for an hour. It was a good argument, the kind that happens between two people who both suspect they're right and dread confirmation. He wanted the letters tested against originals—Vane's marginalia, his account books, the annotations in the reference atlases stacked ten deep along the north wall. I wanted him to understand that testing took time, and time was the one commodity the letters themselves suggested we lacked.

"Why?" he kept saying. "Why fake a death? Why write to a courier? Why draw me a map with no legend?" He wasn't really asking me. He was asking the room, the lamp, the empty chair at the head of the drafting table with its worn hollow where a thin old man had sat for decades. "If he's alive, why not simply come back?"

"Because coming back is the one thing he couldn't do," I said. "Whatever he was carrying in all those packets—whatever this commission is that you're finishing—he spent thirty years building a version of himself that could disappear. A death is just the last envelope, Mr. Montrose. Sealed. Unopened. And now you're holding the wax in your hands."

He flinched at that, which told me he'd already broken some seal somewhere in this shop, already found something he hadn't told me. Good. I wasn't the only one hoarding information. Two couriers, then, in one room, each guarding a route. He would learn, as I had learned, that this gets exhausting.

What the commission paid for. That's what he'd asked me, essentially, when I arrived: what did all of it buy, the secrecy, the routes, the nine years of a woman's life spent as a walking sealed envelope? And I gave him the honest answer, or part of one: it paid my wages, and it paid for whatever this atlas is, and it paid for silence, which is the most expensive commodity in Europe and the only one priced by weight.

But the true answer—the one I swallowed with the last of the cold tea he'd grudgingly produced—is that it paid for hope, mislabeled. For nine years I believed the packets mattered to something. Justice, restitution, history—I never needed to know the word for it. The not-knowing was itself a kind of faith. And then the letters stopped, and the last envelope was addressed to me, and faith acquired a street address: a shuttered workshop on Wehrlein Lane, and a boy with ink to his wrists guarding a door he clearly expected someone to break down.

"If he's alive," Dominic said at the door when I left that evening—I insisted on leaving; sleeping in a hunted man's shop on the first night seemed premature—"then why send you? Why not just tell me himself?"

"Because," I said, buttoning my coat against the river wind, "he has spent thirty years telling people things by proxy. Letters through strangers. Maps instead of testimony. Wax instead of words." I looked at him standing in that doorway, so young, so precisely shaped by another man's discipline, and I said the thing I had traveled four hundred miles to say: "He's a coward, your master. A magnificent one. And cowards, at the end, need someone brave to arrive on their behalf. That's what the commission paid for."

Then I walked down Wehrlein Lane toward a lodging house I'd chosen precisely because it had two exits, and I did not look back, because couriers don't.

I was halfway there when I noticed it—a small chalk mark on the doorframe of the shuttered cooper's shop opposite the workshop. A circle with a single stroke through it. Fresh; the rain two nights past hadn't touched it.

I had seen marks like it before. In Krakau, on the door of a recipient who was gone by morning.

I walked on, unhurried, a stationer's invoice-carrier thinking about nothing at all, and began composing in my head the message I would leave chalked beneath the third lamppost tomorrow—the signal, known only to two people alive, that said simply: *we are being watched, and the commission must be paid faster than we planned.*
