## 6. The City With No Name

I did not decide to show her. That's the part I would want understood, if anyone ever asked—though no one ever would, because the people who might ask were either dead, complicit, or named Hale.

She had been in the workshop four days. Four days of sweeping sawdust I hadn't swept in months, of cooking things in my single pan that tasted better than anything I'd made myself since the funeral, of asking questions with the patience of a woman who had decided she could wait out stone. She sorted Vane's correspondence without being asked and re-inked two of the brass nibs until they wrote like new. She never once touched the flat cabinet at the back wall.

That was how I knew she knew about it.

Everyone looked at that cabinet eventually. The eye went to it the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. It sat against the north wall under the window where the light was worst, plain oak, iron-bound, deliberately duller than everything else in the room, and every visitor to the workshop in fifteen years had let their gaze rest on it one heartbeat too long before pretending they hadn't. Vane used to say the best place to hide a thing was in a room full of things worth hiding better. He said it while standing in front of that cabinet, so I took his meaning to be layered.

Ilse hadn't looked at it. Not once. Four days, and her eyes slid past that corner with the practiced smoothness of someone who had been told exactly where not to look, by whom, and why.

So on the fifth morning, after we'd eaten, I set down my cup, walked to the cabinet, and unlocked it with the key I wore on a cord under my shirt.

She watched me do it. Her face did nothing at all, which told me more than any expression would have.

"Master Vane said," I began, and stopped, because that wasn't true. "No. Nobody said. I'm deciding this myself, and I'd thank you to remember that if it goes badly for me."

"It will go badly for both of us or neither," she said. "Open it."

* * *

The atlas came out in three pieces, because no one piece of it could be lifted alone.

First the base plates—eleven copper sheets, each the size of an open newspaper, each engraved so fine that the lines vanished if you held them at the wrong angle to the window. Then the working copy: the paper map itself, mounted on linen, folded now along creases gone soft as cloth from handling. And last the ledger, which looked like nothing—a surveyor's field book, water-stained, its cover stamped with the initials of a surveying firm that had never existed.

I laid the working copy on the great table and weighted its corners with the ink pots, and then, because there was no way to do this gradually, I unfolded all of it at once.

The table wasn't big enough. The map overhung the edges on three sides, drooping toward the floor like a thing alive. Ilse stood very still. I watched her hands. People's hands tell you what their faces have been trained to hide, and her hands rose halfway toward the paper and stopped, hovering, as though the city beneath them were still warm.

It was a street map. Complete. Every quarter, every square, every bridge over the Kesselwater, every alley narrow enough that two carts couldn't pass. The kind of mapping that takes years and official sanction and a corps of assistants—and there, in the lower right corner, in Vane's hand, the survey details that explained none of it: *A.V., sole author, 1893–1898.*

"Karsthal," Ilse said.

Not a question. I felt the word go through the room like cold air under a door.

"You know it," I said.

"I know the name." She didn't sit, though there was a stool pulled up to the table. She stood over the map the way people stand over graves. "My mother used it as a curse. Tell me what I'm looking at, apprentice. All of it. I've come too far to be fed it in portions."

So I told her what Vane had told me, in the same order he'd told me, because he'd insisted the order mattered—that you could not understand the erasure until you had first understood the city, and you could not understand the city without walking its streets, even if only with your eyes.

Karsthal had stood where the river bent north, ninety miles east of here, in a valley the railways wanted and the mountains made expensive. It had been a manufacturing city, a stubborn one, with guilds old enough to be insolent and a workers' movement old enough to be organized. Forty thousand souls, give or take. A cathedral whose spire Vane had measured himself, twice, because the first measurement embarrassed him. In the atlas it was all there—the tanneries downstream so the smell wouldn't reach the wealth, the wealthy streets climbing the south slope, the Northern Quarter packed tight as a fist where the mill workers lived, ten streets by fourteen, every building numbered in Vane's small exact hand.

"In 1897 the ministry announced a redevelopment scheme," I said. "Drainage. Fever prevention. There were papers, commissions, a parliamentary act. Vane was hired as contracted surveyor—he'd mapped half the river towns by then and his name opened doors. He spent five years walking Karsthal street by street. He said later it was the happiest work of his life." I touched the edge of the Northern Quarter, where the ink was densest. "He knew every family on these streets."

"And then?" Ilse said, though her voice said she already knew, the way you know the shape of a stair in the dark.

"And then 1898 happened."

* * *

Here is what I had been taught to say, back when anyone asked me anything, which they no longer did. Here is the official story, which I recited now to Ilse like a lesson learned in childhood, in the flat voice such lessons deserve.

In the autumn of 1898, fire broke out in the tannery district of the Kessel bend. The fire spread through the crowded Northern Quarter with terrible speed—itself a scandal, later, the tenements being what they were—and when the fire had done its work, fever followed in the ruins, as fever does. Typhus, the gazettes said. The combination of conflagration and epidemic rendered the site uninhabitable. His Excellency's government, in its mercy, relocated the surviving population to labor settlements in the east, razed what remained, and formally dissolved the municipality. A tragedy. A cautionary tale about overcrowding. A paragraph in the almanacs, within a decade.

That was the story. I had believed it at eleven years old. Most people had believed it at eleven years old and simply never updated the belief, because the world produces more fires than one man can grieve.

"Relocated to labor settlements," Ilse repeated.

"That's the phrase."

"How many survived? Officially."

"The act of dissolution gives no figure. The gazettes stopped printing counts after the third week. Vane tried to find one, afterward. Census records, parish rolls, railway manifests of the relocation trains." I shook my head. "There's nothing. Not suppressed—Vane was clear about the difference. Suppressed means hidden, and hidden can be found. These records were *dissolved*. The clerks who kept them were reassigned. The parishes that held baptismal registers were merged and their archives burned in an accidental fire of their own, two years later, very convenient, very quiet. You cannot search for a number that has been unmade."

Ilse had picked up the field-book ledger at some point without my noticing. Now she set it down again, carefully, as if it might go off.

"Then how does this exist?" She put her palm flat over the map, covering the whole dead city at once. "If the city is erased, if the records are unmade—how is there a map on this table finer than anything the Survey Office ever published?"

"Because he finished it before the erasure," I said. "Mostly. And because he spent nine years after the erasure correcting it."

"Correcting it against what?"

I hesitated. This was the part I had never said aloud, the part that lived in my chest like a swallowed coal. Vane had made me swear—not dramatically, not with oaths, just one evening over supper, in the tired voice of a man asking for a small thing—that I would never repeat what he told me about his post-1898 travels. And I had kept that silence through his illness, through his funeral, through three months of working alone, and I had intended to keep it into the grave, mine or his.

But Ilse carried letters in a dead man's handwriting, and the world had already cracked once this season, and a crack is where the light gets in or the fire gets out.

"Against witnesses," I said.

Her hand went still on the paper.

"After the dissolution, Vane traveled. Every year, two or three journeys, always east, always to the labor settlements, always telling me some cover story—commission work, family business, a bad tooth that needed a specialist in the capital. Nine years of it. He came back older each time and worked later each night, and the map changed. Streets renamed with what the survivors remembered them being called. Buildings marked that the gazettes never mentioned. And—" I turned the map so the Northern Quarter faced her, and showed her the small annotations in the margins, in Vane's tight cipher-shorthand that I could read maybe one word in five. "And these. Notes in the margins of nearly every building in the Quarter. Names, sometimes. Dates. He never told me what they meant. He only told me where the information came from, and swore me to it."

"People who escaped," Ilse said quietly. "Before the fire."

"People who escaped. Or people who were moved east and came back, or sent word. However he found them. He'd walk into a settlement sixty miles from anywhere and sit in some widow's kitchen with a pencil and this ledger, and ask her to tell him her street."

For a long moment Ilse didn't speak. When she did, her voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of someone walking on a floor they've just discovered is thinner than they thought.

"You understand what this is," she said. "Don't you. Not a memorial. Look at it properly. A memorial would list the dead. This lists the *streets*. Building by building. Occupant by occupant, if those marginal notes are what I think they are. This isn't a map of a city that burned. This is a map of a city that was *killed*, drawn precisely enough to prove it was alive when it happened—and annotated well enough to prove who lived where when the killing was done."

"I know what it is," I said, sharper than I meant to.

"Do you? Because you've been inking coastlines, boy. I watched you for four days. Coastlines, rivers, honest commissioned work, while this sat in the cabinet." Her finger came down on the Northern Quarter, on one specific block, and rested there as though it had always meant to. "You keep it locked and you keep it secret and you keep working on it in the small hours, and you tell yourself you're finishing a dead man's masterpiece. But this—" she tapped the paper once, "—this isn't a masterpiece. This is evidence. Whoever finishes this holds proof that forty thousand people existed, in exactly the places they existed, at the moment the state says they never did. Do you understand the difference between finishing a map and holding that?"

I understood it in that moment. I confess I had not understood it before. For three months I had thought of the atlas the way you think of a cathedral left unfinished—something owed to beauty and to the dead man's genius. I had not let myself think of it the way a magistrate would look at it. Evidence requires a crime. A crime requires a criminal. And a document naming the criminals' victims, street by street, was not a thing you preserved out of love.

It was a thing you preserved out of fear, or published out of courage, or burned out of mercy to everyone involved. There was no fourth option. There never had been. Vane had known that, and locked it in oak, and gone to his grave—or wherever he had gone—to keep from choosing.

"Yes," I said. "I understand it now."

"Good." She drew a breath, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, the settling of weight onto a bearer who had decided to carry it. "Then we are both of us guilty now. You showed me. I looked. Whatever this costs, apprentice, we pay it together."

Complicity, shared, divides by two and multiplies by something larger than two. I felt it happen. The workshop felt smaller and the map felt heavier and yet my own hands, when I looked at them, felt steadier than they had in three months. It is easier to be damned in company. Perhaps that is a weakness in me. I have had occasion since to think it might be the only strength our species actually possesses.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon. Not on the atlas—on understanding it. She asked me to walk her through it quarter by quarter, the way Vane had once walked me through it, and I did, and it was strange how much came back: the cathedral with the double-measured spire, the guild hall where the insolent old guilds had met, the tanneries, the bridge with the toll house. She listened like a woman memorizing, which unsettled me more than questions would have, because you memorize what you intend to use.

Twice she stopped me and asked me to show her a particular place. Once, the railway spur on the eastern edge—the line built after the dissolution, running straight through where the market square had been. Once, a street in the Northern Quarter. Ten streets by fourteen, and she asked for the one called Weavers' Row.

"Third from the canal," I said, tracing it. "Why that one?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Outside, the light was going amber, and the map drank the color and gave back something warmer, as if the city resented being shown by lamplight alone.

"My brother was a weaver," she said finally. "Apprentice, then journeyman. He wrote home twice a year, and then he wrote once, and then he wrote nothing, and then there was no address to write to." Her finger hovered over Weavers' Row without touching it, the same hover as before, and I understood now that she had been keeping herself from touching this map for reasons far older than today. "That is not why I came here. I need you to believe that. I came for Vane, for the letters, for answers about him. The rest is—" She stopped. Started again. "It is why I will not leave without finishing this. There is a difference, and I want it on the record between us."

"Noted," I said, "for the record between us," and something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone.

I rolled the map with hands that were not entirely steady and slid it back into its long case, and as the paper disappeared I felt the familiar vertigo of it—the whole city folded away into linen and lamplight, forty thousand lives reduced to a cylinder I could carry under one arm. Vane used to say a map was a promise that a place had happened. He said it cheerfully, about coastlines. He never said it about this.

Ilse banked the fire while I locked the cabinet. And it was while the key was still in the lock, while my mind was still full of Weavers' Row and her brother's missing letters, that she said it—casually, turning from the hearth, in the tone of a woman mentioning weather:

"You should know the letters weren't the only reason I came. Vane wrote me one final instruction, sealed, to be opened only if he ever *stopped writing*." She paused. "He stopped writing eleven days before you buried him. And the instruction begins: *If Dominic asks how the atlas ends, tell him to look at the elevations.*"

I turned the key. The lock clicked shut.

"The elevations," I said. "The contour lines?"

"The hills," Ilse said. "What do the hills know that we don't?"
