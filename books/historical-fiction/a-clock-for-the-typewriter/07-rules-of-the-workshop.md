## 7. Rules of the Workshop

The trouble with hiding a person in a workshop built for one man and his ghosts is that there is nowhere for either of you to look.

By the third morning I had learned Ilse's habits the way I had once learned Master Vane's: the creak of the third stair, the exact weight of her step on the floorboards above the flat files, the way she paused at the press each time she passed it as if greeting a horse she didn't yet trust. She rose before I did. She made tea in Vane's dented kettle without asking whether the kettle was mine to lend, which it was not, and which I found I did not mind.

We had not yet divided the space, so we divided it badly, colliding at the wash sink, at the paper cabinet, at the narrow window that looked down on Cooper's Yard. On the second evening she solved this by dragging a chair to the middle of the floor, sitting in it, and announcing, "This line is yours. Everything north of the chair belongs to you."

"The chair is south of the middle."

"Then everything north of the chair is mine, and you may keep your half of my chair." She did not smile when she said it. That was the thing about Ilse—she delivered jokes like invoices, and waited to see whether you'd pay.

I retreated to my side, which contained the drafting table, the atlas, and the oil lamp with the good wick. Her side contained the letters.

That was the other trouble. The letters.

They sat in their packet on her side of the room the way a loaded gun sits on a table in a bad household—everyone aware of it, no one naming it. Eleven days past a funeral. Written in a dead man's hand. Every hour they existed was an hour someone could hang us with.

"We should burn them," I said. It was the fourth time I'd said it. I kept a count because counting kept me from shouting.

"You said that at breakfast."

"It's still breakfast-adjacent."

"Dominic." She unfolded herself from the chair and crossed to my side of the invisible border without ceremony, which was another thing about her: she recognized no borders but her own. "If I burn them, what do we have? Your memory of a handwriting match. Mine of a summons. Two stories, no evidence. If Vane is alive—if he wrote these—he wrote them *to be kept.* Look at the paper."

I had looked at the paper. That was the problem. It was rag stock, heavy, the kind that survives damp and decades. He had chosen it deliberately. A man writing a note meant to be destroyed writes on whatever is at hand. A man writing a document chooses paper like he chooses a witness.

"He chose it so it would survive being found," I said slowly.

"So it would survive," Ilse said. "Being found is our decision, not his."

* * *

We made rules on the fourth day. Not out of any fondness for order, though God knows Vane had beaten order into me until it grew like bone—but because two people concealing one secret in one room will otherwise pick at each other until the seam splits.

Ilse proposed most of them. She had, I came to understand, spent years living by invented rules in strange houses—a courier's discipline, the discipline of a woman whose safety depended on seeming boring.

"Rule one," she said, laying out the morning tea like a treaty signing. "Nobody goes out before dark. Not for food, not for air, not for any reason that feels urgent. Urgency is how they catch you."

"They?"

"People who put other people in workshops to hide. Rule two: the shutters stay angled. Light after midnight only, and low. Rule three—" she pointed the spoon at me, "—you stop apologizing every time your elbow crosses into my territory. It's a small room. Elbows are inevitable."

"I don't apologize."

"You apologized to the kettle yesterday."

"That kettle has earned respect."

For a moment—one moment—the room was almost warm. Then her eyes went past me to the packet on the table and cooled again, and I remembered that we were not roommates. We were co-conspirators who shared a sink.

Rule four concerned the atlas. I would work on it openly—there was nothing to be done otherwise; it could not be hidden under a bed like a love letter—and if anyone came, I would cover it with the false bottom of the plan chest, and she would be my cousin from the coast, come to settle the estate's papers, bored nearly to tears. We rehearsed it once. She was magnificent. She yawned in the middle of her own alibi with such conviction that I believed her completely and disliked her a little.

Rule five was mine: nobody touched the plates. She agreed too quickly, which I should have read correctly the first time.

Rule six: the letters travel on her person or stay under the loose brick by the hearth, never in between, never in plain sight, never discussed aloud after dark, when old buildings listen best.

"And rule seven," she said.

"There isn't a rule seven."

"There is now. When one of us says *burn them,* the other one doesn't answer for a full minute. That gives the anger somewhere to go besides the fireplace."

It was, I thought later, the wisest rule in the set. We used it often.

* * *

The argument came anyway, on the fifth night, over cold sausage and the last of the good ink.

She wanted to burn them. I wanted to keep them. We had swapped positions entirely and neither of us remarked on it, because remarking would have meant admitting the other one had ever been right.

"Hale—or whoever is behind this—will trace you," I said. "Vane sent you letters for years. There's a trail of postmarks from here to wherever you've been. If they find you, they find the letters, and if they find the letters on your body, they know Vane lived. But if the letters don't exist, then it's your word against a rumor."

"My word has gotten me across nine borders."

"Borders don't interrogate."

"No," she said quietly. "Men do. And men believe what's convenient. Burn them and I'm a stranger with no reason to be here. Keep them and I'm a woman with proof of something impossible. Which of those women walks out of a room?" She turned the packet over in her hands. "Besides. You compared the handwriting for three nights. Say it plainly, Dominic. Say what you actually think."

What I actually thought was this: that the error in the letter—the little wrong turn in the ampersand of *Aurelius*, the flaw Vane had made deliberately in his own signature for exactly the purpose of detecting forgery, which he had shown me when I was fourteen and which I had never told another living soul—meant that either the master lived, or his forger knew him more intimately than I had. And there was a third possibility I had not said aloud, because saying it gave it teeth: that Vane had prepared these letters long ago, dated ahead, planted with Ilse like seeds, so that even his death would keep working for him.

But I said none of that. What I said was: "I think the letters are a map."

"A map to what?"

"To him. Or to whoever wanted us to think it's him. Either way, burning it burns the road."

She looked at the fire, and I watched her weigh it, and I understood that she had already decided to lose this argument—that she had argued only to see whether I'd fight for it. People who carry secrets for a living test whether you'll hold the rope.

"Keep them," she said. "Under the brick. Rule six stands."

* * *

The chalk mark appeared on the sixth day.

I want to say I noticed it myself, out of a mapmaker's eye for detail, a craftsman's vigilance. I did not. Ilse noticed it, because Ilse noticed everything that did not belong, and a chalk mark very much did not belong.

It was on the doorframe of the yard entrance, at knee height, where a man carrying something might pause to shift his grip. A small figure—a circle with a tail, like a Q lying on its side—in pale yellow chalk, the cheap kind children use, rubbed halfway away already by weather.

She stood looking at it for a long time. I joined her, and together we performed the excellent pantomime of two cousins discussing drainage, while inside my skull something cold laid itself down and got comfortable.

"Was it there yesterday?" I asked.

"If it was, I missed it. I don't miss things."

"Could be children."

"Children draw suns and rude pictures. Children don't draw surveyors' marks." She glanced sideways at me. "You know what it is. Tell me."

I did know. Every trade has its shorthand, and the trade of watching is older than any guild. A circle with a tail: subject enters and exits through this point; note times. I had seen it once before, years ago, scratched outside a rival's studio in a dispute over a commission, and Vane had wiped it off himself with his own sleeve, cursing under his breath, and then taught me the whole lexicon in one grim afternoon, the way he taught everything—as if preparing me for a war he hoped I'd never attend.

"Somebody counted the door," I said. "Somebody is keeping a ledger of when we come and go."

"Then we've been sloppy."

"We haven't left in six days."

"Then somebody has been watching a door that never opens," Ilse said, "which means they're not watching for what we do. They're watching to confirm we exist." She straightened. Her face, always composed, composed itself further, into something smooth and settled and frightening. "Which means someone gave them our address."

* * *

That night we broke rule three and rule seven both.

She paced; I sat; the lamp burned low per rule two while the city outside went about its ignorant business. Somewhere beyond Cooper's Yard a man might be leaning against a wall with a newspaper he couldn't read in the dark, noting the hours of our candle. Or the mark meant nothing—delivery boys' code, a milk tally, the idle graffiti of a bored child. I am good at building alternative explanations. Vane called it my gift: *You can draft a plausible world out of anything, boy. Mind you don't live in one.*

"The letters," I said finally, breaking my own rule. "If they come in and find the letters—"

"Then we were fools to keep them, and we'll know it retroactively, which is the only way anyone knows anything." She stopped pacing. "There's another option nobody's said."

"Say it, then."

"We finish faster than they move. Whatever this atlas is, whatever Vane hid in it—it's the thing worth all this watching. So we stop guarding the treasure and start reading it." She crossed to the drafting table where the northern quarter lay pinned under weights, its contour lines crawling like contour lines do, patient and meaningless. "You've been inking coastlines for three months. Have you ever once asked yourself why a man would spend his last years mapping elevations of a city that burned down?"

I hadn't. I had been grateful for the work and afraid of the question, which between them had filled three months entirely.

"Tomorrow," I said.

"Tonight," said Ilse. "We've been polite long enough. Rule eight: politeness is for people who aren't being watched."

So we cleared the table, moved the lamp close, and bent together over the contours of a dead city—the two of us, an apprentice of small mercies and a courier of sealed packets, conspiratorially, breath by breath, while out in the yard the chalk mark faded another degree in the dark, patient as its author.

It took Ilse less than an hour to see what three months of my eyes had slid past. She wasn't a cartographer. That was precisely why she saw it: she read the lines as a stranger reads handwriting, looking for meaning rather than terrain.

"Dominic," she said, and her voice had gone very quiet. "These hills don't exist. I walked that quarter once—I know that quarter. There are no hills in Karsthal."

I pulled the lamp closer and measured, and measured again, and the numbers refused to be terrain, and my hands began, very slightly, to shake.

The elevations were a message. Someone had written it in the body of the land itself—and they had chosen the northern quarter, the quarter the fires had eaten cleanest, because no living surveyor would ever come to check the ground.
