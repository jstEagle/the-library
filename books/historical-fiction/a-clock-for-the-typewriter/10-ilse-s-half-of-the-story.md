## 10. Ilse's Half of the Story

He told me once that maps were promises. I thought it was a courting line he'd borrowed from some book. My brother Emil was full of borrowed lines—he was nineteen and beautiful in the way that boys are beautiful when they have never yet been wrong about anything.

*Every map is a promise*, he said, unrolling his surveyor's charts across our mother's kitchen table in the spring of 1898. *Someone drew this line and swore: here is the river, here is the bridge, here is where you are. And it holds. Long after everyone who drew it is dead, the promise still holds.*

I asked him what happened when a map lied.

Emil laughed at me. He had a laugh like a struck match. *Then someone has to draw it true again.*

Six weeks later he was gone, and Karsthal was gone, and no one in any office of any ministry would tell me where either of them had went.

* * *

I want to be exact about those first months, because for years afterward my memory of them refused to stay in order, and I learned that a memory you cannot order is a memory that owns you instead of the other way around.

The official story arrived by newspaper. That was how we all learned it—my mother, me, half the province. There had been a fire, the paper said. A fire, then plague in the ruins, and the government, in its wisdom, had determined that the site could not safely be resettled. The city would be decommissioned. Compensation claims were to be filed through the provincial bureau. Survivors were asked to register their names.

Survivors. As if the fire had been particular. As if it had taken only the city and left its people standing in the fields around it like furniture moved out of a house before demolition.

My mother filed her claim. She filled out every form with her careful schoolteacher's hand, and the bureau returned them twice for corrections that did not exist, and the third time they did not return them at all, and when she went to ask why, the clerk looked at her over his spectacles and said, very gently, as if explaining to a child, *Madam, there is no record of anyone from Karsthal. The registry contains no such municipality.*

She came home and sat down across from me and said, "Ilse. They have lost your brother."

"They've lost the whole city," I said.

"Yes," she said. "But they have lost your brother *specifically*. Do you understand the difference?"

I did not understand it then. I understand it now. Cities can be erased by decree; it takes only ink and indifference. But a person—they had to have done something to Emil individually. He had been there. He had been seen. Someone had watched him walk down a street that officially had never existed. Erasure on that scale is not one crime. It is ten thousand small ones, each with a name attached, and somewhere in the ministry files my brother's name had been crossed out by a particular hand, on a particular day, by someone who went home afterward and ate dinner.

I began writing to that someone. I did not know his name or his desk. I wrote to the ministry itself, to every department that might plausibly have touched a decommissioning, and I wrote the way my mother taught forms—with precision, without heat. I enclosed return postage. I kept copies.

For two years, nothing. Then, in the autumn of 1900, an envelope came with no return address, and inside was a single sheet in a cramped, slanted hand, and the hand said:

*Madam,—Your letters have reached me. Stop writing to the ministry; you are being noticed, and notice is dangerous. If you wish to know what became of your brother, write instead to A. Vane, cartographic surveyor, and tell him the match said to send you.*

No signature. No explanation of who *the match* was, or what the phrase meant, though I understood it must be a password of some kind—a key cut for one lock.

I nearly burned it. I want to say that honestly, because people who hear this story later always imagine I gripped that letter like a lifeline, and the truth is uglier. The truth is that hope, after two years, had begun to feel like an illness, and I wanted to be well more than I wanted to be right. I put the letter in the stove. I took it out again before the corner caught. I stood in my kitchen with smoke rising off my fingers and understood that I had already decided—I had decided years ago, the day the newspaper came—and that everything since then had just been cowardice wearing patience's clothes.

I wrote to A. Vane, cartographic surveyor. I told him the match had sent me.

* * *

What came back was not an answer. It was instructions.

Vane wrote—I know now that he wrote everything himself, never trusted a secretary—the way other men file reports. Short lines. Numbered points. Never a wasted word, never a warm one. He told me where to stand on a Tuesday and what hat to wear so he could see me first. He told me to bring nothing written. He told me that if ever a man named or unnamed asked me about our correspondence, I had never received a letter in my life.

We met in a tram shelter in the rain and he looked me up and down as though appraising a horse, and he said, "You're the one who writes to ministries."

"I'm the one whose brother disappeared."

"Same thing," he said, "from where I sit," and then he was quiet for the length of a tram passing, and I watched an old man decide how much truth a stranger could carry without breaking. "You will run errands for me," he said finally. "Sealed packets. You will not open them. You will not lose them. You will not be caught with them. In exchange, when the work is finished, I will tell you what happened to your brother. Not before. If I tell you before, you will do something brave and stupid, and I will have wasted eleven years learning your face."

"How long is the work?"

"As long as it is," said Vane.

That was 1900. I carried his packets for eleven years.

* * *

People imagine courier work as drama—dark lanes, hidden compartments, pursuit. Mostly it was tedium and nerve. I carried survey documents wrapped inside dress patterns. I carried a brass instrument once, strapped under my coat against my ribs for six hours until I had a bruise shaped like a sextant. I carried letters into offices of men whose names I read in the newspapers the next week under headlines about resignations. I never opened a packet. Not once in eleven years. Not because I wasn't burning to—God knows I was—but because I understood, earlier than most people understand anything, that the seal was the only thing keeping me alive. A woman who has seen the contents becomes a witness. A witness gets erased. A woman carrying a sealed envelope is just the post.

So I carried, and I waited, and every year I asked him—one time a year, on the anniversary of Emil's going, because I had made a rule that I would only beg once annually—and every year Vane gave me the same answer: *Not yet. The work isn't finished.*

Until the year he stopped answering altogether.

* * *

Here is what I never told Dominic, standing in that shuttered workshop with the dead man's letters spread out between us like evidence at a trial: I did not come because of the summons. Well—not only because of the summons. Yes, the last packet had contained the instruction: *if these letters cease, go to the workshop, give my compliments to my apprentice, and tell him the commission stands.* Yes, the letters in my satchel bore dates after his burial, which meant either resurrection or forgery, and both possibilities demanded a journey.

But underneath all of it, underneath the loyalty and the vertigo and even the fear, there was this, plain and shameful: after eleven years, Aurelius Vane owed me an answer about my brother, dead or alive, and I had come to collect a debt.

Dominic thinks the atlas is Vane's work. His master's final obsession, inherited like a debt of another kind. He speaks of it with that particular reverence apprentices have—half grief, half religion—and I let him, because correcting him felt like handing over the last thing I owned. But he has it backwards, or at least incomplete. Vane did not make the atlas and happen to owe me. Vane made the atlas *because* of people like me. Because of the letters I carried sealed. Because of the survivors whose testimony traveled in those envelopes under my coat, past checkpoints, into his hands, one folded page at a time, for eleven years.

I was not his courier. I was his archive. Every packet I delivered was a voice, and I knew it the way you know a thing in your body before your mind consents to it. Why else would a cartographer need a woman with a forgettable face and impeccable nerves? Surveyors have assistants. Men mapping coastlines do not require smugglers.

He was collecting Karsthal. Piece by piece, name by name, testimony by sealed testimony. And he had promised me that when the collection was complete, I would find Emil in it.

* * *

The night I showed Dominic the letters and watched his certainty about the world crack down the middle—his master alive, his grief possibly premature, his three months of secret diligence possibly observed the entire time—I felt something I have never managed to feel since without shame: satisfaction. Not kindness. Not even relief. Satisfaction, cold as a ledger balancing. Because if Vane lived, then the promise lived, and if the promise lived, then somewhere in that atlas of his there was a line, drawn true, that ended at my brother.

Dominic grieved the old man for three months. I envied him that. Grief needs a body, a grave, a date. I had never been given any of those things. For thirteen years I had grieved a vacancy, and vacancies do not close.

"You knew him," Dominic said to me that first night, not quite accusing, not quite believing. "All that time. You knew what he was doing."

"I knew what he was *not* doing," I said. "He was not telling me about my brother. Everything else was inference."

"And now?" Dominic's hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle; he had the look of a boy who has been holding a rope for someone and only just realized the rope goes off a cliff. "Now you want answers out of—that." He gestured at the great portfolio on the drafting table, the forbidden coastline, the streets of a city that officially was nowhere.

"Not answers," I said. "One answer. One street. One date. One *name*."

And then, because he deserved at least the shape of it, I gave him the shape. Emil, nineteen, surveyor's apprentice himself—we had laughed about that, the symmetry of it, the boy who believed in the promises of maps. Gone six weeks before the purge, in that terrible interval when Karsthal was already dying of politics but nobody outside its walls had been told. My mother's forms returned twice. The clerk's gentle voice. *There is no record of anyone from Karsthal.*

"The purge took the city," I told him. "The erasure took my brother twice. Once out of the world and once out of the record. The first one I can't undo. Nobody can. But the second—" I put my hand flat on the portfolio, on the closed cover, on whatever was sleeping inside it. "The second is only ink, Dominic. And ink can be redrawn."

He looked at me for a long moment. In the lamplight he seemed very young, which he was, and very much alone, which he had been, and I realized I had done to him exactly what Vane had once done to me in that tram shelter: handed him a purpose heavier than he'd agreed to carry, on the strength of a promise that was mine, not his.

"He promised you the atlas held the answer," Dominic said slowly. "That's why you really came."

"That's why I really came."

Something moved behind his eyes—hurt, I think, that his master's grand final work had a shareholder he'd never known about, that his solitary vigil had been, without his consent, part of someone else's vigil too. But he was Vane's apprentice to the bone, and Vane had trained him in silence, and so what he said was only:

"Then we'd better decode it properly. Because if your brother is anywhere in these contour lines, I don't know how to read him yet."

* * *

We worked until the candle guttered. He taught me the plates—the projections, the false coastlines, the deliberate errors that were keys rather than mistakes. I watched him move through the workshop like a boy in his father's coat, and I thought: he believes this is history happening to other people. He believes the purge was weather, distant, over. He does not yet know that the men who erase cities keep lists, and that lists have a way of growing new entries.

Later—much later, when the candle was a puddle and he had gone to his cot in the back room—I sat alone with the atlas open to a page near the middle, a district I did not know, streets I had never walked, drawn in Vane's slanted hand with a care that amounted to love. And I understood, finally, the thing my mother had tried to teach me across the kitchen table all those years ago.

They had not merely lost Emil. Somewhere in a ministry, a specific hand had crossed out his specific name. Which meant that somewhere—in a file, a ledger, a confiscated register—his name still existed, crossed out but present, legible to anyone who could get at the paper and read.

And if Vane had spent eleven years getting at paper—

If every sealed packet I ever carried had been a piece of somebody's crossed-out name—

Then my brother was not gone. He was *here*, pressed into elevation lines and parcel boundaries, waiting for the alphabet that would lift him off the page.

I bent close over the northern quarter, where the elevations ran strange and wrong, and traced one contour with my fingertip, and whispered, mostly to Vane, wherever he was: "You kept your materials. Now keep your promise."

From the doorway behind me, Dominic's voice, quiet: "Ilse. There's a chalk mark on the doorframe that wasn't there this morning."

I turned. Outside the fogged window, the street lay empty and silvered under the lamp.

But empty streets, I had learned long ago, are the ones you cannot trust.
