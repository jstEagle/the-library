## 11. A Visit From the Ministry

The knock came at nine in the morning, which was how I knew it was official. Honest visitors come whenever they like; the government keeps hours.

I had been working on the western quays of Karsthal since six, laying a wash of grey over the harbor that made the water look the way Ilse had described it—cold even in summer, the color of old coins. My hands were stained to the wrist. There was ink under my nails that no amount of pumice would shift anymore; it had simply become part of me, the way calluses become part of a laborer's palms. When the knock came—three measured raps, evenly spaced, patient—I stood very still and listened the way Vane had taught me to listen, not to sound but to intention.

Nobody knocked like that unless they already knew you were home.

Ilse was upstairs with the letters, sorting them into an order only she understood. We had agreed on the arrangement two nights ago: if anyone came, she was a cousin of the late landlady, cataloguing the estate for the family's solicitor. It was a thin story. Thin stories hold only if nobody pulls.

"Master Montrose," said the man at the door when I opened it, and I felt the floor tilt a half-degree, because he had my name right and pronounced it correctly, with the soft second syllable, which meant he had read it rather than heard it. "Inspector Ambrose Fenn, Ministry of the Interior. Records Division." He produced a card and held it up long enough for me to pretend to read it. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, dressed the way careful men dress—good cloth, no shine, nothing that would catch in a memory. His face was built for forgetting. "May I trouble you for a few minutes?"

"The workshop is closed," I said. "My master is dead."

"So I understand." He smiled, a small courteous folding of the mouth. "Death, unfortunately, generates paperwork. May I come in?"

Every instinct said no. Every instinct also said that a man who says *no* through a closed door has already answered every question that matters. Vane's voice in my head: *A locked door is a confession, boy. An open door is only a door.* So I stepped aside, and Inspector Fenn entered the workshop of Aurelius Vane as though entering a church, hat in hand, gaze moving slowly across the room with the unhurried appetite of a man taking inventory.

* * *

He did not go to the maps. That was the first thing I noticed, and it frightened me more than anything else he did. A curious visitor looks at the walls—at the framed projections, the hanging charts, the beautiful useless things a workshop shows strangers. Fenn looked at none of it. He looked at the press, at the drying rack, at the cabinet doors, counting them, I think, without appearing to count. He looked at the two cups on the workbench—I had forgotten the two cups—and his gaze rested there for exactly as long as it takes to notice a thing and decide to say nothing about it.

"You've kept everything in order," he said. "That's rare. Most apprentices let a dead master's affairs go to rot within the month."

"He was particular about order."

"They usually are, the good ones." He turned the card in his fingers. "Mr. Montrose, I'll be direct, since your time is valuable and mine is merely expensive. The Ministry is conducting a routine review of outstanding commissions held by deceased tradesmen. Contracts unfulfilled, deposits unreturned, materials unaccounted for. The state has an interest in seeing that estates are settled cleanly. You'll appreciate that—it's really a form of tidiness."

"Tidiness," I repeated.

"It sounds absurd until you consider what happens otherwise. Tools walk away. Papers vanish. Years later someone produces a document bearing a dead man's authority, and we spend months establishing whether it's genuine." He smiled again, mildly, as if confessing to a small vice. "You'd be astonished, Mr. Montrose, at the things that resurface. Documents especially. Paper is patient. More patient than people."

Somewhere above us a floorboard creaked. Ilse, crossing to the window or away from it. Fenn did not look up, and I understood that he had heard it too, and filed it.

"We have no outstanding commissions," I said. "The master finished his contracts before he died. What remains is inventory."

"And the inventory consists of...?"

"Charts. Blanks. Practice plates from my training. Nothing of value."

"Mm." He wandered toward the workbench where I had been working at dawn, and my heart performed a slow, cold revolution, because the western quays were still lying there under their wash of grey—the harbor, the mole, the street grid running back from the water in Vane's fine hand. Not labeled. Never labeled. But a cartographer looking at that grid wouldn't need labels. A cartographer would know a city when he saw one, even a city that didn't exist.

Fenn looked down at the sheet for a long moment.

"Historical work?" he asked pleasantly.

"Reconstruction," I said. "The master liked me to redraw places from old surveys. It teaches projection." And then, because the lie needed flesh, I heard myself add: "Bruges. Fifteen-sixties. The surveyor's name was—well, it hardly matters. He wasn't very good."

Fenn studied the sheet again, and I watched him do something I have thought about every day since: I watched him choose to be satisfied. Not convinced—satisfied. There is a difference, and I saw it cross his face like a shadow under water. He believed nothing. He decided that believing nothing was not yet useful enough to act upon.

"A Dutch exercise," he said. "How respectable." He set the card down on the bench, square with its edge, a small deliberate gesture. "Mr. Montrose, one last matter of routine. Your master died in March, I believe. Buried here in the city?"

"St. Aldric's parish. The eleventh."

"The eleventh of March. Yes, that accords." He put his hat back on, which told me the interview was over before his words did. "You've been most helpful. I'll say this for dead mapmakers—they leave tidy apprentices behind. Do give my regards to your cousin upstairs. Cataloguing is tedious work; I hope she finds it rewarded."

He knew. He had known before he knocked. The whole visit had been a courtesy call from a man who does not need warrants because he prefers invitations, and I had opened the door and poured my fear into the shape he wanted it in.

"Good morning, Inspector," I said, and my voice was steady, and I despised it.

* * *

I watched him from the window. He did not hurry. He paused at the corner, consulted a pocket watch—he actually consulted a pocket watch, the theatrical dog—and then walked east toward the river without once looking back, which is how you know a man is certain of his business. Across the street, the baker's boy who had stood at that corner for two days running was gone. In his place, nothing. They had gotten careless, or they had gotten confident. I couldn't decide which was worse.

Ilse came down the stairs before I turned around. She had heard all of it; the house carried voices like a confessional.

"Bruges," she said. "Fifteen-sixties."

"It came out of me before I knew I was saying it."

"That's what frightened you, isn't it." She crossed to the workbench and picked up Fenn's abandoned positioning—the card was gone but the ghost of it sat square on the bench edge in my mind. "Not him. That it was easy."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to do it and found nothing there but agreement, so I stood holding a rag I had no use for, wiping clean hands.

"I've lied before," I said finally. "Little ones. To landlords. To constables about curfew, when I was—" I stopped. Street years. Ilse knew the shape of those years; she didn't need the map drawn. "But always scared, always badly, always waiting to be caught. This was—" I searched for the word and hated it when I found it. "Comfortable. He asked me a question and the lie was simply *there*, fully made, sitting on my tongue like it had been fitted for it. Bruges. A bad surveyor. I invented a whole dead Dutchman in the time it took to breathe in. And then I watched him decide to believe me, and I understood that I didn't care whether he believed me or not, only that he had no reason yet to do otherwise. That's not lying, Ilse. That's arithmetic."

"That's survival," she said. "Vane taught it to you for a reason."

"Vane taught me projection and silence. Silence is keeping your mouth shut. This was different. This was building a false country and inviting a policeman to tour it, and enjoying the craftsmanship." I looked at my hands, ink-blackened, steady. "Do you know what I felt when the door shut behind him? Not relief. Pride. For one full second, pride in how well I'd done it. Three months ago I couldn't have done it at all. Six months ago I wouldn't have wanted to." I met her eyes. "He took something from me today just by knocking, and I don't even know what to call it. Innocence sounds too grand. But something."

Ilse was quiet a while. Then she went to the hiding place behind the plate cabinet and brought out the letters—not Vane's letters to her, but the smaller bundle, the one dated after the burial, and laid them on the bench beside the unfinished quays of Bruges-that-was-really-Karsthal.

"Fenn said paper is patient," she said. "He's right. That's the whole war, right there. They are patient and we are patient, and the question is whose patience runs out first." Her finger came down on the postmark of the topmost letter. Eleventh of March plus eleven days—a dead man writing dates into living air. "Dominic. If they're doing 'routine reviews' of Vane's commissions, they're not reviewing commissions. They're establishing a timeline. When he died. What he touched. Who came near him. That inspector will be back, and next time he won't be pleasant, and the pleasantness was the warning."

"Then we burn the letters," I said, and heard how the words had changed weight since the last time I'd said them. A week ago it had been an argument. Now it was surrender being considered.

"No." She said it flatly, without heat. "These letters prove he was alive after his grave was filled. You think they protect us? They damn him. But they're also instructions, Dominic—or they were written by someone who knows his hand well enough to forge it, and either way they point somewhere. Look at this one again. Look at the third page. He repeats himself, the same figure, over and over, in the margins. Elevations. Numbers that make no sense for any real terrain."

I had seen them, of course. I had spent three nights telling myself they were an old man's tremor, a habit of pen, nothing. But Ilse laid the pages side by side now, and the repetition stopped looking like tremor and started looking like text.

"He was a mapmaker," she said. "Where else would a mapmaker hide a thing, if not in the land itself?"

I bent over the margins, and the grief that had disguised itself as diligence for three months shifted shape again—became something sharper, closer to hunger. Outside, somewhere east toward the river, Inspector Fenn was writing his report in his tidy patient hand, establishing his timeline, and I had handed him the first entry myself, courteously, at the door.

The numbers in the margins ran north along the page like contour lines climbing toward some high ground neither of us could see yet.

"Fetch the atlas," I said. "We need to compare these against the northern quarter."

Ilse was already moving toward the press room, and the floorboards announced her like a clock beginning, at last, to tick.
