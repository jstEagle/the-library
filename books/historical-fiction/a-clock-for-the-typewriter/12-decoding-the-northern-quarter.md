## 12. Decoding the Northern Quarter

*Dominic*

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time I saw it.

Three nights we had sat over the atlas like priests over scripture, Ilse reading Vane's letters aloud while I traced contour lines with a loupe, and for three nights nothing had come of it but sore eyes and cold tea. The cipher—if it was a cipher—refused every key I tried. Vane's birthdate. The workshop's street number. The alphabet reversed, shifted, folded back on itself. I had begun to suspect the elevations were just elevations after all, and that the old man's last joke had been to let two fools chase meaning into honest topography.

Then, somewhere past midnight, my eye slid off the numbers and onto the lines themselves.

"Read me the fourth letter again," I said.

Ilse looked up from her chair, hair loose, a pencil tucked behind one ear like a carpenter's nail. "The one about the weather?"

"The one about the weather."

She found it in the stack, smoothed it flat against the drafting table between an ink pot and the plaster cast of a coastline I'd been meaning to fix for a month. She read: *"'The winter here is longer than the surveys admit. The hills rise higher than any man has measured, and the river forgets its course three times before it finds the sea. I have walked the northern quarter in all seasons and never twice taken the same road.'"*

"Never twice taken the same road," I repeated.

"You've gone pale, Dominic."

"Give me your hand." I took it without asking—she didn't pull away—and pressed her fingertip to the atlas, to the ridge line north of the city's ghosted walls. "Feel that. The plate is engraved. Every line on this map is cut into copper by hand. But here—" I dragged her finger along the contour. "Here. Do you feel the difference?"

Her brow creased. "It's rougher."

"It's deeper. Cut twice. Once as survey, once as—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real and I wasn't ready for it to be real. "As message."

I put the loupe to my eye and followed the doubled line north. Elevation figures ran beside it in Vane's neat hand: 212, 87, 340, 19. Numbers that had meant nothing for three nights. Numbers that had meant nothing for three months, if I was honest, because I had never wanted them to mean anything. Wanting a dead man's work to be only work is a kind of prayer, and prayers fail.

But now I saw it. The elevations climbed and fell in steps too regular for any hillside. And they cycled. Every seventh figure began again from under fifty. Terrain doesn't do that. Rivers don't repeat themselves in sevens.

"What is it?" Ilse asked. "What are you seeing?"

"A pattern." My mouth was dry. "Hills don't count in weeks."

*Ilse*

He worked like a man defusing something, and I suppose in a way he was. I had watched him for three nights pretend not to hope, and now hope had him by the collar and he couldn't pretend anymore. It made him younger. It made him frightened.

I fetched the letters—all nine of them, dated across four impossible years, every one in a hand I'd have sworn on anything holy belonged to Aurelius Vane. If the numbers cycled in sevens, and if the letters were part of the key, then the letters would tell us what counted.

I read them again, slower this time, hunting sevens. Words of seven letters. Sentences of seven words. Nothing.

"Dominic. How many letters did he send you in your apprenticeship? Real ones, before he died?"

"Before?" He thought. "Eleven. One for each commission he sent me on."

"How many came after you thought he was dead?"

"None. That's the point—these are the first."

"No." I laid out the nine letters in date order across the table, over the map itself, corners overlapping contour lines. "These are nine. Nine letters, seven-figure cycles. Sixty-three." Something moved in me, cold and quick, the way it used to move when a sealed packet turned out lighter or heavier than the manifest claimed. Courier's instinct. You spend years carrying other people's secrets and you develop a nose for when a thing is almost open. "Sixty-three figures in the doubled line before the pattern restarts. Count them."

He counted. I watched his lips move and his finger travel the ridge line northward, past the ruined wall of Karsthal, into the quarter the official maps called forest.

"Sixty-three," he said quietly. Then louder: "Sixty-three. God. It's a sequence. The letters order the numbers—the dates do. Read the elevation at position one, then jump according to the letter dates—"

"Don't explain it. Just do it."

He did it. Pencil, paper, the candle guttering, his hands steady in the way they only became when the work got truly dangerous. I learned that about Dominic Montrose that night: he was built backwards. Calm when he should have panicked, sick with doubt when everything was fine.

The first number resolved was 47.

"That's an elevation," he said. "Forty-seven meters. There's no hill at forty-seven there, the valley floor is at thirty—"

"It's not an elevation."

"Then what—"

"Grid." I pulled the atlas toward me so hard the ink pot rocked. The base map, beneath everything, was ruled in faint squares—standard practice, surveyor's grid. "Column and row. Or row and column. Forty-seven. Fourth column, seventh row. Try the next."

His pencil flew. The second figure was 112—eleventh column, second row. The third, 306. Third column, sixth row. Three points on the grid, and then a fourth, and a fifth, and his hand began to shake, which I had not seen it do before, and he said, in a voice like a boy's:

"Ilse. These aren't in the city."

*Dominic*

They weren't in the city. They were scattered through the hills north of it, in the forest belt, in the abandoned quarry country, in villages that appeared on no map but this one under names I now understood Vane had invented—names that were too gentle, too domestic, too full of old women and apple trees to be anything but covers. Millbrook. Alder Farm. The Two Sisters.

Coordinates. Thirteen of them, resolved from sixty-three figures before the cycle closed, and every coordinate fell inside a structure—a farmhouse symbol, a chapel, a well—that Vane had drawn with the same doubled-line depth as the contour that encoded it.

"He hid them in plain sight," I said. "Every place marked on the map twice. Once as decoration. Once as destination."

Ilse stood very still. She had been still since the fifth coordinate, and I had thought it was concentration, but when she finally spoke her voice had a crack running through it.

"The quarry settlement. The third one. What does it say?"

I read the invented name. "Larch Hollow."

She sat down. Not gracefully—she went down like someone whose knees had received news. "My brother wrote one letter from a place he called the Larches. After the purge. After Karsthal stopped existing. One letter, no return address, and then eleven years of nothing." Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the paper might lift off with her brother inside it. "Vane told me the atlas held answers. I thought he meant answers about what happened. About who gave the orders. I never thought—"

She never finished, and she didn't have to. I understood, sitting there in my master's workshop at two in the morning with a decoded map spread under my hands, that I had spent three months completing an argument. A rebuttal. Proof that Karsthal had existed, aimed at whoever cared to read it. But Ilse had just discovered the atlas was not an argument at all.

It was a directory.

"These are people," I said slowly, making myself say it. "Living people. Survivors. Vane mapped where they went."

"Not where they went." Ilse lifted her head, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit from inside. "Where they *are*, Dominic. Present tense. He kept it current—he redrew these plates, look at the paper stock, the different batches. He updated it. For years. He has been maintaining a list of hiding places for the survivors of a massacre, hidden inside the one object the ministry believes it destroyed." She laughed, one broken note. "And they think they erased a city. They left the city's whole population indexed in copper."

We sat with that. The candle spat. Somewhere below us, in the street, a cart passed, and we both flinched, and then both pretended we hadn't.

*Ilse*

Hope is a dangerous cargo. I know because I carried it professionally for eleven years without knowing that was what the sealed packets contained—not always documents, sometimes just the knowledge that a name still had a body attached to it, that a person hadn't entirely vanished. Corvus Hale's ministry believed erasure meant the end of a thing. But erasure only means the end of the record. The thing itself keeps going in the dark, stubbornly, like a root under a pavement.

At dawn we had thirteen coordinates and a problem.

"We can't go to them," Dominic said. He was pacing, three steps and turn, three steps and turn; the workshop was exactly four paces wide. "If Hale is looking—and he is, you felt that chalk mark, we both felt it—then anyone we approach becomes a loose end he can cut. We'd be leading him to every survivor in the northern hills."

"Then we don't approach. We write."

"With what assurance of safety? Letters get opened. Couriers get bought." He stopped pacing. "No offense intended."

"Some taken, none deserved." I gathered the letters into their stack, squared the edges. "You're right, though. We can't move yet. Which means we sit on the single most dangerous document in this country and we wait, and waiting is its own kind of death." I looked at him across the table, at this boy-man who had inherited an atlas and a war and didn't fully believe in either. "There's another option nobody's said aloud. We finish it faster. The commission voids in spring—your deadline. Vane built a schedule into everything he did. Maybe the schedule matters more than we know."

"Or maybe the deadline is how they'll know when to come."

"Either way, spring is coming regardless of what we do about it."

He almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen anything like humor in him, and it changed his face entirely, and I understood suddenly why the old man had chosen this particular orphan off whatever street he'd found him on. Vane had needed someone stubborn enough to finish a map no one was supposed to want finished. Stubbornness looks exactly like innocence from certain angles.

*Dominic*

She slept in Vane's room, on Vane's bed, under Vane's quilt, and I lay in my narrow cot in the press room listening to the building settle and thinking about coordinates.

Fourth column, seventh row. Eleventh column, second row. Thirteen points, thirteen households, perhaps forty lives. Someone alive remembers Karsthal—Ilse's brother, maybe, if Larch Hollow meant what we dared not yet believe. Ada somebody, maybe. A whole constellation of somebodies, burning on in the dark, and Aurelius Vane up on his hill somewhere—alive, alive, *alive*, the word still hadn't stopped being absurd—tending the flame with a graver and a copper plate.

I had spent my whole life believing history happened elsewhere, to other people. I had built the belief like a wall and lived behind it comfortably. Now the wall had a door in it and the door was open and through it I could see a hillside with a farmhouse on it where a man who remembered a massacre was probably milking a goat, unaware that two strangers in a shuttered workshop held his address in cipher.

Someone alive remembers. That sentence would not leave me alone. It was hope, and hope is loud, and loud things get heard.

Near dawn I heard something else: footsteps on the stairs. Slow ones. Unhurried ones, which was worse than hurrying, because the people who pay our rent hurry, and the landlady drifts, and neither of them climbs at dawn.

I was out of bed and at the door before I'd decided to move. Through the gap I watched Mrs. Ostrova ascend past the landing window, candle in hand, nightcap squared away with terrible dignity. She stopped on our landing. She looked at our door for a long moment—at the doorframe, specifically, at the lower right corner of it, where three days ago Ilse had spotted a small chalk cross we hadn't put there.

Mrs. Ostrova raised her hand and rubbed the chalk mark out with her thumb. Slowly. Completely.

Then she looked directly at the crack in my door, at my eye, I am quite certain, and she said, in the voice of a woman discussing drains: "The corner flat let yesterday. Young gentleman. Very quiet. Works for the government, he says."

She climbed on upward. The candlelight went with her.

I stood in the dark of the press room with thirteen coordinates in my head and a cold spreading outward from my sternum, and I understood that the countdown had already started, and that we had less time than spring.
