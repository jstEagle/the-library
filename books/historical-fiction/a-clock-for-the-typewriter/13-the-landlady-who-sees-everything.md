## 13. The Landlady Who Sees Everything

The landlady was waiting for me on the second-floor landing.

I want to be precise about that word—*waiting*—because I have thought about it often since. She was not standing there by chance, not coming up the stairs with a basket or going down with a key ring. She stood beside Vane's door with her arms folded and her back against the banister as though she had been installed there, like a fixture, like the gas lamp with its green glass shade. She had heard my step before I reached the turn of the stair, and she had arranged herself.

"Good morning," she said.

"Good morning," I said, and started to move past her toward the door.

"The corner flat is let."

I stopped with my hand already lifted toward the latch. Behind me, below me, somewhere in the walls of that old building, a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, and in the quiet I understood that she had chosen her sentence carefully, the way Dominic chose his lines, the way Vane had chosen his letters—the ones dated after his death.

"I beg your pardon?" I said, which was foolish, because I had heard her perfectly.

"The corner flat." Mrs. Ostrova was a small woman, wide through the shoulders, with grey hair pinned so flat it looked ironed and eyes the colour of dishwater—colourless until you looked twice and found they were not colourless at all but pale, pale blue, the blue of ice over deep water. "Third floor, front. Empty since the Kesslers moved to Prague in the autumn. It's let now. To a gentleman."

"That's very interesting," I said. "But it isn't our concern."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't your concern at all." She unfolded her arms and produced, from some pocket of her enormous grey cardigan, a ring of keys, and held one up between finger and thumb as if weighing it. "You'd like tea. You've been drinking that instant rubbish up there for two weeks and it shows in your face. Come down when you've decided to stop pretending you don't need anything from anyone. Second bell. The door under the stairs."

She turned and went down the landing without waiting for an answer, and I stood there with my hand still raised toward a door that no longer felt like mine, thinking: *she knows.* Not guessed—knew. And then, following that thought down into its colder cellar: *how long?*

* * *

Dominic said we should ignore her.

He said it while hunched over the atlas with a loupe pressed into his eye socket, tracing contour lines with a fingertip, and he said it in the tone of a man declining an invitation—which was, I understood by then, how Dominic declined most things. Invitations. Conversations. Hope.

"She's fishing," he said. "Landladies fish. She wants to know what we're doing so she can raise the rent, or complain to someone, or feel important. Ignore her."

"You didn't hear her voice."

"I've heard her voice. She collects the rent every Saturday and comments on my boots."

"When did she last comment on your boots?"

He looked up at that. The loupe dropped out of his eye and left a red ring around it, and behind the ring his face did something complicated—went from dismissal to arithmetic in the space of a breath.

"Two months," he admitted. "She hasn't collected in two months. She slides the envelope back under the door with a note saying *when you're ready, dear.*"

"And a dead man's apprentice has been living rent-free for two months," I said, "in a building where a stranger has just rented the flat with the only window that looks directly into this workshop's windows—and your landlady doesn't mind?"

I watched him arrive where I had already been for two days. It came into his face like weather. He set down the loupe very carefully, as if it might detonate.

"The corner flat," he said slowly. "Front corner. Which windows—"

"Look at the courtyard side of the building sometime and count the lit windows at night. Then tell me which ones face us."

He was already moving, pulling his coat off the hook, muttering something about needing air, cigarettes, anything. At the door he stopped with his hand on the frame and didn't turn around.

"If she wanted to hurt us," he said, "she's had three years of opportunities with me alone."

"Yes," I said. "That's what frightens me. She's had time. People who have time aren't curious anymore. They're certain."

* * *

The door under the stairs opened on warmth.

Not just heat—warmth, the particular warmth of a room lived in continuously by one person for decades. A kitchen low-ceilinged and gleaming, copper pots hung in ranks, a black stove radiating like a hearth-god, dried herbs hanging from beams in bundles tied with red thread. The window above the sink was furred with frost on the outside and crowded with geraniums on the inside, absurd in January, blooming their heads off against the dark. On the wall above the range hung a clock—not ornamental, a working clock, brass-faced, ticking with the patience of something that had outlasted empires.

Mrs. Ostrova sat me at a table scrubbed white and poured tea from a pot that had clearly never met instant granules in its life. She did not sit immediately. First she went to the door, opened it, looked both ways along the corridor, closed it, and shot a bolt I hadn't noticed until that moment.

Then she sat, folded her hands, and looked at me the way an examiner looks at a paper.

"You are Ilse," she said.

"How do you know my name?"

"Aurelius spoke it once, near the end, when his heart was bad and his mouth ran loose. He said, *if a woman comes asking, feed her.*" She sipped her own tea. "He didn't say your name that night. He said it another night, months earlier, drunk on cherry brandy, crying into my good tablecloth. So. You are Ilse. And upstairs is the boy, Dominic, who believes he is alone in the world and invisible, which is the most dangerous belief a person can hold in this city."

"He isn't—"

"He is exactly that, and you know it, or you wouldn't be sitting in my kitchen instead of telling him you're here." She set down her cup. Her hands were broad, scarred across the knuckles, hands that had done laundry and worse. "Let us not spend the whole pot being careful with each other. I will tell you what I am, and then you will decide how much of yourself to give me, and either way the tea stays hot. Yes?"

I nodded.

"I have kept this building for thirty-one years," she said. "Before the war, before the Republic, before all the governments that have come through this city like weather. In thirty-one years I have learned three things. One: everyone has something they cannot afford to have known. Two: the people who seem most harmless are usually collecting. Three—" she tapped the table once, hard—"the state pays badly and forgets faster than any private buyer. So when Aurelius Vane came to me eleven years ago with a sum of money and a question, I knew which side of my own rules to stand on."

"What question?"

"He asked what I saw. Everyone asks landlords what they see, you understand—it is a joke, *what do you see, Ostrova, tell me the secrets of the house*—but he asked seriously. What do you see. Who comes and goes. Who watches. And I told him the truth, which is that I see everything and repeat nothing, and he said, *good, then I would like to buy the repeating nothing,* and paid me quarterly ever since." She refilled my cup without asking. "Rent, he called it. Very tidy man. Paid in advance, always cash, always exact."

"For what?" I asked, though I knew—or half-knew, the way you half-know a shape through frosted glass. "What did he need protected?"

"Everything." She said it flatly. "The hours he worked. The men who visited at strange times and left before morning. The crates that went down to the cellar and did not come up. The year he stopped appearing at church, which everyone noticed because he had gone every Sunday for a decade, and then one day stopped, and no one asked why because I told them not to ask." She looked at me over the rim of her cup, those pale eyes gone hard as enamel. "You have seen what he was making up there. Don't lie to me; I can see it in your face anyway. You've seen the maps of the city that doesn't exist."

My spine went cold. "You know about Karsthal."

"I know about a locked room and a man who burned more paper than he kept and a boy apprentice taught to answer the door and say *the master is away.* I don't need to see a map to know what a man guards like that. But yes." She rose, took down a tin from the high shelf—stretching on her toes, utterly unhurried—and brought back a photograph, yellowed, cracked across one corner. She laid it on the table between us.

A street. Cobblestones, tram wires, a café awning, four people caught mid-step by the camera, blurred at the edges the way photographs blur the living. An ordinary street. Except that I had drawn that street myself, twice, from the atlas's plates, and knew the names of the buildings, and knew that every record of it had been fed to a furnace in 1898.

"My sister married a Karsthal man," Mrs. Ostrova said. "That's all. That's my whole story, girl, so don't look at me like that. My sister married a Karsthal man and in the spring of 'ninety-eight the post stopped coming and the trains stopped running and eventually the government published a notice saying the district had suffered a fire, and then later notices said plague, and then later there were no notices at all and the maps were redrawn. My sister's name appears in no register anywhere in this country. Neither does mine, incidentally, in connection with her. I made sure of that." She took the photograph back and looked at it for a long moment before returning it to the tin. "Aurelius knew that about me. It's why he picked me. He was careful that way. He bought protection from people with reasons to give it free."

The clock ticked. Somewhere above us, faintly, I heard Dominic's footsteps cross the workshop floor—he paced when he thought, three strides, turn, three strides—and Mrs. Ostrova's eyes went up toward the sound with something in them that was almost tenderness and almost contempt and was, I think, both at once.

"The boy thinks history happens to other people," she said. "Aurelius thought the same at his age. It takes some men forty years and a corpse's worth of guilt to learn otherwise." She turned back to me. "Now. The part you actually came down here for, though you're too proud to ask it straight."

"The corner flat," I said.

"The corner flat," she agreed. "Let on Thursday. To a gentleman calling himself Mr. Renn. Reference papers in perfect order—I looked, of course, I always look, and the papers are *too* perfect, the ink is right, the seals are right, everything is right, which is itself wrong, because nothing in this world is that clean except things made deliberately." She folded her hands again. "He paid six months in advance. Cash. Exact."

"Like Vane," I said quietly.

"Like Vane." She let that sit between us. "He has moved in a trunk, a folding desk, and a telescope."

"A telescope."

"On a tripod. Set up at the front window, pointed—" she tilted her head, and the gesture needed no completion. Every window of the corner flat faced outward. Two sides of the building. The street below, and the rear courtyard, and—my stomach dropped as I counted it in my head—the workshop's windows themselves, twelve feet away across the light-well, close enough to read a man's lips if he stood talking at the glass.

"He's been watching for five days," Mrs. Ostrova said. "And in five days he has learned your faces, your hours, the boy's habit of pacing, and the fact that the curtains in the workshop stay drawn except at the top corner, which tells a patient man that something on the worktable must not be seen from outside. He is not stupid. I watched him watch, the first evening, and he does it in shifts—window, chair, window—like a man who has done it before, professionally, many times." She leaned forward. "And yesterday afternoon, girl, a second man came to the corner flat carrying a leather case, stayed twenty minutes, and left whistling. Do you know what a man whistles when he leaves a job well begun? I do. My late husband was a policeman, before the purge made his profession embarrassing. They whistle on the way home from surveillance."

The tea had gone cold in my cup. I hadn't touched it. My hands were wrapped around the warm ceramic like something dying clings to warmth, and I made myself unclench them, finger by finger, and lift the cup, and drink, because I understood suddenly that she was watching for panic and that composure was a currency in this kitchen and I could not afford to be poor.

"Why tell us?" I said. "You've been paid to see nothing and repeat nothing for eleven years. We're not your payment. We're not even his problem anymore—he's buried, or he's gone, or he's whatever he is. You could wash your hands tonight and be clean by morning."

For the first time, Mrs. Ostrova smiled. It was not a warming smile. It was the smile of a woman showing you the knife she has kept sharp for thirty-one years precisely so that she would never have to use it.

"Because the new tenant," she said, "knocked on my door Tuesday evening, introduced himself as Mr. Renn, complimented my geraniums, and offered me double my usual rate—for a room I had already let him—to provide, quote, *a general sense of the tenants' comings and goings, nothing personal, purely administrative.*"

She drew a breath.

"I have taken money for silence my whole life, and I will take it again, because poverty is not noble, it is just poor. But there is silence, and there is service. Aurelius paid me to see nothing. This man wants me to see *for* him. Those are different trades, and I do not cross over." She stood, gathered my cup, and carried it to the basin, her back to me, her voice level as a spirit level. "Also—because you deserve honesty in exchange for honesty—there is my sister. Her name is nowhere in any register in this country. If a certain atlas were printed, in certain places, with certain testimony appended... names could come back. Names could be *restored.*" She shut off the tap and turned around, drying her hands. "So. Now you know what I am. A bought woman, changing buyers."

I stood. My legs felt distant, borrowed. Through the ceiling I could hear Dominic pacing—three strides, turn, three strides—a man rehearsing invisibility while a telescope ate the sky twelve feet above his head.

"Second thing," Mrs. Ostrova said, before I reached the door. "The important thing. You'll want to run straight up and tell the boy, and you should, tonight, before he sits down at that table with the lamp lit and his back to the window. But hear this first." She came close, and lowered her voice although the bolt was shot and the stove roared and the clock ticked over everything. "Mr. Renn is not the danger. Mr. Renn is eyes. Eyes report to hands, and hands act on orders, and orders take days. Whatever you intend to do—finish it, move it, burn it—you have a short number of days in which to do it, and the number is smaller than you think, because a man who rents with six months cash does not plan to need a lease."

She unbolted the door, opened it, and looked both ways down the corridor, an old ritual performed ten thousand times, smooth as breathing.

"Go up," she said. "Tell him the house has grown another pair of eyes. And tell him from me—Ostrova says—that the next envelope under his door should contain not rent, but a decision."

* * *

Dominic listened without interrupting, which frightened me more than anything else she had said, because Dominic interrupted constantly; it was how he breathed. When I finished he walked to the window, stood to one side of the curtain gap where he could not be silhouetted, and looked out across the light-well at the corner flat's dark windows.

There was no light in them. There had been no light in them, I realized, on any of the nights I had counted lit windows—because a watcher doesn't need light to watch by when his work is watching others work. All those evenings I had mapped the building by its illumination, and the one window that mattered had been dark as a pupil.

"He's seen the atlas," Dominic said at last. His voice was very quiet. "Or its shape. Something. Enough to know it exists and that we sit with it under lamplight every night like fools."

"Then we move it."

"Where? It's forty pounds of engraved plates and paper, Ilse. It doesn't fit in a flour sack." He turned from the window, and in the dimness his face had changed—the last softness burned off it, leaving something harder and younger and more like Vane than I had ever seen him look. "No. We don't run. Running is what they're built for; they herd runners. We have a press in the cellar and a landlady who's just switched sides and—" he stopped.

"What?"

"The cellar," he said slowly. "How long has she had keys to the cellar?"

"Dominic—"

"Thirty-one years, Ilse. She's had thirty-one years of cellars and empty flats and tenants coming and going at strange hours, and she has never once, in eleven years, mentioned to anyone what Vane kept here." He crossed to the worktable and stood looking down at the atlas, at Karsthal's streets lying open under the lamp like a wound that had finally been cleaned. "We've been worried about being watched," he said. "We should have been asking who built the blind spot we've been hiding in."

Above us, muffled by two floors and a ceiling, a floorboard creaked in the corner flat—one slow footfall, then silence, then another. Deliberate. Patient. A man crossing to his window for the night shift.

Dominic reached over and pinched out the lamp.
