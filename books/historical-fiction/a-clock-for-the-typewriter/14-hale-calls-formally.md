## 14. Hale Calls Formally

He came at four o'clock on a Thursday, which was the most alarming thing about him. Men who mean to break down doors come at night. Men who come at four o'clock, in a good coat, with their hat held correctly under one arm, want something that cannot be taken by force.

Dominic heard the knock from the press room and stood very still over the plate he was cleaning, rag in hand, ink to the wrist. Ilse was upstairs mending the curtain. Below, Mrs. Ostrova's footsteps crossed her landing, paused, and withdrew — she had answered the door herself, and whatever she said sent the visitor's voice up through the floorboards in polite, measured tones.

"Master Montrose," the voice said. "Corvus Hale. Of the Ministry Archive. I wonder if I might impose."

Dominic wiped his hands twice. It did nothing. The ink had gone into the creases days ago and seemed determined to stay there, a mapmaker's stigmata. He looked at the false wall behind the drying racks, at the letters bundled in oilcloth beneath the floorboard by the stove, at everything in the room that would hang him, and then he went downstairs because not going down was itself an answer.

Hale stood in the workshop's front room where Vane used to receive clients. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned, and he had the stillness of a man who had spent his career being introduced into rooms where people were afraid of him. He did not look around. That frightened Dominic more than looking would have. A curious man looks. Hale already knew what was here; he was only confirming the inventory against some list he carried behind his eyes.

"You'll forgive the informality of my card," Hale said, offering it. "The Ministry prefers its archivists to travel light." The card was heavy stock, plain, the name engraved without ornament. *C. Hale, Keeper of Closed Records.* Dominic wondered who wrote titles like that, and what room full of clerks had decided that *closed* sounded better than *buried*.

"We have no business," Dominic said. "The estate is settled. There are no papers."

"No papers," Hale repeated, pleasantly, as though tasting the phrase for defects. "That is a remarkable thing to say about a man who worked forty years. Master Vane must have been the tidiest man in the capital." He set his hat on the table with great care, brim down, as if it were an exhibit. "I confess I admired him. We corresponded once or twice, years ago. He had a beautiful hand. You'd know — you trained under him."

"I swept floors and mixed ink."

"And learned projection, and graticule work, and how to hold your tongue." Hale smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "You underrate yourself. The estate's valuation lists three presses' worth of fittings, eleven hundred copper plates, and a library. A library, Master Montrose. No man amasses a library and leaves no papers."

"The plates were sold with the fittings."

"Were they." Hale turned his head, very slightly, toward the back room, and Dominic understood that this too was theater — that Hale knew precisely where the press stood, that the whole visit was conducted on a stage whose dimensions the visitor had memorized before crossing the threshold. "Then you won't mind showing me the bill of sale. Purely for the archive's records. We are thorough about dead men's property. It's rather our whole profession."

Dominic felt the trap close with the softness of a ledger page turning. If he produced no bill of sale, the plates existed, and the plates might be anything. If he produced one, it would be examined, dated, questioned. He heard himself say, "The solicitor holds the estate documents. You're welcome to write to him."

"Oh, I shall." Hale said it the way other men said *I shall pray for you*. He moved then, finally, to the window — the window Vane had kept for checking light against vellum — and stood with his hands clasped behind him, regarding the street below as though it were a territory recently annexed. "Let me tell you why I've come, and then I'll trouble you no further."

* * *

What followed was the strangest quarter hour of Dominic's life, because Hale never once raised his voice, never once threatened, and never once said anything that could be quoted in a courtroom. He spoke instead about archives. About the burden of them. About how a nation's memory, like a house, required regular attention — that things accumulated in attics, that damp got into foundations, that occasionally a wise owner cleared out a room before the rot spread to the joists.

"Karsthal," Hale said, and watched him say it.

Dominic kept his face still. He had practiced stillness for years — Vane had drilled it into him across long afternoons: *your face is a map too, boy, and every feature is a landmark someone can navigate by.* But hearing the name spoken aloud in this room, casually, like a place one might visit, was different from any drill. The word sat in the air between them with all its weight, and neither man pretended otherwise.

"A fire," Hale continued. "A sad business. Plague followed, I'm told. The records were lost — all of them, regrettably, to the same conflagration. When a record is lost, Master Montrose, the thing it recorded becomes... negotiable. Do you follow? History is not what happened. History is what the paperwork says happened. I have spent thirty years learning the difference, and I will tell you the lesson free of charge: the paperwork always wins. Always. Because the paperwork has ministers behind it, and printers' budgets, and time. And what does the other side have?" He turned from the window. "Sentiment. Grief. A few old men who misremember. Paper beats memory every single time, and it should. Memory is chaos. Paper is order."

"You came here to give me a lecture on filing?"

"I came here," Hale said, "to make an offer." And now the courtesy dropped away like a coat, not replaced by menace but by something quieter and far more terrible: sincerity. "The estate's papers. All of them. Sketches, drafts, correspondence, marginalia, plates — everything. Purchased outright, at a generous figure. Generous enough that you could leave this city tomorrow and never work another day. Or stay, if you prefer — the Ministry has need of draughtsmen. Honest work. Pensioned. Respected." He let that stand a moment. "Or refuse, and we proceed differently, and I promise you the difference will not be apparent at first. That is the cruelty of my profession, Master Montrose. Refusals are never punished today. They are punished in eighteen months, quietly, through channels you didn't know existed, for offenses you didn't commit. I would rather spare us both. Consider it a professional courtesy between men who understand documents."

Dominic looked at the card in his hand. Heavy stock. Plain. He thought: *this man signed something once, or ordered something signed, and eleven hundred people stopped existing, and now he stands in a dead man's workshop talking about order.*

"I'll consider it," Dominic said, and was startled to hear how level his voice came out.

"Do." Hale retrieved his hat, settled it with two precise movements, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "One more thing, purely archival. Eleven days after Master Vane's burial, a letter arrived somewhere in this city in his hand. Letters, plural, I believe. Correspondence from a dead man is a fascinating category of document — it exists nowhere in any index, which means it belongs to no archive, which means—" he smiled again, briefly, "—it belongs to whoever holds it. Whoever holds such letters would be wise to remember that paper beats memory. But also that paper burns." He opened the door. "Good afternoon, Master Montrose. Give my regards to Miss Brandt."

* * *

The door closed. Dominic stood in the empty room with the card in his fist until the card began to cut his palm, and then he took the stairs two at a time.

Ilse was standing at the top, white-faced, the mended curtain still bunched in one hand. She had heard all of it through the floor. "He knows my name."

"He knows more than your name." Dominic laid the card on the table between them, face up, like a body laid out. "He knows about the letters. He knows the count of them, near enough. 'Letters, plural, I believe.' He's telling us he has a source. Someone who has seen inside this house."

Ilse sat down slowly. "Ostrova."

"Or the postman. Or the man who sold us the flour sacks. Or someone watching the windows since the day you knocked." He found he was pacing, Vane's old circuit, three steps to the window and back. "It doesn't matter which. What matters is what he said at the door. *Paper burns.* That wasn't philosophy. That was a schedule."

"He offered you money."

"He offered me a pension." Dominic laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too high. "Eighteen months, he said. Quietly. Through channels. Do you understand what that means? It means he doesn't need a warrant. He doesn't need soldiers. He needs time, and patience, and a clerk in every office between me and the rest of my life. He's going to erase us the way he erased Karsthal. Slowly. On paper first."

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Then we stop being paper."

Dominic stopped pacing. "What?"

"Everything he owns is paper — records, indexes, filings, signatures. Everything we own is ink and copper and one old press behind a false wall. He told you exactly how to beat him. He thinks paper wins because paper has ministers behind it." She picked up the card, turned it over, and set it down again. "So we put the atlas behind more hands than he can buy. We make it so many pieces of paper that burning one, or ten, or a hundred changes nothing. He erased a city because the city existed in only one set of files. Fine. The atlas will exist everywhere."

"That's weeks of printing. Maybe longer. And he's given us—"

"—eighteen months," Ilse said. "Quietly. Through channels." She almost smiled. "He's arrogant, Dominic. He told you his timetable to frighten you. Arrogant men tell you their timetables."

But when she left the room, Dominic stayed at the window, watching the street below. Across the road, outside the chandler's shop, a man stood reading a broadsheet he held upside down. He read it with great concentration for the better part of ten minutes, and then folded it, tucked it under his arm, and walked unhurriedly toward the corner flat above the chandler's — the flat that had stood empty since autumn, and that, Dominic now remembered with a cold settling in his stomach, had been let just last week to a quiet gentleman who kept irregular hours.

Paper beat memory. Perhaps. But memory had just moved in across the street, and it was keeping watch.
