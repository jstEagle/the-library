## 15. What Vane Died For

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when I understood what I was holding.

We had been at the cipher for six nights by then. Six nights of Dominic hunched over the atlas with a magnifier and a lamp, tracing contour lines the way other men read scripture, while I copied elevations into a ledger and checked them against the tables Vane had left folded inside the back board. It was slow work, patient work, the kind of work I had done for years without ever knowing its purpose—carrying sealed packets, memorizing routes, asking no questions. I had gotten good at not knowing things. Now every hour of not-knowing sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed whole.

The northern quarter had given itself up two nights ago: coordinates hidden in the elevations, each one pointing to a cellar, an attic, a hayloft outside the city limits. Names followed, encoded in the intervals between contours—Vane had built a second alphabet out of the distances themselves, and once Dominic found the trick of it, the names came spilling out like water from a kicked-over bucket.

Ada Kessler. Marta Vogel. The Brandt brothers. Eleven names before dawn on the first night. Fourteen more since.

But tonight was different. Tonight Dominic had turned the atlas to the western districts—the river quarter, the part of Karsthal that the official histories said had burned first, accidentally, in the chaos of the purge—and he had gone very still over it in a way that frightened me.

"Look at this," he said.

I came around the table. The candle guttered. Outside, Mrs. Ostrova's cat crossed the yard below the window, and I caught myself tracking it out of habit, the way you track anything that moves when you believe you're watched. We were watched. That had been established. The chalk mark on the doorframe had been joined by a second, then a third, small white ticks like a tally being kept by someone with all the time in the world.

"Look at what?" I said.

"The contours." He tapped the map with a fingernail. "The river quarter is flat. Everyone knows it's flat—it's floodplain, that's why the docks were there. But look what Vane drew."

I leaned in. The lines wobbled. Not much—a meter here, two meters there, gentle undulations across terrain that should have been level as a table. My first thought was that it was a mistake, that the old man's hand had shaken. But Vane's hand never shook. And mistakes didn't repeat.

Because they did repeat. Once you saw it, you couldn't unsee it. The same pattern of rises and dips appeared again along the eastern canal, again in the southern warehouse district, transposed slightly, like a melody moving through different keys.

"It's the same sequence," I said slowly.

"Yes."

"Every district that reported fires."

"Yes." Dominic straightened up and rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. He looked terrible. We both looked terrible. Six nights of cipher work will do that to you, and so will sleeping in shifts in a shuttered workshop with a dead man's coat on the hook and a live man's enemies on the street. "It's not topography, Ilse. It never was—not in these sections. He mapped something else."

"What?"

"I don't know yet. But it's ordered. It repeats. And it only appears where Karsthal burned."

* * *

We worked until the candle died and lit another, and neither of us suggested stopping, because we both knew what we were afraid we'd found and neither of us wanted to say it alone.

It took me until nearly four in the morning to break the transposition. Dominic had found the encoding principle; I found the key. It was my brother who'd taught me puzzles—Emil, who vanished into Karsthal in the spring of 1898 and never came out again—so perhaps it was fitting that it was me, not the master mapmaker's apprentice, who saw that the elevation deviations weren't just a repeated sequence but a *count*. Each rise was a number. Each dip was a number. And numbers, arranged in threes, arranged in rows, arranged in columns marching across the western river quarter—

"Dates," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "They're dates. And times."

Dominic looked up. "Times?"

"Hours of the night. Look—" I pulled the ledger toward me and wrote them out in a column, my hand steady in that way your hand goes steady when the thing you're doing is too important to shake. "Here. Western district. The first sequence resolves to the twelfth of May, 1898. Between one and two in the morning. Then the next: the twelfth of May, between two and three. Then three and four. District by district. Quarter by quarter." I kept writing. "It's a schedule, Dominic. Someone went through Karsthal fire by fire, street by street, in order, all through the night, and Vane mapped the route."

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere below, the cat or something else moved across the cobbles. Dominic had stopped breathing, I think, or close to it.

"Fires spread randomly," he said finally. But his voice said he didn't believe it either.

"Fires don't keep appointments."

* * *

I want to set down what happened in my head in that hour, because I have tried to explain it to people since and never found the words. Perhaps I will here.

I had grown up on the official story the way you grow up on weather. The Karsthal fire, the plague that followed it, the tragedy that emptied a city of forty thousand souls and scattered the survivors to the winds. It happened when I was nineteen. It was in the newspapers for a week and then it wasn't, and then the city itself wasn't, and then even the name wasn't—you couldn't find Karsthal on a map anymore, couldn't find it in a gazetteer, couldn't find it in the census rolls. As if forty thousand people had lived their whole lives in a rumor.

And I had believed, in the way you believe things that let you keep functioning, that it had been a catastrophe. Terrible, yes. Unjust in its aftermath, in the erasure, in the way the government had closed the file and locked the drawer—yes, that had always smelled wrong to me. Emil's letters had stopped coming six weeks *before* the fire, which meant nothing and everything, and for eleven years I had carried the arithmetic of that without once letting myself finish the sum.

But I had believed in chaos. Chaos was survivable. Chaos meant my brother had died in an accident of history, in the ordinary cruelty of crowded cities and wooden buildings and slow ambulances. Chaos meant no one had *decided*.

The pages in front of me were not chaos.

Vane's contour lines laid it out as plainly as a train timetable. The fires had begun at the river at one in the morning and moved outward in a fixed rotation, district by district, each ignition separated from the last by almost exactly fifty minutes. Fifty minutes. Not the interval of spreading flame—that depends on wind, on building materials, on luck. Fifty minutes is the interval of men walking. Men walking from place to place with lamps or oil or whatever they carried, working down a list.

"They had a route," I said. "They had an itinerary. Someone planned this the way you plan a delivery round."

Dominic didn't answer. He was copying the eastern district sequences now, checking them against mine, and I watched his face do what faces do when a certainty dies: nothing at first, and then everything.

"And the ministry knew," he said quietly. "That's the part that—" He stopped. Started again. "Ilse, these aren't just coordinates and times. There are annotations. Look at the marginal codes, the ones we thought were surveyor's marks. Once you apply the date-key, they resolve." He pushed a sheet toward me. "This one reads 'Min. Warrant 114.' This one, 'Min. Warrant 117.' They're numbered, Ilse. The fires had *file numbers*."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, the way flames do when there is no draft, no movement, no reason for anything in the room to change.

"The Ministry of Interior," I said.

"The same ministry that employs Hale."

* * *

We sat with it for a long time. I don't remember how long. An hour, maybe more. Two people at a table covered in papers, in a city that had decided eleven years ago that some things should not exist, holding in our hands the proof that the decision had been made before the fact, not after. Before the fire, someone had drawn up warrants. Before the warrants, someone had drawn up a plan. Before the plan, someone had decided that forty thousand lives were a problem with an administrative solution.

And Aurelius Vane—dead man, living man, coward, saint, I didn't know which and I began to suspect he hadn't known either—had been hired, sometime afterward, to make sure no map of it survived. And had instead spent his remaining years making the only honest map anyone would ever see: not a map of streets, but a map of the crime. Every contour a timestamp. Every benchmark a warrant number. The massacre rendered in the language of topography, hiding in plain sight under the guise of an obsolete atlas commissioned by a dead client for reasons no one would ever ask about.

"He planned it," I said. "From the beginning. From before his death. This atlas was never a commission. It was a testimony."

Dominic nodded slowly. His hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the papers might fly away.

"Then why hide it?" he asked. "Why encode it? Why not simply print the thing and stand in front of it?"

"Because printing it gets you killed and buries the truth with you." I heard my own voice and was surprised by how cold it sounded. "You scatter the evidence into a code only patient people can crack. You let it survive by being invisible. It's what he taught you, isn't it? Projection and silence."

"That's not silence," Dominic said. "That's something else. That's—" He searched for the word and didn't find it, so I gave him one.

"Treachery," I said. "Against the ministry. Or loyalty. Depends which side of the map you're standing on."

* * *

Near dawn I went to the window and opened the shutter a finger's width, which was as much as either of us dared. The yard below lay gray and still. Across the lane, the corner flat's curtains were drawn, but behind them, faintly, a lamp burned. It had burned at that hour every night that week. Hale—or whoever Hale had installed there—kept late hours. Patient hours. Tally-mark hours.

I thought about Emil again, the way I had thought about him every day for eleven years and every minute for six nights. Emil, who wrote letters full of jokes and cheap coffee complaints and socialist pamphleteering he thought Maman didn't know about. Emil, who moved to Karsthal because it was cheap and lively and full of printers. Emil, whose letters stopped in April 1898—*before* the fire, before the purge, before any of the story we were told.

If the purge was scheduled—if it was planned in advance, mapped, warranted, filed—then the arrests had come first. Of course they had. That was how you did it properly: take the organizers, the printers, the loud ones, the letter-writers, weeks ahead, quietly, so that when the fire came there was no one left to organize the screaming. Emil hadn't died in the chaos. Emil had been disappeared in the *preparation* for the chaos, and the chaos had been invented afterward to account for the empty houses.

Eleven years I had grieved an accident. The grief itself had been lied to.

Something settled in me then. I can't call it resolve; resolve sounds clean, and this was not clean. It was heavy. It was the weight of a thing that had been true all along and was only now resting on my shoulders because I had finally walked underneath it. The atlas on the table behind me weighed perhaps four pounds. What it proved weighed more than the building.

"Dominic," I said, without turning from the window. "Do you understand what this is?"

He was quiet a moment. "Evidence."

"No. It's the only evidence. Everything else is gone—the warrants, the files, the bodies, the witnesses, the city itself. The ministry erased all of it. They missed one thing: a mapmaker who was better at hiding things than they were at destroying them." I turned around. The candle had almost finished again; his face floated above the table, half-lit, young and old at once. "There is exactly one copy of the truth of Karsthal in the world, and it is sitting on this table, and Corvus Hale knows it exists. That's why he's across the street. That's why the chalk marks. He doesn't know what we have, but he knows Vane, and he knows what Vane knew."

"Then we finish it," Dominic said. "We decode the rest and we—"

"We can't decode the rest." I came back to the table and showed him what I'd been staring at for the past hour: the southern districts, the industrial quarter, the section we'd saved for last because it was densest. The cipher ran dry halfway through. The sequences repeated without resolving, the transposition collapsing into noise. "The second layer needs a key. A literal key—a word, a phrase, something. It's structured for it. Vane designed it so the atlas alone isn't enough. So that anyone who stole it would hold half a testament."

Dominic bent over the unresolved sequences, and I watched hope and dread cross his face in the same moment, because we both arrived at the same conclusion together, silently, the way two people in a small room eventually share every thought.

"So where's the key?" he asked.

"On his person," I said. "Wherever Vane kept things he couldn't afford to lose, he kept the key there. Which means—" I stopped, because saying it made it real. "Which means it was buried with him. Three months ago. In the parish ground at Saint Aldric's. Under his name, if that's even his name."

Dominic stared at the dead cipher. Then at me. Then at the window, where the gray was turning to pale gold and the lamp across the street still burned, patient as a spider.

"There's something else," he said, very quietly. "Something I haven't told you. About the funeral."
