## 16. The Second Key

The cipher broke our hearts before it broke open.

For six days Ilse and I worked the northern quarter of the atlas by lamplight, tracing contour lines that were not contour lines, and every answer we pulled from the paper only asked a better question. Vane had built the thing like a lock with two tumblers. The first was the atlas itself—the elevations, the false rivers, the streets that bent at angles no surveyor would forgive. We had that tumbler. We could feel the mechanism turning beneath our fingers.

The second was missing.

We found its ghost on the seventh night, in the margin of plate nineteen, where Vane had drawn a small compass rose unlike any of his others. Eight points instead of sixteen, and at the center, instead of a star, a tiny blank circle—a socket, waiting for something to be set into it. Beneath it, in letters so small I had to bend until my nose nearly touched the vellum:

*It is on me. Where I am, it is.*

Ilse read it upside down, the way she'd learned to read everything in that workshop, and then she sat back and looked at me across the table.

"Where you are," she repeated. "He means his grave."

"I know what he means."

"Do you?" She pushed the lamp aside so the flame threw her shadow long across the plates. "Because you've been staring at that sentence for four minutes without blinking, and a man who knows what a sentence means doesn't stare at it like it's bitten him."

I hadn't blinked. I noticed it only when she said so, and then my eyes stung, and I blamed the lamplight the way I blamed everything those days.

"He kept the key on his person," I said. "That's what *on me* means—not hidden, not deposited, not entrusted. On his body. Which means when they buried him—"

"—it went into the ground with him." Ilse finished it flatly, because finishing my sentences had become one of her small cruelties, and mine to endure. "Or it didn't."

"Or it didn't," I agreed, and we sat with that a moment, the two of us and the dead man's handwriting, while the stove ticked and somewhere above us Mrs. Ostrova's floorboards creaked their slow nightly arithmetic.

Here is what neither of us said aloud: that a man who fakes his own death does not leave the proof of his cipher buried six feet under parish earth. That if the key was truly *on him*, then either the grave held nothing but a coffin weighted with stones—or it held a coffin weighted with stones *and* a dead man who had never needed a key at all, because the whole funeral had been theater staged for an audience of mourners he never intended to deceive twice.

Or—and this was the thought I kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth—it held exactly what the coffin was supposed to hold, and someone else had already been there after him.

* * *

We argued about it the way we argued about everything now: quietly, in fragments, with one ear tuned to the street.

"We dig," Ilse said.

"It's consecrated ground. St. Aldhelm's. There's a verger, there are parishioners, there is—" I gestured vaguely at the window, the city, the whole apparatus of law and propriety— "society."

"There's frost," she said. "The ground will be soft on top and frozen an inch down, which means spadework, which means noise, which means we go at three in the morning when even the verger's God is asleep." She said it the way she said most things—as though she had already done it once, somewhere, for worse reasons, and survived. "Besides. You want to stand in a churchyard and *inquire politely*? Ask the vicar whether anyone's robbed the grave of Aurelius Vane lately? Shall I write to the ministry and save them the trip?"

"No one said inquire politely."

"You bristle whenever I say the word dig. You've been bristling for an hour. If you have another idea, produce it. Otherwise fetch your coat."

I didn't have another idea. What I had was a childhood spent learning that invisible men survive by never touching anything that leaves a mark, and a grave is the largest mark a man can leave. But Ilse was already moving through the workshop with that terrible economy of hers, pulling the oilcloth off the tool chest, wrapping a hand spade and a lantern in sacking, and arguing with Ilse mid-preparation was like arguing with a tide.

So I fetched my coat.

We went out through the yard at half past two, over the wall behind the coal store where the bricks had been loosened by boys whose names I had once known, and we crossed the sleeping city carrying a dead man's tools between us like something ashamed of itself.

* * *

St. Aldhelm's sat in its own weather at the top of Parrow Hill, a squat gray church with a graveyard that sloped away toward the river fog. The gates were chained but the chain was old and the gap between the lowest bars admitted a person who had spent her youth being small in places she shouldn't be, and then admitted me, who had spent the same years being exactly that.

Frost silvered the stones. Our breath hung. The city below made its low continuous sound, coal smoke and distant trains, indifferent as water.

Vane's grave was in the northeast corner, under a yew, marked by a modest slab I had chosen myself three months ago because there had been no family to choose it and no money for anything grander. *Aurelius Vane, Cartographer.* No dates. He had dictated the inscription himself weeks before his death, laughing into his beard about how dates were for men who intended to stay put. At the time I had taken it for an old man's joke. Standing over it now, with a spade in my hands and frost soaking through my knees, it read differently. It read like stage directions.

"He wrote his own epitaph," I said.

"They usually do," Ilse said, and set the lantern down with its shutter turned so the light fell in a blade across the stone. "Dig from the foot end. Less chance of breaking the lid if the coffin's shallow."

"How do you know these things?"

"Brother," she said, and nothing else, and took the other spade.

We cut through the crust of frost and then the ground gave way to softer going, dark and root-threaded, and we dug in shifts because the noise had to be rationed—four strokes each, then stillness, listening, the city's sound checked against ours. The yew dripped. Somewhere a fox screamed once, far off, and my heart slammed so hard I had to lean on the spade. Ilse didn't even pause. Grief had given her a steadiness I envied and did not understand; mine came out as diligence, hers as armor, and neither of us had yet learned to trade.

Two feet down we hit stone. Not wood—stone. A slab, flat-laid, spanning the whole width of the pit.

"That's not a coffin," I said.

"No," Ilse said. "It isn't."

We cleared it with our hands, cold water and cold earth working into every seam of our gloves, until the slab lay bare under the lantern's blade: a flat flag of granite, unmarked, laid directly over—nothing. Because when we levered it up on edge, edging it carefully aside with both of us grunting like dock workers and hating every sound, the space beneath was empty.

Not a coffin. Not stones. Not bones. A void, neat-sided, the walls of the cut still holding their shape in the frozen clay, exactly the dimensions of a graveshaft and containing absolutely nothing at all.

The lantern hissed. The yew dripped. Ilse knelt at the lip of the hole with the light, and I watched her face do something complicated—hope and horror arriving together, twins, indistinguishable.

"He was never here," she said.

"He was never here," I agreed, and the words came out strange, half prayer, half curse.

Three months I had grieved for this plot of ground. Three months of ink and silence, telling myself the master was in the earth the way other people's beloved dead were in the earth, because believing it was the only way to keep my hands steady on the coastlines. And the whole time I had been mourning a hole. A hole in the ground, a hole in the world, a hole shaped exactly like a man who had looked me in the eye over supper and lied to me with the full authority of forty years of trust.

I sat back on my heels in the ruined grave and laughed. It came out wrong—too loud, too raw—and Ilse's hand closed on my wrist, hard, warning, but I couldn't stop it. Horror and vindication, entwined exactly as I'd feared: he was alive, which meant everything; he was alive and had let me bury him, which meant worse than nothing.

"Dominic." Low, urgent. "Look at the sides."

I looked. My laughter died in my throat.

The walls of the shaft weren't virgin clay. On the near side, where the lantern's blade fell cleanest, there were marks—spade marks, old ones, softened by months of frost but unmistakable to anyone who had spent ten years cutting sections for map foundations. Someone had opened this grave after the funeral. Cut down through the backfill neatly, from the foot end, the way a professional would—*the way Ilse had just told me to*—removed whatever needed removing, and closed it again with care. The slab had been re-laid. The turf re-fitted. From above, in summer grass, no one would ever have known.

Except the fill was different. Fresher soil settles darker, and three months of English winter had not quite erased the seam.

"Someone beat us here," Ilse said. Her voice had gone very quiet, very level—the voice, I would learn, she used when she was most afraid. "Months ago."

"Or—" I started, and couldn't finish it, because the alternative was too large.

"Or he opened his own grave on his way out of it," she finished for me, because that was her cruelty and my burden, "and took his own key with him underground and out the other side. Either way, Dominic—" She stood, brushing clay from her knees, and swept the lantern blade slowly around the churchyard, over the leaning stones and the black yew and the fog thickening along the wall. "Either way, the key isn't in the ground. It's walking around wearing a dead man's coat. Or it's in the hands of whoever dug here before us. And if it's the second—"

She didn't have to finish. If it was the second, then the men who erased Karsthal now held the second tumbler of Vane's lock, and everything we had decoded, every survivor's hiding place coaxed out of those lying contour lines, was knowledge they would soon share.

We filled the grave because leaving it open was unthinkable—we owed the empty hole that much, absurdly—and we did it fast and badly and in silence. Then we climbed the wall and dropped into the lane on the far side, and it was only there, in the black cold between gas lamps, that Ilse caught my sleeve.

"The clerk," she said. "Graves don't open themselves. Somebody keeps the keys to the churchyard, keeps the burial register, remembers who came and went. A man doesn't move a body—or a absence of a body—without leaving a paper trail, even a thin one."

I stood in the fog with clay freezing to my trousers and understood that I had stopped being an apprentice somewhere between the wall and the hole, because what I felt rising in me wasn't fear anymore. It was method.

"Parish registers," I said. "St. Aldhelm's keeps them under lock. Burials, fees, disbursements—for gravel, for extra labor, for anything unusual." I was already assembling it, the way Vane had taught me to assemble a coastline: one truthful measurement at a time until the lie of the land had no choice but to hold still. "Somebody paid. Somebody was paid. Money writes its name even when mouths won't."

Ilse smiled in the dark, and it was not a warm smile, and I loved it the way you love a blade handed to you handle-first.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we go find out who's been visiting my brother's grave."

And we walked home through the sleeping city, two thieves carrying nothing, having stolen from a grave the only thing it had ever contained: the certainty that we had been wrong about everything, and that the man we mourned was ahead of us still—one hole, one lie, one living breath beyond our reach.
