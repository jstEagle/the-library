## 17. Grave Robbery, Politely Done

The parish register of St. Anselm-in-the-Marsh weighed more than I expected. Dominic had warned me it would—he'd said it in the tone of a man who has carried registers before, which should have told me something about his apprenticeship that I hadn't yet understood—but knowing a thing and feeling it settle into your hands are different countries.

"Burial records," I said to the clerk at the desk, "for January of this year. Aurelius Vane."

The clerk was a small man named Petch, sixty or thereabouts, with ink-stained cuffs and the particular stillness of someone who has spent thirty years deciding what not to see. He looked at me over his spectacles for one second too long before answering.

"You'll want the sexton's book for that, madam. The register only holds the names."

"Then I'll want both."

Another pause. Dominic stood by the door with his hat in his hands, doing an admirable impression of a grieving nephew, though grief was the wrong costume for either of us. We were wearing curiosity now, and curiosity fit us badly, like borrowed coats.

Petch disappeared into the vestry with a lamp. When he came back he carried two ledgers, set them down without ceremony, and opened the second to a page already marked with a slip of paper.

Already marked.

I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn—half a beat after everyone else would have. A ribbon bookmark, faded red, sitting exactly at the entry for *Vane, Aurelius, of the parish of St. Bride, interred 14th January*. Somebody had been here recently. Somebody had wanted to find this page quickly.

"Someone has visited this record before," I said.

"People visit records all the time, madam."

"This ribbon is placed precisely. And it isn't the church's ribbon—it's silk."

Petch looked at the ribbon as if noticing it for the first time, which was a performance, and not a good one. "Perhaps the previous incumbent left it."

"The previous incumbent," Dominic said mildly, "would have died before Vane did. The burial happened three months ago."

The clerk's throat moved. He was not a liar by nature; that was clear within a minute of watching him. He was a man who had been paid, once, generously and years ago, to do a small thing, and had spent every day since waiting for the consequence of that thing to walk through his door wearing traveling clothes and asking precise questions.

I leaned forward and lowered my voice.

"Mr. Petch. I am not from the ministry. If I were from the ministry, we would not be having this conversation—I would simply be taking the ledger." I let that sit. "Who moved the body?"

* * *

He told us in the vestry, because he would not say it aloud in the office where the other clerk could hear. He sat down heavily on a chair that creaked under him, and he rubbed his hands together the way cold men do even when they are warm.

"I don't know where he is," Petch said. "That's the truth, first thing. I never knew. I know what I was paid to do, and I know who paid me, and those are the only two facts I own."

"Tell us both," Dominic said.

"The payment came eight years ago. Eight—maybe nine. It was a letter, private courier, no return direction. Inside was forty pounds"—he said the number the way other men say the names of dead children—"and instructions. The instructions concerned a plot in the churchyard. The north wall, third row, a plot belonging to a family called Hesselow who had all emigrated and sold their burials back to the parish. I was instructed to keep the plot empty. Officially maintained. Never sold. Never used. And never—not under any circumstances—to be entered in any ledger as reserved or assigned. On paper, it was to remain what it had always been: a Hesselow family plot, forgotten."

"And the money kept coming?" I asked.

"Ten pounds every year, same courier. For the upkeep of a grave that held nobody." He smiled without any humor in it. "I stopped wondering about it after the third year. You can train yourself out of wondering. Most of a clerk's education is learning how."

"But then Vane died," Dominic said. "In January. And he was buried."

"He wasn't buried." Petch's hands stopped moving. "That's the second fact I own. In January a coffin arrived—private funeral, very small, the deceased's wishes, nothing unusual in that for a man of his age. The undertaker was one I didn't know, brought in from outside the parish, paid in advance. The service was read. The mourners—you, sir, and the workmen from your shop—stood at the north wall. The coffin went down." He looked up at us. "But I was standing behind the yew hedge, because I had been told in December—instructed again, by letter—that when the time came, whatever I saw, I was to make sure the sexton's book recorded the interment and nothing else. No transfer. No exhumation. No note. And I was to lock the north gate myself that night and keep the key until further instruction."

Dominic had gone very still. Stillness was his native language; I was learning its grammar.

"When was the coffin removed?" I asked.

"It was never removed. That's the part that took me longest to understand." Petch drew a breath. "The coffin they lowered was empty. Or near enough—it had weight, sandbags or ballast, the undertaker handled it like he'd done it before. Whatever came out of that workshop in January, Mr. Vane was not in it. The plot has been empty since before anyone died in it. The grave wasn't robbed afterward, madam. The grave was prepared beforehand. Like a theater."

The vestry was cold, but my hands were not cold. They were hot, and slightly unsteady, and I recognized the feeling: it was the same heat that had come over me eleven days ago when Dominic had laid the letters on the workbench and the handwriting of a dead man had stared up at us from the dates.

Eight years. Not eight weeks. Not a panicked arrangement made when the ministry began closing in. A plot purchased under another family's name, a clerk on retainer, an undertaker rehearsed, a funeral staged to the last hymn—all of it built while Aurelius Vane was alive, and well, and teaching an orphan boy to hold a pen so the ink wouldn't blot.

"He was never planning to die in that grave," I said. "He was planning to die somewhere else entirely. The funeral was just—" I searched for the word. "A receipt. Proof of death for the official record. Something Hale's people could file and forget."

"That's beyond me, madam. I only do the books."

Dominic spoke quietly. "The letters Ilse carries. Dated after the burial. If the grave was prepared eight years ago, then the letters aren't a trick played on us. They're the continuation of a plan older than our part in it."

"Which means," I said, and the thought arrived whole, cold, finished, "that everything since January—the hiding, the cipher, sending me to your door—he arranged while he still had use of his legs and his post. He didn't fake his death because he was cornered. He faked it on a schedule."

Petch was looking between us with the expression of a man who has realized the two strangers in his vestry are carrying something far heavier than a genealogy inquiry, and who wants, very sincerely, to be excused from history.

"There's one more thing," he said, and his voice dropped as though the walls might inform on him. "The December letter. The second instruction. There was a line in it I've turned over a hundred times since. It said: *When the young man and the woman come asking—and they will—give them the parcel.*"

The room narrowed to the size of his sentence.

"He described us," Dominic said slowly. "In December. Before we ever—Ilse arrived in February. I didn't know anything until she knocked."

"Not described, sir. Named, if you follow me. The letter said *the apprentice and the courier.* Those were its words." Petch hauled himself up, went to a cabinet behind the vestment chest, and knelt with the effort of a man whose knees had opinions. From beneath the bottom drawer he drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, dusty as a thing dug from a garden. He set it on the table between us and stepped back from it, physically, as if distance were safety.

"He paid me to hold this too. Ten pounds a year, same as the plot. He said—" Petch paused, remembering. "He said, *keep it under God's floorboards, because no government man has ever knelt voluntarily.*"

* * *

We did not open it in the vestry. Dominic had opinions about that—that a packet which had waited eight years could wait another hour, that streets were for walking and workshops were for opening other people's mail—and though I disagreed with nearly everything about the way he said it, I agreed with the substance. Two strangers leaving a parish office visibly shaken would be remembered. Two strangers leaving with a wrapped bundle hidden in Dominic's document case, chatting about the weather, would not.

So we walked. The long way, past the coal market, through streets where the gas was coming on and shopkeepers were drawing in their signs. Dominic carried the case against his ribs like a man carrying a wounded bird. Neither of us spoke much. My mind was running the same circuit again and again:

*The apprentice and the courier.*

Not *if they come.* *When they come.* He had written about us to a stranger before we had ever met each other. Before I had ever knocked on that door with my bundle of impossible letters. Which meant that I had not found my way to Dominic Montrose by accident, or by grief, or by the logic of a dying man's final instruction. I had been delivered. Routed, like freight. And Dominic—

Dominic had been routed too. Taken off the streets as a boy, taught projection and silence, kept for years in a workshop above a city that had been erased—kept not because Vane was kind, though perhaps he had also been kind, but because an atlas of Karsthal required an apprentice who could finish it. We were instruments in a score written years before the overture. The master's shadow, I thought, does not fall where he stands. It falls where he intends to stand.

Back at the workshop, Dominic barred the door, lit two lamps, and cleared the workbench down to the bare wood with the ceremony of a surgeon. Then he cut the string.

Inside the oilcloth were three things.

The first was a key—small, brass, recent, the kind that opens a rented locker or a private box. No tag. No mark.

The second was a folded note in Vane's hand, and seeing that hand again did to my chest what it always did now, a slow closing of a fist. It was brief. It was addressed to neither of us. It was addressed, simply, *To the ones who follow*, and it read:

*If Petch has done his work, you are holding this because you have begun to understand that the grave was always a fiction. Good. Understanding arrives late in my workshop; it always has. The key opens premises I have kept under another name these six years. What is stored there will explain why I could not tell you while I lived among you, and why I cannot ask your forgiveness except at a distance, which is the coward's favorite range. Go at night. Go separately if you have learned anything at all.*

The third item was a single sheet of heavy paper, and it was not a note at all.

It was a map—or the beginning of one. The northern quarter of a city grid I knew now better than my own face, drawn in Vane's hand but unfinished, the contour lines trailing off into pencil ghosts. And along the margin, in the tight coded shorthand we had spent three nights cracking, ran a column of figures that Dominic's eyes went to immediately and stayed on.

"That's the cipher," he said. His voice had gone flat, which was how I knew it had gone frightened. "The same layer we broke in the atlas—the coordinates. But this sheet predates the atlas. Look at the paper stock, the watermark. This is six years old at least. Ilse—" He looked up at me across the lamplight. "He was encoding survivor locations six years ago. Before the commission. Before the funeral. Before either of us. The atlas isn't the message. The atlas is the envelope. It was never anything but the envelope."

Outside, somewhere below, a cart passed, and its wheels found every cobble in the street, and I thought about how a sound travels when the night is quiet enough, and about a man who had spent eight years digging tunnels through the ordinary world—a false grave here, a bribed clerk there, a name that wasn't his on a lease somewhere across the city—while the rest of us believed maps only described the world instead of building it.

"We should go tonight," I said. "Now, if—"

Dominic raised a hand. Not to silence me—to stop himself. He was staring at the unfinished map, at the pencil ghosts trailing off the edge of the northern quarter, and his expression had changed again, deepened, into something I hadn't seen on him before.

"Ilse," he said. "This watermark. This stock. It comes from a printer's supply house. It's press paper." He set the sheet down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Vane owned a press. He's owned one for years, under another name—and that key doesn't open a locker. It opens a print room. Everything we've been pulling proofs of on our old press, risking our necks for one copy at a time—he built the means to print hundreds, years ago, and hid it, and never used it." He straightened, and the lamplight caught the question in his face before he voiced it.

"Why would a man build a weapon and refuse, for six years, to fire it?"

We looked at each other across the bench, across the dead man's paper, and I understood that the answer was somewhere in that hidden room, waiting under the dust with the type and the rollers—and that whatever it was, it would change what we thought we knew about Aurelius Vane all over again.
