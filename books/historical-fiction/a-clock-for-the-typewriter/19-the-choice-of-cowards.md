## 19. The Choice of Cowards

The argument began over a cup of tea and ended over a knife.

Not that either of them reached for it. It was simply there on the table between them—the bone-handled letter opener Vane had used to slit the seals of ministry dispatches—and at some point in the fourth hour of their quarrel Ilse picked it up, not as a threat but as punctuation, and drove its point into the tabletop an inch deep, and said: "Say it again. Say *run* again, and I'll put this through your hand."

Dominic looked at the knife. He looked at her hands, which were steady in a way his had never been in his life. And he understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when a thing is already broken, that they had passed the point where words could be taken back.

It had started smaller than that. It always does.

---

The tea had gone cold twice before either of them touched it. They sat across from each other in the workroom, the atlas open between them like a patient on a table—plate forty-one, the northern quarter, the cipher half-cracked, Ada Kessler's name sitting in the contour lines like a seed in frozen ground.

"We have the testimony," Ilse said. "We have Kessler's statement written out and signed. We could be in Rotterdam by Friday."

"Rotterdam," Dominic said.

"Or Antwerp. Or anywhere the presses aren't owned by men who answer to Hale. Print it there. Sell it there. Let it come back here as contraband, in crates, in coat linings—"

"And how long does that take?" He heard his own voice go thin and reasonable, the voice of a man laying out a sensible plan. "Six months. A year. In a year Hale has the workshop, the plates, the press pulled apart rivet by rivet looking for more. In a year Kessler is dead of old age or worse, and her signed statement is a curiosity without a witness. You want to run because running feels like doing."

"I want to run because *staying* feels like dying," Ilse said, "and I've buried enough people who mistook the two."

* * *

Here is what Dominic believed, though he had never said it aloud to anyone, including himself, until that night: he believed in smallness. He believed a man could live under history the way a mouse lives under a floorboard—hear the boots, feel the tremor of them, survive by never once coming up into the light. Vane had taught him this without teaching it. Twelve years in the workshop, and the lesson underneath every lesson of projection and ink had been *be nothing, be nowhere, be the apprentice no one remembers the name of.*

He had watched it work. Vane had erased himself so thoroughly that even his own funeral had been a kind of forgery, and the world had accepted the forgery without a flicker. History happened elsewhere, to other people, to cities with names struck from registers. If you were careful, if you were quiet, you could stand at the edge of it your whole life and never be touched.

That belief was the last thing his master had left him, and it was wrong, and he did not yet know how wrong.

"Finish it properly," Ilse was saying now, in a voice stripped down to wire. "That's what you keep saying. *Properly.* As if there's a proper way. As if Vane spent twenty years being proper and look where it got him—a hole in the ground with another man's name on it and his life's work rotting in a drawer."

"It's not rotting in a drawer. We're finishing it."

"*I'm* finishing it." She stood up. The chair went over backward and neither of them moved to catch it. "You're hiding. There's a difference, and I've watched you make it for three weeks, Dominic. Every time we crack another layer you find a reason the next one can wait. Every time we talk about printing, you talk about *conditions.* The right paper. The right season. The right moment that will never come, because you don't want it to come."

"That isn't fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness left the room around the time a ministry burned a city and called it weather. What's left is true."

* * *

He tried, afterward, to reconstruct the exact sequence of his own cowardice, the way he'd reconstruct a damaged plate—where the line broke, where the ink pooled, what the original intention had been before the flaw. He was good at that kind of work. It was the only kind of honesty available to him.

The truth, laid out flat: he wanted to run because running was what he knew. He had been running since he was nine years old, off the streets and into the workshop, and the workshop itself had been a run—from hunger into labor, from the city into the map of the city, from the world into the representation of the world. Maps were safe. A map of Karsthal held everything terrible about Karsthal at arm's length, flattened onto paper, scaled down until massacre fit inside a margin note. He had spent three months inking coastlines of a dead city and telling himself it was grief. Some of it was. But some of it was the oldest trick he knew: if you render a thing precisely enough, completely enough, you can convince yourself you've done something about it.

And now Kessler's face had ruined the trick. An old woman in a rented room, describing the sound the northern quarter made on the third night—not screaming, she'd said, by then past screaming, a sound like a harbor at low tide, all the water going out at once—and Dominic had understood that the contours he'd been inking were not information. They were people. Eleven thousand of them, drawn in elevation lines, and he had been *admiring the draftsmanship.*

So yes. Part of him wanted to run because running was honest cowardice, and cowardice honestly named hurt less than courage performed badly.

But part of him wanted to run for reasons that would have survived cross-examination, and those were the parts he kept putting forward, because even cowards argue their better half first.

"The press is old," he said. "You've seen it. Half a night's run and the rollers need re-cutting. We pull maybe sixty proofs before it fails, and then we've told Hale exactly what we have and where we have it, and we've got sixty copies that prove nothing without the full atlas behind them."

"So fix the press."

"With what money? With what parts? Every supplier in this quarter knows whose workshop this was. One order for new rollers and it's on someone's desk by morning."

"Then smuggle the plates out and print abroad, like I said—"

"—and lose the year, and lose the timing, and hope the foreign printer doesn't sell us to the highest bidder, which they will, because everyone sells, Ilse, that's the whole engine of the world—" He stopped. Breathed. "And meanwhile Kessler sits in that room alone with a name that's on a ministry list somewhere, because the moment we move, she moves up it."

Ilse went very still. It was, he would think later, the stillness of a person deciding something.

"You've thought about this," she said quietly. "The timing. The press. Kessler's exposure. You've thought about all of it, carefully, the way you think about everything." She came around the table. "And none of it is why you want to run. Say the real reason or I swear to you I will walk out of this workshop tonight and finish this without you, and you'll deserve it."

"Ilse—"

"Say it."

"The real reason," Dominic said, and his voice came out flat and strange, like a stranger reading his confession, "is that I am not brave, and I never have been, and every plan I've ever made assumed I wouldn't have to be. Vane knew it. That's why he chose me—an apprentice of small mercies, a boy who'd learned to survive by being beneath notice. He built me to be invisible. And invisible men don't stand in doorways at dawn while soldiers kick the door in. Invisible men are *gone* by then, and alive, and ashamed, and alive."

The room held the silence the way water holds a stone.

"There," Ilse said. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it—there was no cruelty in her at all, only a terrible patience, the patience of someone who had lost a brother to exactly this arithmetic and had done her sums since. "Now we're finally talking."

* * *

They fought for another hour after that, but it was a different fight—cleaner, meaner in places, but honest, the way a wound is honest once it's opened. She accused him of loving Vane's approval more than Vane's purpose. He accused her of wanting martyrdom because she hadn't been allowed to die in Karsthal with her brother, and saw her flinch, and hated himself with a completeness that felt almost restful, like lying down.

"You don't know anything about what I want," she said, when she could speak.

"No. Tell me."

"I want my brother's name spelled correctly. That's all. Somewhere, in something permanent, spelled correctly—Emmerich Vogel, not *casualty, male, unrecorded.* I carried sealed packets for that man for six years and never once opened one, because I trusted him, and the trust bought me a coordinate and a name in a contour line, and I will see it printed if it costs me everything, and I would rather it cost *you* something too, because if I do this alone then it stays what it's always been—one woman shouting at a government, and governments don't hear women shouting. They hear books. They hear *many* books. So help me make the book, Dominic, or get out of my way and let me make it badly without you."

He looked at the atlas. Plate forty-one. The northern quarter, where the elevations climbed too steeply for any real ground, where Vane had hidden the names of the hiding places like bulbs under snow. His master's whole life compressed into lines of ink, and his master's whole failure compressed into the fact that the lines had stayed hidden for thirteen years.

"He ran," Dominic said. "That's the thing I can't get past. He mapped the massacre and then he ran, and he let a city stay dead for thirteen years because running was easier, and I am his apprentice, Ilse. I learned everything he taught me. Including that."

"Yes," Ilse said. "Including that."

She picked up the letter opener. He didn't flinch—some animal part of him noted that he didn't flinch, and filed it away as evidence for the defense—but she only used it to point, at him, at the atlas, at the shuttered window beyond which Mrs. Ostrova's house sat dark against the dark.

"So here is the choice," she said, "and I'll make it simple, because you hide in complications. We stay, we repair the press, we print the whole atlas here where Vane built it, and we scatter it ourselves, tonight after tonight after tonight, until there are too many copies to burn. Or we run, and I run alone, and you get to spend the rest of your life being invisible and alive, which you'll discover are not the same thing." She set the knife down. Her hand, he noticed, shook now that the pointing was done. "Choose. But choose knowing that whichever one you pick, that's the man you are. There's no third version of you waiting. There's just this."

* * *

What decided him was not courage. He wanted to be able to say, later, that some noble thing had risen in him—but the truth was smaller and more useful. What decided him was a memory: Vane at the drafting table, near the end, correcting a coastline for the ninth time, and catching Dominic watching, and saying, without looking up: *A map is a promise, boy. Every line says: I was here, I saw this, I attest. Break the promise and the map is just decoration.*

Vane had broken the promise. Thirteen years of silence, a false grave, a confession locked in a press room. And on his deathbed he had handed them the ledger anyway, and asked for forgiveness, and received silence, because some debts aren't payable, only transferable.

Dominic looked at the knife in the table. He looked at Ilse, who was watching him the way you watch a tide gauge.

"We stay," he said.

Her breath went out slowly.

"But we do it my way for the first week," he added, because he was still himself, and would always be somewhat himself. "The press first. Rollers, packing, the frisket—I know a man in the ironmongers' trade who owes Vane money and doesn't ask questions, and we buy nothing under this roof's name. And Kessler moves tonight. Tonight, Ilse. Before we cut a single plate, she goes somewhere with two exits and a landlady who owes me. If we're building a weapon, we don't leave the witness standing in the window."

"Agreed." She was already pulling the chair upright, already clearing the table, the fight metabolized into motion the way she metabolized everything. "And Dominic—"

"Don't say thank you. I'll want to take it back by morning."

"I wasn't going to say thank you." She paused, and something crossed her face that wasn't gratitude and wasn't quite forgiveness either, but was adjacent to both. "I was going to say: it took you long enough."

He laughed—or made the sound that stands in for laughing when a man is too tired and too changed—and turned to the shelf where Vane's tools hung in their appointed places, and that was when he saw it.

The window. The one facing the street, the one with the shutter closed, the one he checked every night the way other men check a lock.

There was a gap in the slats. A finger's width, no more, where the wood had warped—or been warped. And through the gap, across the street, in the ground-floor flat of the corner house that had stood empty since autumn:

a light. Small. Steady. The color of a lamp turned low and hooded, the color of a man working late over papers.

Ilse followed his eyes. Neither of them spoke. Outside, somewhere down the boulevard, a clock tower began striking the hour—all the hours they had wasted, all the hours they had left—and the light across the street did not waver at all.
