## 21. A Night Visit to the Archive

I had never stolen anything in my life until the night I stole my way into the Ministry of Records.

That isn't quite true. I had stolen once—a loaf, when I was nine, from a baker on Corvin Street who caught me and, instead of calling the watch, made me sweep his shop for a month. Vane liked to tell that story back at me whenever I grew too proud of my hands. *You have honest fingers, boy,* he'd say. *They only steal when they're starving.* He meant it as a compliment about my draftsmanship.

He would not have called this starvation. But he would have understood it.

* * *

The archive sat behind the Ministry like a coffin behind a church—grey stone, no windows above knee height, a single entrance flanked by two lamps that burned all night because the men inside were paid to distrust darkness. Ilse had drawn me its face from memory three days earlier: she had delivered packets there twice a year for Vane, always to a side door, always to a clerk with a soft voice who signed for nothing.

"Nothing goes into that building without a record," she'd said, "and nothing comes out of it ever."

"Then how did Vane's effects get in?"

"The same way everything does. Carried by someone who didn't ask questions." She looked at me across the atlas, her hands flat on the table as if pinning it down. "You're going to ask me to help you break in, aren't you?"

"I was going to ask you to wait outside."

"That's worse. That's what you do with children and dogs." She said it without heat. She had been saying things like that to me for weeks now—corrections, small and precise, the way Vane used to tap my compass hand with a knuckle. *North is not where you feel it is.*

We spent four evenings preparing, which sounds absurd for a burglary and was entirely necessary. Ilse knew the side door's habits; I knew locks, or rather I knew mechanisms, because a man who builds instruments understands that every lock is an instrument built by a tired person on an off day. Mrs. Ostrova gave us nothing but a look when we came down after midnight dressed in dark coats, and that look said: *I see you, and the price of my seeing has gone up.* We left through the courtyard and did not speak again until we were three streets away.

The fog helped. November fog in this city is not weather; it is policy. It lay over the ministry quarter like a sheet pulled up to the chin, and the lamps along the wall became smudges of orange, and the guard at the main door became a shape, and the shape became nothing.

The side door Ilse remembered had been bricked over. Of course it had. Hale would have seen to that—he struck me as a man who closed doors the way other men opened them.

So we went over the roof.

There was an adjoining stable block, low-slung, its ridge within reach of the archive's flat roof if one climbed the drainpipe and trusted the pipe. I want it recorded, wherever such things are recorded, that I am not brave. My hands shook so badly on that drainpipe that I nearly came down twice, and the second time I hung there against wet iron with my feet pedaling air and thought, very calmly, *Dominic, you are going to die for a piece of paper, and the paper will be found on your corpse, and they will burn the atlas out of spite.* And then I thought of Vane's marginal note on the northern quarter plate—the one error, our private error, the little wrong longitude he put in every map he loved—and I climbed.

Ilse followed me up like she'd been born on roofs. Later she told me she hadn't. Later she told me a great many things in that flat way of hers that made heroism sound like housework.

The roof had skylights. Archives need light by day and ventilation by night, and whoever designed this building had been honest about both. The third skylight was latched with a hook-and-eye, interior, reachable with a knife blade slipped through the gap. It took me twenty minutes. It felt like twenty years. Somewhere below us a clock struck two, and Ilse whispered, "If anyone's inside, they know we're here," and I said, "Then we're committed," which was the first truly stupid thing I said all night, though not the last.

* * *

Inside smelled the way I imagine tombs smell: cold paper, dust, lamp oil, and something faintly sweet that might have been the glue they used on ledgers. I dropped first, onto a table stacked with document boxes, then held the rope while Ilse came down. We stood in the dark among shelving that ran off into blackness like streets, row after row, and I confess I felt something I'm ashamed of: awe. Every shelf a city. Every box a life filed under a number. This was where Karsthal lived now—not in stone or memory or any map a citizen could buy, but here, boxed and numbered and owned.

"They keep everything," Ilse breathed. "That's the horror of it. They erase a city from the world and file it."

"Find me the V's."

We worked fast and quiet, by shuttered candle, reading labels. The archive was organized by ministry department, then by year, then alphabetically by subject or surname—the logic of people who believe the world can be ordered. Under CONFISCATED EFFECTS — 1910–11, PRIVATE CITIZENS, there was a run of boxes with string-tied tags. VANE, A. — CARTOGRAPHER — DECEASED ESTATE (PARTIAL).

*Partial.* I noted the word and hated it.

The box was heavier than it should have been. Inside: his good drafting pen, the one with the cracked nib he refused to replace. His brass parallel rule. A folded coat. His tobacco tin, empty. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth, a flat object the size of a prayer book.

I unwrapped it with hands that had finally stopped shaking, which should have warned me.

It was a slate. Not paper—slate, the kind apprentices use, the kind Vane made me use for my first two years because ink forgives nothing. On it, in chalk, worn almost to nothing by handling, was a single word:

**OSTROVA.**

And beneath it, smaller: *ask the woman who sees.*

I stood there holding a dead man's handwriting on a dead man's slate, and the word did not resolve. Ostrova? Our landlady? The key to the cipher was our landlady?

No. No—it wasn't the name itself. It was what the name did. I had the cipher half-cracked in my head for weeks; the northern quarter coordinates resolved around a substitution driven by a keyword, and Vane had told me once, laughing over wine he pretended not to drink, that the best keys are words hiding in plain sight, words so close the searcher walks past them daily. The landlady who saw everything. Who had protected him for years—for a price. Whose name was already written into the architecture of our days.

"She was the key," Ilse whispered beside me, reading over my shoulder. "She's known the whole time. She charged him rent to be the key."

"We'll test it tonight—" 

"Gentlemen."

The voice came from the dark between the shelves, and it was courteous, and unhurried, and it stopped my heart the way a slammed door stops a pendulum.

A lamp came up—not a match flaring but a lamp already lit, turned up slowly, a man who had been sitting in the dark with light in reserve. Corvus Hale stepped into our candle's small radius as though entering a drawing room. He wore a dark overcoat and no hat, and behind him, at the ends of two aisles, stood two men I hadn't heard arrive, which frightened me more than anything else that night. You do not hear two large men take up positions unless they are very good or you are very distracted. I had been very distracted.

Hale looked at the slate in my hands for a long moment.

"Mr. Montrose," he said. "You've been busy."

* * *

Here is what I learned about fear in the next ninety seconds: it does not make you clever or stupid. It makes you exact. Every detail arrived separately, catalogued, the way I'd catalogue a strange coastline—his boots were polished; the two guards did not carry lanterns; Ilse had drifted one step to my left, putting herself between me and the nearest aisle; the candle in my hand was guttering and I could not relight it because my matches were in my coat, which suddenly felt a hundred miles away.

"You knew we'd come," I said.

"I hoped you'd come. Hoping and knowing are different professions." He gestured, mildly, at the open box, the spilled coat, the slate. "I've had this room watched since the letters surfaced. I assumed the apprentice would eventually grow curious about where his master's things went. Curiosity is the one quality I've never managed to have abolished." He smiled slightly, as if at a private joke. "Sit down, Mr. Montrose."

There was a chair. Of course there was a chair. He had arranged the room.

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes. Apprentices always would." He turned to Ilse. "Frau—no, forgive me. You don't use surnames, do you? Just Ilse. Courier. Eleven years in his service and you never once opened a packet. Remarkable discipline. Or remarkable fear. In my experience the two are often the same animal in different coats."

Ilse said nothing. She had gone very still in the way she went still before delivering the sentence that ended an argument, and I wanted to warn Hale: don't mistake the stillness for surrender. I didn't warn him. I owed him nothing, and besides, some part of me wanted to watch it land.

"Here is my situation," Hale continued, walking slowly along the shelf, trailing one finger across the box spines as a bishop trails a ring across a rail. "Three months ago a dead man's correspondence appeared in the hands of a living courier. Two weeks ago my inspectors report a shuttered workshop burning lamp-oil at hours workshops don't work. Last week a parish clerk who took money from Aurelius Vane in 1907 dies quietly of a heart attack in his sleep—which may even be true. And tonight, the apprentice breaks into the national archive and leaves"—he paused—"well. He hasn't left yet. So you will understand that I find myself in possession of many threads and no garment."

"And what's your situation offer?" Ilse said. Flat. Even. But I heard the blade under it.

"My situation," Hale said, "is that I could arrest you both tonight. Sedition, theft of state papers, conspiracy. You would vanish the way Karsthal vanished—filed, numbered, owned." He let that sit. "And in ten years, some other apprentice would break into some other archive, because there is always another apprentice, and the thing you're carrying would still exist somewhere, waiting. Arresting you solves nothing. I'm a patient man, Mr. Montrose, and patience has taught me the difference between cutting a thread and following it."

"You want us to lead you to the rest," I said.

"I want," Hale said, turning, and for the first time the courtesy thinned enough to show the machinery underneath, "to finish what I started in 1898 properly. Completely. With nothing left over. Your master understood incomplete work better than any man alive—that's why he ran, that's why he buried himself, that's why he left his little breadcrumbs for sentimental souls like you to follow. He couldn't bear an unfinished map. Well. Neither can I."

"The atlas is destroyed," I said. "Burned. There's nothing—"

"Mr. Montrose." He said my name gently, the way a doctor says the name of a man whose test results he's already read. "You broke through a roof hatch, crossed forty feet of archive floor, and went directly—one aisle, one turn, no searching—to a box marked VANE. You walked to it like a man walking to church. Do you know why? Because your hands knew where it was before your head agreed. Men who rehearse." He spread his hands. "I don't need the atlas from you. I need only to walk behind you while you assemble it."

The candle died. In the sudden dark I heard Ilse breathe once, slow, controlled, and I heard the guards shift their weight, and I heard Hale—this was the detail I carried home and examined for weeks—Hale say nothing at all. He waited in the blackness with us as though darkness were a room he owned furniture in.

When the guard relit a lantern, Hale was standing exactly where he'd been.

"Go home, Mr. Montrose," he said. "Finish your grief. Follow your breadcrumbs. I'll be behind you." He picked up Vane's cracked-nib pen from the box, weighed it, and set it back down with terrible gentleness. "And when you find him—if you find him—give him a message from me. Tell him: *the northern quarter was my idea too.*"

* * *

They let us out through the front door. That was the obscenity of it—the guards unbolted the great doors and held them open and nodded us through like doormen at an opera house, and the fog took us, and the street was empty and ordinary and wet, and somewhere a cab horse stamped at its picket, and the world went on as though I had not just been released from a trap precisely because the trap wanted me running.

We walked six blocks before either of us spoke.

"He let us keep the slate," I said finally. My voice sounded borrowed.

"He wants the cipher solved for him." Ilse kept her eyes forward. "Every step we take, he takes for free."

"Then we stop taking steps."

"No." She stopped under a lamp and turned, and the fog moved behind her shoulders like a slow tide. "We change direction. Tonight. Before he's settled into following." Her eyes went to my pocket, where the slate rode against my ribs. "The word was under our roof the whole time, Dominic. He thinks you'll go home and puzzle it out over weeks. He's a patient man. So we won't be."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we wake Mrs. Ostrova."

We walked on toward the workshop, two small figures in a large fog, and behind us—I did not look, but I felt it the way you feel sun through a window—the archive stood open a moment longer than it needed to, letting us go, keeping the light on behind us like a man who intends to see you home.

The slate pressed cold against my side the whole way, and the word on it seemed less like a key now than like a debt: *ask the woman who sees.* Vane had hidden the answer inside our own walls, charging us rent on it for years. And Hale had sent a message for a man three months buried, in a voice so certain it chilled me worse than the guards' hands on the door:

He believed Vane was alive.

Which meant that somewhere in that grey building, in that courteous mind, the question was no longer whether a dead mapmaker had left a map—

—but where a living one was hiding.
