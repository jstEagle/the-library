## 26. Printing in Secret

*Dominic*

The press had not been pulled in six years, and it complained about it the way an old man complains about stairs—creak by creak, each one a negotiation.

I worked the screw press loose with walnut oil and patience, wiping away the felt of dust that had settled over Vane's beloved Albion like snow on a grave. It was past midnight. Ilse held the lamp low, its flame turned down to a bead of light, and together we listened to the building breathe. Mrs. Ostrova's floorboards overhead. The settling of joists. Nothing else.

"Say something if you hear footsteps," she whispered.

"In this neighborhood, footsteps mean drunkards."

"In this neighborhood," she said, "drunkards are the least of what walks at night."

The first proof was a disaster. I had forgotten how much pressure the plate wanted, how the dampened paper had to be coaxed rather than commanded, and the sheet came up from the bed with the northern quarter of Karsthal smeared into a grey bruise. A city erased twice in one minute. I almost laughed at that, and then almost wept, and did neither, because Ilse was watching me with those level eyes that demanded competence before comfort.

"Again," she said.

The second proof came up clean. And there it was—Karsthal, black on white, real in a way no drawing had ever been real. I had spent three months inking that coastline in secret, and it had always been a ghost, an apology, a conversation with a dead man. Now it was an *object*. You could hold the city in your hands. You could fold it and put it in a pocket. Someone in another country could unfold it a year from now and run a finger down the Vltava-Canal district, the Fishmarket Row, the eleven streets of the Northern Quarter where—according to the cipher—the survivors had scattered like spilled coins.

"There," Ilse said softly, and touched the wet ink, and her fingertip came away black, and she looked at it the way other people look at relics.

We hung proofs along the drying line like washing. By two in the morning there were nine sheets of Karsthal swaying faintly in the draft from under the door, and the workshop smelled of oil and lampblack and something I couldn't name until I named it: *Sunday*. It smelled like Sunday printing, like the smell of my childhood church, which is to say it smelled like ceremony.

"We need paper," Ilse said. "A hundred sheets won't do. We need a thousand, and good stock, and we need it without anyone asking why."

"I know a flour merchant who owes Vane money."

"Then tomorrow you're buying flour."

* * *

*Ilse*

He bought flour. Fourteen sacks of it over five days, from a merchant named Pfeil who never once met his eyes and never once asked a question, which told me he had done this kind of errand before, or worse kinds, and had decided long ago that curiosity was a tax he couldn't afford. Each sack went up the workshop stairs on Dominic's shoulder, and each sack was heavier than flour had any right to be, and if Mrs. Ostrova noticed—and Mrs. Ostrova noticed everything—she said nothing, which cost more than saying something would have.

Inside each sack, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the flour: plates. I had engraved them myself, working by daylight disguised as ordinary commission work while Dominic slept after his nights at the press. The testimony pages. Kessler's account, set in type small enough to fit four hundred words onto a single plate. The order itself—the purge order—with Hale's signature reproduced so faithfully that when Dominic saw the first proof he stepped back from it as though the signature might reach up off the page and take his hand.

"He'll know it's forged," Dominic said.

"It isn't forged. It's copied. There's a difference, and the difference is going to hang him."

The ink came easier. Vane had laid in stores against exactly this, tins of best printing black sealed under the floorboards beneath the press, along with a note in his hand: *For whoever finishes it. Mind the rollers; they dry out. Feed them glycerin.* Even dead, even vanished, even dying in whatever room they'd hidden him in, the man was still giving instructions. I found I wasn't angry anymore. I was just taking dictation.

We worked only between midnight and four. Dominic called them "the deaf hours"—the time when even the most diligent watcher's attention thins, when Mrs. Ostrova's corner flat across the yard showed dark, when the constable on his rounds passed our street at half-past one precisely and could be set your watch by. We muffled the treadle with blankets. We ran the press with the windows hooded in Vane's old charts, so that from outside the workshop glowed the dull amber of a man working late over accounts.

And every night, the stack of printed Karsthals grew.

That was the thing none of the planning had prepared me for: how it *felt*. I had thought printing would be like writing—private, slow, one copy at a time. But a press is a machine for multiplication, and multiplication changes the nature of a thing entirely. One atlas is a secret. Ten atlases are a conspiracy. A hundred are a fact.

On the sixth night I stood back from the drying rack and counted forty-one complete copies, each one folded and stitched, and I understood suddenly what power was frightened of. Not the truth. Power can survive the truth—it does it daily. What power cannot survive is the truth made *numerous*. A single witness can be discredited, exiled, buried. A thousand identical witnesses cannot all be reached in time.

"Dominic." He was cleaning the roller, his hands black to the wrist. "How many correspondents do we have addresses for?"

"Eleven foreign ones. Three domestic who might be brave enough, or foolish enough."

"And how many copies will we have by the end?"

"If the plates hold—" He calculated. "Three hundred. Perhaps three-fifty."

Three hundred and fifty cities, risen from the dead. Three hundred and fifty copies of an order signed by Corvus Hale, traveling in flour sacks and luggage false-bottoms and diplomatic pouches, fanning out toward London and Zurich and Amsterdam like seeds on wind. Somewhere in a ministry office a man had built a career on the sentence *there was never any such place*, and we were manufacturing the contradiction by the hour.

I picked up a fresh proof, still warm-smelling, and read the words I had set myself, the line that would close every copy:

*Karsthal existed. Here is the proof. Ask why you were never told.*

"Say it again," Dominic said, and I realized I'd spoken aloud.

"Karsthal existed."

"Again."

"Karsthal *existed*." And this time he said it with me, both of us standing in a lamplit room full of drying ghosts, saying the forbidden sentence over the sound of Mrs. Ostrova's floorboards creaking overhead.

* * *

*Dominic*

It was near dawn on the ninth night when the press jammed, and jamming saved us.

The roller seized mid-pull—I hadn't fed it enough glycerin, Vane, forgive me—and while I wrestled the screw we both heard what we would otherwise have missed, because the press was silent for the first time in three hours: boots in the alley below. Two pairs. They stopped. They stayed stopped.

Ilse pinched out the lamp. In the dark we stood among forty-one finished copies and fourteen empty flour sacks and a press warm as a body, listening to two men breathing in the cold below the window.

Then a match scraped. A pipe was lit. The boots moved on.

Not patrolmen. Patrolmen walk. Those men had stood still, and men stand still only when they are being paid to.

"They know," Ilse breathed.

"They suspect. There's a difference, and—" I caught her smile in the dark, my own words handed back to me. "We've been too loud. Too regular. Deaf hours or not, nine nights makes a habit, and habits are legible."

"So we change."

"We change everything. Different hours, ragged ones. Some nights nothing. We finish the run in three long sprints instead of twelve short ones, and we move the copies out as we go—not stored here, gone by morning, into Ostrova's cellar until collection."

"The landlady," Ilse said slowly, "who watches us for someone."

"The landlady," I said, "whose price we have never asked, and whose son we have never met, and who has kept Vane's secrets for fifteen years without once selling them. At some point, Ilse, you have to decide whether the people around you are walls or doors. I've decided she's a wall."

"You decided that about Vane, too. That he was a wall. Look how that turned out—he was a door the whole time, and everyone walked through him."

I didn't answer, because the answer was yes.

We reinked the roller and pulled ten more proofs before sunrise, and as the sky over the yard went the grey of wet newsprint, I hung the last sheet and stood looking down the row of them—forty-some Karsthals swaying gently, black and white and true—and felt something I hadn't felt since Vane died. Not grief. Not fear, though fear was there, curled at the bottom of everything like a dog by the stove.

Momentum. The atlas was no longer mine. That was the whole point and the whole terror of it. Tomorrow the copies would begin leaving this room, one and two at a time, and within a month they would be beyond anyone's reach, including mine. A map escapes its maker the way a word escapes the mouth.

Below in the alley, the boots came back. Stopped again. Stayed longer this time.

And when they finally moved off, I heard—one of them say to the other, low, but the alley carries sound on cold mornings, and the cold mornings were coming earlier now—

*"Tell Hale the sacks aren't carrying flour."*
