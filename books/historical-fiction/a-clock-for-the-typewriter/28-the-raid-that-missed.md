## 28. The Raid That Missed

They came at nine in the morning, which told me everything about how confident Hale was. Men who fear resistance come at night. Men who know the answer already come in daylight, with paperwork.

I heard the carriage first—two horses, heavy wheels, the particular rhythm of four men dismounting instead of two. By then I'd had three weeks to learn the building's vocabulary: Mrs. Ostrova's tread on the stairs, the coal man's whistle, the difference between a knock and a summons. This was neither. This was arrival.

"Ilse," I said quietly. She was at the worktable with a proof of the western plates spread under her hands, and she didn't startle, didn't look up—she'd gotten better at that. "Back room. Now."

She rolled the proof, slid it into the false wall's gap without hurry, and went. I had time to notice her steadiness and to be briefly, stupidly proud of it before I heard boots on the stairs.

Four of them. Hale in front, hat in hand like a man calling on a neighbor. Behind him a sergeant I didn't know and two clerks with canvas satchels—the satchels were the bad sign. You don't bring satchels to an arrest. You bring them to a confiscation.

"Master Montrose." Hale set his hat on our worktable as if he owned the surface. He'd done that the first time he came; it wasn't carelessness, it was a signature. "I regret the hour."

"You don't," I said. "But come in."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Interest. He liked that I'd stopped being afraid of him, or he found it useful, the way a surveyor finds a new landmark useful. He produced a folded document, wax seal, the whole liturgy. "A warrant for the papers of the estate of Aurelius Vane, deceased. There has been a question raised about the disposition of certain materials. Irregularities in probate."

"Raised by whom?"

"By me." He said it without shame, and I understood again what we were dealing with. Most men need a fiction before they rob you. Hale simply filed the robbery under public order and slept well.

The clerks were already moving. That was the part I hadn't prepared for properly—not the violence of a raid but its tidiness. They didn't throw things. They lifted them, examined them, wrapped them in cloth before placing them in the satchels. Vane's dividers. The box of nibs sorted by wear. The ledger of commissions with its columns of invented clients. Each object handled like evidence, because that is what it had become the moment they crossed the threshold.

I stood where I was and let them work, and I will tell you honestly what was in my mind: not the press behind the wall—I'd checked the bolts twice that morning, half-convinced myself I could hear plaster settling wrong over it—but Ilse, one thin wall away, breathing as quietly as a woman can breathe while men catalog her life. And beneath that, cold in my stomach, the letters.

We should have burned them. We'd argued about it in the second week, when burning still felt like prudence instead of surrender. Ilse had held Vane's last letter over the stove flame and couldn't do it—"It's his hand, Dominic. It's the only hand he'll ever write with again"—and I hadn't pushed, because some part of me wanted them kept too. A dead man's voice, dated after his death. Proof of the miracle we were living inside.

Proof. That was the word that should have frightened us both.

* * *

Hale walked the workshop the way he'd walked it on his first visit, slowly, reading it. But this time he read differently. Before, he'd been looking for what was hidden in plain sight—papers, correspondence, the smell of fresh ink. Now he knew there was something to find, and his attention had changed quality entirely. It was the attention of a man checking his own work.

He stopped at the north wall. Our false wall. Three feet of lath and plaster and a seam I'd finished with lime so fine you needed a candle raked sideways to find it.

"The room is smaller than the footprint," he said. Not to me. To himself, almost pleasantly, the way a man notes a river that matches his map.

"It's an old building. Walls wander."

"Mm." He rapped the plaster once with a knuckle. The sound came back solid—he'd hit between the studs, luck or skill—and I kept my face doing nothing at all while my heart performed its business somewhere near my ears. He rapped again, a foot left. Hollow.

"Sooty flue," I said. "The chimney's been bricked through years ago."

"Has it." He looked at me then, fully, and I saw him decide—not against pressing, but against pressing today. That was worse. Hale never spent a certainty before he'd compounded its interest. He turned from the wall as though leaving a book open at his page.

The sergeant brought him the satchel of letters. All of them. Eleven letters, every one in Vane's hand, every one dated after the funeral—after the coffin, after the gravediggers, after I had stood in the rain at Halloway Field and watched earth go down on the only father I ever had. Hale untied the packet with fingers that did not hurry and read the topmost letter right there in our workshop, standing beside Vane's inkstone, and I watched his expression do something I had never seen it do.

It settled.

Whatever he had suspected across all those months of chalk marks and rented rooms and courteous threats—now he held it. Not suspicion. Fact. Vane lived, or had lived, and everything else followed like water finding its level.

"Well," Hale said softly. It was almost tender. "Well."

"He was my master," I said. "His papers are his papers."

"His papers are evidence that a man declared dead has been conducting a correspondence within this jurisdiction for the better part of a year." Hale folded the letter along its existing creases—a careful man, even with other people's treasures. "Which makes you, Master Montrose, either a dupe or an accomplice. I confess I no longer much care which. Though I'll note—" and here he smiled, a small true smile that was more frightening than any threat he'd made, "—that dupes make better witnesses."

"You have nothing that says where he is."

"No," Hale agreed. "Not yet. But he wrote these *somewhere*. Paper has suppliers. Ink has chemists. Postmarks are confessions, Master Montrose, written by no one." He tucked the letters inside his coat, against his own body, and I understood that gesture too. These weren't confiscated documents to him. These were personal now. Karsthal had been a file, an entry, a job done cleanly and put away—and Vane's living hand had reached out of the grave and reopened it. Men forgive many things. They do not forgive being made to dig up their own finished work.

At the door he paused, retrieved his hat, and set it on with two precise movements.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I gave you every chance to be a small man with a dead master's furniture. You've chosen otherwise. I respect it more than you'd think." He looked back once at the north wall, unhurried, letting me watch him look. "Mind the damp in that chimney. Buildings like this burn terribly."

Then they were gone, and the carriage wheels faded, and the workshop was so quiet I could hear the stove ticking as it cooled.

* * *

Ilse came out of the back room white to the lips but level, and the first thing she said was not about the raid.

"The proofs?"

"Safe. The wall held."

"The letters didn't."

"No."

She sat down slowly on Vane's old stool, the one with the worn seat shaped to a man twenty years gone from sitting in it, and put her face in her hands—not weeping, just holding herself together manually for a moment. When she lowered her hands she was angry, and I was grateful for it. Anger is a fuel grief can't be.

"All of it," she said. "Every letter. He has Vane's hand dated after the death, he has your handwriting on the receiving end, he has—" She stopped. "Dominic. The last letter. Did they take the last letter?"

"Yes."

"The one with the hospice reference. The one that started us toward the northern coordinate."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stood, and the stool rocked behind her. "Because if he reads it the way we read it—if he works out that the cipher points somewhere *outside* this city—he doesn't need us anymore. He doesn't need to follow us to Vane. He can move first. He can reach the hospice before we do, and whatever Vane knows dies with him a second time, permanently this time."

"He won't move yet." I heard myself say it and realized I believed it. "You saw him. He's not hunting Vane. He's hunting the *atlas*—the whole thing, complete, with every loose thread tied. A letter tells him Vane lives. It doesn't tell him the atlas is finished. As long as he thinks the weapon isn't loaded, he'll keep stalking us and hoping we lead him to the rest."

"That's a comfort built entirely on his vanity."

"It's the only architecture we have left."

Ilse crossed to the window and stood with her back to me, looking down at the street where the carriage had been. Her shoulders were rigid. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something flat and practical, the voice she used over the press at three in the morning.

"Then we leave tonight."

"We can't. The final run isn't—"

"The run can happen anywhere with a press. *Vane cannot.*" She turned. "He's dying, Dominic. You saw him. Days, maybe. And now the man who signed the order has proof he's alive and a head start on the postmarks, and we're standing here arguing about ink coverage. If we lose Vane, we lose the testimony ledger, we lose the last corrections, we lose—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, I watched her hate it. "We lose him. Whatever he is to you. Whatever he was to me."

I looked around the workshop. Vane's stool. The nibs in their boxes. The wall with the press behind it, eleven weeks of our lives pressed into lath and plaster. Everything I had been protecting since the funeral—since before the funeral, since the day a gaunt old man caught me stealing bread and said, *you have steady hands, boy, wasted on theft*—was either seized or hidden, and the hidden part was worth nothing without the seized part's silence.

Losses mounting. That was the arithmetic of the morning, and I ran it again hoping for a different sum: the letters, gone. Our anonymity, gone. The pretense that we were craftsmen finishing a commission rather than conspirators assembling an indictment—gone, and good riddance, perhaps, because pretending had never once slowed Hale down.

What remained: the plates. Ada Kessler's testimony, transcribed. The ledger copy Ilse kept sewn into her coat lining. And Vane, dying in a hospice outside the city, who did not know that the hunt had just become personal for a man who had personally erased a city and thought himself done with it.

"We go tonight," I said. "But not empty-handed. If Hale follows the postmarks, he follows them here first—he'll want to watch what we do next. So we give him something to watch." I put my hand flat on the north wall, on the plaster that hid the press. "We let him see us loading paper. Let him think we're printing tonight, right where he can count the carts. While his men sit in Ostrova's corner flat counting shadows, we're on the east road."

Ilse considered it, and I watched her weigh it the way she weighed everything now—against the clock, against Vane's breath running out.

"He'll burn the workshop," she said. "If he thinks the press is here and we've fled, he burns it."

"Then the press has done its work or it hasn't. Buildings aren't the atlas." I heard the words come out of me and recognized Vane's cadence inside them, and for one second the grief of that nearly buckled me at the knees. "Get your coat. We pack for the road, loudly."

She was halfway to the door when she stopped, and her face changed, and she said:

"Dominic. The landlady."

"What of her?"

"Hale has her son. Or soon will. She sold us once in small ways—the schedule, the comings and goings. Tonight we ask her to sell us in a large one." Ilse pulled her coat from its hook. "Pray she's better at betrayal than we are at trust."

Below us, in the street, a match scraped against a wall. Someone lighting a pipe. Someone patient enough to stand in the cold and wait for lamplight in our windows.

We gave it to them. We made the packing loud.
