## 29. Vane Found

The last coordinate resolved at four in the morning, and it was so ordinary that I laughed until my eyes stung.

A hospice. The Hospice of Saint Aldric, on the canal road beyond the tanneries — a place where the city sent people to be forgotten politely, with clean linen and laudanum and no questions asked. Vane had encoded his own hiding place in the atlas he'd let the world believe died with him, and the hiding place was a charity ward full of dying clerks and ruined sea captains.

"He's been there," Dominic said, staring at the decoded figures as if they might rearrange themselves into something more dramatic, "the whole time. Three months. While we dug through cipher and archive and grave dirt—"

"While you dug up his empty coffin," I said. "Yes."

Dominic had not slept properly since the night at the archive, and it showed in the fine tremor of his hands, in the way his voice kept sliding between fury and something softer that he hadn't yet permitted himself to name. He had spent three months mourning a man who was, by every indication we now possessed, sitting in a room with a view of a tannery chimney, watching the seasons change.

"We go now," Dominic said.

"We go at visiting hours," I said. "Like anyone else. Like family."

He looked at me then, and I saw him understand what I'd understood the moment the coordinate resolved: whatever waited for us at Saint Aldric's would require us to be ordinary. No maps, no ciphers, no codes. Just two people walking down a corridor to a bed where an old man was dying at the usual speed, by the usual means, with nothing hidden about it at all.

* * *

Visiting hours began at ten. We arrived at half past nine and sat on a bench by the canal, because I refused to walk in looking like what we were — two fugitives who had crossed a city by night carrying a decoded testimony ledger under Ilse's coat. Dominic kept checking his hands for ink. They were clean. He checked them anyway, four times, the way a man checks for a wound that has already stopped bleeding.

"Ask him," Dominic said finally.

"Ask him what? There are forty questions."

"Ask him why."

I watched a barge loaded with hides slide past, low in the water. "I know why," I said. "Don't you? He told you himself, in that confession you keep folded like a letter from a sweetheart. He couldn't publish. He wasn't brave enough. And then he couldn't bear to be near the work without publishing it, so he removed himself from the work. He took himself out of the equation like a man amputating his own hand."

"And left us holding the knife."

"He left you holding the atlas. He didn't know about me." That still caught somewhere under my ribs when I said it aloud. All those years of sealed packets, all those deliveries through six cities, and he had never once told me what I was carrying. He had used me the way he used everything — carefully, silently, with perfect craftsmanship and no confession.

"You're angry," Dominic said.

"I'm saving it," I said. "Anger spends quickly. I want mine to last through the corridor."

At ten we went in.

* * *

Saint Aldric's smelled of carbolic and cabbage and the particular sweetness that no amount of lime can scrub out of a building where people come to finish. A matron with spectacles on a chain looked at us over her ledger. "Names?"

"Family," I said. "Of Mr. Garnet. The old gentleman in the corner room."

"Garnet," she repeated, and there was the smallest pause before the word, the pause of a woman who has known for years that the name is wrong and has decided it is none of her business. "Third door on the left. He's having a good morning. Don't tire him."

The corridor was long and dim and lined with doors, each one shut, each one with a small card bearing a name that was probably also wrong. Dominic walked beside me with his shoulders up around his ears, and I realized he was afraid — not of soldiers or inspectors, but of a door handle.

I opened it myself.

* * *

He was smaller than I remembered.

That was the first thing. In my mind Aurelius Vane had always been a fixed quantity, a monument: the tall figure in the courier letters' signature block, the hand that never varied across eleven years of correspondence, the voice I had imagined a hundred times behind those sealed packets. The man in the bed was thin as a bundle of laths, propped on pillows, with a blanket over legs that had clearly stopped being consulted about anything. His hair was white now all the way through, no iron left in it. His hands lay on the coverlet, spotted, trembling faintly even at rest.

But his eyes were exactly as I remembered them, because I had invented them: gray, precise, already reading the room.

"Ah," he said. "Both of you. So you finished it."

Not *hello*. Not *forgive me*. Not even my name, though he must have known it — his handwriting had addressed me often enough. *So you finished it.* As if we were apprentices arriving late with a proof, and he the master with a correction ready.

Dominic stopped just inside the door. He did not sit. He stood the way a man stands at a graveside, except that the grave had gotten up and spoken.

"You watched your funeral," Dominic said.

"I did. From the lych-gate, in a borrowed coat. It rained." Vane's voice was dry as old paper, but steady, each word set down like a compass point. "Small turnout. You wept, Dominic. I found that difficult."

"Difficult." Dominic's laugh had nothing in it. "I carried your coffin."

"No. You carried a box of bricks and my best coat, which I regret — it was a good coat. But yes. You carried them well." He turned his head slightly toward me. There was something in the movement like a man conserving fuel. "And this is Ilse Brenner, whom I have never met and have deceived for eleven years. Sit down, both of you. I have perhaps two hours of good sense left today and I intend to spend them being honest, which is exhausting, so we should begin."

I sat. Dominic remained standing a moment longer, jaw working, and then he dragged the visitor's chair around to face the bed squarely and sat with his back straight as a plumb line.

"Why?" he said. Just that. The question he'd rehearsed on the bench.

Vane considered him. Outside, a cart went past on the canal road; the window shivered in its frame.

"Because I mapped Karsthal after the purge," he said, "and then I buried it, and then I spent nineteen years discovering that a burial is not the same as forgetting. You know the shape of it by now — you've read the confession, so I won't perform it twice. I saw enough. Enough that when Hale's men came asking what I'd surveyed along the northern quarter in the spring of '98, I gave them fire-damage studies and plague boundaries and they believed me, because I was very good, and because they wanted to believe that paper could kill a city as easily as soldiers could. I kept the true atlas. I told myself I kept it safe rather than secret. Those are different things, Dominic, and it took me nineteen years to learn the difference, and by then learning it wasn't enough. Publishing it required more courage than I had ever needed for any survey. So I did the next best thing to courage. I disappeared."

"That's not the next best thing," Dominic said quietly. "That's the opposite."

"Yes," said Vane. "I know that now. Thank you."

The plainness of it stopped Dominic cold. I watched him grope for the next accusation and find that the old man would simply agree with all of them, one by one, laying them down like plates on a press, and that agreement is a terrible thing to argue against.

"The commission," Dominic tried instead. "You set the deadline. Spring, or it voids. Why put a clock on it if you were gone?"

"Because a dead man can't hold a pen, and I needed the work to pass out of my hands into someone's. Yours. If I wrote to you directly — if I said, *finish it, publish it* — you'd have been an accomplice of mine, doing my will, carrying my risk, and you'd have hesitated at every page wondering what the old man would have wanted. Instead you inherited. An inheritance argues with no one." His eyes moved to me. "And the letters to Ilse — dated after my death, so that she would come, so that someone would arrive at that workshop with authority you couldn't dismiss. I'm sorry, Miss Brenner. You were the key that turned the lock. I never told you, because telling you made you dangerous, and I had promised your mother—"

"My mother," I said, "never met you."

"Her brother did," said Vane. "Emil Brenner, typesetter, Karsthal northern quarter. He printed the first leaflets the committee distributed before the purge. Afterward, when they were hunting everyone whose names appeared in print, he came to me with a survey question — a man asking about streets, nothing more — and I lied to him beautifully and sent him toward the river gate, and the river gate was where they were shooting. I have thought about that lie every day for thirteen years. When your mother wrote to me afterward — she guessed, women usually guess — I promised her that if there was ever anything to be recovered from Karsthal, I would see it recovered, and that her daughter would never be involved." He looked at my hands, folded in my lap. "I broke half the promise. Forgive the arithmetic of an old coward."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a man was coughing in long, patient spasms, and nobody went to him, because at Saint Aldric's everyone was coughing and the going-to was rationed.

I had carried his packets through customs searches and border snows and one revolution that never quite happened. I had kept his seals intact, his wrappings unopened, his schedule sacred. Eleven years of loyalty to a man I had built entirely out of handwriting. And here he was, smaller than his signature, telling me that my brother's name — the name I had followed into this whole affair like a thread through a labyrinth — had been in his keeping all along.

"What was Emil's packet?" I asked. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "In all those years of deliveries. Was I carrying pieces of this?"

"No. I was too careful for that. You carried survey fees, mostly. Some instruments. Once, a great deal of money to a widow in Brno, which I hope you never wondered about." He paused. "You were carrying my conscience, Miss Brenner. I outsourced it to couriers because I couldn't afford to keep it at home. That is what your service was. I don't dress it up as anything finer."

Dominic made a sound that might have been a breath or a word bitten in half. Vane turned back to him, and something shifted in the old man's face — the craftsman's mask slipping toward something rawer.

"You have the right to hate me," he said. "I'd think poorly of you if you didn't. But hear the terms first, because I'm short of time and you are not short of enemies. Hale let you leave the archive. Understand what that means — he has known roughly where this work lived since spring. He hasn't moved because he wants the whole apparatus: atlas, plates, testimony, witnesses. He wants it gathered in one room so it can be burned in one fire. Every day you spend finishing it, you are assembling his bonfire for him. Do you follow?"

"We follow," I said.

"Then here are the terms of whatever forgiveness you're deciding whether to extend. First—" he fumbled beneath the pillow with a hand that shook badly, and Dominic moved instinctively to help, and Vane slapped the help away with a flicker of the old ferocity, and got out a slim package wrapped in oilcloth — "the final plate corrections. The northern quarter elevations were wrong by design; I never corrected them because correcting them meant finishing, and finishing meant choosing. I've chosen. They're corrected. Your cipher resolves fully now."

Dominic took the oilcloth package as if it might be hot.

"Second. The testimony ledger you have is complete — Kessler's account, the others'. But the order itself, the document with signatures — I never held it. I only saw its consequences. Kessler saw the signature. Protect her; she is the load-bearing wall of the whole indictment."

"Third," Vane said, and here his voice dropped, and for the first time the compass-point steadiness wavered, "you may tell me I'm forgiven, or you may not. I'd ask you to decide quickly, but honestly — I've had three months in this bed to compose arguments and I find none of them survive daylight. So instead I'll say only this. I taught you projection, Dominic, and silence, and how to hold a pen so the line doesn't tremble. I did not teach you the one thing I knew and never used: that a map is a promise made to strangers. *This place exists. These people were here.* Everything else I did was draftsmanship. What you're holding is the promise. Keep it better than I did."

Dominic sat with the oilcloth package in his lap and looked at the old man for a long time. I braced for the anger to break — it had been building in him for days, for weeks, maybe for three months of grief disguised as diligence, and surely it had to break now, at the source, with the dead man alive to receive it.

But when he spoke, his voice was quiet, and that was worse than shouting.

"You watched me bury you."

"Yes."

"You watched me weep at the lych-gate, and you found it *difficult*."

"Yes."

"And then you went back to your room and let me grieve for three months while the men who erased a city closed in on the shop." Dominic's hands were steady. Only his voice carried the tremor. "Master — I can't forgive you today. Maybe I will when you're actually dead, and I'll feel foolish about it. But I'll finish the atlas. Not for you. For Emil Brenner, whose name I learned an hour ago. For Ada Kessler. For the street corners in the northern quarter that officially never existed." He rose. "You'll get no tender deathbed scene from me. I'll send Mrs. Ostrova with proper food, because this place feeds people cabbage, and I'll come back when there's a printed copy to put in your hands, and you'll live long enough to hold it, because you don't get to die twice and skip the ending."

Vane looked up at him. And I saw it — the thing I imagine he had crossed his own funeral rain to avoid feeling — relief, naked and enormous, at being refused gently rather than forgiven falsely.

"That," he said, "is the finest correction you have ever made to one of my drafts."

* * *

In the corridor, Dominic stopped and leaned his forehead against the wall for a moment, just one moment, and I pretended to read the admission cards so he could have it privately.

Outside, the canal glittered with midday. The tannery smoke stood straight up in the windless air like a drawn line.

"He's dying," Dominic said. "Really dying. Weeks, not months. Did you see—"

"I saw."

"Hale will find him eventually. The matron knows the name is false. One clerk, one bribe—" He straightened, and I watched the plan assemble behind his eyes the way Vane's coastlines used to assemble under his pen, feature by feature, inevitable. "We move the printing forward. Not next week. Tomorrow night. Everything — full run, split the plates, scatter it before Hale can gather his bonfire. And we move Vane."

"He can't be moved."

"He can't stay here. Ilse — if Hale gets to him, he doesn't just lose his life, he loses the story. They'll make his death mean whatever they want it to mean. A dead collaborator. A madman. An erasure inside an erasure." He was already walking, fast, toward the canal road. "We need a place. Ostrova won't— no, Ostrova's compromised, we can't—"

"Dominic." I caught his sleeve. "Look at the hospice."

I don't know what made me turn. Perhaps the matron's pause over the name *Garnet*, still ticking somewhere at the back of my mind. At an upper window of Saint Aldric's — the corner room, Vane's corner room — a figure stood looking down at the canal road. Not Vane. A heavyset man in a good coat, hatless, unhurried, watching the road the way a man watches water he intends to fish.

Corvus Hale had not needed a bribe or a clerk.

He had simply followed the same coordinate we had, and he had arrived first, and he was letting us visit — the way he had let Dominic leave the archive. Counting us. Gathering his fire.

Dominic saw him, and went very still, and then said, in a voice I had never heard him use before:

"He's already dying, Ilse. Whatever Hale does now, he's only racing us to a deathbed. So we stop running." He took my arm, and we walked — not hurrying, exactly matching the pace of two visitors leaving a hospice after saying their farewells. "Tomorrow night we print. And tomorrow morning, whatever it costs, I am moving my master out from under that man's window — or burning the whole arrangement down trying."
