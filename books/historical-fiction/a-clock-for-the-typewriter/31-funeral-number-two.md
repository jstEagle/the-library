## 31. Funeral Number Two

The parish clerk's hands shook when he handed us the paperwork, and I understood why. He was the same clerk Vane had bribed years ago to empty a grave that wasn't yet occupied. Now he was being asked to fill one with the man himself, under a name everyone in the ministry believed had died three months earlier and been buried under another name entirely. The paperwork said *Aurelius Kessler-Vane, known also as Aurelius Brandt, born Karsthal 1841*. Ilse had found the birth record in the cipher's final pages. His real name. Not the one on the first tombstone, not the one on the workshop license—*Karsthal-born*, which meant the man we were burying had come out of the erased city like everything else we'd printed.

"He can't be buried here," the clerk whispered. "The register will show it. Anyone who checks the register—"

"Let them check," Ilse said.

I had spent my whole apprenticeship learning that the safest place for a truth was inside a line so fine no one could read it without a lens. Ilse had spent hers learning that some truths needed to be shouted in a register office with bad heating. We buried Aurelius Vane on a Thursday morning in March, and I finally understood the difference between us, and why the atlas needed both.

There were eleven of us at the graveside. Mrs. Ostrova came, though she stood apart from the others and did not remove her gloves, and when the priest stumbled over the name she repeated it once, quietly, correctly, as though correcting an elevation on a proof sheet. Ada Kessler came, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, leaning on the arm of a young woman I had never seen before—later I learned she was a typesetter from one of the foreign presses, and that she had asked to come because she had set two pages of our atlas herself without knowing what they were until they were done. There were three men I did not know, whom Ilse greeted with a nod and no names exchanged, and whom I understood, by the way they stood at the foot of the grave rather than the head, to be survivors. Karsthal men. They had come because a map had told them where to stand.

And there was Corvus Hale, across the road.

He arrived after us—he wanted us to see him arrive—and he stood on the opposite pavement with his hat brim low and his hands clasped behind his back, exactly as a man stands when he has decided that attending is itself the message. He did not cross. He did not send anyone across. He simply watched, and I understood that this was the most honest thing he had done since the inspector's visit: he was telling us he knew, and that knowing was, for now, enough.

* * *

I should go back to the beginning of that morning, because beginnings are what maps are for.

We had buried Vane in January under the name on his workshop papers—*Aurelius Vane, cartographer, aged seventy-one, died of the chest complaint natural to his years*—and the burial had been a small gray affair conducted in weather that made even the priest hurry. I had stood at the grave then feeling nothing I could name, because the man in the box was a stranger wearing my master's story, and the man I mourned was alive somewhere I couldn't reach, having chosen to watch his own funeral rather than attend it. Grief needs a body. That January burial had given me a coffin with no grief inside it, and I had walked home through the snow feeling like a cartographer whose survey shows coastline where every chart says open sea.

Now, in March, we put the right man in the ground.

His body had come to us from the hospice in a plain box that smelled of carbolic and pine, and Ilse had insisted on washing him herself, and I had helped—not because I was brave but because she asked me to hold the basin, and holding the basin turned out to be its own kind of education. His hands were mapmaker's hands to the end: ink in the creases that would not wash out, the left thumbnail ridged where he had habitually scraped his pen knife. I held those hands for a moment longer than the washing required. Ilse saw me do it and said nothing, which is the greatest mercy one collaborator can show another.

We dressed him in the good coat from the workshop press room—the one with the brass buttons he wore when clients came—and Ilse sewed into the lining a folded square of paper. I asked her what it was. She said, "The northern quarter," and I stopped asking, because there are things you carry into the ground and things you print, and both are ways of keeping them.

The service was held at ten in the morning, at the parish church whose clerk had spent eleven years keeping one secret and now, in a single week, had acquired a second. The priest was a young man with careful eyes who had clearly been told the deceased's name only that morning and had spent the night deciding how much of it to say aloud. In the end he said all of it. "Aurelius Kessler-Vane," he read, and then, whether from courage or simple inability to find another word, "of Karsthal."

The word went through the church like a draft through a broken window.

Mrs. Ostrova's chin lifted. The three strangers at the foot of the grave went very still—one of them closed his eyes and kept them closed for perhaps ten seconds, and I watched him do it and understood I was watching a man hear his city named in public for the first time in thirteen years. Ada Kessler reached up and gripped her typesetter's hand hard enough that I saw the girl wince.

I looked at the road. Hale had not moved. But he had taken off his hat.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the trade I was trained in. Corvus Hale removed his hat while the word *Karsthal* was spoken over the grave, and he did not put it back on until the reading was finished. I do not know what it meant. It may have been mockery; it may have been professional courtesy between erasures; it may have been the single unguarded second of a man remembering something. But I noted it, the way I note an unmapped current, and I note it here because noting is all I have left, and because whatever else Hale was, he was never careless with symbols, and a man who removes his hat knows exactly what removing his hat says.

* * *

After the committal, while the gravediggers waited with the studied patience of men who have dug too many holes that winter, Ilse did the thing we had argued about for three nights.

She addressed the grave.

Not a eulogy—there was no pulpit, no permission, no form. She simply stood at the head of the pit with the damp earth already going in, and spoke in the carrying, unhurried voice of a woman who had spent years delivering sealed packets and knew exactly how much volume a sentence needs to cross a street.

"Aurelius Kessler-Vane," she said, "was born in Karsthal. He mapped Karsthal. He watched Karsthal destroyed and he spent the rest of his life drawing the evidence, and he finished it, and it is printed, and it cannot be recalled. Eleven hundred copies exist. They are in six countries. His last correction is in the plates."

One of the gravediggers stopped shoveling.

"The Ministry of Archives," Ilse went on, "has stated that no city called Karsthal exists. This morning a registered parish record states that a man of Karsthal has been buried in consecrated ground, with witnesses, under license. Both statements cannot survive inspection. We have chosen to let them fight it out in the open."

She stepped back. That was all. No names of the dead, no accusations, no speech about justice—she knew better than any of us that a funeral is not a tribunal, and that the power of the moment lay precisely in how little she claimed. She had stated facts. Facts are worse than accusations; accusations can be denied, but a register entry has to be *amended*, and amendments leave marks.

Across the road, Hale put his hat back on.

Then he did something I did not expect, and have thought about every day since. He crossed the road.

He walked slowly, alone, past the gravediggers, past Mrs. Ostrova—who drew herself up like a fortress gate—until he stood at the edge of the gathering, at a distance polite enough for a stranger and precise enough for an enemy, and he looked down into the half-filled grave for a long moment.

"Master Vane taught projection at the Technical Institute," he said, to no one in particular, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the graveside and no farther. "Winter term, 1889. I was not his student. I attended one lecture." He straightened his cuff. "It was about distortion. What a map chooses to flatten." A pause. "He made the point that flattening is always a decision. Someone decides what gets stretched, and what gets allowed to tear."

He looked up then, directly at me, and his face was perfectly courteous and perfectly empty, the way it had been in the workshop when he offered to buy the estate's papers.

"Good morning, Mr. Montrose," he said. "My condolences on your loss. Again."

And he walked back across the road and stood where he had stood, and waited, as we lowered the master into the ground under his true name, with the whole street watching and the whole city, eventually, going to hear about it.

* * *

That evening, back in the workshop—with the false wall sealed and the press cold and the last copies already gone south in flour sacks—I sat at Vane's desk with the lamp turned low and did the thing I had avoided since the hospice: I opened his final ledger and began copying the testimony pages in my own hand, in duplicate, in the small invisible script he had taught me for marginal notes.

Ilse came in past midnight. She stood in the doorway with snow melting on her shoulders and looked at the desk, the ledgers, the duplicates drying in rows.

"You're making backups," she said.

"I'm making a map of what can't be burned," I said. "It's a new discipline. Very small territory. Extremely detailed."

She almost smiled. Then she took off her coat and hung it, and pulled a chair to the other side of the desk, and picked up a pen, and for two hours we copied in silence, the two of us, the survivors' names moving from one book into four.

At some point she said, "Hale didn't stop us today."

"No," I said.

"He could have. A funeral is the easiest arrest in the world. Everyone standing still, all the suspects in one field."

"Yes."

"So he's not waiting to see if we break." She put down her pen. "He's waiting for something else. Something he needs us to do first."

I thought about the hat coming off. I thought about the word *distortion*, and the lecture he claimed to have attended, and the fact that a man like Hale does not attend lectures by accident.

"There's a register entry now," I said. "Consecrated ground, witnessed, licensed. If the Ministry amends it, the amendment becomes news. If they don't amend it, the entry stands, and every genealogist and lawyer and parish clerk who opens that register finds Karsthal looking back at them. He can't erase a burial the way you erase a city. Burials have too many eyes."

"Then what does he do?"

I closed the ledger. Outside, the frost was rebuilding its white survey of the streets, and somewhere across the roofs the corner flat was dark or lit—I couldn't tell, and no longer tried to.

"He escalates," I said. "Quietly, legally, with documents. He's an archivist. He'll fight a record with a record." I looked at her. "Ilse—somewhere in the ministry tonight there's a file being opened with our names in it, and probably Vane's real name, and probably the words *sedition* already drafted. The funeral wasn't the declaration. It was the invitation."

She nodded slowly, and blew out the lamp, and we sat together in the dark of the workshop like two people in the hold of a ship, listening for weather.

"They'll come for the plates," she said.

"The plates are gone."

"Then they'll come for us."

"Yes," I said. "Soon. And when they do, the only thing left to decide is what we're holding when the door comes down."

Far off across the city, a clock struck one, and kept striking in the other towers, each tower a few seconds behind the last, so that the hour seemed to travel across the rooftops like something being handed from district to district—from hand to hand, the way a page is, the way a name is—and I remember thinking, with the strange calm that precedes all disasters worth surviving: let it travel.
