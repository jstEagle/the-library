## 32. Everything Burns Eventually

Mrs. Ostrova brought up the tea at four o'clock and would not meet my eyes.

She set the tray on the press-room table among the drying proofs, said something about the cold coming in from the river, and left before I could thank her. The door clicked shut behind her with the particular softness of a door closed by someone who does not want to hear what happens next.

I stood there with the teapot cooling and thought: *she knows.*

Or rather—I had known for two days, in the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop falls, from the pressure in your ears, the strange hush of the birds. Ostrova had stopped looking at us. She had started looking *through* us, which is what people do when they have already decided which side of the wall they will be standing on when it comes down.

"Dominic," I called through to the front room. "Stop counting plates. We have to talk."

* * *

He came in wiping ink from his fingers, and I made him sit, because he had taken to standing over the proofs the way a physician stands over a patient he is not honest enough to tell dying.

"Ostrova," I said.

"I know."

"You know she knows, or you know what she'll do?"

He was quiet for a moment. That was answer enough. Dominic Montrose had spent his whole life believing that if you were careful enough, quiet enough, small enough, the world would pass you by like a ship passing an unlit buoy. He had learned better these last weeks—the chalk marks, Hale's courteous visits, the inspector's polite questions—but the lesson sat badly on him still. He kept reaching for the old faith the way a man reaches for a banister that is no longer there.

"She has a son," he said finally. "In the north. Hale will have found him by now, if he hadn't already. She won't betray us out of malice, Ilse. She'll do it the way everything in this country gets done—reluctantly, politely, and completely."

"Then we assume tomorrow night. Maybe tonight."

"Sooner." He looked at the press—old Vane's press, iron and oak, older than either of us, older than Karsthal's grave. "We assume dawn."

Outside the window the street lay grey and ordinary. A man stood at the corner by the lamppost, reading a newspaper he held upside down. He had been reading it, in one position or another, since noon.

"We can't run," I said.

"We've discussed—"

"No. We haven't discussed. You've proposed and I've refused, and now we're past discussing, because running takes a day we don't have, and even if we had it—" I spread my hands over the proofs, forty sheets of the northern quarter, Kessler's testimony set in type beside the coordinates of the mass graves, the whole murdered city rising off the paper in contour lines and street grids as though it had never been erased at all. "Look at this. If we run, we carry one atlas. One. It burns with us or it rots in a satchel in some barn, and Hale wins by simply waiting. Vane ran for thirteen years, Dominic. Thirteen years of being careful. And where did careful get him? A hospice bed and a funeral he attended from the next room."

Dominic flinched. He always flinched at Vane; grief and fury had not yet finished their argument inside him, and perhaps they never would. But he stayed seated. He was learning.

"So we don't run," he said slowly.

"We finish."

* * *

We worked out the plan on the back of a spoiled proof, in pencil, and when we were done we burned the pencil sketch in the stove and committed the whole thing to memory, which is the only vault Hale cannot raid.

The arithmetic was brutal and simple. We had three hundred sheets of good paper left, smuggled in over five weeks in flour sacks whose contents Mrs. Ostrova had once weighed with suspicious care. At full pace the press could turn out perhaps two hundred and fifty finished copies before dawn—if nothing broke, if no plate cracked, if our hands held. Every copy had to leave the building before the ink on the last one was dry.

"Not one copy stays here," I said. "Not one. If they find a single sheet, they don't just destroy it—they prove it never existed. They arrest the correspondents who received it, they burn whatever reached abroad, and the story becomes exactly the story they told in 1898: nothing happened, and anyone who says otherwise is a liar or a madman."

"But if two hundred copies scatter—"

"Then it stops mattering what Hale burns. He can burn this workshop and us with it, and the atlas walks into Vienna, into London, into Amsterdam without us. A book doesn't need its authors alive. That's the whole mercy of books."

He looked at me strangely then—not with pity, which I had grown used to, but with something closer to recognition. My brother had been dead fifteen years, swallowed by Karsthal in the spring of 1898 along with six thousand other names, and I had spent more than a decade carrying his absence the way you carry a stone in your boot: painful, familiar, private. Somewhere between Vane's cipher and Ada Kessler's kitchen table, that stone had turned into an engine.

"You're not afraid," Dominic said. It was almost an accusation.

"I'm terrified." I began squaring the stack of clean paper against the table edge, needing my hands busy. "But fear is a bad cartographer. It only ever maps one place—the way out—and most of the time the way out isn't on it. Vane taught me that, in his letters. He wrote that a map is a promise: that the world exists whether or not the powerful admit it. Well. Tonight we keep the promise."

* * *

The division of the plates took until dusk.

There were thirty-one of them—copper, each one etched with a quarter of the city, the coastlines, the index of graves, the appended testimony. Too heavy and too conspicuous to move together. So we split them: sixteen into the false-bottomed trunk that would go with me through the coal chute and down the alley to the river steps, where a fishmonger named Brandt—who owed Vane a debt he had never explained to me—would have a boat waiting. Fifteen into the lining of Dominic's coat, if it came to that, if it came to the worst.

"Don't say it," I warned him.

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You were going to say that you stay and I go, and that I shouldn't argue, and that it's simple arithmetic—one of us can be caught more cheaply than two."

"It *is* simple arithmetic."

"It's wrong arithmetic. They catch the one who runs, they follow the runner to the plates. No. We both go, or—" I stopped myself, because the third branch of that sentence was the one we had been circling for days, and saying it aloud would give it furniture, would let it sit in the room with us like a guest.

Dominic said it anyway. Quietly. "Or one of us stays long enough to make them certain nothing got out."

"Absolutely not."

"Somebody has to stall them, Ilse. If Hale comes at four and finds the workshop empty and cold, he'll have riders at the river before the boat clears the bridge. If he comes and finds a nervous apprentice burning papers badly—"

"Badly?"

"Badly. I've been practicing looking guilty for twenty years; it's the one thing I'm genuinely qualified for."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to throw the inkstone at him. The two impulses arrived together and cancelled somewhere in my chest, leaving only a cold clear anger, and under the anger something worse—affection, terrible, inconvenient affection for this stubborn, self-effacing man who had decided, sometime after Vane's second funeral, that his own life was the least expensive object in the workshop.

"Listen to me," I said, and took his face in my ink-stained hands, so he had nowhere to look but at me. "You are not buying my escape with yourself. That is not what tonight is. Tonight is a printing job. We print, we pack, we scatter, we *both* walk out—that's the plan, and if the plan bends, it bends toward both of us living, do you understand? I lost everyone to Karsthal already. My brother, my mother's whole street, the city itself. I am not donating you to it. I refuse."

He didn't answer. That frightened me more than anything Hale could do.

* * *

By nine o'clock the press was running.

There is a sound an old iron hand-press makes when it is working well—a sound like a slow heartbeat, like a clock keeping honest time—and by midnight the workshop was full of it, and full of the smell of oil and fresh ink, and I understood for the first time why Vane had chosen this trade and this room and this long, patient, dangerous obedience to paper. Each sheet came off the platen warm. Each sheet was Karsthal, alive again, its streets named, its northern quarter decoded, its dead counted and *located*, which is the one thing erasure cannot survive.

Dominic pulled. I fed. We had done it enough nights now that we moved around each other without speaking, passing sheets and plates and rags in a rhythm close to dance. Twice the roller stuck; once a plate hairline-cracked at the corner and we printed through it, accepting the flaw, because a flawed map beats a burned one.

At half past one we heard footsteps cross the yard below and stop.

We killed the lamp and stood in the dark, listening. The footsteps waited. Then—lighter, quicker—they crossed back toward the house, and a key turned in Ostrova's side door, and we heard her climb the stairs slowly, pausing on every landing like a woman rehearsing something.

"She's already given them the time," Dominic murmured against my ear. "Tonight or tomorrow. She's coming up to say goodbye to us while she still can."

She didn't come up. Her door closed. The footsteps above went silent.

We lit the lamp and pulled the next sheet.

* * *

At three o'clock we packed.

Two hundred and eleven copies, stacked in bundles of twenty-five, wrapped in the brown paper from the flour sacks and tied with twine. Eight bundles to go out through the coal chute with me and Brandt's boat—to the *Neue Freie Presse*'s correspondent, to the Englishman Marsh at the shipping office, to the Dutchman, to the Swiss, to four addresses Vane had encoded years ago in the atlas's margin notes, correspondents who owed him favors or grudges against our government, which amounts to the same currency. Three bundles to go out the window at the back onto Ostrova's own woodshed roof, where the iceman would collect them at six on his rounds—he didn't know what he was collecting and must never. One bundle to stay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, addressed to no one: a seed, buried where only fire would find it, and fire finding it would mean the rest had gone.

And the plates split. Sixteen and fifteen. My trunk and his coat.

I looked at the fifteenth bundle, the seed under the floor, and felt the shape of the night change. Because a buried bundle means someone expects the burial. And Dominic had gone very quiet, the particular quiet of a man who has made a decision and is waiting for the right moment to confess it, and I recognized that quiet because it was Vane's quiet, exactly, precisely, the quiet of the master reproduced in the apprentice like a watermark in paper.

"No," I said.

"The ink on the last sheets won't be dry until nearly six. Dawn's at ten past. Hale won't come in the dark—he's a bureaucrat, he'll want witnesses and daylight and the appearance of procedure—so he comes just after dawn, and someone needs to be here, visibly, ordinarily, burning papers in the stove, so that for one hour he believes—"

"I said no."

"—he believes the work died with the workshop. Ilse. Listen to the logic—"

"I have listened to the logic! I've listened to nothing else for three days!" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated giving him that. I lowered it to a whisper that was worse than shouting. "You want me to take the plates and row away while you perform a play for Corvus Hale, and the ending of the play is written by Corvus Hale. You saw what he did to Vane's letters. You think he arrests mapmakers? He erases them. That is his entire profession. He will erase you the way he erased a city, and I will be in Amsterdam reading about an accidental fire, and I will spend the rest of my life being the only person in the world who knows you existed, and that—" my hands were shaking, so I pressed them flat on the wet proofs, on the streets of Karsthal, on my brother's street, cool and damp under my palms, "—that is exactly what happened to me already. Once. I won't survive it twice."

For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the press cooling and, outside, the river pushing against the embankment.

"Then change the plan," Dominic said softly. "You're better at it than I am. You always have been."

It was not surrender. I knew that. It was a door held open, and whatever walked through it would be mine to answer for. But I walked through it anyway, because the alternative was leaving him behind, and I had told him the truth: I would not do that twice.

So we changed it. We would both go out the coal chute at half past five. We would leave the lamps lit, the stove fed with spoiled proofs, the workshop wearing its costume of ordinary guilt—and we would take the last hour of darkness together, all of us, the plates and the paper and the two of us, down the alley to the water. If Hale's men watched the alley, we would be caught together, and I found I preferred that arithmetic absolutely. If they did not—

"If they don't," Dominic said, turning the last proof on the table, "then we row."

"Then we row," I agreed.

He smiled then, the first real one in days, brief and tired and crooked. "Vane would say we're sentimental."

"Vane faked his own death and hid in a hospice for thirteen years. Vane doesn't get a vote."

* * *

At a quarter past five the sky over the rooftops had gone the colour of dirty pewter, and two hundred and eleven atlases of a city that officially never existed lay bundled and tied in a shuttered workshop, and a man across the street put away his upside-down newspaper and spoke quietly to another man who had just come around the corner, and the second man began to walk toward the yard door without hurrying, because he was quite sure there was no need to hurry at all.

I blew out the lamp.

"Chute," I whispered. "Now."

Dominic hoisted the trunk onto his shoulder, and in the grey light before the dawn of the longest day's end, we went down into the dark together—the coal dust rising around us, the plates hard against our backs, and behind us, upstairs, the stove already beginning to glow through the seams of its door like something waking up.

The yard door creaked open as my feet found the coal heap.

A voice from the shadows said, pleasantly: "Going somewhere, Fräulein?"
