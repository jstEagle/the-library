## 33. The Longest Night

**DOMINIC**

The press had a voice, and at two in the morning it sounded like forgiveness.

*Thump. Squeal. Thump.* The old Albion in the back room hadn't run in three years—Vane had oiled it one last time and covered it like a man covering a cage—but the machinery remembered. That was the thing about presses, about maps, about ink: they remembered what people were made to forget.

I fed it the first plate of the northern quarter and pulled the roller across, and Karsthal came up out of the metal in black lines, street by street, as if the city were being born instead of resurrected.

My hands were already ruined. Ink under every nail, in the creases of my knuckles, in a cut on my left thumb I'd stopped noticing hours ago. It didn't matter. By dawn I would be nothing but hands.

"Plate four," Ilse said from the drying racks, without looking up. She'd learned the rhythm faster than I had. She hung each sheet by its corner with a clothespin, forty to a line, and when a line was full she started another, and the workshop slowly filled with hanging paper like a laundry of ghosts.

We had until the flour cart came at six. Mrs. Ostrova's nephew drove it; whether he was ours or Hale's by then, none of us said aloud. We printed anyway. There was no other direction left to move.

"Pull," Ilse said, which meant *stop stalling*, and I pulled.

* * *

**ILSE**

There is a moment in every sheet when the image is still wet and anything is true. The ink gleams. The streets shine. Karsthal looks less like a map than like a window someone forgot to close before winter, and if you lean close enough—and I leaned close, God help me, on nearly every sheet—you can almost hear it: tram bells, a market arguing about fish, my brother's laugh somewhere in the third district where the contour lines spelled out his name.

I did not let myself cry over the wet pages. Tears spot ink. I had learned that by midnight.

Dominic worked like a man possessed by something gentle. He'd been Vane's for ten years, shaped by the old man's silences, and tonight all that training came home: he moved through the print run the way a priest moves through mass, every gesture rehearsed, nothing wasted, everything sacred. When a sheet misfired—a ghost of the previous plate bleeding through—he didn't curse. He set it aside, face unreadable, and squared a fresh sheet on the tympan.

"Misfires go in the stove," he said. "Not the bin."

"The bin burns too."

"The stove burns *tonight*. The bin can be searched."

That was the night in miniature. Everything we touched had to be either finished or ash by morning. No middle ground. Hale had taught us that much, in his courteous way.

By three we had two hundred sheets of the first plate. My arms ached from the racks. Dominic's shoulders had locked into the pull-and-release of the press bar like a piston. Neither of us spoke more than five words at a time, and it was the best conversation of my life.

* * *

**DOMINIC**

The second plate fought us.

It was the testimony plate—the one carrying Ada Kessler's account, set in letters so small they looked like weather. Vane's hand had cut most of the text blocks himself; you could feel him in the burin strokes, impatient, angry, alive. Halfway through the run the plate began to fill in, ink creeping into the fine hatching, and suddenly we were printing a gray smear where a massacre should be.

"No," I said, as if scolding a dog. "No, no—"

"Stop. Clean it." Ilse was already at the solvent tin. "How long?"

"Ten minutes if we're lucky. The plate has to cool too."

"Then we cool it and clean it and we don't waste the ten minutes." She pointed at the third plate, waiting on the bench. "Set that one up. We're not a press, we're two presses."

She was right, and I hated her a little for how easily she was right, and I loved her entirely for it. Ten months ago I couldn't have shared this room with anyone. Now I couldn't imagine finishing without her.

While the plate soaked I set up plate three—the survivors' coordinates, the hiding places, the whole quiet network Vane had encoded into hills that never existed. Laying it on the bed, I caught myself whispering the names. Kessler. Brandt. Ostrowski. The ones who ran. The ones who hid. The ones who are still, this morning, breathing somewhere because an old coward kept a ledger.

"You're doing it again," Ilse said softly.

"Doing what."

"Praying. You do it with your hands on the plates."

I hadn't prayed since Vane's funeral—the false one. I didn't correct her.

* * *

**ILSE**

At four the lamp oil ran low and the light went amber and honest, and we saw each other properly for the first time in hours: two people blackened to the elbows, hair stuck down with sweat, ink on Dominic's cheekbone where he'd pushed it back with a dirty wrist. He looked like a typesetter in a story. He looked like someone who would survive.

The plate was clean. The run resumed. *Thump. Squeal. Thump.*

I hung the sheets and read them as they dried—I couldn't stop reading them, even though I knew every word. *On the fourteenth of October, 1898, the order was signed...* Hale's signature reproduced in facsimile, because Vane had understood, years before any of us, that a name in ink is harder to kill than a city.

Somewhere around sheet eighty I understood what exhaustion was for. It wasn't punishment. It was permission. Tired enough, you stop guarding yourself, and the grief gets to walk around inside you like a paying guest. I hung page after page and let my brother's street go past me, and the market, and the bridge where he was last seen, and I did not look away, because the whole point of tonight was *not looking away*, was making sure that no one, ever again, would be able to.

"Dominic." My voice cracked on his name. He looked up from the press. "How many?"

He counted the racks, quick and exact. "Three hundred and forty complete sets, once plate four finishes. Plus proofs."

"Is it enough?"

"It's enough that they can't catch them all." He wiped his hands on his apron, which only redistributed the ink. "That's the only arithmetic that matters now. Not enough to win—enough to make erasure impossible."

Outside, far off, a cart rattled. We both froze, rollers lifted, listening like animals. It passed.

Six weeks ago I had come to this door with dead man's letters and nowhere else to go. Tonight I was going to walk out of it with a city under my arm.

* * *

**DOMINIC**

Plate four was the master's own plate—Karsthal entire, the title plate, VANE'S hand in the lettering, AURELIUS spelled out plain for the first time in thirteen years. Pulling it felt less like printing than like signing his name for him, one hundred and forty times, until the dead man had autographed his way back into the world.

Near the end I got careless. Fatigue does that—it makes you bold in exactly the wrong places. I let a sheet sit crooked on the bed and pulled anyway, and the impression came out doubled, Karsthal printed twice, slightly offset, so that every street ran beside its own shadow.

I should have culled it. Instead I hung it on the rack at the end, alone, and left it there.

Some mistakes are truer than the plan.

Ilse saw it and didn't say anything, just touched my shoulder once, briefly, and went back to the racks. That was our whole language by then. We'd used up ordinary words around two o'clock.

The sky beyond the shutters had gone the color of wet slate when we pulled the last sheet. Four-forty by the workshop clock—the clock Vane kept wound for a typewriter he never owned, the clock he'd told me once was for *the book somebody would write someday*. I wound it while Ilse collated. Some habits are inheritances.

Complete sets: three hundred and thirty-eight. Plates cleaned, wrapped in oilcloth, packed in the false-bottomed flour crate. Proofs burned in the stove along with every letter, every scrap, every misfire. The fire ate them greedily, as if it had been waiting all night for its share.

"Ilse." I took the crate and put it in her arms myself, because I wanted her to feel the weight and remember it. "Coal chute, out the yard, down Tanner's Row to the second arch. The boat doesn't wait past six."

"And you?"

"I'm going to keep printing," I said. "Empty press, but they won't know that until they're inside. Every minute they watch me pull blank sheets is a minute you're not on the road."

"Don't." Just that. Flat, furious, afraid.

"The ink's drying on three hundred and thirty-eight cities," I told her. "Let me be worth one of them."

* * *

**ILSE**

I could argue with him. I had argued with him about everything for months—letters, plates, whether to trust Ostrova, whether hope was a strategy. But there was nothing left to argue with. He had already become a man standing in front of a door, and doors like that don't take corrections.

So I did the only obedient thing left. I kissed his ink-black cheek, tasted soot, and said, "You finish your sheets, mapmaker. I'll finish mine."

Then the chute, the dark, the cold iron scraping my back, the crate held above my head like something newborn.

Behind me, muffled through brick and coal dust, I heard the press start up again. *Thump. Squeal. Thump.* Blank sheets, probably. Or maybe not. Maybe he'd hang one more crooked, doubled Karsthal before the door came down—one more city beside its own shadow—because that was who he'd finally decided to be.

I ran toward the river with morning coming up behind me like a witness.

And somewhere across the roofs, already, I could see the wagons turning onto our street.
