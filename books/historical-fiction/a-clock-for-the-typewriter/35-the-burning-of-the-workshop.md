## 35. The Burning of the Workshop

They did not let me watch them carry out the press.

That was the first thing I understood, standing between two constables in my own workshop while Hale's men moved through it like appraisers—lifting the rollers, tapping the frame of the old Albion press with a crowbar, testing its weight against their shoulders. They were careful with it. That surprised me until I saw why: they intended to burn it, and men who intend to burn a thing like to know how much heat it will give.

Hale stood by the door with his hands folded behind him, watching me rather than the room. He had not slept either; I could see it in the gray under his eyes, in the way his collar sat slightly crooked, as though he had dressed in a hurry or in anger. For a man who had spent his career being patient, he looked impatient now, and I understood that this was what losing looked like on him—a small crookedness, a crease where there should have been none.

"The plates," he said. It was not a question.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Montrose." My name, spoken the way a man speaks to a horse he has decided not to buy. "The girl went down your coal chute twenty minutes ago. I have a man at the bottom of it. Or I had one. He reports she was carrying a satchel, and that she knew the alleys better than he did, which tells me she has walked them before, at night, recently—which means you printed here, in this room, and carried the results out through the coal." He turned his head slowly, surveying the workshop: the drying lines I had taken down an hour before, the ink smell that no amount of open window could fully kill, the flour-sack paper stacked in what I had hoped was an innocent-looking pile. "I am not angry. I want you to understand that. Anger is for men who are surprised. I am only doing arithmetic."

"Then do it quietly," I said. "It's early still. Mrs. Ostrova sleeps badly."

Something moved across his face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would go if he had kept one in working order. "Mrs. Ostrova is being looked after. As you will be."

* * *

They had come at half past four, when the sky is the color of dishwater and even the boldest thief is asleep. Six men, no shouting, boots wrapped in cloth so they made no sound on the stairs—I would never have known except that I had not been asleep either, because none of us had slept since Vane's second funeral, and because I had been sitting up with the last sheets, waiting for the ink to set.

Thirty-one copies. That was all the night had given us. The paper had run short around three o'clock; Ilse had counted the sheets twice, bitten her lip, and begun feeding the press more slowly, as if slowness could stretch paper the way it stretched time. We had worked without speaking most of the night, in the rhythm we had learned over weeks—the crank, the pull, the lift, the lay—our hands black to the wrist, our faces smeared where we'd pushed hair out of our eyes. At some point she had laughed at something, I don't remember what, a small exhausted laugh, and I had thought: this is what a print shop sounds like when it is also a church.

Then the knock. Three knocks, polite, at an hour when no polite person knocks.

Ilse had looked at me across the press and I had looked back, and there was nothing to say because we had already said it, days ago, in the argument we never finished—the one about who stays. She had won it by simply picking up the satchel while I was still composing my case. She was through the false wall and into the coal chute before I finished saying her name, and by the time I had latched the wall panel behind her the door was opening and Hale was filling it, courteous as a banker's letter.

Now the copies lay where I had left them: in the cooling drawer, under a layer of proof sheets, thirty-one pages of a city that three governments had agreed did not exist. Karsthal, street by street. Karsthal, quarter by quarter. And folded with them, the appendix that Ada Kessler had dictated in her cracked, unhurried voice while Ilse wrote and I checked names against the ledger: two hundred and eleven names of people who had been in the Northern Quarter on the fourth of November, 1898, and what had been done to them, and by whose order.

Hale wanted those pages. Everything else—the press, the type, the letters he had already seized—he could afford to destroy. The pages he needed, because destroyed things can be argued with, but testimony cannot.

"Here is what happens now," he said, and his voice took on the tone of a man reading terms of sale. "The press goes. The type goes. This building has poor wiring and a landlord who owes the ministry several favors; by tonight it will be an accident, and the accident will be blamed on you, and you will be a criminal who burned his own shop for the insurance, which is a small crime, an ugly little crime that no one writes pamphlets about." He paused, letting the room settle around the words. "Or. You tell me where the girl is going, and the copies surface in my hands instead of in some foreign correspondent's dispatch, and you walk out of here with money and anonymity, the same offer I made you before, improved by circumstance."

"And if I say nothing?"

"Then you leave in irons, and the fire starts anyway, and everything you stayed to protect burns with you in it." He said it without heat. That was the terrible thing about Corvus Hale; he had removed heat from himself the way a surgeon removes a bullet, cleanly, and what remained was only procedure. "I don't enjoy this part. I want you to know that too. Vane didn't believe me when I told him, years ago, that I take no pleasure in my work. Perhaps you'll believe me."

"You knew Vane," I said. "Before."

"I audited him," Hale said. "He was the best mapmaker the ministry ever employed and the worst liar, which is why he is alive and I am tired." He glanced toward the window, where the sky had begun, very faintly, to lighten—not dawn yet, but the rumor of it. "Well, Montrose. The arithmetic."

I looked at the cooling drawer. I looked at the window above it, the one that faced the alley where the coal chute emptied, narrow, dark, overlooked by nothing but the blank wall of the chandler's storehouse. I thought about the distance from the drawer to the sill. I thought about how far a bundle of thirty-one sheets weighs almost nothing, and falls almost silently, if you throw it flat.

And I thought about Vane, in the hospice bed, his hands too weak now to hold a pen, telling me without drama, the way he told me everything: *A map is only finished when someone else reads it. Until then it is a diary.*

"The arithmetic is wrong," I said. "You've forgotten to carry something."

"And what is that?"

"How long ink takes to dry on damp paper." I smiled at him. It cost me everything I had, and it was worth it. "Your men wrapped their boots, Mr. Hale. Very quiet. But quiet takes time, and time is the one thing last night's run didn't have. Those sheets in the drawer aren't set. Smudge them and every copy is ruined; handle them gently and it takes another hour, and in another hour—" I shrugged. "Well. In another hour anything might happen. A city might exist."

For the first time since he had entered my workshop, Corvus Hale moved quickly.

* * *

They say afterward that I fought. I want it on record, in whatever record survives, that I did not fight. Fighting was Ilse's department; she had the satchel, the alleys, the morning train, the whole running future. What I had was slower and stranger: the particular courage of a man standing in front of a piece of furniture.

When Hale lunged for the cooling drawer I got there first—or rather, I had been there all along, because I had planned it in the moment I smiled at him, and possibly before that, possibly in some chamber of myself that had been planning it since the night I watched Master Vane correct his final plate with dying hands. I swept the stack off the drawer and into my arms—thirty-one copies plus the loose sheets, warm and faintly tacky—and I turned my back to the room, and I opened the window with one hand, and I threw.

Not far. That was the trick of it. A strong throw goes over the roofline of the chandler's storehouse and lands where anyone can find it. A flat, weak, desperate shove sends the bundle skimming down along the wall, into the shadow between the buildings, onto the soft coal-dust slope where the chute discharges, where a girl who knows the alleys has been standing in the dark for the last twenty minutes precisely because a boy who plans badly and maps well once told her that if everything went wrong, the window was the second exit.

The bundle hit the coal dust with less sound than a dropped book.

Then a constable had my arm, and another had the other arm, and the sheets were gone, and I confess—I record it honestly—that my first feeling was not triumph. It was the sick, cold plunge of the gambler after the card leaves his hand. I had thrown the only thing that mattered out a window on a calculation involving coal dust and a girl's nerve, and I would not know, perhaps ever, whether it had landed safe or scattered or smudged beyond reading.

"She'll never make the station," Hale was saying behind me, already recovered, already procedural. "Bring the lamp. Check the alley."

"Don't bother," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Check the coal dust instead. You'll find footprints going the other way. She left before you knocked—she's been gone an hour. What came through that window was scrap. Proof sheets. You're welcome to them."

He didn't believe me. Of course he didn't; he was Hale. But belief was not the point. Time was the point, and sending four of his six men down into the alleys on a false trail bought minutes the way Ilse's slow printing had bought hours, and minutes, in the economy we had been living in all winter, were currency enough.

They found the bundle, of course. I heard the shout from the alley, heard Hale's boots cross the floor, heard him crouch. And then I heard the silence that followed, which lasted three seconds and contained, I would swear on any map ever drawn, the sound of a man counting.

Thirty-one copies. He knew the number because I had let him count, because a man who counts believes he has the whole of a thing, and a man who believes he has the whole of a thing stops looking for its parts. Thirty-one copies in the coal dust—but the satchel had held more than thirty-one. Ilse had printed forty on the last run. Nine had gone out the previous evening, folded inside a flour delivery, riding in Mrs. Ostrova's nephew's cart toward the river and a Swedish freighter whose captain owed a debt I will not describe here. Hale was holding thirty-one truths and missing nine, and nine was the number that would kill him, though he didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for months, would learn it the way he taught everyone else to learn things: slowly, and in public.

"They're smudged," one of his men said from the alley. "Edge of the stack, mostly. The middle ones—"

"The middle ones are fine," Hale said quietly. He rose. When he turned to me, his face had gone very smooth, the way water goes smooth over something deep. "Burn it," he said—not to me, to the room. "Lamp oil in the stairwell, the varnish store, under the press. Ten minutes' start and we're away before anyone raises the cry."

"Sir," one of the constables holding me began, "the prisoner—"

"—goes in the carriage with me," Hale said. "He's seen nothing worth reporting and will see nothing worth burning for." Then, to me, in a lower voice, almost gentle, which was the cruelest register he owned: "You understand I have to do this. Not for the ministry. For the arithmetic. A press that exists can print again. A press that is ash is only a story, and stories—" he almost smiled, "—stories need witnesses."

* * *

They put the fire in efficiently, the way they had done everything else: oil poured low, along the baseboards, under the great body of the press where the old varnish and sizing had soaked into the floorboards over forty years, so that the wood itself was fuel and had been waiting all this time to burn. The man who struck the match did it without looking at me, and I have wondered since whether that was shame or simple professionalism, and I have concluded that with such men the two are indistinguishable.

By the time they marched me down the stairs the stairwell was smoking. By the time they put me in the carriage the first window on the upper floor had gone orange. Hale sat opposite me, gloved hands on his knees, watching the workshop window the way other men watch a grave close.

I pressed my face to the glass. Not to watch the fire—I had stopped caring about the fire the moment the sheets left my hands—but because from the carriage, at that angle, for perhaps ten seconds, I could see down into the alley mouth.

There was nothing to see. Coal dust, gray walls, the chute's black slot. No girl, no satchel, no footprints leading anywhere. Which meant either that she was blocks away by then, running flat out with the atlas against her ribs—

—or that she had never picked it up.

I did the arithmetic all the way to the ministry cells, and it came out differently every time, the sums refusing to hold still, hope and horror trading places like men changing seats on a moving train. Thirty-one sheets on soft coal dust: they would smudge at the edges, survive in the middle. If she had them, they were already in motion. If she hadn't—if she'd been driven off, caught, if the man Hale had posted at the chute—

The carriage turned a corner and the fire went out of sight, and I realized I was pressing my palm flat against the cold glass as if I could hold the workshop up through it, which is the sort of thing a man does when he has just learned the difference between sacrifice and heroism. Heroism, I think, feels like something. Sacrifice feels like arithmetic that comes out too high—you paid more than the thing was worth, and there's no one to complain to.

Behind us, smoke stood up over the rooftops in a straight column, and I caught myself doing the apprentice's reflex, the thing three years of Aurelius Vane had burned into my hands deeper than any craft: I measured it. Wind from the northwest, moderate. Column leaning east. The chandler's storehouse downwind, the parish records office beyond it, Mrs. Ostrova's windows—empty, thank God, evacuated or bribed into absence, either way safe. Even burning, the workshop was making one last map of itself in the air, and I read it automatically, helplessly, the way a musician flinches at a wrong note.

Then the column bent, and thickened, and I saw the second smoke rise—not from the workshop but from behind it, from the direction of the chandler's yard, and I understood with a lurch that the fire was not going to stay where Hale had put it, that fires never do, that the man who erased a city because erasure was mercy had just set a real fire in a real street with real houses sleeping on both sides—

—and in the same instant, in the smoke, at the far end of the alley, briefly, unmistakably, a figure with a satchel strap across her chest stepped out into the lightening morning, looked back once at the burning workshop, and ran.

She had them. She had all of them.

I sat back in the seat. Hale was watching the flames and did not see. And I did the only thing left to a mapmaker with empty hands: I began, silently, methodically, to memorize everything—his face, the route the carriage took, the number of steps down into the ministry yard, the names the guards called each other—mapping my own captivity as fast as it closed around me, because a map is only finished when someone else reads it, and I intended, whatever else they took, to remain readable.

The carriage door opened. Cold air, stone steps, the smell of official ink.

"Name?" said the clerk at the desk, not looking up.

"Montrose," I said. "Dominic Montrose. Apprentice to Aurelius Vane."

The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere north of the city, a train was pulling out with forty copies of a city on board, and the roof of my whole life was falling in behind me, and I have never in my life been so certain that the map was finished.
