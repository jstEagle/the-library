## 39. A Clock for the Typewriter

The clock on my desk was a bad clock. It lost four minutes a week, and I had never once corrected it.

I had bought it in a pawnshop in Basel in the winter of 1919, from a man who wanted to talk about the war and would not be deterred by monosyllables. It was brass, dented at one corner, and it had belonged, according to the pawnbroker, to a railway stationmaster who had died of influenza. I liked that provenance. A clock that had kept time for a man whose whole profession was keeping time, and who had still not managed to keep himself. Everything we own outlasts the use we put it to. That seemed to me the entire lesson of the century so far.

I set it beside the typewriter and let it run wrong.

The typewriter itself was worse than the clock—a Remington, secondhand, with an *e* that struck faintly and an *r* that doubled if I hurried. I did not hurry anymore. Hurrying was for people who believed someone might take the work away before it was finished. I had learned otherwise. Work that matters is taken away anyway; you simply finish it first when you can.

It was March again. It was always March in my mind, though outside the window Geneva was doing its indifferent spring—buds on the plane trees along the quay, lake water the color of hammered tin, tourists already photographing the jet. Somewhere behind me the radiator knocked its arrhythmic knock, and somewhere below, Madame Feller's dog dreamed aloud in its sleep.

On the platen: a sheet of cheap paper, half filled.

*In the autumn of 1911, in a city that has asked to remain unnamed in this account, a mapmaker's apprentice named Dominic Montrose sat down at a press that smelled of linseed and cold iron, and began to print the streets of Karsthal.*

I read the sentence four times. Then I rolled the platen back and struck it out.

Not because it was untrue. Because it was mine. The history I was typing—the true one, the one with flour sacks and coal chutes and a dying man correcting plates with a hand too weak to hold the burin—was supposed to be impersonal. Ada Kessler had insisted on that. *They will call it a woman's grievance,* she said, *if you let them see it grieves you.* And she was right, she was always right about how men in ministries read, but I was fifty-one years old now and tired of writing like a witness statement. The atlas itself had gone out into the world wearing no one's name. Vane had printed it anonymously. The testimony ledger bore only initials. Even Kessler, in the end, had allowed herself to appear as A.K., a survivor of the Northern Quarter, age given, address withheld.

Everyone had vanished from their own document. That was the method of the thing: make the city visible by making ourselves invisible. It had worked. The book could not be killed because it belonged to no one to kill.

But a method is not a life, and this account—I had finally admitted it to myself that winter—this account was going to have names in it. All of them. Starting with mine.

*My name is Ilse Reiner. In 1898 my brother Emil went into the city of Karsthal to buy a violin string, and the city swallowed him, and then the country pretended the city had never been there at all.*

There. Let the ministers chew on that.

* * *

I worked until the light failed, which in March in Geneva meant working past six without noticing, and then I lit the lamp and made tea I forgot to drink, and the clock ticked its four-minutes-short tick, and the pages accumulated. Eleven years of accumulation now, since Basel, since the crate of atlases had come off the boat with the customs stamp that said PRINTED MATTER — EDUCATIONAL and the clerk had waved it through without looking, because who looks twice at maps? Maps are furniture. Maps are schoolroom wallpaper. No one believes a map is a confession until it is too late to unprint it.

By then the book had done what books do slowly and then all at once. First the foreign correspondents, as planned—a dozen copies to a dozen men in a dozen capitals, each told the same thing: print nothing yet, but keep it where your government can't burn it. Then the excerpts, quiet ones, in papers that could afford to be brave in other people's countries. Then, two years after the printing, a deputy minister in our homeland resigned citing health, and three weeks after that a parliamentary question was asked aloud, with the word *Karsthal* in it, spoken in a chamber where the word had been legally unspoken for fifteen years. Kessler sent me the clipping. She had underlined nothing. She didn't need to.

Hale's career had unraveled the way wet rope unravels—slowly, fiber by fiber, no single breaking moment anyone could point to. He had been moved sideways, then downward, then out. Last I heard he kept a private archive, cataloguing something, in a provincial town. Men like Hale do not retire; they are decommissioned, like guns with a flaw in the casting. I thought about him sometimes the way you think about weather that ruined a harvest years ago—not with hatred anymore, just with a kind of meteorological attention. He had believed he was merciful. That was the part I could not type. I had tried, four times over the years, to write his chapter, and four times the page stayed blank, because I could not find a sentence that contained both *what he did* and *why he slept at night*, and any sentence containing only one of those things was a lie.

Vane's chapter I had managed. Vane took nine drafts. Cowardice, I wrote at last, is not the opposite of courage; it is courage that arrived late and had to work twice as hard to make up the distance. Aurelius Vane watched a massacre through the window of a hired room, mapped it afterward from survivors' testimony, hid the mapping inside a commissioned atlas, faked his death to protect it, and spent his last months dying honestly while his apprentice learned that the master had been a coward and then, belatedly, in ink, not blood, a brave one. I received silence when I asked him—no. I never asked. I received his last correction instead, on plate forty-one, a street I had drawn straight that was in fact curved, and under the correction, in his unmistakable hand, one word: *forgive*. Not a request. An instruction, like everything else he ever left behind.

And Dominic's chapter—

Dominic's chapter was the reason the manuscript was eleven years long instead of four.

* * *

Because you cannot write a man's chapter when you don't know how his story ends.

I knew how it ended publicly. Everyone who cared to know did. Dominic Montrose, arrested at the burning of the workshop in March 1912—he had waited, the witnesses said, until the last sheets came out through the third-floor window into Mrs. Ostrova's waiting hands, and only then turned to face the men with the lanterns, as though he'd simply been keeping an appointment. Tried that summer for sedition and theft of state papers. Sentenced to twelve years. Refused to speak at trial—not once, not to save himself, not even to correct the prosecutor when the prosecutor mispronounced Karsthal. He said nothing for four hours of proceedings. The newspapers called him *the silent cartographer*, which he would have hated, because he talked constantly, endlessly, about projections and paper stock and whether the ink was drying properly, and silence was the one art he'd had to learn late and learn hard.

That was 1912. Then the war ate everything, including the news, including the archives, including—for a few terrible years—my certainty that there was anything left to tell.

He wrote to me from prison. Of course he did; he was Dominic; he wrote to everyone. But the letters were censored, and censored letters from Dominic were a special genre of torment: pages about prison bread, about a guard who liked chess, about the quality of the light in the exercise yard at different times of year—he couldn't help himself, he mapped everything, he'd have drawn contour lines on his own cell wall if they'd given him chalk—and never, not once in eight years, anything about the case, the atlas, me, or what he'd seen from that window while the roof came down. He protected the route. He protected the people. Ilse escaped with the plates; no name attached; nothing to find. He built his own erasure, deliberately, the way Vane had built his, and I understood it and forgave it and resented it, all three at once, for years.

Then the amnesty came. November, 1918—the same week the war ended, as though the century had decided to clear its ledgers all at once. Political prisoners, general release. I read the wire in a newspaper office in Lausanne where I'd gone to argue with an editor about a proof sheet, and I stood in that office among the shouting typesetters and understood that I did not know what to feel, because hope is a muscle and mine had atrophied.

I wired every prison in the district. I wrote to the Ministry. I wrote to the Red Cross, which was overwhelmed and kind and useless.

No record. Transferred during the war—prisons emptied into camps, camps emptied into columns, columns walked east, or west, or nowhere. The paperwork, such as it was, ended in February 1917 with a transfer order and a signature I could not read.

For seven years I have typed around that absence. Every draft, the same hole where his ending should be. Kessler told me once, in her flat way, pouring tea she never drank either: *You're waiting for permission.* I told her she was wrong. She looked at me the way she looked at inaccurate elevations and said nothing, which was worse.

So the manuscript sat with its hole in it, and the clock ran four minutes slow, and I typed the parts I could bear, and did not type the part I couldn't.

* * *

The parcel came on a Thursday, in the last grey hour of the afternoon, when the lamp was already lit and the radiator had given up for the season.

Madame Feller brought it up herself, which she never did—she left parcels on the landing like offerings and rang twice and was gone. But this one she carried in both hands, and she stood in my doorway holding it out as though it might go off.

"Customs," she said. "There was a man asking questions downstairs. Customs, he said." Her eyes, small and quick under her widow's cap, went past me to the typewriter, the clock, the stacked pages. "I told him you are a widow who writes recipes."

"I'm not a widow."

"You are whatever keeps the man from coming upstairs." She set the parcel on my table with exaggerated care, as if it were made of eggshell. "Sign nothing," she added, and left, and I heard her lock her own door across the hall, which she also never did.

The parcel was brown paper, tied with twine, addressed in pencil. No return address. The handwriting made the room tilt.

I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall after twenty years. The forward slant. The way the *g* dropped below the line like something diving for cover. The crossbars on the *t* slightly too long, always reaching for the next letter. Eight years of censored letters had dulled it into something careful and cramped, but this was not censored. This was the old hand, the workshop hand, the hand from the margins of plate forty-one, from the flour-sack manifests, from the note he'd pinned to my door the morning of the raid saying *coal chute, don't argue, count the plates.*

I sat down. My knees informed me they had opinions. I sat anyway.

I cut the twine with the letter knife Vane had given me—it had survived three searches and two border crossings and was the only object I owned older than my grief—and unfolded the brown paper, and inside was:

A notebook. Cheap, prison-issue, the cover soft with handling, swollen with damp and dried again. And beneath it a second notebook. And a third. Five in all, bound together with a strip of the same brown paper, on which was written, in that hand:

*Ilse — These are yours. They were always going to be yours; I just hadn't finished them. Forgive the delay. You'll notice I've gotten faster since you last saw my handwriting, and worse at spelling, and neither of these facts should surprise you.
Don't look for me. Finish the book.
— D.M.*

I did not open them immediately. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the true version: for a full minute—by the bad clock, which means perhaps fifty-six seconds—I held the notebooks in my lap and did nothing at all, because the clock ticked and the radiator dripped and somewhere below Madame Feller's dog had woken and was staring at the ceiling as dogs do when the building's weather changes, and I understood that whatever was in these pages, my life divided at it, the way a map divides at a border, into the territory before and the territory after.

Then I opened the first notebook.

The first page was dated February 1917. The month of the transfer. The handwriting was different—looser, larger, the letters of a man writing in bad light or worse conditions—and the first sentence was:

*They transferred us at night, which is how you know the destination isn't in any report. Nobody documents what happens at night. I learned that from Vane. The state does its real cartography after dark.*

I turned pages. The notebooks were not a diary, exactly. They were—of course they were—field notes. He had mapped his captivity. Population counts of each camp, drawn to scale. Walking distances between barracks, in paces, converted to meters in the margins. Water sources. The bearing of the latrines from the guard tower, noted with a professional's disapproval of the sight lines. Names—he collected names the way Vane had once collected survivors' testimonies, hundreds of them, men who had stepped onto the trains beside him and stepped off nowhere, each one dated, each one with a last known position, because someone had to be the map of this too, apparently, because he was incapable of being anywhere without turning it into a survey.

And threaded through the surveys, in the gaps between the data, in the margins where Vane used to hide his confessions:

*Day 40. The man in the next bunk is named Tomas Beck and he had a daughter and he showed me a drawing she made of their house, and I have committed the house to memory: two windows facing south, a crooked gate. If I get out I am drawing that house. If I don't, someone else will have to, and I'm leaving this note so that someone knows it existed.*

*Day 112. Realized today I have been here longer than I knew Karsthal. The city took me three weeks to map. This place refuses to be mapped, which I believe is deliberate.*

*Day 190. Ilse: I know you'll read these someday, because you read everything, it's your worst quality. I didn't stay at the press out of courage. I stayed because the last sheets needed ninety more seconds and I have never once in my life left ink to dry unattended. Courage was available. Habit is what answered the door.*

*Day 203. Heard today that Hale signed the transfer orders personally. He knows I'm alive. He wants me somewhere the book can't reach. He still doesn't understand the book. Nobody who burns a workshop understands the book.*

*Day 340. Escaped. Details later, if there is a later. Going north. If this notebook leaves my possession, send it to Ilse Reiner, poste restante, Basel. She'll know what to do. She always knows what to do. Tell her the plates arrived safely—I saw the reprint in a café in—*

The entry stopped mid-word. The next entry was dated five days later, in a different pencil, harder, and the handwriting had changed again—shakier, smaller, a man writing cold.

I turned to the last notebook. The last dated entry was November 1918. The week of the amnesty.

*Free papers at last. Walked out the gate at noon with fourteen other men and none of us looked back, which the commandant must have found insulting. I am going home. I mean the city. I mean the workshop corner. I need to see what grew there.
Then I am finding Ilse. I have an apology eleven years overdue and a correction for her final chapter, which I am sure is brilliant and definitely wrong about at least one street.*

Below that, undated, in the old confident hand, as though he had written it yesterday:

*I'll know where to find her. She'll have a typewriter, a clock that runs slow, and tea she forgets to drink. Some instruments can't be disguised.*

I looked up. At the typewriter. At the clock, ticking its dishonest tick. At the untouched tea, stone cold, exactly where I'd left it that morning.

Outside, far down toward the lake, a church bell began striking seven, and counted it correctly, and for once I envied it.

He was alive. Seven years of holes in the manuscript, seven years of typing around an absence, and the absence had been walking toward me the whole time—or standing still, somewhere north, waiting for the roads to open, the way I had waited, the way the city of Karsthal had waited under its erasure, patient as ink, certain as a coordinate, knowing that anything truly drawn cannot stay hidden forever.

I put on my coat. I put the notebooks in my bag, wrapped in the brown paper. I blew out the lamp, then relit it—one habit I permitted myself, checking the flame before trusting the dark—and I locked the door on eleven years of unfinished pages, on the hole that wasn't a hole anymore, on the clock still ticking its four minutes short.

Let it run wrong. I'd correct it when I got back.

Downstairs, Madame Feller's door was locked and the hallway smelled of boiled cabbage and vigilance. On the landing, chalked small at knee height on the doorframe where no honest eye would look—where a landlady's eye would look, or a customs man's—I saw, freshly drawn, a single vertical mark.

One line. A tally. A count begun.

Someone knew the parcel had come.
