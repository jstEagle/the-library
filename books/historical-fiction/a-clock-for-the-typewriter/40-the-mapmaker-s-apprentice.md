## 40. The Mapmaker's Apprentice

**Dominic**

They opened the gates on a Tuesday, which struck me as the sort of bureaucratic joke Vane would have appreciated. Not a Sunday, when a man might be forgiven for walking out of prison like a pilgrim. Not even a Friday, with its suggestion of mercy before rest. A Tuesday. The day of errands.

The amnesty came down in March, part of the new government's first hundred days—everything they'd promised and half of what we'd hoped for, signed in ink that smelled, to me at least, faintly of the workshop. I had been inside four years. Long enough that the light through the gate looked manufactured, too clean, too yellow, like the gilt on a cheap frame. I stood in it for a long moment while a clerk behind me shuffled my release papers and said, "Montrose? You're free to go," in the tone of a man telling me the soup was getting cold.

Free to go. As if I hadn't already gone somewhere else entirely, every night, for four years.

I walked. I could have taken the tram but I wanted the streets to count themselves out under my feet, the way they do when you've memorized them from above. That was the strange inheritance of those years: I knew this city now the way I had known Karsthal, from the atlas, from contour lines and street widths and the cipher Vane had folded into his elevations. I had spent a decade learning a city that never existed and then four years learning one that did, and the two had begun, in my head, to overlap. There were corners where I expected buildings that weren't there. There were corners where I expected people who weren't there either, and those were worse.

The workshop district had changed less than I feared and more than I hoped. The bakery on the corner was still a bakery. The coal merchant's yard was still a yard, though the chute was bricked over—I looked at it longer than I meant to, thinking of Ilse going down into the dark with the plates wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, thinking of how I had told her not to look back, and how she hadn't, and how that had felt like both the best and worst thing anyone had ever done for me.

Then the lane itself. And there it was.

Or rather: there it wasn't.

The workshop was gone. Not burned—burned would have left a scar, a black tooth in the row, some honest ruin. It had been *removed*. Cleared, graded, and built over with something new and pale and correct, a municipal building or an insurance office, the kind of architecture that exists specifically so no one will ever look at it. In front of it, where our door had been, where the chalk marks had multiplied like a slow contagion, where Hale had stood in his grey coat and offered me wealth and silence with the courtesy of a man discussing weather—

There was a plaque.

I stopped. The morning traffic went around me. A woman with a pram gave me the wide berth people give men who have just come out of prisons, and I suppose I must have looked like exactly that, because I was.

The plaque was bronze, maybe two feet high, mounted on a stone post. It had been put up by the Historical Commission—the new one, the one with Ilse's testimony appended to its founding documents, the one whose report I had read in fragments passed hand to hand in the exercise yard until a warder confiscated it and then, remarkably, read it himself and gave it back.

The plaque showed a map.

Karsthal. The northern quarter, the river bend, the market square where Ada Kessler had stood at fourteen years old and watched soldiers move through her city with the efficiency of men following instructions. The name was there. Cut into bronze, official, load-bearing: **KARSTHAL — REMEMBERED**. Below it, a dedication in the small self-important typeface of committees:

*In memory of the quarter destroyed in 1898, and of those who kept its memory alive against all instruction.*

I read it three times. I put my hand flat on the bronze and felt the cold of it, the sun barely warming the surface, and I thought: Vane, you old devil, you are in this metal somewhere. Your handwriting is not here, your error in the eastern rooftops is not here, but you are in it, the way a river is in the sea—not visibly, but everywhere.

And then I saw the map itself properly, and the apprentice in me—the part Vane had trained off the streets, the boy he'd caught trying to steal his coat and instead of turning him in had handed him a pen and said, *if you're going to take things, learn to make them*—that apprentice woke up and looked, and winced.

The plaque was wrong.

Not wrong in spirit. Wrong in fact. The river bend was drawn too shallow—a cartographer working from the pre-purge surveys rather than Vane's corrected ones, or working from memory, or simply copying the commission's own report, which had reproduced the old errors faithfully because reports reproduce whatever came before them. The market square sat a block too far east. And the northern quarter, Kessler's quarter, the streets where the survivors had hidden and where the cipher had finally led us—it was compressed, crowded into the corner of the bronze as if the engraver had run short of space and nerve at the same time.

Anyone else would have seen a memorial. I saw a proof sheet.

I stood there for what might have been five minutes or fifty. Then I reached into my coat pocket, and found what I always found, because four years in a cell teaches a man two things: patience, and never to be without a pencil. They had let me keep it in the last months; the warder who read the report had argued for it, apparently, saying I was harmless, saying I only ever drew. He had shown the governor my sketches of the courtyard from above, the drainage grates, the angle of the wall, and the governor had laughed and said, *let him keep it, he maps the way other men pray.*

I took out the pencil. I licked the tip of my thumb and rubbed a patch of the bronze clean of road dust. And I began, very quietly, in graphite that would wash off with the next hard rain but would last long enough to be noticed, long enough to matter, long enough for someone to ask *who did this*—

I began correcting the map.

**Ilse**

I learned about the amnesty from a newspaper in a language I still read slowly, in a boarding house in a port city whose name changes every time the border moves, and I put the paper down and cried into the dough I was kneading, which is how the landlady knew something important had happened, because she had never once seen me cry over flour.

Dominic was free. Four years, and he was free, and I had known he would be—Ilse, you told yourself that every morning, the way you tell yourself the tide will come back—but knowing and knowing are different animals, and only one of them lets you sleep.

I had built a life the way you build a fire in wet wood: patiently, in small protected pieces, feeding it everything dry you own. The plates had crossed three borders sewn into the lining of a coffin I had legitimately rented for the purpose, which remains the single most practical thing grief ever taught me. The atlas published eighteen months after the workshop burned—*Karsthal: A Complete Atlas of the City and Its Destruction*, with Ada Kessler's testimony bound into the back like a keel, like ballast, like the thing that keeps the whole vessel upright in bad water. It sold modestly at first. Then the excerpts appeared in the papers at home. Then it sold in ways that made governments nervous, which has always been, in my experience, the surest sign a book is printed correctly.

Hale's career unraveled the way a rope does—from the frayed end inward, slowly, and then all at once. The inquiry. The testimony. The signature, reproduced in a dozen newspapers, his own neat archival hand authorizing the 1898 operation, the same hand that had once offered me and Dominic a fortune for silence. I heard he retired to a house in the hills. I heard he kept a library. I heard, once, from a mutual acquaintance of the unpleasant sort, that he had read the atlas cover to cover and annotated it, and that his marginal notes were corrections—cartographic objections, quibbles about projection—and that this was somehow the saddest thing anyone had ever told me.

What I did not hear, for four years, was anything from Dominic. That was by design. His trial had taught me the shape of his courage: he said nothing, not one word, not even the words that would have shortened his own sentence, because every word was a thread and every thread led back along my route, and he chose to sit in a cell and hold all the threads in his fists. So I sent nothing. Letters are threads too. I wrote him anyway, hundreds of letters, and burned them, which Vane would have understood better than anyone—he was, after all, the great composer of letters dated after his death.

Now he was free. Now the tide had come back. And I stood in a kitchen smelling of yeast and salt air, four borders away, and understood that the question was no longer whether I would go home, but how quickly, and whether I would recognize the city, and whether he—

Whether he would forgive himself. Because that was the work remaining. Not the atlas; the atlas was done, was loose in the world, was multiplying in hands we would never see. The remaining work was the private cartography, the interior survey, the mapping of what each of us had lost and carried. Dominic had stayed behind so the pages could escape. I had escaped so the pages could live. Neither of us had ever said any of this aloud. We were, both of us, trained by the same dead man in the same discipline: say nothing, mark everything.

I packed that afternoon. One bag. The first edition of the atlas, inscribed to no one, because inscriptions can be traced. And in the bottom, wrapped in a stocking, the thing I had carried since the hospice, since Vane's deathbed, since his last correction and his request for forgiveness that neither of us granted and neither of us refused: his drafting pen. The good one. The nib worn to the exact shape of his hand.

You don't bury a tool like that, Mrs. Ostrova had told me once, in her kitchen, in the last week, when she was deciding what side of herself to feed. You pass it forward. That's the whole religion, girl. Everything else is furniture.

I bought a ticket for the morning boat.

* * *

**Dominic**

By the second hour I had attracted an audience, which was inevitable and also, I decided, fine. Let them watch. The days of chalk marks and held breath were over; if a man cannot correct a memorial in broad daylight, then the memorial is a lie and the daylight is too.

A shopgirl brought me coffee without being asked. An old man stood at my shoulder for twenty minutes, breathing through his nose, before he said, "The square's wrong, isn't it. My mother used to say it sat closer to the water."

"Half a block," I said. "Maybe more. The surveys disagree."

"She'd have liked you," he said, and went on standing there, and I understood that he had lost someone in Karsthal, or been someone from Karsthal, or simply belonged to the vast quiet congregation of people whom erasure had touched without ever being allowed to admit it. The atlas had given them permission. That was the thing I had finally understood in the cell, in year three, staring at my courtyard sketch: a map is never information. A map is permission. Permission to believe a place existed. Permission to grieve it. Permission to stand in front of a bronze plaque with a pencil stub and refuse, politely and permanently, to let the record stay wrong.

The trouble with correcting in graphite, of course, is that rain comes. Rain always comes; Vane had taught me that too, in his roundabout way, the winter he made me re-ink a whole coastline because I'd used the cheap fixative. *Everything burns eventually,* he said, watching me work. *Everything fades. So you don't make things to last, boy. You make things worth redoing.*

Worth redoing.

I straightened up. My knees cracked like kindling—four years of stone floors. I looked at the graphite lines sitting on top of the bronze, the true river bend, the true position of the square, the northern quarter expanded to its honest width, and I thought: this needs ink. This needs proper ink, and proper tools, and a plate, and time. This needs to be cut into the metal itself, or cast anew, or—if the commission could be persuaded, and commissions these days could be persuaded of almost anything with the right testimony attached—engraved correctly, permanently, with the source credited. With *our* source credited. With a line at the bottom reading something like *after the survey of A. Vane*, which would have made the old man furious with embarrassment and secretly pleased for a decade.

That was when I heard footsteps behind me, unhurried, with a rhythm I knew the way you know a heartbeat—not your own, but one you've slept beside the sound of for years.

I didn't turn around. I finished the stroke I was making, extending the true line of the northern quarter out past the edge of the bronze, onto the stone, onto the pavement, out into the living city where Karsthal's ghost actually stood.

"You've got the river wrong," said Ilse, behind me. "It bends sooner than that. I've got his pen in my bag, and his correction ledger, and I've come four borders to tell you that you've got the river wrong."

I smiled at the bronze. At the graphite. At the pale new building standing on the grave of everything, wearing its face of permanent correctness.

"No," I said. "I've got it right where it starts. Help me finish the rest."
