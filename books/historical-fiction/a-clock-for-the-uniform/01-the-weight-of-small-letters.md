## 1. The Weight of Small Letters

The punch was three millimeters wide and it was going to ruin her, or she was going to ruin it, and either way the lamp would burn out before the question resolved itself.

Marguerite Blackwood bent closer to the flame. The graver sat in her right hand the way a pen sits in other people's — lightly, obediently. In her left, clamped in the little brass vise her father had called *le patient* because you could not rush it and it would not forgive you, the steel blank waited for its letter. A lowercase *g*, double-story, for a job the notary wanted by Thursday: fifty deeds of sale, run off quick, nothing elegant.

Nothing elegant. As if elegance were a thing you could decline to do when your hands knew no other way.

She set the tip against the steel and cut away from herself, one clean stroke along the bowl's inner curve. The shaving curled up like a blond eyelash and dropped onto the bench among its brothers. She blew them aside without looking. Her eyes stayed on the letter.

Outside, the wind came down off the Vosges and rattled the shutters of the print shop the way it always did in November, but tonight there were voices under the wind. Two men crossing the square below her window, their words torn to shreds by the gusts, though enough arrived intact: *the roads, the trains stopped at Épinal, they say the whole line is moving.* The town had been whispering for a week now. Whispering is what Sainte-Colombe did instead of praying. It whispered about the harvest, about the widow Roussel's lodger, about the war that had been going on three years and had so far taken only the sons of families nobody liked anyway.

Marguerite cut another stroke. The counter of the *g* began to open like a small dark eye.

Her father had taught her that every letter was a room. You carved the walls; the light did the rest. "You are not making ink," he'd said, the first day he let her hold a graver, eleven years old and all knuckles. "You are making the space where meaning can live. Cut the space wrong and the word dies in the press." He'd said it in French with his English accent wrapped around it, and the boys who passed the shop window had heard *l'Anglais* talking again, the foreigner with the fancy tools, and one of them — Henri Aubry, the baker's son, twelve then and already loud — had cupped his hands and called through the glass: *Hey, English girl! Does the ink wash off, or does it stay on you like the stain?*

The stain. That was the word the town used. Not traitor, not spy — those were too large, too interesting. Just *stain*. The Blackwoods had been in Sainte-Colombe nineteen years by then, long enough that most people had forgotten why they disliked them and kept disliking them anyway, the way you keep a fence up after the bull is dead. An Englishman in a border town in the years before the war was a walking suspicion. That he'd married a Frenchwoman, that she'd died giving him a daughter, that he'd raised the daughter alone over a print shop and never once attended mass — none of it helped. That he made the finest letters between here and Nancy helped least of all. Excellence, Marguerite had learned early, was just another form of not belonging. It gave people something to resent precisely.

* * *

He had died in March, two years ago now, and even his death had managed to be inconvenient.

It was the foundry furnace. The shop still cast some of its own type — small runs, special sorts — and the furnace stood in the back room like a squat iron god that demanded tending. He'd gone out on an ice night to check the flue because a cold snap had cracked chimneys all over town and he trusted no one else's masonry, least of all the landlord's. Marguerite had woken at four to an empty bed and a shop that smelled wrong — hot metal and something under it, sweetish, like burnt sugar — and found him in the back room with the flue door open and carbon monoxide doing its quiet work. He was kneeling, she remembered that. Kneeling as if he'd finally decided to pray, after a lifetime of refusing.

The town had come to the funeral. That was the strange part. They filled the little church, the same people who hadn't spoken to him in years, and Madame Roussel had wept genuinely, and afterward the mayor shook Marguerite's hand and said, "Your father was an artist, mademoiselle. The town honors him." And she had stood there in her black coat thinking: *Where were you when the schoolmaster wouldn't take his advertisement? Where were you when the grain merchant shorted him on paper stock for six years running?*

But she'd said thank you. Because the shop was hers now, or rather the debt was hers, with the shop attached to it, and the town's tolerance — cold, thin, seasonal, like light in February — was the only climate in which the business could survive. They tolerated her the way you tolerate a tool that works. The notary brought his deeds. The *curé* brought his parish bulletins. The wine cooperative brought its price lists. Nobody invited her anywhere. Nobody asked how she lived alone above a shop with the war eating the country alive. When soldiers' trains passed through and young men in uniform looked in the window, the townspeople pointed her out sometimes, she was sure of it — *there's the printer girl, careful with her, odd blood* — and the trains went on carrying sons away, and the town went on whispering.

So she belonged to the work. There was no competition. The work asked nothing about her name.

* * *

By midnight the *g* was nearly done. She held it up to the lamp with the tweezers and turned it, and the light ran along the freshly cut walls and pooled in the counters, and it was good. It was very good, if she said so herself, which she never did aloud, because there was no one to say it to. That was the arithmetic of her life now: excellent letters, divided by silence, equaled this — a woman of twenty-six talking to steel.

She should sleep. Thursday's job needed proofs by morning, and the army requisition notice pinned to the post office door — *all able-bodied men born 1897–1899 will report* — meant her delivery boy was gone and she'd be hauling paper down the stairs herself.

Able-bodied men born 1897–1899. She'd read it twice on her way back from the chandler's. The baker's son Lucien Aubry was born in '98. Henri's boy. Henri, whose voice had cracked through the window glass at her father all those years ago, and who had grown into a man who still crossed the street when he saw her coming, and whose son was now exactly the age the republic wanted for its machinery.

She thought of Lucien the way you think of weather — distantly, as someone else's condition. Then she thought of the ledger.

Every town kept one: the civic register in the mayor's office, every birth, every name, every household in Sainte-Colombe pressed into official type by the government printer in Épinal. When the lists came, the lists came *from* the ledger. Names in, names out. Type at the bottom of everything, the way her father used to say. *Everything, ma petite, is printing underneath. Wars, marriages, debts. Someone cuts the letters first.*

Someone cuts the letters first.

She snuffed the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the wind work the shutters, to the last voices dying in the square. Somewhere east of here the armies were doing whatever armies did before winter closed the roads, and the whispering in the town had changed key lately — less gossip, more fear, the kind of fear that keeps people's lamps burning late.

Marguerite climbed the stairs with the finished punch still in her pocket, its small weight against her hip like a coin she hadn't decided to spend.

In the morning, she told herself, proofs. Paper. The ordinary machinery of staying alive.

She did not yet know that by the end of the week the machinery would belong to someone else — that boots would come into her square at first light, and a captain with tired eyes would put his hand flat on her father's bench, and the ledger, and her life, would be requisitioned together.
