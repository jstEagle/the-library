## 2. Requisition at First Light

They came with the frost, before the baker even lit his ovens.

Marguerite heard them first as a sound she could not name — not hoofbeats, not wheels, but a low collective grinding, the sound of many boots on frozen mud — and she lay in her narrow bed above the shop listening to it roll through the square like something tide-like and inevitable. Then a voice barked an order in French clipped too clean for the town, and the grinding stopped all at once, the way a clock stops.

She dressed in the dark. This was habit. Four winters of opening the shop alone had taught her that the first hour belonged to her father's tools and no one else's, and she was downstairs with her lamp lit and her apron tied before the sky had gone from black to iron.

Through the front window she watched soldiers unfold across the square like ink dropped in water. Grey coats, grey breath. They moved without hurry, which frightened her more than speed would have. A lieutenant was already on the mairie steps, unrolling a paper as though he had rehearsed it in some other town, on some other morning, and would rehearse it again tomorrow. Two men went to the well. Two more to the church doors. One walked the perimeter of the square kicking at the ice in the gutters, unhurried, thorough, a man taking inventory of a place that now belonged to him.

The requisition notice went up on the mairie door with four nails and a hammer blow that cracked across the square. Marguerite did not need to read it. She had cut the type for the last one, three years ago, when the war was young and everyone still believed in notices.

*Requisitioned for the use of the Army. All property within the marked perimeter passes under military authority. Resistance will be treated as collaboration with the enemy.*

Her father had cut type for notices like that his whole life, she thought. Notices about taxes, about floods, about which days the market would move to accommodate a visit from some prefect nobody remembered. The town read his letters every week of its life and never once said his name aloud except to complain about a smudged apostrophe. Blackwood the printer. Englishman. The name they used like a stain on the square.

She banked the lamp down to a bead of flame and began work anyway, because the alternative was standing at the window being afraid, and her father had taught her that fear was just another material you learned to hold properly or it cut you.

* * *

The knock came at seven, and it was not a knock. It was the flat of a rifle against the door frame, twice, in the rhythm of someone who had never in his life waited to be admitted anywhere.

She opened the door herself. It seemed important to do that — to be the one standing in the doorway rather than found hiding behind it.

There were two soldiers, and then a third man who was neither quite soldier nor quite anything she had language for. He wore a captain's insignia on a coat too good for the mud on it, and he carried his cap in one hand instead of wearing it, which made him look strangely like a caller come to discuss a debt. He was younger than the insignia suggested. There was a fresh scar along his jaw, pink and new, the kind that came from shrapnel and field surgery and very little conversation.

"Madame—" he began, consulting a folded list.

"Mlle. Blackwood."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise exactly. Recalculation. *Blackwood* meant English blood in this town, meant the family that had buried its men here and still been counted half-foreign, meant the shop every child in the square had been warned away from by mothers who couldn't say why. She watched him decide what to do with that information, and she watched him decide wrong.

"The press," he said. "It functions?"

"You may come and see."

"I intend to." He stepped past her into the shop without waiting for invitation, and the two soldiers came after him like a tide following a moon.

She had forgotten, until that moment, how the shop looked to strangers. To her it was simply the world: the bench with its worn hollow where her hip fit, the racks of punches in their oiled paper, the galley where yesterday's work still sat locked in its chase, the smell of lead and ink and lamp oil that she had stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. But she saw it now through the captain's eyes — small, dim, absurdly modest — and she saw his face flatten with disappointment.

"This is all of it? One press?"

"One press. One compositor."

He turned. Looked at her hands, then her face, then back at her hands, and she understood with a cold sliding feeling that she had made an error. She had answered too precisely. She had stood too near her bench without thinking about it, near enough that her fingers had found the nearest punch-cutter and closed around it the way other women held rosaries.

"You set type," he said.

"My father did. He is dead. I finish his contracts."

"Your contracts are finished as of this morning." He took the folded list from his pocket again, and this time he unfolded it fully, and she saw that it was not a list of properties at all. It was a list of presses. Towns. Names struck through in red pencil, one after another, and hers was the only one on the page without a line through it yet. "The army requires printed matter. Rations schedules, movement orders, casualty circulars, proclamations. Every town on this route is printing for us now. Yours will too."

"We print for the town."

"The town is the army now, mademoiselle. That is what the notice says." He said it without relish, and somehow that was worse than relish would have been. He spoke like a man reciting arithmetic. "Where is the civic ledger?"

The question landed in her stomach like a dropped weight.

"The ledger," he repeated, patient, terrible. "Register of residents. Births, deaths, households, occupations. Every commune keeps one. Yours will have been maintained here or at the mairie, and the mairie is occupied, so. Here."

"It isn't—"

"Mademoiselle." He looked around the shop slowly, and she followed his gaze and saw what he saw: the cabinet against the far wall, tall and oak and locked, which every visitor to the shop had always known held the ledger because there was nowhere else it could be. Her lie had lasted four seconds. She felt her ears go hot.

She fetched the key from the drawer where her father had kept it for thirty years and opened the cabinet herself, because she would not stand aside and let soldiers put their gloves on it. The ledger came out heavy as a sleeping animal, bound in green leather gone bald at the corners, and she set it on the press bed because there was nowhere else to set it.

Captain Vasseur — the name was on his collar, stitched small; she read it upside down as he bent over the book — opened it almost gently. His gloved fingers moved down the columns of names, and Marguerite watched him and understood suddenly, completely, what a ledger was. It was the town. Every person she had known since childhood reduced to lines in her father's careful italic hand: *who lived where, who married whom, how many sons.* Her neighbors. The ones who crossed the street to avoid her. The ones whose sons had thrown stones at the shop window the winter her father died, shouting that foreigners started wars.

And now all of it lay open under the hands of an occupying officer.

"These rolls will be cross-referenced for conscription," Vasseur said, to the book more than to her. "Ages sixteen to forty-five. The lists must be printed and posted within the month. Your press will produce them."

"Or?"

He looked up. For a moment neither of them said anything, and in the lamplight his scar caught orange and his eyes were a color she couldn't fix — grey or green, undecided, like the sky over the square. He was not a cruel man. She would have preferred a cruel man. Cruelty you could hate cleanly.

"There is no *or*," he said quietly. "That is what I am trying to explain to you. There is no or. There is only what is ordered and what is done, and the space between them, which is where people like you and I live." He closed the ledger. Set both hands flat on its cover. "You will print for the army, mademoiselle. You will print well, because badly will be noticed. And you will begin today."

"I don't take orders in my father's shop."

"Then take wages." From his coat he drew a paper and laid it beside the ledger: a conscription-of-labor form, blank but for the header, the kind she had seen posted on walls since 1914 binding strangers to strangers' factories. "Sign, and you are a contracted printer under military administration. Refuse, and you are a woman obstructing requisitioned property, and I am obliged to find another arrangement for you. There are arrangements." He let that sit. "I would prefer you signed. Your hands don't shake."

She looked at her hands. Steady. Of course steady — twenty years of cutting steel to tolerances finer than a hair, of holding still while the graver took its pass. She had always thought of steadiness as her inheritance, the one thing the town couldn't refuse her. Now it was the exact quality that made her useful to them.

The soldiers waited by the door. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, boots ground through the frost past the shuttered windows of every house on the square, and behind those windows she imagined her neighbors watching, counting the soldiers, whispering the word *requisition*, and none of them — not one — thinking of the Blackwood girl as anything but scenery attached to the shop.

*Let them find another printer*, said a voice in her, the proud voice, her father's voice. *Let them ruin the matrices. Let them post their lists crooked.*

But there was another voice, quieter, and it sounded like the ledger itself. Because she had seen, in the four seconds Vasseur had given her to look, the column of the Faure household, and beside it the number of sons. And the Léger boys. And old Marchand's grandsons at the mill. Names flagged in the margin now or flaggable within the month, names that would go onto lists printed on her press with her father's type — unless.

Unless.

She picked up the pen. The nib scratched in the quiet like a type-cutter taking steel.

"First name?" Vasseur asked, when she reached it.

"Marguerite."

"You spell it yourself," he said, and turned away to give her that much privacy, which was either courtesy or shame, and she decided she would think about that later, if there was a later.

She wrote her name. It looked strange on an army form, foreign in both senses, and when she finished she realized her hand had not shaken and probably never would, and that this — this signature she had never chosen, this contract written in her own steady letters — was the first thing she had ever signed that anyone official had wanted from her. They had never wanted her vote or her opinion or her grief at her father's funeral. They wanted her hands. Very well. They could have the hands.

Vasseur took the form, checked the signature, and folded it away. At the door he paused, half-turned, and said — not to her, quite, more to the room —

"The ledger stays under key here. My key. Proofs nightly. If a single name in that book prints differently than it reads, mademoiselle, I will know, because I read everything that leaves this shop." A beat. "I read everything."

Then the door shut behind him, and the frost-light came through the window and lay across the press bed where the ledger had been, and Marguerite stood alone in her father's shop, which was now the army's shop, which was still, in whatever way mattered, hers.

They would post guards on the mairie tonight, she knew. Which meant the mairie's duplicate rolls — the loose papers, the working copies with the pencil marks, the marginal flags that told you *which* names mattered — would sit unguarded in the confusion for perhaps one night. Perhaps only hours.

She got her coat.
