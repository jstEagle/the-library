## 3. Names in Cold Type

The ledger came home with her the way stolen things do — too easily.

Captain Vasseur had ordered it carried to the print shop's back room, where an army clerk could collate it against muster rolls, and then, in the chaos of the requisition's second day, he had simply stopped asking about it. It sat on Marguerite's father's workbench under a stack of army proofs, wedged between a crate of ten-point garamond and a tin of ink rags, and nobody guarded it. Nobody needed to. Who would steal a book of names? Names were the cheapest thing in France that winter. The war spent them faster than any press could set them.

She told herself she was only tidying.

The shop at night was a different country from the shop by day. By day it belonged to the occupation: soldiers stacking paper, the clerk Aubert coughing over his forms, boots on her father's floorboards. But after ten o'clock the stove went down to embers and the square outside went quiet except for the sentry's slow circuit, his footsteps arriving on the hour like a clock with a limp. Then the shop was hers again, or as close to hers as anything got now.

Marguerite lit the lamp, turned the wick low — light drew eyes to windows — and pulled the ledger out from under the proofs.

It was not a beautiful book. The civic registry had been rebound twice in her lifetime, its spine held by paste and stubbornness, and the handwriting inside changed character every few pages like weather. Entries going back fifty years. Baptisms, property lines, trades, deaths. Her father's name was in it, entered by some long-dead clerk in a copperplate hand: *Blackwood, Édouard — punchcutter — arrived 1889.* And below that, added later in a cramped, disapproving script, *foreign-born. Naturalized 1894.* As if the second line were a stain the first one couldn't wash out.

Her own name was there now, amended in someone's careful pencil: *Blackwood, Marguerite — printer. Shop retained for Army use.* Retained. A word for horses and buildings. Not even a word for people, and yet here she was, filed between the church and the flour mill.

She turned pages. She hadn't meant to be looking for anything; she was looking for everything, reading her town the way she read type — upside down, backwards, letter by letter until the shapes resolved into sense.

The Fontaine family, six entries, three of them crossed off by fever years ago. Old Marchand the saddler, widowed, trade listed as *infirme* since 1914. The Guérins. The Roussels. The Perrin girl born in 1901 who used to leave bread crusts on the Blackwood step because her mother forbade her to leave good bread.

And then the flags.

They weren't called flags. They were small marks in red grease-pencil, a vertical stroke beside certain names, applied recently, the wax still faintly tacky under her thumb. Beside each stroke, in the clerk's hand, a number. She counted four strokes on the page before she understood what she was counting, and when she did, her hands went cold on the paper the way they never went cold on brass or steel. Brass and steel kept their temperature. Paper didn't. Paper took the warmth of whatever touched it and gave it back.

Conscription classes. The numbers were birth-years. The strokes were men.

*Perrin, Luc — b. 1898.*

Luc Perrin, who was nineteen and delivered flour and had once, when they were both children, stood outside this very shop shouting that his mother said Blackwood ink was foreign dirt and no Christian should touch it.

*Roussel, Étienne — b. 1899.* Étienne, who'd been turned back from Verdun with a shattered knee and walked with a cane, and whose stroke had been drawn anyway, as if the grease-pencil could straighten what shrapnel had bent.

*Guérin, Tomas — b. 1900.* Seventeen. The boy wasn't seventeen yet. He wouldn't be eighteen until spring, and the entry beside his name said so, in his mother's hand, recorded faithfully by a clerk who had apparently believed the date still mattered.

Marguerite sat down on her father's stool and looked at the lamp flame for a while without seeing it.

Here was the thing she had not let herself think in the two days since the requisition: she was printing for them now. Army broadsheets. Movement orders. Ration schedules. And somewhere in the coming weeks — she had seen the requisition papers, she knew how armies thought — the conscription notices for this town would come through this shop, set by her hands, pulled on her press, her father's press, carrying these names and these little red strokes out into the square where the whole town could read them.

The town that had shunned her father for thirty years. That had paid him late and short and whispered *foreigner* at his back and bought his type anyway, because craft is a language even hatred has to speak. They would stand in the square and read those names and know which of their sons were marked, and there was nothing in the world she owed them.

Nothing. Except the type was already in her hands.

That was the discovery, and it arrived not with horror but with a terrible, quiet clarity, like a proof coming off the stone: survival and complicity were printed on the same page. Her survival. The army kept her because her hands were precise, because a punchcutter who could cut a new *e* in forty minutes was worth more fed than dead. Every day she worked well was another day she ate, another day the shop stood, another day she existed inside the machine instead of under it. And every letter she cut well served the machine. There was no setting her hands to neutral. Type didn't have a neutral. Type either carried the word or it didn't exist.

She ran her thumb along the ledger's edge. Fifty years of a town in one binding. Four red strokes on this page alone. How many across the whole book?

She counted. It took half an hour, and she counted twice because the first time she thought her mind had slipped a gear. Thirty-one. Thirty-one strokes in the civic ledger of a town small enough that everyone's name touched everyone else's, drawn in grease-pencil by a clerk working under Captain Vasseur's authority, waiting to become type, become ink, become paper, become orders, become boys marching toward the German guns with French rifles and eight weeks' training.

Thirty-one names, and she was the only person in Saint-Rémy who would hold every one of them in her hands before they were printed.

* * *

The knock came just past midnight, and Marguerite's heart performed a full maneuver — stop, drop, take cover — before she recognized the rhythm. Two knocks, pause, one. Not military. Military knocked like it owned the door.

She opened it a hand's width. Céline Roussel stood in the cold with a covered basket and the expression of a woman delivering more than bread.

"Madame Roussel."

"Printer." The old woman looked past her into the shop with quick black eyes, taking inventory of soldiers' absence, lamplight, the smell of hot metal. "You're alone. Good." She pushed the basket forward. "Étienne's ration loaf. He heard the army took your shop and wanted—" she paused, choosing, "—he wanted you to have it. He says your father ground a fine edge for the town and the town forgot it, and he'd rather not forget it twice."

Marguerite took the basket because refusing it would require a sentence she didn't know how to build. Bread from the Roussels. In thirty years, the closest the town had come to apology was buying her punches at fair price and never saying why.

"Thank Étienne for me," she managed.

Céline didn't leave. She stood on the step with snow gathering on her shawl and studied Marguerite the way Marguerite studied a flawed matrix, looking for the crack that explained the flaw. "They've taken the ledger," she said. Not a question. The whole town had watched the requisition from behind shutters; everyone knew about the ledger.

"It's in the shop."

"And you have access."

"I set their type, madame. I don't read their papers."

"No," Céline agreed softly. "Of course not." She adjusted the basket's cloth where Marguerite's grip had shifted it, a small domestic gesture, absurd in the doorway of a requisitioned print shop at midnight. "My son walks on a bad knee. The army has a habit of deciding bad knees are good enough when the numbers run thin. If ever a name needed misplacing, Printer, it would be a name like his." She stepped back into the snow. "You didn't hear me say so. Nobody heard anybody say anything. That's how we've all survived this long — hearing nothing."

She went. Marguerite stood in the doorway until the cold drove her in, the loaf warm through the cloth against her ribs like something alive, and hated how much warmth there was in it, and hated more that she noticed.

* * *

Back at the bench, she didn't touch the ledger again right away. She cleaned the press instead, the way her father had taught her — work when you can't think, think when your hands are busy enough that thinking can't escape.

He had died at this bench. That was the plain fact under every other fact. October 1915, a winter coming early, and Édouard Blackwood cutting a punch for a commission from a Lyon house that had finally, grudgingly, acknowledged his skill. Heart, the doctor said, as if naming the organ excused the town that had worked it to failure. He'd finished the *k* first. It was still in the drawer, wrapped in chamois, the finest thing he ever cut, and no Lyon house ever received it because Marguerite had sent them nothing and they had never asked twice.

The town had come to the funeral. That was the strange part people wouldn't have predicted. Forty-some faces in the cold, standing awkwardly around a grave they'd helped dig with thirty years of small refusals, and afterward they'd gone back to buying her type and not speaking to her, which was the arrangement the town understood best. Tolerance. Cold tolerance, the kind that keeps a fire at exactly the distance where it neither warms you nor goes out.

Now the army wanted lists. Now the town's sons had red strokes by their names. And the woman at the typecase was the one person in Saint-Rémy both able to read the strokes and able to cut the letters they would become.

She opened the ledger again, to the page with the baker's family. The Perrin entry. Luc Perrin, b. 1898, and beside his father's entry the word *boulanger* in faded brown ink, and above it all the shop's address, which she knew, which everyone knew, because the smell of that bread had been the one honest pleasure of her childhood mornings — smelling it from the street, never being invited in.

Luc had mocked her father at the top of his voice, age eleven, from the safe side of the square, and old Perrin had watched from the bakery door and done nothing, and that nothing had taught Marguerite more about the town than any shout.

His name had a red stroke.

She thought: he'll march. She thought: he deserves it. She thought both things honestly, in sequence, and then she thought the third thing, the one that didn't resolve: *and I'm the one who cuts the letters that send him.*

If she cut the type badly — a slipped serif, a worn counter, a name that printed smudged and unreadable — the clerks would simply reset it. Aubert had a spare font of body type in the army crates. Nothing stayed wrong in print for long; that was the whole faith she'd been raised in. Errors were corrected. That was what type was *for.*

Unless the error was in the source.

She looked at the ledger for a long time. At the grease-pencil strokes, sitting on top of the clerk's careful ink like blood over bone. Ink could be matched. Grease-pencil could be lifted. Dates could be... dates were just entries. Entries were just writing, and writing was just type that hadn't been cut yet, and type was the only country she had ever belonged to.

Somewhere out in the square, the sentry's footsteps marked the hour — one in the morning, arriving with its limp. Two hours until the army proofs began again, until the shop stopped being hers.

Marguerite closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person, and reached for her graver.

The flagged names would need new matrices by week's end. Small ones. Unremarkable ones. A date here, a letter there — errors no inspector would flag because they'd look like nothing more than a tired clerk's tired hand.

She lit a fresh candle. Thirty-one names, she thought, and picked up the brass.
