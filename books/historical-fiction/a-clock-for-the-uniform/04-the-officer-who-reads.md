## 4. The Officer Who Reads

Captain Aurel Vasseur had requisitioned forty-three buildings since the war began, and he could tell within a minute what each would yield. Barns gave up hay and resentment. Châteaux gave up maps, wine cellars, and the particular silence of families counting silver in another room. The print shop on the Place des Tilleuls had given up something he hadn't expected: a woman who held a graver the way his father had held a scalpel.

He stood at the back of the shop that second evening, ostensibly reviewing the army proofs drying on the slanted stone, actually watching her work.

Marguerite Blackwood sat at her father's bench with a lamp pulled close, and the lamp did something to her face that daylight hadn't — it took the wariness out and left only attention. She was cutting a punch. He knew enough of the trade to know it took men years to cut a single letterform in steel, and she was doing it freehand, correcting an error in the army's supply manifest, her breath shallow and even, her thumb rotating the blank with small precise movements like a safe-cracker's.

"You're cutting it backwards," he said, before he could stop himself. "Mirror image. You're not — forgive me. Of course you are."

She didn't look up. "It cuts easier if I forget it's backwards."

"How long have you done this?"

"Since I could hold a graver." Steel whispered against steel. "My father thought eyes were trained by eight. Hands by ten. Character never."

"Character can't be cut?"

"Character is what's left when you've cut everything else away." She blew brass filings from the punch and held it to the lamplight, turning it, dismissing it, beginning again on another face. "The manifest said *quarante-trois caisses de conserves*. Your clerk wrote *quarente*. If I let that stand, some quartermaster shortchanges you three crates and blames arithmetic."

Vasseur felt something shift in his chest that he refused, for the moment, to name. He had drafted her because her hands were steady and the shop needed a compositor who wasn't a conscripted boy from Lyon who set type like he was dealing cards in a game he was losing. He had not expected competence of this order. This was not a printer's widow minding her late husband's trade. This was mastery, sitting at a bench in a town of nine hundred people, cutting punches for grocery manifests because there was nothing else in this town worth cutting them for.

"Your father taught you," he said.

"My father built half the typefaces in this region. None of them carry his name." A flat statement, without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "An English name, in a French foundry. He learned to sign his work in ways that couldn't be traced back."

"And you?"

"I don't sign my work."

He almost laughed. There it was, then — the whole history of this family told in one line of trade talk. He had read the file on the Blackwoods, thin as it was: an English type-founder who came after the Franco-Prussian war, married a local girl, outlived every grudge the town held about 1870, died two winters ago of the damp that got into his lungs at this very bench. The townspeople tolerated the daughter the way they tolerated the old Roman bridge — useful, foreign, quietly resented.

"You could work in Paris," he said. "Lyon. Anywhere."

"I work here."

It was not loyalty. He heard that clearly. It was something harder — a woman standing in the one place that would have her, defending it by being indispensable to it. He recognized the architecture of that kind of pride because he had built something similar himself out of a commission no one had wanted him to hold.

* * *

The proofs were done by ten. His clerk had gone to billets; the soldiers in the square had settled into their card games; and Vasseur found he had no reason to stay in the shop and every reason not to leave. Marguerite was locking the type cabinet, and the lamp threw their shadows long up the wall, so that his shadow seemed to bow toward hers.

"Captain." She said it like a door closing.

"Mademoiselle Blackwood." He picked up his cap, put it down again. "There's a letter I can't send. To my mother, in Vichy. Do you know why?"

"I imagine the censor knows why."

"The censor is not the difficulty." He heard himself speaking and did not entirely approve of it, but the shop was warm and quiet and he had been alone with these words for four months. "The difficulty is what the letter says. My brother Étienne was at Verdun. My mother writes to me — she has written eleven letters — asking whether the lists from his sector are final. Whether names can still be struck. She writes as though I am the sort of man who could strike a name, and the terrible thing, mademoiselle, is that I am exactly the sort of man who could strike a name, and I have not struck any, because the names are not mine to strike."

Marguerite had gone still at the cabinet, her hand flat on the closed lid.

"She asks if Étienne might come home on leave," Vasseur went on, quieter. "And I cannot answer, because answering means writing down either a hope or a lie, and both would arrive in the same envelope, and she would smell the lie through the paper. Mothers do." He turned his cap in his hands. "So I write letters about weather. Eleven letters about weather. She thinks her son the captain has become dull."

For a moment she said nothing, and he braced for the obvious reply — *then you know what your ledger does to mothers here* — the accusation he had been carrying unspoken since he'd sealed the civic register in its oilcloth and carried it to this shop like stolen silver.

Instead she said: "Does she keep your letters?"

"All of them. In a biscuit tin. She keeps everyone's letters. When we were children she kept even the envelopes."

Something crossed her face then — not softness exactly. Recognition. He understood, uneasily, that she was revising him, the way a compositor revises a line, pulling out a word that doesn't fit and setting a truer one in its place. He did not know which word she'd replaced him with. He wasn't sure he wanted to.

* * *

Over the following week he watched her the way he'd been trained to watch terrain — noting elevations, weak points, lines of sight. He noted that she proofed everything twice, including army matter that no one would ever audit. That she corrected errors she wasn't ordered to correct, in the manifests, in the pay rolls, once in a casualty dispatch where a corporal's surname had been misspelled — *Moreau* rendered *Moraux* — and she fixed it without comment, though the wrong spelling was already official.

"Why?" he asked her that night. "The dispatch is filed either way. No one reads it but a clerk in Dijon."

"A man dies however his name is spelled," she said. "But his mother will read it. She'll know if it's wrong. And she'll spend the rest of her life thinking the army couldn't be bothered to spell her son correctly, which is a smaller grief than the first one, but it sits on top of it, and grief is heavy enough already without misprint."

Vasseur stood very still in the middle of her shop.

He thought of the conscription flags in the civic ledger — the red pencil marks his superiors had placed beside thirty-one names in this town of nine hundred. Thirty-one boys between seventeen and fifty-one, bakers' sons and farmhands and the schoolmaster's assistant, reduced to entries in a register that he, Aurel Vasseur, captain of the 214th, had been ordered to convert into call-up lists by spring. He had looked at those names the way he'd looked at freight tonnage and fodder allotments — as requisitions. Numbers wearing clothes.

Until a woman at a type bench had explained to him that a misspelled name was a grief sitting on top of a grief.

That night he took out the civic ledger after she left — she always left at nine, walking home along the rampart road with a lantern he pretended not to watch from the window — and he read it. Not audited it. Read it. He made himself attach faces. The Dubois boy, flagged, was the one who'd thrown snowballs at the sentries last week and been laughed at rather than shot, mercifully. The name *Perrin*, flagged, belonged to the baker's son — he'd seen the young man hauling flour sacks that morning with his shirt off despite the cold, sixteen years old at most, arms like saplings. The boy had grinned at Vasseur across the square with the entire, idiotic confidence of someone who believes wars happen to other people.

Sixteen. The red pencil did not care about sixteen. The red pencil cared about quotas.

Vasseur closed the ledger and sat in the dark of the print shop for a long time, listening to the cooling press tick like a clock wound too tight.

His mother's eleventh letter was in his breast pocket. He had read it so many times the creases had gone soft. *You are in a position to know things, Aurélie's boy. Tell me if the lists are final. Tell me if there is anything a mother can do.*

He had always answered: nothing. Nothing a mother can do. Nothing anyone can do. Names go onto lists and lists become orders and orders become trains, and the machinery does not stop for sentiment, because if it stopped for sentiment it would stop entirely.

Except.

Except that he had spent ten days watching a woman handle the machinery of names — the actual physical substance of them, lead and steel and ink — and he now knew something he wished he did not know: names are made of movable parts. Names are set and corrected, spelled and respelled, proofed and pulled. The machinery that puts a boy on a train passes, somewhere along its length, through human hands.

He was looking at his own hands as the thought completed itself, and he hated the thought, and he could not unthink it.

* * *

In the morning he found a discrepancy.

Small. The kind he would have waved through a month ago. Page fourteen of the ledger, the roll of adult males resident as of the census, and one entry where the surname had been re-set — not altered, exactly. *Thévenot* where the original hand wrote *Thévenoz*. One letter. An *o* where there should have been a *z*. A variant so ordinary that any clerk in France would pass it without blinking; half the surnames in the department drifted like that between registers.

But Vasseur had spent ten days learning how names were made, and he looked at that small round *o* and saw the graver-work behind it — saw the spacing, the fit, the unmistakable rightness of professionally set type sitting inside a page of hurried clerical hand. Someone had corrected this page. Someone with a lamp and a bench and hands steadier than God's.

His first instinct was the correct one, the professional one: report it. Flag it. Note the discrepancy in the inspection journal, initial it, date it, let the machinery do what machinery does. His second instinct arrived a half-second later and stayed much longer: *if she is doing what I think she is doing, then reporting it is killing her, and not reporting it is —*

Is what? Treason? Mercy? He stood over the open ledger with his pen uncapped and understood that he had come to a place his training had no map for. On one side lay his oath, his rank, his mother's biscuit tin full of weather reports. On the other side lay a baker's boy with sapling arms and a schoolteacher's assistant and twenty-nine other names in red pencil, and a woman who had told him that grief is heavy enough already without misprint.

He recapped the pen.

Then he took out his notebook and wrote, in his own careful officer's hand, an innocuous note: *P.14 — census variants consistent w/ regional orthography. No action.* He initialed it. He dated it. It was the first lie he had ever put in an official document, and it was so small, one letter wide, an *o* instead of a *z*, and it weighed on the page like a body.

That evening, when Marguerite came in and hung her coat by the stove, he was proofing army placards as though nothing in the world had changed, because nothing in the world had changed except him.

"Captain," she said, in her closing-a-door voice.

"Mademoiselle Blackwood." He did not look up from the proof. "A question, while we're alone. Purely professional." He set the galley down and finally met her eyes, and saw there — beneath the wariness, beneath the door — a flicker of something that might have been fear, or might have been the beginning of an answer. "If a man found an error in a ledger. A small error. One letter." He held up his thumb and forefinger, a letter's width apart. "The kind of error anyone could make."

Her hands had gone very still at her sides.

"What," he said, "would be the honorable thing to do with it?"

Outside, in the dark square, the church clock began striking the hour, and Marguerite Blackwood looked at him the way one looks at a lock whose tumblers have just, unexpectedly, begun to turn.
