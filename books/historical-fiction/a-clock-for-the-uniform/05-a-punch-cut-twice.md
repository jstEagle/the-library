## 5. A Punch Cut Twice

The army proofs were done by half past nine — requisition forms, ration chits, a proclamation about curfew hours that Captain Vasseur had dictated in a voice flat as slate. He signed the pull sheet without looking at it and told the sentry at the door that Madame Blackwood worked too slowly to be worth guarding after dark.

That was the closest thing to kindness the occupation had produced, and I meant to spend it well.

I waited until his boots faded down the rue Basse, until the lamplighter made his round and the square went the color of old pewter. Then I bolted the door, drew my own curtain, and lit the bench lamp low — a flame no bigger than a walnut, hooded with tin so it threw nothing past the window frame.

On the bench before me lay the ledger's standing type, locked in its chase like men locked in a cell.

* * *

My father used to say a punch cutter had two virtues: patience and dishonesty of the smallest possible kind.

He didn't mean forgery, not exactly. He meant that every letter is a small lie told so consistently it becomes true. No two cuts of an *e* are identical; the eye forgives because the hand insists. A good punchcutter lies two hundred times per page and calls it typography.

I thought of him as I lifted the first flagged name out of the form with tweezers, the way you'd lift a splinter from flesh.

*Marchand, Étienne.* The baker's son. Eighteen years old, broad shoulders, a laugh that had once carried across the schoolyard while other boys threw chalk at my father's coat sleeves and called him the Englishman, the heretic, the printer who set type for Protestants and worse. Étienne had been twelve then, and he'd laughed loudest.

His name sat in cold type now, flagged with the conscription clerk's red pencil mark on the proof copy upstairs: fit, single, eligible. One line among four hundred. One *M* among thousands.

I took a blank piece of type-high steel and began to cut.

* * *

You cannot simply remove a name from a locked ledger page; the space would show like a pulled tooth. You cannot substitute another name wholesale — the clerks compare against parish rolls. What you can do, if you are mad enough or desperate enough or both, is make the existing name into a different, unremarkable version of itself.

*Marchand* could become *Marchant*. A single stroke. An *a* where the *h*'s shoulder narrowed. On paper, at reading distance, under lamplight, in the hurry of a clerk's audit — nearly invisible.

Nearly.

So the variant had to exist somewhere else. That was the true labor, the part that would take weeks. Every altered name needed a ghost: a baptismal record recut, a margin note re-struck, a second copy of the ledger page printed with the changed spelling so that any comparison found two documents agreeing on a lie. Two lies agreeing look exactly like truth. My father taught me that too, though he'd have wept to hear it applied here.

I cut through the night. The graver sang against the steel, that thin high note I'd known since I was seven years old, when my father set me on a stool and put a blank punch in my fist and said, *Now you will never be bored, and never be rich.* The *h* came off clean. The *a* went on with a slight forward lean that no one but a fellow craftsman would ever catch, and Vasseur was not a craftsman.

By three in the morning, Étienne Marchand had become Étienne Marchant, a man who did not quite exist, and therefore could not quite be conscripted.

I printed one proof of the altered line, held it to the hooded lamp, and burned it over the sink. The ash went down the drain with the water, and I stood there shaking, not from fear exactly — though there was fear, plenty of it, sitting in my chest like swallowed ice — but from something else. Something I didn't have a word for yet.

For the first time since my father died, the town needed me. And it would never know.

* * *

Fourteen names were flagged in the first conscription list. Fourteen names, fourteen variants, fourteen ghosts to manufacture. At two hours a night, working after the army proofs, I calculated I had perhaps five weeks before the next audit. The armistice rumors said sooner. The rumors always said sooner.

I fell into a rhythm that felt like living two lives stitched at the seam. By day I was the obedient printer, setting army forms in their ugly condensed gothic, keeping my eyes down, answering Vasseur's occasional questions in as few words as courtesy allowed. By night I was someone else entirely — a woman with a graver and a grudge against the machinery of lists, cutting the town free of them letter by letter.

The widow Marchand came on the ninth night.

I heard her key — she still held a key to the shop's back door from the days when my father had let her take flour deliveries through the alley — and I had perhaps forty seconds. Forty seconds to kill the lamp, sweep the altered chase under the proofing stone, and stand up looking like a woman doing nothing at all.

I managed the lamp. I managed the chase. I did not manage my hands; they were black with ink to the wrist, and there was a graver on the floor where I'd dropped it, glinting.

She came through the storeroom slow, the way she moved everywhere since her husband died — like a woman perpetually listening for a knock. She carried a covered dish. Bread, probably. The widow Marchand still left bread on our step some Sundays, had done it for years, and had never once stayed to be thanked, because thanking would mean acknowledging that the town owed the Blackwoods anything at all.

"Madame," I said, stepping into the doorway with my inked hands behind my back. "It's late."

"I know what time it is." Her eyes went past me, into the dark of the shop. "I saw your light from the square. Then it went out all at once, like a snuffed candle, and I thought — a snuffed candle means someone hiding it."

My heart was going hard enough that I could hear it. "I was cleaning. The soldiers want the shop kept—"

"Marguerite." She said my name the way nobody in this town had said it in eleven years — not *the Blackwood girl*, not *your father's daughter*, but *Marguerite*, plain and close, like family. "I buried a husband and I've raised a fool of a son who thinks war is a parade. Do you think I don't know what a hidden lamp means?"

We stood there in the dark, and I could smell the bread through the cloth, ordinary bread, the bread of the living.

"Whatever it is you're doing in there," she said quietly, "whatever your father's tools are being made to say — I'm not asking. I don't want to know, because knowing is dangerous and I've a son they've already marked." Her voice caught, just barely, on *marked*. "But if you need flour, there's flour. If you need a reason to be out after curfew, I've got a bad hip and a doctor who writes excuses. And if—" she stopped, started again. "If it goes wrong, you come to the bakery. The cellar has a door into the coal yard. Understand?"

I couldn't speak. Eleven years of cold shoulders and Sunday bread left on the step unthanked, and now this woman was offering me a hole in the ground like a promise.

"Understand?" she repeated.

"Yes," I whispered.

She set the dish inside the door and left. At the threshold she paused. "Étienne," she said, not turning around. "Is he in it? Whatever it is?"

And I understood that she knew. Not everything — not the variants, not the ghosts, not the two lies agreeing — but enough. A mother reads a red pencil mark on a conscription list and then watches a printshop burn oil at midnight, and arithmetic does the rest.

"He's safe," I said. "As long as I finish."

She nodded once, a shadow moving against shadow, and was gone.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat at the bench with the lamp hooded and the thirteen remaining names laid out on a proof sheet like patients awaiting surgery, and I felt the terror transfiguring itself, grain by grain, into purpose.

Before, the work had been mine alone — a secret hoarded in the dark, sustained by nothing but stubbornness. Now it had a witness. Now the widow Marchand knew there was something in this shop worth a hidden light, and Étienne's life hung on a single stroke of a graver, and the whole rotten apparatus of occupation rested on the assumption that a woman alone with type was harmless.

They had seized the ledger. They had seized the press. They had posted a sentry and taken my signature on a requisition I never chose.

But they hadn't taken the punches. And a man who controls the letters controls what the words can say.

I picked up the second flagged name — *Delacroix, Henri* — and fitted a blank steel into the vise, and began.

Somewhere below the window, boots on the cobbles. The night patrol, making its round. I killed the lamp without hurrying and sat in the dark with my hands folded, listening to them pass, counting the seconds between their steps like a clock that measured not hours but how much longer I could keep the lie alive.

Thirteen names. Thirteen nights, if I was lucky.

I wasn't lucky. Because the next morning, when I climbed to the office to collect the day's army orders, I found the proof drawer open, the red-pencil marks disturbed — and Captain Vasseur standing at the window, holding the conscription list in both hands, reading it as though he'd never seen it before.
