## 6. Inspection

Marguerite heard the motorcar before she saw it — the first motorcar in the square since the occupation began, its engine grinding against the frozen cobbles like something being dragged to slaughter.

She was at the bench with a proof of the December rolls drying under her palm when Vasseur appeared in the doorway without knocking. He had learned to knock in October; he had unlearned it by November.

"Colonel Berthier," he said. "He arrives within the hour. He will want the ledger, the fresh rolls, and a comparison done in front of him."

Her hand stayed flat on the damp proof. Under her fingers, through the thin paper, she could feel the faint relief of type — her own type, cut and recut across eleven nights while the shop slept.

"Comparison," she said.

"Name by name. He is thorough. It's why they sent him." Vasseur's eyes went to the ledger on its shelf, then back to her, and for one unguarded second there was something in his face that was not command at all. "Madame Blackwood. Is there anything about those pages I should know?"

The question was a door left open exactly wide enough. He knew something. Not everything — if he knew everything he would not be standing here offering her an exit shaped like a question. But he suspected. She could see it in the way he stood too straight, the way officers stood when they were compensating for what their hands wanted to do.

"Every name is set correctly," she said. "I would stake my father's bench on it."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what you'll get."

He held her gaze a moment longer than regulation allowed. Then he turned and left, and Marguerite sat alone with the proof, listening to the motorcar grow louder, and understood that she had just lied badly to a man who was lying badly right back at her — and that both of them knew it, and neither of them could afford to say so.

* * *

Vasseur met the colonel's car at the foot of the church steps because there was nowhere better to stand.

Berthier descended with the unhurried precision of a man who had never once been late or early, only correct. He was sixty perhaps, silver at the temples, gloves of good leather, and he looked at the square — at the shuttered café, the frost-killed plane tree, the print shop with its requisition notice curling off the door — the way an accountant looks at a column of figures: no hatred, no appetite, only the search for error.

"Captain Vasseur."

"Colonel."

"You've kept the press running."

"The press runs, Colonel. Paper is short and the ink is stretched with lamp oil, but it runs."

"Ink stretched with lamp oil." Berthier removed one glove, finger by finger. "The Ministry will hear that the provinces are improvising. They will be charmed." He walked toward the shop without waiting for escort. "Show me your ledger."

* * *

Marguerite had arranged the room in the forty minutes she had: proofs squared, punches racked, the altered pages buried mid-ledger where a tired eye would slide past them. She stood at the composing stick when they entered, hands moving, because a woman working was furniture and a woman idle was a question.

Berthier crossed the floor slowly. He touched nothing at first. He read the spines of the type cases, ran a thumb along the bench edge, and stopped before her father's graver rack with the expression of a man encountering a dialect he'd thought extinct.

"Who cuts your punches?"

"I do, Colonel."

"You." Not cruel. Merely recalibrating. "Where did you train?"

"My father. This was his shop."

"Ah." The word carried the whole weight of the town's history with the Blackwood name, though Berthier couldn't have known it. To him it was simply data. "A family trade. Good. Trades survive wars; opinions don't." He turned to Vasseur. "The comparison, Captain. You've done it already, I assume."

"Twice, Colonel."

"Then we'll do it a third time. Together." He drew out a chair — her father's chair, Marguerite noticed, the one with the worn crescent in the seat — and sat, and opened the fresh rolls from the prefecture beside the ledger, and said, "Begin."

* * *

They read names aloud.

It was Vasseur who read them, in a voice pitched flat as parade ground, and Marguerite who answered from the ledger, turning pages, keeping her breathing even. Ardant, present. Bachelier, present. Coudert — her stomach did not move, her voice did not move — present.

The baker's son was four pages deep. Tomas Fournier. Flagged in red pencil in the original roll, marked *conscription priority*, and recut by her own hand into a variant so ordinary it hurt: the final letter softened, the crossbar shortened, a name that now read as clerical shorthand rather than sentence. A man reading quickly would see a boy accounted for. A man reading slowly —

Berthier read slowly.

"Turn back," he said, three pages after they'd passed it.

The room contracted. Marguerite felt Vasseur go still behind her, felt the silence take on weight, and made herself turn the pages forward again with the boredom of long habit.

"Fournier," the colonel said. "Read me the entry as printed."

Vasseur read it. His voice did not waver. Later Marguerite would try to remember whether it wavered and would be unable to swear either way — which was itself a kind of answer.

"And the roll says?"

"Conscription priority. Accounted for in the town census. Present."

"Present," Berthier repeated. He looked up, and his eyes went past Vasseur entirely, to Marguerite, to her hands resting on the ledger's edge. "Your typesetter has steady hands, Captain. Steadier than most men I've employed. Where is your original plate work stored?"

"In the foundry cabinet, Colonel. Standard practice."

"Standard." He rose, buttoned his coat, and for a long moment examined the wall of drawers where the matrices slept in their compartments like a city at night. "I'll want a full audit copy pulled and sent to headquarters. Every page. Within the week."

"Yes, Colonel."

He put on his second glove. At the door he paused, and said, without turning around:

"The war is ending, Captain. Everyone can smell it. That is precisely when errors get made — men relax, records slip, someone decides a name here or there doesn't matter anymore." Now he turned, and his face was mild, almost kind. "Names always matter. That is what ledgers are *for*. Good day."

* * *

The motorcar ground away eastward, and the silence it left behind was worse than any noise.

Marguerite locked the door. Her hands were not steady now; they shook the way they hadn't shaken through any of the eleven nights of cutting, because fear during work is fuel and fear afterward is just fear.

"He knew," she said. "Or half-knew."

"He suspects." Vasseur stood by the window, watching the empty road. "There's a difference, and the difference is a week. He wants audit copies. If he compares them against this ledger and finds variants—"

"There won't be variants. The audit copies come off my plates. They'll match perfectly."

"They'll match each other," he said. "That's the danger, Madame Blackwood. Two documents agreeing too well. Forgers make things consistent. Truth is messier." He turned from the window at last. "You should stop."

"No."

"The colonel—"

"The colonel leaves, and the lists still go out, and Tomas Fournier still goes into a uniform he won't survive, and every other name I've recut goes with him." She pulled off her apron, folded it, set it on the bench with terrible neatness because her hands needed something orderly to do. "You should report me. That's what you're circling. You've been circling it for weeks."

The words landed in the room like dropped type — small, hard, scattered.

"If you reported me tonight," she went on, "you'd sleep well, and in spring I'd be shot or transported, and the ledger would go to headquarters intact, and the town would lose six sons whose mothers wave to you in the market. Weigh it. You're an officer. You're good at weighing."

"I am not good at it," Vasseur said quietly. "That is the entire problem."

He came toward the bench, and stopped on the far side of it, the width of her father's workbench between them, the way a border runs between two countries that used to be one.

"Three nights ago I wrote in my report that the ledger was accurate," he said. "I wrote it knowing I hadn't verified it. Knowing I didn't *want* to verify it. Do you understand what that makes me?"

"A man."

"A traitor. Or an honest one. I genuinely no longer know which word applies, and I have stopped caring to find out." His jaw worked. "When Berthier asked about Fournier, I lied. Badly. I'm trained to lie well and I lied badly, in front of a colonel, because you were standing ten feet away and I could not—" He stopped himself. Recut the sentence, as she might say. "I could not do otherwise."

Marguerite looked at him across the bench, across the racked punches and the ink-stained wood her father had planed smooth thirty years ago, and felt the terrible arithmetic of it: he was ruining himself in increments, and she was letting him, and the town would never know either of their names.

"The audit copies," she said. "I'll pull them myself. And I'll make them messy, Captain. A smudge here. A kerning fault there. Enough truth in them to satisfy your colonel."

"And the flagged names?"

"Already safe. Already ordinary. You read Fournier yourself and heard nothing wrong."

"I heard nothing wrong," he agreed, "because I chose to hear nothing wrong. There's the confession. Write it down. You're the one with the type."

For the first time in weeks she laughed — one breath of it, quickly strangled, but real.

He moved toward the door, then paused with his hand on the latch, and said, without looking at her: "Berthier will send a clerk to collect the copies personally. A corporal named Duret. He counts everything twice and trusts nothing once. Whatever you give him must survive *him*."

"And if it doesn't?"

Vasseur opened the door onto the blue winter dusk. Somewhere across the square, the bakery chimney had gone dark — the widow's ovens cold, the house where Tomas had grown up now silent as a closed book.

"Then I will have lied badly one more time," he said. "And this time it will be the last lie either of us gets."

He stepped out into the cold, and Marguerite stood alone in the lamplight among her father's letters, and reached for the case where the Fournier matrices slept, because there were still five names to check before morning — and now, somewhere on a road east, a colonel was thinking about them too.
