## 7. What the Widow Knew

The widow came on a Thursday, in the gray hour when the lamps still burned but the sky had begun to consider dawn.

Marguerite heard the knock — three soft raps, spaced like a careful hand setting down cups — and her whole body went rigid at the bench. No one knocked like that. Soldiers kicked. Vasseur tapped once and entered, because the shop was his now in every way that mattered. The knocking was civilian, feminine, deliberate.

She slid the punch into the drawer of type specimens, laid a blank proof over the half-finished ledger page, and opened the door on the smell of bread that no longer existed to bake.

Madame Ferro stood in the blue dark with a basket on her arm and frost in her eyebrows. She had been beautiful once; the town said so with the tone people use for things ruined by weather. Now her face was all angles and patience, and her eyes went past Marguerite's shoulder into the shop with the unhurried sweep of a woman counting stock.

"You're up early," Marguerite said.

"So are you." Madame Ferro stepped inside without being asked, the way she stepped everywhere, as if doors were suggestions. She set the basket on the bench Marguerite's father had built, and from it she drew a loaf — real bread, dark and dense, not the sawdust ration — and broke it in half. She held out one half. "Eat first. Then we'll talk."

"I'm not hungry."

"Everyone is hungry. They've only forgotten the word for it." She pushed the bread into Marguerite's hands and waited, watching, until Marguerite bit into it. The crust cracked. In four months she had not tasted anything so honest.

"Good girl," said Madame Ferro. "Now. Show me what you're doing to my son's name."

* * *

The words landed like a dropped chase — type scattering across the stone floor of her chest. Marguerite did not let her face move. Her father had taught her that: *the hand shows what the face hides, so train both.* But Madame Ferro was watching her hands too, and they had gone white around the bread.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Étienne Ferro. Page nine of the conscription rolls. Flagged twice — once in red ink, once with the little cross the colonel's clerks use when they want a boy found quickly." The widow's voice never rose. It moved through the shop like water finding cracks. "You have changed it. I don't know how yet. But I've watched your lamp burn until three, four o'clock, night after night, long after their proofs are done. And I've seen the fresh proofs of page nine. Étienne's name has grown a silent *e*. A small thing. A nothing thing. Unless you are a clerk comparing two lists, and then it is everything."

The stove ticked. Somewhere across the square a sentry coughed.

"Who told you about the proofs?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"The same person who told me which nights the corridor outside your door goes unwatched." Madame Ferro took back the other half of the loaf, folded it in cloth, and set it down with terrible gentleness, as if it were an offering on an altar. "I have known for five weeks, Mademoiselle Blackwood. I have said nothing. Now you will understand why I have come."

She reached into the basket again and brought out a scrap of oilcloth, and unwrapped from it a card — a printed card, cheap stock, the kind of jobbing work any village press could turn out. On it was a name, an address in Lyon, and a single line: *Ask for the price of flour.*

"There is a route," Madame Ferro said. "South, through the hills, run by people who have been doing this since before either of us could walk. It moves papers. It moves men. Last month it moved the Duval brothers out ahead of the second levy, and no one in this town has heard their names spoken since, which is how you know they got clean away." She laid the card on the bench between them. "It can move you. Tonight, if you want. There is a cart leaving from the tannery at moonrise."

The card sat there, gray and ordinary, and Marguerite looked at it the way she might look at a letter written in a language she almost remembered.

"Why me?" she said. "Why now?"

"Because the colonel comes again within the fortnight. Everyone knows it. And because a woman who has been altering a military ledger cannot do it forever without being caught, and I would rather not watch another Blackwood go into the ground." Madame Ferro's mouth thinned. "Yes. I knew your father. We all knew him. We bought his bread and we did not speak to him in the street and we let our children mock his accent, and we told ourselves it was only prudence, prudence, prudence, until prudence wore a hole right through the middle of us. I am not here to apologize. An apology costs nothing. I am here because I owe you more than nothing."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you are a fool," said the widow, without heat. "A brave fool, which is the worst kind."

* * *

Afterward — after the door had closed and the frost-breath quiet had settled back over the shop — Marguerite sat at her father's bench with the card in her palm and tried to weigh her life against itself.

Lyon. A press somewhere in that city, perhaps, or simply safety, which she had not owned since she was twelve years old and the town decided that an Englishman's daughter was the reason the mill flooded, the reason prices fell, the reason for whatever needed a reason. She could be on the cart tonight. She could be free of the soldiers' boots on her floors, free of Vasseur's unreadable eyes tracking her across the shop, free of the ledger lying open downstairs in the army office like a wound that would not close.

And page eleven would stay as it was.

Page eleven was the one that defeated her. Pages one through ten she had done — ten names unmade and remade, ten families' sons dissolved into typographical fog, each alteration small enough to pass a tired clerk's eye and large enough to break the chain between list and man. But page eleven held six names, and the army had begun requiring counter-proofs now, and the counter-proof required her to cut variants that matched nothing, invented nothing, existed nowhere — a deeper forgery, slower work, letters she had to design from scratch because no existing punch would serve. Three of the six names were finished. Three were not.

Three names. Three boys who would march east in the spring and feed themselves into the same machinery that had taken her father's health, her mother's mind, and half the youth of two nations.

She thought of the cart. Moonrise. The tannery.

Then she thought of something else, something that surprised her: the way Madame Ferro had said *my son's name.* Not *the name.* Her son. Who at fourteen had thrown mud at Auguste Blackwood's apron and called him a spy in two languages, and who at seventeen had come back to the bakery thin and ashamed and never apologized, and whom his mother loved with a ferocity that had walked across a frozen square before dawn carrying contraband bread and a confession five weeks old.

That was what belonging looked like, Marguerite realized. Not the town tolerating her because it needed a press. This. A woman risking everything to sit in an enemy-occupied shop and bargain for the life of someone who had once been cruel to her.

And the price of it was the very thing the widow was asking her to abandon.

* * *

She found Madame Ferro in the doorway still, standing with her hand on the latch, looking at the presses the way one looks at a grave that has been well tended.

"If I don't take the route," Marguerite said, "will your people help me anyway? Paper. Ink. Anything that keeps the route open for others afterward."

The widow turned. For a long moment her face did something complicated, grief and respect fighting for the same ground.

"You'd stay," she said. "For three names."

"For three names, and for the principle of finishing what I start. My father used to say a punch cut halfway is worse than no punch at all — it prints a lie badly." Marguerite laughed, short and dry. "He meant typography. He didn't know he was giving me a rule for treason."

"He knew," said Madame Ferre — and then stopped, corrected herself. "Ferro. Forgive me. Yes. He knew. Auguste Blackwood understood exactly what words cost. It's why we feared him, I think. None of us could read the way he read us." She came back from the door. She took Marguerite's hands — the hands, the famous steady hands, ink-stained and scarred at the knuckles — and held them the way you hold something you intend to keep. "Listen to me. If you stay, you stay as one of ours. Whatever happens. Whatever the town did before. When this ends, if you are still alive, you will not eat alone again. That is my word, and my word is the only property I have left."

Marguerite's throat closed. She looked down at their joined hands, at the widow's cracked knuckles and her own, and thought: *twenty-three years, and this is the first time anyone has asked me to stay.*

"I need paper," she made herself say, because if she did not say it now she would say yes to the cart instead. "Ledger stock, the army grade, and a wax that matches their seal impression. I can't forge the counter-proofs on anything else."

"I'll find them."

"And Madame — the route. Keep it open. When these pages are done, there may be others who need it more than I did."

"There will always be others." The widow released her hands and gathered her basket, and at the door she paused, backlit by the graying window, suddenly ordinary again, just a baker's widow in a shawl. "One more thing. The captain. Vasseur."

"What about him?"

"He reads," said Madame Ferro. "My contact in the corridor tells me he stands behind you while you work and reads every page upside-down. Either he is hunting you, mademoiselle, or he is learning from you. You would know better than I which." She pulled the door open onto the cold. "But decide soon. The colonel arrives within the week, and men who read upside-down eventually read everything."

The door shut. The frost-breath quiet returned.

Marguerite stood alone in the shop with the card still lying on the bench, and above it the half-finished proof of page eleven, its three remaining names waiting in her father's type cabinet like seeds in winter soil.

Across the square, the sentry coughed again. And beneath it, fainter, she heard the army office door open — the particular creak of its hinge, unmistakable, four months familiar — and footsteps crossing toward the shop while the sky outside went from gray to the color of a fresh-pulled proof.

Vasseur was awake. Vasseur was coming.

Her heart slammed once against the card, the paper, the whole impossible arithmetic of her life — and she swept the escape route into the type drawer, laid her palms flat on the bench, and turned to face the door.
