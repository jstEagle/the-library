## 8. Armistice Rumors

The orders arrived at six in the morning, carried by a corporal whose horse had frothed itself nearly white on the road from Arras. Vasseur read them standing in the courtyard while the man waited, stamping his boots against the cold, and by the time he reached the second paragraph his hands had gone very still.

*All regional conscription rolls to be finalized and certified within seventy-two hours of receipt. Discrepancies to be resolved by physical inspection. Non-compliance to be treated as sabotage.*

Seventy-two hours. He read it three times, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something more survivable. They did not.

The corporal cleared his throat. "The colonel requests confirmation of receipt, Captain."

"You'll have it within the hour." Vasseur folded the paper along its crease, once, twice, until it was a small hard rectangle he could have fit inside a fist. "There is a kitchen around the back. There is coffee, if you ask the cook nicely. She does not like soldiers, so ask nicely anyway."

The corporal went. Vasseur remained in the courtyard a moment longer, watching his breath hang in the air like something he'd been unable to say. Across the square, the baker's chimney was already smoking — the widow rose before dawn now, had risen before dawn every day since her son's name first appeared on a list Vasseur had signed with a steady hand and a hollow stomach. He wondered if she knew what was coming down the road toward all of them. He suspected she baked regardless. There was a kind of courage in that he had never learned in an officer's academy.

He went inside to write the confirmation.

* * *

He found Marguerite at the composing stone, as he always found her, as though she were not a woman stationed there but a fixture of the shop itself — another iron bed, another press screw, something the building would be incomplete without. The lamp was lit although it was past nine and grey daylight came through the windows well enough to work by. She preferred the lamp. He had noticed this weeks ago and never asked why; some part of him understood that she worked where the light could not reach her face, and that asking would only remind her he'd noticed.

"The corporal's horse nearly foundered," he said, by way of announcing himself.

"Then the roads are bad." She did not look up. Her graver moved through a punch with the small precise sound of a knife through apple skin. "Bad roads are good for us. They slow everyone equally."

"Not everyone." He set the order down on the stone beside her elbow, face up, deliberately. It was a risk dressed as carelessness. "Read it."

She read it. He watched her hands while she did — they stopped moving, which for Marguerite Blackwood was the equivalent of another woman dropping something. When she looked up at him her face was composed, but he had spent enough evenings in this shop now to see the seams. The stillness beneath the stillness.

"Three days," she said.

"Seventy-two hours. They were specific."

"They usually are, when they mean it." She turned back to the stone and began setting her tools in order, each one aligned to the last, and he recognized the gesture for what it was: a woman putting her hands somewhere useful before they could shake. "You shouldn't have shown me this."

"No," he agreed. "I shouldn't have."

They stood in the smell of ink and hot metal and neither of them moved toward the door or away from it. This was the geometry their lives had taken — two people always in the same room, always at the distance a pressed handkerchief could not quite cross, conducting whatever it was they conducted through proofs and corrections and the grammar of type. He had stopped pretending to himself that it was nothing. He had not yet started pretending it could be anything else.

"There will be a physical inspection," he said. "Not a reading of proofs. Men standing in a square answering to their names."

"I understand what a roll is, Captain."

"Do you." He kept his voice level, kept it officer-flat, because anything warmer would burn them both. "Then you understand that type is not the only thing that can be compared against a ledger."

For the first time since he'd known her, she looked frightened in front of him — openly, without arranging her face first. It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then she took it back, folded it away the way she folded everything, and nodded once.

"Thank you for the correction," she said, loudly enough to carry to the doorway, where — yes — Dupont the sergeant was passing with an armful of requisition forms. "I'll see the amended proofs are pulled before noon."

Dupont's boots receded down the corridor. Vasseur waited until the sound was gone entirely.

"Three days," he said again, quietly. "Whatever isn't finished—"

"It will be finished."

"That is not what I asked."

But she had already bent back over the stone, and he understood he would get nothing further, and that this too was a kind of answer: *whatever isn't finished will be abandoned, and I will not tell you which names those are, because you are still an officer and I am still not fool enough to hand you my neck.* He left the order on the stone when he went. A warning lying open on a table was a correction. A warning passed hand to hand was conspiracy. He knew the difference precisely, and knew also that the difference was shrinking daily, and that soon there would be no honest distance between them at all — only the question of who lied first, and for whom.

* * *

Peace talks had stalled. Everyone in the garrison knew it by afternoon; the knowledge moved through the occupied town the way water moves under ice, invisible and irresistible. In the mess, Lieutenant Rousset held forth on diplomacy as though he had attended it personally — "Another month, easily, before they sign anything" — and Vasseur ate his soup and said nothing and calculated.

A month would have been a mercy. They did not have a month. The order said seventy-two hours, and orders like this one were issued by men who counted hours the way misers counted coins, and if the talks collapsed outright the lists would go final even faster, because a government that cannot win peace begins to eat its own.

He walked back across the square in the early dark. The bakery window was lit. Through it he could see the widow working, flour to her elbows, and beside her — briefly, carrying a tray — a young man with her son's jaw and her son's way of ducking through doorframes. Not supposed to be there. Every hour he stayed in that town was an hour bought by someone's forgery, and Vasseur walked past the window and did not stop and did not look again, and understood that looking-away was becoming his principal skill as an officer.

In his quarters he took out the letter.

It was the same letter he had been writing since autumn, the one addressed to his sister in Lyon, the one that grew a paragraph and lost a paragraph and had never once been finished. He reread the last lines he'd managed.

*Clotilde — I find myself keeping accounts I cannot show anyone. Not of money. Of people. I know a number now, the exact number of men in one small town, and I know which of them the paper says must die and I know that the paper lies, and I have begun to think that knowing this and doing nothing is the only sin I was ever actually taught at the academy, though of course they called it something else. They called it duty.*

He put the letter away unfinished, as always. Then he sat at his desk and drew the inspection report form toward him and filled in the date and the regimental marks and left the substance blank, blank, blank — a document waiting to become either truth or treason depending on the next three days.

* * *

At eleven that night he went to the print shop. He had no official reason. He had stopped requiring official reasons some weeks ago and told himself this was supervision.

The lamp burned low. Marguerite was at the furnace end of the room, sleeves rolled, and the heat had brought color into her face that the winter had drained out of it, and for a moment he simply stood in the doorway watching her work — this woman the town had shunned for a dead man's debts, cutting steel better than any journeyman he'd seen in Paris, holding in her two steady hands the difference between a town and a graveyard.

She heard him. She always heard him.

"If you've come to check the proofs, they're drying."

"I've come to bring you a correction." He crossed the room and laid a proof sheet on the bench — one he had marked up himself that evening, in red pencil, in the margin where inspectors' eyes would not go. Three words, in a hand disguised as editorial fussiness: *Time is shorter.*

She read it. She looked at the pencil marks much longer than three words required, and then she looked at him, and the lamplight made one small bright point in each of her eyes.

"How much shorter?"

"Talks have stalled. If they fail—" He stopped. Chose the sentence carefully, the way she chose a letterform, weighing every stroke. "If they fail, the lists close tomorrow night instead of Saturday. And if they hold, we gain a day. Perhaps two. No one in this room controls which it is."

"No one in any room does." She set the proof down and wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, and he saw her doing arithmetic with the movement — counting hours against letters, letters against names, names against the dawn. "How many do I have? Truly. If I work all night, every night."

"You're asking me to help you spend your hours."

"I'm asking you to count them. You're the one with the clock, Captain. I only have the type."

Outside, far off toward the eastern ridge, artillery spoke — three heavy voices, then silence, then nothing. They both listened to the silence afterward as though it might amend itself into news.

"Marguerite." He had never used her first name. It sat in the room between them now, enormous, irrevocable, and he did not take it back. "When the inspection comes — and it will come, a colonel, a real one, with real rolls — I will be standing in that square answering for this ledger. Whatever is in it. Whatever isn't."

"I know what you'll be standing next to."

"And you should know that I have decided—" But the sentence failed him, as sentences had been failing him for weeks, because the end of it was either *nothing* or *everything* and he was not yet the man who could say either. He took out his watch instead, opened it, tilted the face toward the lamp. "Eleven-forty. Fifty-nine hours, if the talks hold. Thirty-five if they don't."

She looked at the watch face too. Then she reached out and closed the cover over it herself, her fingers brushing his knuckles for exactly as long as the gesture allowed and no longer, and turned back to her graver, and the small clean sound of steel being cut began again, patient as breathing, metered now against something neither of them could hear ticking.

"Good night, Aurel," she said.

He had never given her his first name either. He stood a moment in the warmth of the furnace light, wanting to answer and having nothing equal to offer, and then he went out into the cold — and it was not until he was halfway across the dark square that he realized what she had done, and stopped, and laughed once, alone, at the audacity of it.

Behind him the lamp in the print shop window stayed burning. It would burn, he understood, until the work was done or the door came down.

And at four the next morning, before the town woke, a rider in colonel's insignia came over the east bridge at a gallop.
