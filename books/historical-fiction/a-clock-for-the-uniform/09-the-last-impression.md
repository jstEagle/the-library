## 9. The Last Impression

The furnace took an hour to heat and her father used to say you could read a man's patience in the way he fed it. Marguerite fed it the way he had taught her: little coal, much air, no hurry. Above her, through the floorboards, the army proofs sat stacked and finished, forty pages of requisition orders drying under felt weights, everything exactly as Captain Vasseur had ordered it, everything correct, everything a lie built on top of another lie.

Below, in the cellar that had been her father's foundry before it was anyone's anything, the original plates lay in their crate like sleeping animals.

She had not opened the crate in three weeks. She had not needed to. Every flagged name now lived twice — once in her memory, once recut in the standing type of the altered ledger, buried in variants so small a colonel's eye had slid past them twice. *Renard* with the terminal *d* shaved to a whisper. *Émile Fournier* set a half-point light, the *m* narrowed until it read as an *n* at arm's length. Eleven names. Eleven small murders of ink committed in the dark while the garrison slept.

The plates were the only proof the originals had ever existed.

She heard Vasseur come down the stairs before she saw him — he had a way of descending that announced itself, one hand flat on the wall, as if the house might object to him and he wished to apologize in advance. His left arm was still bound against his chest, the wound from autumn gone stiff and silver beneath the sling. He carried a lantern and a leather folio, and he set both down on her father's workbench without asking permission, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"The garrison changes watch at two," he said. "You have until then."

"And after?"

"After, I file the inspection report." He looked at the furnace, at the crate, at her hands, which had not stopped moving since sundown because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant her father's punches going into the fire. "Marguerite."

No one called her that. In this town she was *the Blackwood girl*, *the printer's daughter*, *that Englishwoman* — eleven years of small erasures. He said her whole name the way he read, carefully, like it mattered.

"If you sign that report clean," she said, "they will hang it around your neck when they find out. And they will find out. Fournier's boy can't spell his own name but he can count, and there are eleven mothers in this town who will notice their sons missing from a list."

"I know what I am signing."

"Do you?" Her voice came out harder than she intended, forged-hard, bench-hard. "You are an officer. You have a letter home that you rewrite every week and never send because your mother would have to know what you've become to read it. That is what you are signing."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst of him, how he never flinched. He simply opened the folio, took out the report form, laid it flat, and uncapped his pen.

"Feed your furnace," he said.

* * *

Printing a ledger is not like printing orders. Orders forgive you; they are read once by tired men who want their numbers. A ledger is read by everyone, forever, against everything. She locked the chase herself, checked the quoins twice, and made the first pull at half past ten with the press that had printed christenings and tax notices and, for one shameful week in 1903, the slander about her father that half the town still believed.

The proof came up wet and black and true.

She read it under the lamp, name by name, street by street, and the town rose off the page — the baker's row with the widow's ovens, the tannery lane where nobody spoke to Blackwoods, the schoolyard where a boy named Henri Renard had once thrown mud at her father's apron and been laughed at for his accuracy. She had saved that boy tonight. She had saved him in half-points and hairlines, in letters no bigger than a fingernail, and if anyone asked her why, she would not be able to say it in words. It was all in the type. It had always been all in the type.

Vasseur stood at the edge of the lamplight, writing. She watched his pen move and understood that whatever sentence he was composing, it was the last composition of his life as an officer of France, and he was taking care with it. Officers take care with things. It is how they forgive themselves.

At eleven-forty the second pull dried. At midnight she collated. The pages went together like hands folding, signatures nesting in signatures, and when she held the finished ledger — the civic record of four hundred souls, weighted, official, smelling faintly of her own sweat over the ink — she found her eyes burning and refused to let them do anything about it.

Then she picked up the crate and carried it to the furnace.

* * *

The first plate went in like a betrayal.

It was her father's master plate, cut in 1889, the year she was born. She knew every nick and burr of it the way other daughters know their mothers' hands. When it touched the coals the lead did not flame; it sighed. It went soft, then bright, then shapeless, and the letters that had named this town for twenty-eight years ran together into a silver puddle that looked like nothing at all, like weather, like the moon fallen into the fire.

"That's enough," Vasseur said quietly. "One plate is evidence. Two is carelessness. No one audits a melted ingot."

"All of them or none." She was already lifting the next. "A colonel who finds one surviving plate finds the story. I didn't survive eleven years of this town by telling stories halfway."

He said nothing more. Perhaps he understood: this was not destruction. This was the only immortality her craft allowed — to become part of the metal again, waiting for the next hand, the next word. Her father had taught her that too. *Lead remembers nothing, Marguerite. That is its mercy.*

Plate after plate. The cellar grew hot and orange-lit and terrible, and she worked in her shirtsleeves with her hair coming loose, sweating like a foundryman's daughter, which was precisely what she was. Somewhere above them the clock in the square struck one.

The pounding started at ten past.

* * *

Fists on the street door, boots on stone, a voice she knew — Sergeant Malraux, who took his coffee sweet and his authority sweeter. "*Print shop! Open in the name of the column command!*

Vasseur moved faster than she'd seen him move since his wound. He was up the stairs two at a time, one-armed, folio abandoned, and she stayed exactly where she was because staying was the plan: the last plate went into the fire as the bolt drew back above her, and she raked the molten mass into the quench trough and stood in the roar of steam with her arms crossed like a woman interrupted at honest work.

Boots on the cellar stairs. Malraux filled the doorway, lantern high, two soldiers behind him, and behind them — impossibly, calmly — Vasseur, saying, "Sergeant, if you barge into my print works again I will have you printing burial details until spring," and Malraux stammering something about the captain being found *here*, in the cellar, at one in the morning, with a civilian—

"With the printer," Vasseur said. "Finalizing the conscription ledger you people demanded be ready for the six o'clock courier. Which it is. Signed, sealed." He produced the report from inside his coat — he had written it upstairs, in the four minutes between the knocking and the door, she would learn this later and not believe it — and held it out. "Counter-signature is on page two. Unless the sergeant wishes to explain to the courier why the column's own paperwork delayed the column."

Malraux read it. Men like Malraux always read slowly when they suspect and quickly when they're afraid, and he was afraid of Vasseur the way weather is afraid of stone. His eyes flicked to her, to the furnace, to the trough of dulling metal.

"The plates, Captain? There's supposed to be—"

"Melted down for the war effort, per directive fourteen. You signed the acknowledgment in October, Sergeant. Shall I find it?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Then Malraux clicked his heels, which was the sound of a man surrendering without admitting it, and they went up, and the door closed, and Marguerite discovered that her legs had been holding her by stubbornness alone for some time and no longer wished to discuss terms.

She sat down on the quench trough's edge. The last plate was gone. All of them. Twenty-eight years of her father's hands, dissolved into an ingot that could have been a candlestick or a bullet casing, and she put her face in her palms and laughed once, a hard short sound with nothing funny anywhere near it.

"You burned your report," she said. "I saw the folio. You wrote it fresh, tonight, in four minutes. What does it say?"

Vasseur crouched in front of her, and up close his face was gray with exhaustion and something else, something almost like peace.

"It says the ledger is authentic, verified against parish rolls, discrepancies attributable to clerical variance in the October census. It says the plates were destroyed under my direct supervision. It is signed Aurel Vasseur, Capitaine, and countersigned, God help me, by Sergeant Malraux, who believes every word of it because men believe what they are afraid to doubt." He paused. "In four hours the courier rides. In six, if the rumors hold, there will be no columns left to conscript anyone."

"And if the rumors are wrong?"

"Then I am finished, and you are finished, and eleven boys go to the front anyway." He stood, and offered her his good hand, and pulled her upright, and did not let go immediately, which neither of them remarked upon. "But the pages survived, Marguerite. Whatever comes, the pages survived."

Above them the square clock began to strike two, and far to the east — so faint it might have been wind, might have been thunder, might have been the world holding its breath before letting it out — came a sound like the horizon clearing its throat, and both of them turned toward the cellar's high window to listen.
