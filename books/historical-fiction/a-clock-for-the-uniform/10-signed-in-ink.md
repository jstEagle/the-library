## 10. Signed in Ink

The bells woke me before the light did.

Not the church's ordinary call to Mass — a ragged, tumbling peal, all the bells at once, the ones that had hung silent for years and the small cracked one in the town hall that only rang for fires. I lay on the cot behind the press room with my hands still black from last night's work, and I listened, and I understood before I let myself understand.

I went to the door in my father's coat. The square was filling with people in nightclothes and coats thrown over them, breath steaming, faces turned up toward the belfry as if the sound were weather. Old women. Children. Men who had spent four years making themselves small. And down the rue des Tanneurs came Madame Ferrand, the baker's widow, still wearing her apron, flour to her elbows, walking faster than I had ever seen her walk.

She saw me in the doorway and did not slow.

"It's done," she said, gripping my wrist through the sleeve of the coat. "They signed it in the night. A courier came through on the Paris road. Eleven o'clock yesterday, they signed it."

The bells kept falling over themselves. Somewhere a boy was crying without knowing why.

"The lists," I said. My voice came out flat, a proof pulled too lightly. "The new rolls — they were to be finalized—"

"Marguerite." She shook my wrist once, hard. "Count your sons. Go on. Count them."

I looked at the square. Luc Ferrand stood by the fountain with his hat in his hands, twenty-two years old and whole, arguing cheerfully with the Perrin boys about whose fault the war had been. Behind him, half the flagged names of the ledger, alive and unremarkable, standing in the cold morning light like men who had never been written down at all.

"They're all here," I said.

"Every one." Her eyes were wet and furious and triumphant all at once. "Every name some clerk in some office will look for this morning is either here, or was never on any list worth the paper." She squeezed my wrist again, then let go, and something passed across her face — not gratitude exactly. Gratitude would have been simpler. It was closer to recognition, the look you give a thing you have finally bothered to read. "Go wash your hands," she said. "You'll want clean hands today."

* * *

They did not come for the shop that day, or the next. There was no one left with the appetite for it. The occupation dissolved the way frost does — not an event but a warming, hour by hour, until you looked up and the square held only French voices again and the army trucks were a rumor on the south road.

Captain Aurel Vasseur came on the third morning.

He wore no insignia. That was the first thing I noticed — the tunic stripped bare, the collar plain, a man who had unmade himself overnight into no rank at all. He stood in the doorway of the shop with his cap in his hand, and behind him the morning lay pale and clean on the cobbles, and he looked older than the man who had seized the ledger five months ago, and younger too, in the way of someone who has finally stopped performing.

"I'm not reporting anywhere," he said, by way of greeting. "There's nowhere to report to. The commission collapsed; the paperwork will sort itself out for years. By the time anyone audits anything, I'll be—" He paused, chose the word carefully, as a compositor chooses a sort. "Elsewhere."

"Lyon?"

"Farther." He glanced past me into the shop, at the press under its dust sheet, at the type cabinets against the wall. His eyes found the foundry door, closed now, the furnace cold since that night. He did not ask what I had burned. He had watched me carry the plates to it himself; he had stood at the broken door with his report in his hand and lied to a colonel's face with the furnace glow still on him. Some things do not need asking twice.

"You could stay," I said. It surprised me, saying it. It shouldn't have. "A town needs a press. It will need one more than ever now — notices, prices, marriages, deaths. Ordinary ink for ordinary lives."

For a moment he considered it. I watched him consider it, honestly, the way he had read my lettering that first week — slowly, giving the thing its due. Then he shook his head.

"If I stay, I'm the officer who occupied your square," he said. "Ten years from now, some boy throws a stone, and he'll be right to. But if I go—" he lifted one shoulder, "—I'm just a man who passed through and left nothing behind but a letter."

"A letter."

"Ah." Almost a smile. He reached into the breast of his coat and produced it: a thin envelope, soft at the corners from much handling, addressed in his own careful hand to a village I did not know, near a city I had never seen. "The one I couldn't send. I told you about it once, in the dark, when I thought you weren't listening. You were always listening. It was very inconvenient."

"You never sent it because the censors would have read it."

"And now I'll deliver it myself. She deserves to hear it in a voice, not a stamp." He set it on the corner of my father's bench, squared its edges with two fingers, precisely, the gesture of a man who had learned neatness from watching other people work. "It isn't for you. But I wanted it to leave from here. From the bench where the truth got made, this winter." He straightened. "That's sentimental. Forgive me. Five months in a print shop and I've developed opinions about paper."

"What will you tell them? At home. When they ask what you did in the war."

He looked at the press for a long moment.

"I'll tell them I met a craftsman," he said, "who taught me the difference between following orders and doing the work. And that I failed the first test and passed the second, late, and paid full price." He put on his cap. At the door he stopped, half-turned. "Mademoiselle Blackwood. When the histories are written, if they ever trouble themselves with towns this size — no one will know your name. The ledger will simply say what it says. Whole families, present and accounted for. That's the cruelty of good forgery: it erases its own author."

"Then it's a good thing," I said, "that I'm not finished writing."

He held my eyes, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him, because he nodded once — the nod of one professional to another across an impossible distance — and walked out into the light, and took the south road, and did not look back. I am told he kept walking for eleven hundred kilometers, which is roughly the distance between duty and home, though he never wrote to say whether the arithmetic held.

* * *

The shop was quiet after he left, the particular quiet of a room that has decided to belong to someone. I swept the floor. I oiled the press. I opened every cabinet and went through the cases sort by sort, setting right what the soldiers had jumbled, and when I reached the bottom drawer of my father's bench — the one he had kept locked, that I had opened with his key the week of his funeral and never touched since — I took out the little box of new brass and the burnisher and sat down at the lamp as I had on the first night of this story, when the town whispered of armies and I belonged to my work because nowhere else would have me.

But I was not cutting a punch for a client's heading this time. I was not recutting a neighbor's name to save his life. I lined up the graver against a blank blank of type-metal, and I breathed the way he taught me — in through the nose, the hand riding the cut, the letter arriving out of the metal rather than being forced into it — and letter by letter I set the hardest string I had ever cut:

MARGUERITE BLACKWOOD.

Not the ledger's version. Not the cramped, apologetic entry the census clerk had made twenty years ago, *Blackwood, widow's household, trade: printer (unlicensed)*, filed where the cold entries live. This was my own hand, my own weight, each letter cut to sit beside its neighbors like a citizen among citizens.

The sun crossed the workshop floor and climbed the far wall while I worked. Down in the square Luc Ferrand was laughing at something; Madame Ferrand's oven had lit, and the smell of bread came up through the boards, ordinary and astonishing. The town was counting its sons and finding them all present, and nobody would ever know by whose hand, and that was correct. That was the work done right.

Near dusk I locked the finished sorts into a composing stick, justified the line, set it in the middle of a clean white sheet with room around it like a signature deserves, and pulled one proof.

The press took the impression the way my father's press always had — a sigh, a bite, a release. I lifted the sheet by its corners.

*MARGUERITE BLACKWOOD*, in ink still wet enough to shine, crisp and unhidden and entirely legible, on paper that belonged to no requisition, no ledger, no list. Proof that she existed. Proof that she had been here, all winter, holding the type.

I hung it on the wall above the bench, next to my father's first punch, and stood back in the lamplight to read it as many times as I liked.

Then I blew out the lamp, unlocked the front door, and turned the sign — freshly printed, my own design, the letters mine down to the serifs — to face the morning:

**OPEN.**
