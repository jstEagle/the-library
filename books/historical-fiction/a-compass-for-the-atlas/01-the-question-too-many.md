## 1. The Question Too Many

There is an art to being forgotten, and I had perfected it by the age of twenty-two. It requires nothing dramatic — no disguises, no midnight departures. You simply become the man who is always bent over his workbench, always slightly dull in conversation, always precisely where a clockmaker ought to be and never anywhere more interesting. People forget the boring the way they forget Tuesday.

I was counting on that, the morning I climbed the hill to the observatory with my tool bag over my shoulder and the November wind trying its best to take my ears off.

The master clock up there had been dying for three months. Old Hargrove, the current keeper, had sent word twice through the boy who swept the chandlery, and both times I had said what I always said: *When I can spare the afternoon.* The truth was that I could have spared the afternoon in September, but the observatory sat on the hill like a headstone, and going up there meant walking into the building where my father had spent the last years of his respectable life, before the voyage that unmade him and the river that swallowed the evidence. I am not a superstitious man. Clockwork teaches you that everything has a mechanism, everything a cause. But I had avoided that hill for six years, and avoidance, like any escapement, only holds so long before it lets the wheel turn.

Three months of a limping master clock was apparently all the town's patience would bear. So: the hill, the wind, the bag of tools, and me rehearsing dullness all the way up like an actor studying his lines.

* * *

Hargrove met me at the door with a lantern, though it was barely noon. He had gone grey and papery since I'd last seen him up close, which told you something about what keeping that place did to a man. He looked at me a moment too long, the way people do when they're searching a face for another face.

"Galloway," he said. "You're young Whitlock's boy, aren't you? The apprentice."

"Jude," I said. "Yes." Both true, neither complete. Whitlock had taken me in two years back, given me the bench by the window and my keep above the shop, and asked no questions, which is the finest quality a man can have.

"Your master said you've good hands."

"Master Whitlock is generous."

"He'd want to be. Come on, then. She's in the transit room." He called the clock *she*, as keepers do, and led me down the corridor where the smell of oil and cold stone took me somewhere I didn't want to go — six years back, a smaller hand inside my father's larger one, learning how a pallet should kiss the escape wheel. *Gently, Jude. A clock doesn't want force. It wants understanding.*

I put that voice away in whatever drawer I keep it in and got out my loupe.

The master clock was a fine piece even dying — a regulator with a deadbeat escapement and a mercury compensation pendulum, built to keep time well enough to set the stars by. It stood taller than I do, in its walnut case, and it ticked the way a sick man breathes: unevenly, with little catches, as though it were deciding between one second and the next.

"When did she start losing time?" I asked.

"Spring," Hargrove said. "Gradual, then worse. Some days she's near a minute slow by evening. Other days she runs almost true, then stumbles again. Like she can't make up her mind."

That was wrong in a way that made the back of my neck prickle, though I couldn't yet have said why. A worn pivot loses time steadily. A dirty escapement loses it erratically but predictably. This was something else — this was a machine being inconsistent on purpose, if a machine can be said to have purposes.

"I'll need the case open and a few hours," I said. "Nobody touches her while I work."

"Nobody touches her anyway," Hargrove said. "That's rather the trouble."

* * *

I worked through the afternoon with the shutters cracked for light and my sleeves rolled past my elbows, and if my hands remembered more than my mind wanted them to, well, hands are loyal creatures and keep old knowledge whether you ask them to or not. I cleaned the escape wheel tooth by tooth with a peg of soft wood. I checked the pivots under the loupe. And the longer I worked, the more the clock felt less like a patient and more like a puzzle someone had left half-assembled, though I pushed that thought down where I pushed everything else about this place.

Near four o'clock there were boots on the stair, and Hargrove's voice saying, "Down here, Miss Ashe, mind the low beam," and then a woman came into the transit room carrying a leather folio and an expression of brisk good cheer that I distrusted immediately, on principle, because cheerful people asking questions are rarely asking for your benefit.

"Census," she announced, opening the folio against the clock case's flank as though it were a desk placed there for her convenience. "Miriam Ashe, assistant to the district registrar. You'll be Jude Galloway, apprentice to Whitlock the horologist, lodging above the shop on Cordwainer Row?"

"That's me," I said, and kept my eyes on the escapement.

"Born here in town?"

"Born upriver. Harrow Bend."

"Hm." Her pencil moved. "Parents?"

"There's a question I don't get often." I smiled at the gears. Routine. Dull. A man with nothing behind his face but gear trains.

"Occupations of parents, ideally," she said mildly. "It's for the rolls. They like completeness."

"My father's dead. My mother lives with her sister, downriver. Kingsbridge way." Each sentence arrived pre-tested, smoothed, worn smooth by six years of not quite lying. My mother did live with her sister, when anyone official was watching. That my mother's sister had been coached until she could say *my dear Ruth Hale* without blinking was a detail the census did not require.

"And you came to town when?"

"Two years ago. For the apprenticeship."

Her pencil paused — just a beat, the length of a dropped stitch — and then resumed. "Whitlock speaks well of you. Says you have a feel for complicated work."

"Master Whitlock is kind."

"Mm. He also says you came with excellent references for a boy of twenty, from a shop nobody in Harrow Bend seems able to name."

The pendulum ticked. Somewhere overhead a shutter banged in the wind. I set down my peg of wood with great care, as though the fate of the world depended on gentle handling, and gave her the smile I gave customers who wanted their grandfather clocks cleaned by Friday.

"Small towns keep poor records, Miss Ashe. That's most of why I left."

Something flickered across her face — not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a fisherman feeling weight on the line and choosing not to strike too soon. She turned a page in her folio, and I saw that the page was not blank, was not even fresh, was covered already in pencil entries in her small upright hand.

"Well," she said. "You're in good order, Mr. Galloway. Lodging, trade, age, origin — all accounted for." She blotted the line neatly. Then, without looking up, in exactly the same tone she'd used for all of it: "And the other Galloways? Shall I mark the family extinguished, or merely relocated?"

The tool in my hand was a screwdriver, small, brass-handled, entirely harmless. I have never in my life been so aware of holding a thing.

"I'm sorry?" I said. Which was itself a lie, and I suspect we both knew it.

"The other Galloways." She looked up then, and her eyes were brown and steady and entirely without malice, which somehow made it worse. "It's odd. The old parish register lists a family of four. Father a keeper here, at this very observatory, as it happens. Mother. A daughter. And the records simply stop. No deaths filed, no removals, no marriages. A whole household of people who walked off the page. As a census woman, I find that—" she tapped her pencil against the folio, twice— "untidy."

"Names attract coincidence," I said. "Galloway isn't so rare."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Though how many Galloways does Harrow Bend have, do you suppose? Enough that one might be mistaken for another's son?" She closed the folio, and the closing had a finality to it that no casual question ever carries. "You're right, of course. Small towns keep poor records. It's remarkable what falls through them. Whole families, sometimes."

She wished me good afternoon. She thanked Hargrove for his courtesy. Her boots went up the stairs, unhurried, and the door thumped, and the wind took her wherever the wind takes census takers, which I had always assumed was nowhere important.

I stood there a long moment with my screwdriver and my heart going harder than any escapement I'd ever serviced.

Then I did what I always do with trouble: I put my head down and went back to work. Because that is the whole trick of survival — you keep your hands busy and your mouth dull and eventually the questions move along to some other unlucky household.

Except.

Except that when I finally got the movement eased forward on its bed, angling the plates to reach the third wheel where the wear was worst, I found the trouble. Not wear. Not dirt. A single tooth on the escape wheel, filed — subtly, expertly, a hair's depth off profile, so the pallet caught late every ninth swing and stole a fraction of a second each time. Done by someone who understood exactly what they were doing. Done slowly. Done so it would look like age.

My father taught me, when I was eleven, that a sabotaged wheel can hide from any eye but the patient ones. *Anything a craftsman alters,* he said, *he alters in the language of the craft. Learn to read the language, and nothing stays hidden.*

I sat back on my heels in the cold transit room with the dusk coming on and the sick clock ticking out its stolen seconds, and I thought: *the other Galloways.*

And I thought: *someone has been asking after us before today.*

And beneath both thoughts, quieter and colder than either, turning like a great wheel far down in the works of things I could not see:

*Who else remembers us — and what do they want found?*

Outside, the first flakes of the year came down onto the observatory hill, small and dry and early, and the river below had begun, weeks ahead of any honest winter, to think about ice.
