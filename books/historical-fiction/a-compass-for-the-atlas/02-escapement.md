## 2. Escapement

The master clock of the Harrow Observatory had been dying for eleven months, and it had chosen to do so with all the dignity of a drunkard falling down a stair. Every night at four minutes past two it lost time — never the same amount, never in the same way, so that the astronomers had taken to calling their star charts *approximations*, a word that in an observatory is roughly equivalent to heresy.

I was sent for because I was cheap, young enough to climb, and — though the observatory's steward did not know this — because my hands had been taught by the same man who built the thing.

My father, Elias Galloway, keeper of this very clock from the year I was six until the year the river took him. Nine years now. Long enough that when I climbed the tower stair and smelled the oil and cold iron, I did not weep. I only noticed that the smell was exactly the same, and that noticing felt worse than weeping would have.

The steward, a Mr. Pell, met me at the base of the stair with a lantern and an expression suggesting I was being entrusted with the crown jewels rather than invited to fix a machine.

"You'll be careful with it," he said. "It was built by Galloway. The Galloway. There'll never be another like him."

"No," I said. "There certainly won't."

He didn't know me. The Galloway name had gone into the river along with the man, and what surfaced afterward was Hale — my mother's maiden name, dredged up like a plank from a wreck and used to build a smaller, quieter house. Jude Hale, apprentice clockmaker. A boy with no history, which in a town like Harrow is the next best thing to a fortune.

"Mind the chime barrel," Pell said. "And mind yourself. Old man Voss fell from the third landing in '39 and they say he spoke Latin on the way down."

"I'll try to limit myself to English."

* * *

A great clock is honest in a way people are not. It cannot pretend to be well. It can only tell you, over and over, precisely where it hurts, and your whole craft consists of listening.

I opened the case and let the mechanism run down, which took the better part of an hour — you do not stop a master clock by force any more than you stop a sermon by coughing — and while I waited I cleaned, oiled, and listened. The tick came up through the floorboards and into my boots, and somewhere in that rhythm was a hitch, a small swallowed stumble every fourth swing of the pendulum, like a speaker losing his place in a memorized speech.

*There,* my father's voice said in my skull. *Do you hear it, Jude? Don't look for where it's loud. Look for where it's quiet.*

He had taught me on a bracket clock no bigger than a hatbox, my feet on a stool so I could reach the works, his breath smelling of pipe smoke and river water. *A fault hides in silence. Any fool can find a noise.*

I found the noise first, as it happened, because I was not nine years old anymore. The fourth wheel was the culprit's neighbor; the fault itself sat in the escapement, which is the heart of any clock worth the name — the little gate that lets power out one tooth at a time, turning a coiled rage of spring or a hanging weight into patience. My father used to say the escapement was the only honest philosopher ever built: it took everything the world pressed upon it and released it in equal, measured portions.

Except this one had been taught to stammer.

I got my loupe out and looked, and the first thing I saw was so small I nearly laughed. A tooth on the escape wheel — the third from where the pallet dropped — had been filed. Not broken. Filed. Someone had taken a needle file to hardened steel and drawn perhaps half a hundredth of an inch off the face of that single tooth, then polished the mark away until only a jeweler's eye could find it. The wheel would run for weeks between failures. It would limp, recover, limp again. It would drive astronomers to Latin profanity and clockmakers to drink.

You cannot file a tooth like that by accident. You cannot do it without knowing exactly what you are ruining and exactly how slowly it will ruin. This was not damage. This was *authorship*.

I sat back on my heels in the cold tower and considered the possibility that Mr. Pell's crown jewels had been cursed.

Then I looked at the pallet stones, because if the wheel had been tampered with, the pallets would tell me whether the saboteur had been thorough. They had. The entry pallet rode a hair too deep — a fraction so slight that the clock would keep fair time all day and lose it all at night, when the cold thickened the oil and the fault grew teeth. And the adjustment screw that would have corrected it hadn't slipped. Its retaining varnish had been softened and re-set. Deliberately. Neatly.

Somebody had done this work with warm wax, steady hands, and all the time in the world.

* * *

Here is what I would like to tell you: that my first thought was for the observatory, for poor Mr. Pell and his approximations, for the outrage of a public instrument abused. That I thought of the magistrate, of culprits, of justice.

My first thought was of my father's hands.

Because there is a signature in fine work, the way there is in handwriting, and most people never learn to read it. The filing on that tooth had been done with a draw stroke — pulling the file, letting its weight do the cutting — and finished with a burnish so the fault would hide even under a lens. It is a technique for invisible mending. It is also, if you are going to ruin something invisibly, the correct way to do it. And I knew exactly one person who worked that way, who taught it to me across a hatbox clock with a pipe in his teeth: *Slow hand, Jude. The file knows when you're hurrying.*

My father had vanished on the eighteenth of October, 1838, three days into an expedition he had helped outfit, leaving behind a ledger with money missing from it, a widow with a face like a closed door, and a son who spent the following decade learning to make clocks so that he could spend the rest of his life listening to things tick honestly.

The town had settled the matter years ago, the way towns do — not with a verdict but with a shrug that hardens into fact. Galloway stole the funds, Galloway ran, the river took him, good riddance. My mother had never argued. Argument requires speaking of him, and she did not speak of him, and Ada — Ada had been four, and so kept alive instead the other father, the one made of pipe smoke and stool-top lessons, the hero, and no one had the heart to take either version from them.

And here in the dark above Harrow, in the works of a clock my father had built with these very hands, someone had committed a crime requiring those exact hands' skill.

I want to be precise about what I felt, because it was not grief, or not only. It was the sensation of a floorboard giving slightly underfoot — that sickening half-inch of travel before it holds. The past I had buried was supposed to stay buried. Instead it had apparently climbed the tower stair ahead of me, taken up a needle file, and begun keeping appointments with my future.

Because think of it. Who sabotages an observatory clock? Not a madman — madmen break things loudly. You sabotage a clock that governs star observations, transit timings, the town's sense of what is true. You do it slowly, deniably, so the failure looks like age. Whoever did this wanted the observatory discredited without ever being blamed, and had the patience of ice.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, wearing my father's face — someone had hidden something in this clock, and built the fault as a lock. A dying clock summons a repairman. A repairman opens the case. A case opened by skilled hands reveals skilled hands' work, and skilled hands go looking further.

I am aware how that sounds. I am aware I was constructing a romance out of a filed tooth, because the alternative was that my father had been a thief and a saboteur both, and I had already spent nine years rationing myself on that theory and found it didn't keep.

But there was one way to know. My father loved hiding places the way other men love drink — moderately in company, excessively alone. A loose back panel on the hatbox clock, where he kept peppermints. A false bottom in his tool chest. Once, memorably, a compartment behind the strike train of the church clock that turned out to hold nothing but a note reading *Found you* — left, I realized much later, deliberately, for whichever apprentice finally went looking. He hid things the way some men whistle. Constantly. Cheerfully.

So I did what he trained me to do. I stopped looking where it was loud and started looking where it was quiet.

I pulled the chime barrel forward on its arbor — carefully, because a chime barrel weighs what regret weighs and swings accordingly — and set it on the bench cloth, and shone the lantern into the space behind it, expecting dust, dead moths, the usual congregation of forgotten towers.

Instead I found that the back plate was wrong.

Not damaged. *Wrong.* The bluing of the steel was slightly deeper in one rectangle the size of a hymnal, and the retaining screws were of a later vintage than the rest of the plate — my father's own habit, mixing hardware, because he said God made screws in batches and He never threw a good one out. A seam ran around that rectangle so fine my thumbnail found it before my eye did. A sealed chamber, built into the heart of his masterwork, behind the part of the clock least likely to be disturbed.

I sat there a long while with my thumb on the seam, and the pendulum swung above me patient as a heartbeat, and the tower creaked the way old buildings do when they are pretending not to listen.

Whatever was behind that plate had been waiting nine years for somebody with my father's training to come looking. Which meant the sabotage might not be sabotage at all. It might be a letter, posted eleven months late, addressed to whoever in this town still knew enough to be dangerous.

Which raised the question I did not want to ask, sitting alone in the dark with my father's ghost ticking around me: dangerous to whom?

I got my drivers out. I told myself I'd sleep on it, that a man should never open strange compartments at the end of a long day, that Pell would be pacing the stair by now wondering if I'd spoken any Latin yet.

I unscrewed the first screw anyway. Of course I did. I'm my father's son — the file knows when you're hurrying, and so does the blood.

Behind that plate, folded in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, was a book. A ledger. And on its cover, in handwriting I would have known blind, in the dark, at the bottom of the river —

Well. I knew then whose letter it was. What I didn't know — couldn't know, not yet — was why my mother had changed our name, and what she was so determined none of us should ever read.

That answer was waiting for me across town, on Ferry Lane, behind a door with HALE painted on it in fresh white letters.

I put the ledger inside my coat, against my chest, where I could feel it with every heartbeat, and went down the stair without speaking a word of Latin.
