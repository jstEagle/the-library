## 3. The House on Ferry Lane

My mother's house sat at the crooked end of Ferry Lane, where the street gave up on geometry and let the river decide. It was a narrow thing, two rooms stacked on two, with a garden out back that Ruth Galloway — forgive me, Ruth *Hale* — kept in winter cabbage and strict order. I had learned to knock the way the neighbors did: twice, briskly, as though I were selling buttons.

Ada answered before my second knuckle fell. She was fourteen going on prophecy, with our father's dark hair and a way of looking at me like I was a letter she'd been expecting.

"You're late," she said.

"The clock was worse than advertised."

"Everything is." She stepped aside to let me in, and the warmth of the kitchen rolled out to meet me — coal smoke, onions, the particular smell of a house where someone had been singing earlier and stopped when they heard footsteps on the lane. That last part wasn't cooking. That last part was policy.

Ruth stood at the stove with her back to me, which was how she delivered most of her greetings. "You look thin," she said to the onions.

"Good evening to you too, Mother."

"Hale," she said quietly, without turning. "If you must."

I hung my coat on the peg that didn't exist officially, since officially no son of hers lived in this town at all. Officially I was a boarder at Merrick's workshop, an orphan of no particular geography, apprenticed and unattached. Officially, the Hale family consisted of a widow and her daughter who kept to themselves, paid their rates, and caused no one any trouble. Unofficially, unofficially was the whole architecture of our lives — every beam and joist of it made of things unsaid, load-bearing silence all the way up.

We ate. Ada talked enough for the table and then some: about the schoolmistress, about a boy who'd fallen through the wharf ice up to his knees and come up laughing, about the ferry schedule pinned to the post office door. She had the ferry schedule memorized. She had everything memorized, our Ada, especially anything that involved leaving.

"When the spring ferry runs," she said, "you can see the paddle-wheels from the bluffs. Elias says they throw up spray like a swan arguing."

"Elias," said Ruth, "says a great deal."

"He's not so bad."

"No one is so bad," Ruth agreed, "until they are."

That's my mother. A woman who could turn a compliment into a warning without lifting her voice. She carved her cabbage with surgical attention, and when Ada drifted — as she always drifted, always, always — toward the subject of Father, toward the voyage, toward the hero's shape she'd built him into and slept under like a quilt, Ruth stood, collected plates that were not yet empty, and washed them.

"There was a man came round the shop today," I said, mostly to test the air.

Ruth's hands did not stop moving. But they slowed, a half-second, the way a clock slows when something catches in the train. I've been raised among escapements. I know what hesitation looks like in machinery, and in mothers.

"A census taker," I went on. "Ashe. Miriam Ashe."

"I met her Tuesday," Ada offered brightly. "She asked ever so many questions. Where Papa was buried. Whether we had—"

"And what did you tell her?" Ruth asked. Not sharply. Worse than sharply. Gently.

"That he was lost on the river expedition," Ada said, chin lifting, daring anyone to make it shameful. "Because he was. Because he's coming back, or if he isn't, he died *doing something*, which is more than most people here can say for how they'll go."

The tap dripped once. Outside, a dog barked itself out against nothing.

"You told a stranger," Ruth said, "that our name was Galloway."

"I told her Papa's name. It's not the same as ours. You've made very sure of that." Ada turned to me, wanting backup, wanting the brother who fixed things to fix this. "Tell her it matters. Tell her names matter — you work with engraving plates, you'd know."

Names matter. I thought of the census book with its ruled lines, each line a small gallows waiting for the right word. I thought of the observatory clock upstairs in its tower, ticking away the hours until spring, and wondered — for the first time, but not for the last — whether time was really on anyone's side or just running out at everyone equally.

"It matters," I said carefully, "to be boring. To be the dullest family in the parish. Boring families don't get visited."

But even as I said it, I heard the flaw in the mechanism, the filed tooth. We had never once been boring. We had only been quiet, and quiet is not the same animal at all.

* * *

From the census book of Miriam Ashe, this fourth day of interviews:

I will set down honestly what I am, since honesty is the one tool I have brought to this town that cannot be confiscated.

They call me the census taker. The title fits me the way a borrowed coat fits — well enough to pass the door. In truth I keep records for Magistrate Corven, and the record I am keeping now has eleven years of dust on it and one loose thread, and the thread is named Galloway.

I knew the father, you understand. Not well. I was nineteen when the *Meridian* went downriver with the expedition, and I remember standing on the landing watching the boats go, and I remember Elias Crane's father on the dock counting strongboxes aboard, and I remember — later, when the scandal broke — how quickly everyone agreed on a villain. Agreement comes cheap when the accused cannot answer back.

I do not know if Nathaniel Galloway stole that money. That is precisely why I am here. Corven wants the file closed; closed either way, he is indifferent to which. But I have spent three weeks in this town asking dull questions behind dull questions, and I have found a widow and a daughter living under a name that belongs to neither of them, and a son who does not exist anywhere on paper between his apprenticeship papers and his mother's rent receipts. Innocent people are sometimes careless. Guilty people are sometimes careful. But nobody is this careful unless they are hiding, and nobody hides this thoroughly over a theft they believe was committed by a dead man.

Unless they do not believe the dead man took anything at all.

Tonight I mean to knock on the door of the house on Ferry Lane with the book open in my hands, because there is a kind of question you can only ask across a threshold, in the cold, with ink drying on your fingers, when everyone can see exactly what is being written down and no one can pretend afterward that it was not.

I take no pleasure in it. I want that recorded too. There is a boy in that house — grown now, but still a boy around the eyes — whose hands shook when I asked him about the other Galloways, and he lied to me so badly that I knew immediately the lie was love and not guilt. You do not get to be my age in this trade without learning to read the difference.

Still. A thread is a thread. And somewhere at the end of it is the truth about a man who taught his daughter to say his name like a flag and taught his son to swallow it like a stone, and both teachings came from the same silence, and that silence began the day the money went missing.

* * *

Jude again, and the night going badly.

I stayed past supper, mending the latch on the pantry door because my hands needed employment or they'd start talking on their own. Ruth banked the fire. Ada went to bed reluctantly, pausing on the stairs to inform the room that she was *not* asleep yet and could still hear conversation, should anyone wish to have an interesting one.

Nobody wished.

"She's going to bury us all with that mouth," Ruth said when Ada's door shut.

"She's a child."

"She's a child carrying a lit lantern through a powder store, and she doesn't know what the powder is because we never told her." Ruth sat down across from me, and for a moment she looked her age and then some — looked like a woman who had been holding a door shut against weather for eleven years and was beginning to wonder what her shoulder was worth. "Jude. What did this Ashe woman ask you? Exactly. Word for word."

"'The other Galloways.' Those were her words. Whether I knew them."

"And you said?"

"That I didn't."

She nodded slowly, the way you nod at a bill you expected. "Then she already knows," Ruth said. "A real census clerk would have written your answer down and moved along. One that keeps asking has stopped taking answers and started checking them."

"How long have we got?"

"That depends on what she's selling and who sent her." Ruth rose and went to the window, standing to the side of it the way she always did, never square in the glass — another habit I'd mistaken for eccentricity and understood suddenly, stupidly, only now. "Your father used to stand like that," she said, before she could stop herself. Then she pressed her lips together, and whatever else was climbing up out of her was pushed back down where it had lived since before Ada could walk.

"Mother. If someone official is asking — if it ever comes to a choice between the name and the girl —"

"Don't." The word came out flat as a struck bell. "Don't build that choice in your head. Choices like that arrive only for people who've already imagined them."

Which was, I admit, excellent advice that I had already failed to follow.

I left shortly after. She walked me to the door and held my sleeve a moment longer than a landlady would, longer than a stranger named Hale had any cause to, and said, "Come Sunday. Bring nothing. Knock twice."

"Like always."

"Like always," she said, and closed the door, and I stood a moment on the step listening to the bar slide home, that honest wooden sound, the sound of a family bolting itself against the world for one more night.

I was halfway down Ferry Lane when I heard it behind me.

Knuckles on wood. Twice — but not the way I knocked. Slower. Deliberate. The knock of a person who intends to wait for an answer and has already written the question down.

I turned in the dark. At the end of the lane, lamplight spilled from the doorway of my mother's house, and Miriam Ashe stood on the step with her census book open against her forearm, pen already wet, and Ruth Hale — Ruth Galloway, my mother, whatever her name was by morning — had begun, visibly, terribly, to open the door.
