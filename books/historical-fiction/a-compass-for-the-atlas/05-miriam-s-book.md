## 5. Miriam's Book

People imagine a census taker's work is dull arithmetic — so many souls, so many hearths, so many pigs. They are not entirely wrong. I have counted pigs in three counties now, and I can report that pigs have no interest in being counted and neither, mostly, do their owners. But the arithmetic is only the surface of it. Underneath, every name on my page is either a door or a wall, and my trade is learning which is which.

Jude Galloway was a door pretending very hard to be a wall.

I knew it from the first afternoon at the observatory, when I asked him his name and he answered too quickly, the way a man answers when he has rehearsed the answer. *Jude Galloway, apprentice clockmaker.* He said it smoothly enough, but smoothness itself is a kind of tell. Most people fumble their names. They cough, they correct themselves, they spell things out apologetically. He poured his out like water from a jug that had been filled that morning with exactly this in mind.

And then there was the matter of his hands. A census taker learns to read hands the way a farrier reads hooves. His were clean at the fingertips and black in the creases, oil worked deep into the knuckle-folds — an honest craftsman's hands. But he kept them folded while he spoke to me, tucked away like evidence.

So when I left the observatory that day, I did not go home. I went to the town records office, where old Mr. Pell keeps the registers, and I asked him, in my most bored clerical voice, whether any Galloways had ever been born in this parish.

Pell blew dust off a ledger older than both of us put together and said, "Galloway? There was the keeper. Elias Galloway. Observatory man. Went off on that river expedition, oh — seven years back. Never came home."

"And his family?"

"Gone." Pell said it with the finality of a man closing a drawer. "Wife and daughter cleared out not long after. Shame, what happened. Good family, before."

"Before what?"

Pell looked at me over his spectacles with the mild rebuke of a man who has lived long enough to know which questions ruin a quiet afternoon. "Before the money went missing," he said, and turned back to his ledger, and that was the end of our conversation as far as he was concerned.

It was not the end of mine.

* * *

I should explain how I came to be standing in a river town in November with a magistrate's commission folded inside my census book, because I have found that people trust you more once they understand you are not merely nosy but officially nosy.

The expedition of 1840 was funded by one Ambrose Vane, shipping magnate, pillar of the provincial capital, owner of a house with more chimneys than some villages have roofs. Seven men went downriver to chart the upper reaches. One man — Elias Galloway, keeper of the observatory, hired for his instruments and his mathematics — was entrusted with the purse. Somewhere between the second rapids and the winter camp, four hundred pounds of expedition funds vanished, along with Galloway himself, swept away in the spring breakup, they said, though the timing suited everyone's suspicions beautifully. Dead men make convenient thieves.

Vane's company absorbed the loss without visible distress, the expedition's charts were published under Vane's name, and everyone agreed it was a tragedy and stopped discussing it. Everyone except Magistrate Corven, who had been a young clerk on the original inquiry and never liked the arithmetic. Four hundred pounds does not simply evaporate, Corven told me last spring, in his office that smelled of ink and cold tea. Money moves. It has weight. Follow the weight.

Corven could not follow it himself — his jurisdiction ended at the county line, and Vane's friendships began well above it. So he sent me instead, with a census book as cover, because nobody guards their tongue against a woman counting pigs. People say extraordinary things to census takers. They assume we are harmless, and then they assume we are forgettable, and they are wrong twice.

For six months I had followed the vanished money upriver and downstream, through ferry manifests and bank drafts and one memorably drunken shipping clerk who talked himself into a cell. The trail led, always, back toward Vane. And every thread needed an anchor, and the anchor was the Galloway family — specifically, whatever Ruth Galloway knew about her husband's accounts, and specifically, specifically, whether she still held any papers.

Which meant finding her. And the town insisted no such person existed.

* * *

I knocked on the door of the house on Ferry Lane on the fourth evening of December, with the ice groaning out on the river like a ship changing its mind.

A girl opened it — fourteen or fifteen, dark-haired, with her father's quick grey eyes and none of her father's caution. Ada Hale, according to my book. I had already written the name. I had written it knowing it was false, which is a peculiar sensation, rather like signing a letter you intend to burn.

"Mrs. Hale?" I asked, and the girl called over her shoulder, "Ma, it's the census woman again," in the aggrieved tone girls reserve for officialdom.

Ruth Hale came to the door wiping flour from her hands, and I watched her take me in — my book, my pen, my face — and I watched her decide, in the space of half a breath, to lie to me. It was almost admirable. She had clearly done it many times and gotten good at it.

"You've got us already," she said pleasantly. "My husband and daughter and myself. You were here in October."

"I did," I agreed. "I find I've made an error, though. Clerical. Happens more than you'd think." I opened the book to the page I had prepared and turned it toward her, and let her read what I had written beneath her false household: *See annexed memorandum, R. v. inquiry into disappearance of expedition funds, 1840.*

The color left her face in an orderly fashion, top down, like water draining from a basin. To her credit, she did not slam the door. She stood very straight and said, quietly, "You'd better come in. Not in front of Ada."

We settled in the kitchen, and I will record here, for the sake of honesty if nothing else, that I did not enjoy what came next. People suppose that investigators relish these moments — the unveiling, the fear. Some do. I have never been one of them. Fear in a kitchen smells like fear anywhere, and it sits in the room with you like a third person who won't leave.

"Mrs. Hale," I said. "Or Mrs. Galloway. We'll come to which."

She sat with her hands flat on the table, the way people do when they are holding themselves down.

"In 1840 your husband took charge of four hundred pounds of expedition funds belonging to Ambrose Vane. In the spring of 1841 those funds were missing and your husband was dead. You changed your name within the year. You have lived seven years under it, which means seven years of falsified parish entries, false registrations, a false schooling record for your daughter. That is not shame, Mrs. Galloway. That is a crime, and it compounds annually, like interest."

"He didn't steal anything." She said it fast, and then caught herself, and I saw the strange thing happen behind her eyes — the thing I had come three hundred miles hoping to see. Not a wife defending a husband. Something more complicated. She believed he was guilty, and she defended him anyway, and the two beliefs ground against each other audibly.

"That's not what you believe," I said gently. "That's what you say."

Her jaw tightened. "You don't know anything about it."

"I know the sums don't work." I laid it out for her, because Corven had taught me that frightened people can hold onto numbers when they cannot hold onto hope. "I know Vane drew a draft against the expedition account two weeks after the loss was reported — a draft for nearly the full amount, routed through a shell house in the capital. I know the expedition's own quartermaster signed an affidavit in '42 and withdrew it a month later, around the time he came into ownership of a boat he could not afford. I know your husband kept meticulous books — every keeper does; it's the temperament of the trade — and meticulous books are the one thing in this affair that has never surfaced. Papers burn, Mrs. Galloway. But sometimes they're kept. Sometimes they're kept somewhere clever."

"Somewhere clever," she repeated. Her voice had gone thin.

"A clock, perhaps," I said. "An observatory clock. A man who trusts mechanisms more than men might choose such a place."

I had gone too far, and I knew it even as the words left me. Her hands came off the table and folded together hard, knuckles white, and she looked at me with something worse than fear — recognition. "Who sent you?"

"A magistrate. Corven. He wants the truth, Mrs. Galloway, and I want you to understand something plainly, because I am going to say it only once." I leaned forward. "There are two ways this ends. In one of them, Corven's inquiry finds you by other means — and there are other means, we are closer than you think — and your daughter learns everything in a courtroom, and the falsified records are prosecuted, and the name Galloway is dragged through the mud of two scandals instead of one, guilty or innocent. In the other, you cooperate. Testimony, papers, whatever you hold. And Corven, who is a fair man in an unfair year, will see that the false names were survival, not fraud, and lose the paperwork accordingly. He has that discretion. I'm offering you the door before someone else offers you the wall."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire settling and, beneath it, faint as a pulse, the ice shifting on the river.

"My son," she said finally. "The clockmaker. Does Jude know? What you are?"

"Not yet."

"Then I'll thank you to keep it that way until I decide what he must hear." She rose, and there was iron in it now, the particular iron of mothers. "Ada thinks her father walked on water. She was eight years old when he left. Whatever the truth is, Mr.—" she paused, corrected herself, "—Miss Ashe, I have spent seven years choosing which truth she could survive. Don't you dare spend one afternoon undoing that."

"It isn't my intention to undo it," I said, packing my book. "It's my intention to finish it. There's a difference, and I suspect you'll see it before the ice goes out."

At the door she stopped me with one question, quiet enough that the girl in the next room couldn't have heard it.

"If you find what you're looking for," Ruth Galloway said, "and it proves he was innocent — all these years, all these lies — what then? What do you say to a family that buried him guilty?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I genuinely did not know.

"I'd say I'm sorry," I told her. "Sorry doesn't restore seven years. But it's a start, and so is the truth."

* * *

I found Jude Galloway the next morning at the observatory, of course, sleeves rolled, head inside the master clock like a surgeon at the table. He came out when I announced myself, wiping his hands, and gave me the polite blankness of a man who has decided I am weather.

"Mr. Galloway," I said, sitting down uninvited on the keeper's stool — his father's stool, though he may not yet know I knew that. "Let us skip the part where you deny knowing the Hales of Ferry Lane, since you visited them Tuesday evening and again Thursday, and since your mother has already spoken with me. Let us also skip the part where you ask how I know. Instead, let us discuss what your mother would not discuss, which is your father's ledger, and where a careful man might keep one, and why the observatory clock has been failing in ways that suggest a hand at work — a skilled hand, a grieving hand, perhaps a hand that wanted the works opened and examined by exactly one sort of person. Someone who could recognize what he found."

He had gone very still. Stillness, in my experience, is what honesty looks like when it arrives without permission.

"The magistrate reopening the case," I said, "has offered terms. Cooperate — testimony, papers, whatever surfaces — and your family walks free of the falsified records. Refuse, and the law takes its ordinary course, which is to say the course most convenient to the men it serves. Your mother knows. Your sister doesn't. And you—" I closed my book and stood. "You are the loose thread I've been pulling at since October, Jude Galloway. So I'll leave you with a single question to keep you company among all those gears. If your father hid proof of his innocence inside that clock — proof someone sabotaged the mechanism to hide — how long do you intend to let it tick in the dark?"

I left him there with his father's clock, and the sound of its uneven heartbeat followed me out into the cold, and I confess that as I walked down through the observatory yard I felt less like a census taker than like a woman who had just set something winding — something that would run, now, whether I willed it or not.
