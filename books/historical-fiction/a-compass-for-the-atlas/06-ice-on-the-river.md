## 6. Ice on the River

The winter of 1847 came down from the north like a creditor. No gentle first frost, no polite scrim of rime on the pump handles — it arrived in a single night with a wind that sounded like the whole county clearing its throat, and by morning the river had gone still and grey and thoughtful in the way that rivers do just before they stop being rivers at all.

I noticed it first at the observatory, because I noticed everything at the observatory that autumn; I had begun to live at the level of the clock's pulse. Old Meridian — I had taken to calling her that, though the town had christened her something dignified like *Regulator No. 3* — kept her time through anything, but even she seemed to tick harder in the cold, as if she knew what the ice on the river meant for those of us downstream of our own histories.

The ferry company posted its notice on the third of December, nailed to the board outside Harrow's General Provision where every announcement in town went to be ignored. I read it standing next to two millhands who were reading it too, all three of us silent, which is how men in this country read anything that frightens them.

*Due to early ice, the steam packet *Larkspur* will make one final crossing before ice-out. One (1) berth remaining, first-class passage to the capital, guaranteed departure upon receipt of payment. Enquire at the ferry office. No exceptions will be made.*

One berth. The words sat there in official ink, tidy as a filed tooth.

The millhands looked at each other and then at me, and one of them said, "You'd have to be rich or desperate," and the other said, "Or both," and they laughed and went back to the mill, because for them it was only an oddity, a curiosity of weather and commerce. For me it was a door slamming shut at the end of a corridor I hadn't known I was standing in.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody had spoken aloud yet but everybody in my family must have been doing privately, scratching sums into the margins of their days: Miriam Ashe had told me plainly that the magistrate was reopening the case. She had told me, with that terrible gentleness of hers, that summons would be served when the roads permitted — and the roads did not permit, and would not, until spring. But the river permitted one more crossing. One. Before the ice closed like a ledger snapping shut.

Whoever took that berth left before the magistrate could reach them. Whoever stayed, stayed for whatever came.

I stood there long enough that Harrow himself came out to ask if I wanted anything, and I bought a spool of wire I didn't need, because that is what I do instead of thinking: I buy small metal objects and go home and work with my hands until the thinking gives up and leaves.

* * *

That night I walked out to Ferry Lane. I told myself it was to check on Mother's stove flue, which was a lie so thin you could read a newspaper through it, but lies have to be good enough only for the liar, and I am an expert audience for my own.

The house was lit. I could see them through the frost-ferned window before they saw me: Ada at the table with a book — she was always with a book, our Ada, hoarding other people's lives the way I hoarded tools — and Ruth across from her, sewing, or pretending to sew. The lamp between them made a small warm island in the blue dark, and I stood in the cold like a fool and watched my family through glass, because apparently I had become the kind of man who did that.

Ada saw me first. She always did. The door opened before I knocked.

"You've been standing out there four minutes," she said. "Your ears are red as sealing wax."

"Flue," I said, holding up the toolbox I'd brought for verisimilitude.

Ruth looked at the toolbox, then at me, and said nothing, which from Ruth was a full paragraph.

I inspected the flue. It needed nothing. I made it need something for twenty minutes, tapping and scraping, while the women sat in the lamplight and let me perform my errand without comment. Then I could stall no longer, and I sat down, and I said it.

"The ferry posted notice. Last crossing. One berth left."

The needle in Ruth's hand stopped mid-stitch. Ada put her book face-down on the table, keeping her place in someone else's story because ours had gotten interesting.

"When?" Ruth asked.

"They sail when the berth sells. Could be tomorrow. Could be a fortnight. Ice-out after that is April at the earliest, and Miriam says—" I stopped myself, but not fast enough.

"Misssss Ashe says," Ada finished, sweetly, "that the magistrate's summons comes when the roads open." She was nineteen and had grown up learning to hear the things our family declined to say, and she had gotten very good at saying them herself. "So whoever buys that berth buys their way clear. And there's exactly one berth." She looked around the table slowly, counting us, doing the arithmetic aloud that we had each been doing in silence. "And there are three Galloways. Or Hales. Or whatever we are this winter."

"Ada—" Ruth began.

"No." Ada's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it; it came down instead, flattened, dense as river ice. "No, Mama. I know what you're going to say. You've been rehearsing it for a week — don't think I haven't seen you writing letters you burn. You're going to say the berth is mine. You're going to say I'm young, I'm the future, I carry nothing incriminating, the summons isn't served against children, whatever the legal fig happens to be. And I'm telling you now, in front of God and Jude and this very ugly lampshade, that I will not take it."

"You will," Ruth said.

"I will not leave without Papa's name."

"There *is* no name to leave with!" It burst out of Ruth before she could catch it, and then she pressed her hand flat against her mouth as though she could push the words back in, but they were already in the room, already settling into the corners like soot. Her eyes went bright and furious. "There is a stolen sum and a drowned reputation and a family that buried itself alive to escape them, and that is *all* there is, and you cannot dig a dead man's honor out of a frozen river—"

"He's not dead."

The silence that followed was worse than shouting. Even Ruth went still.

"He's not dead," Ada said again, quieter, and I understood suddenly that this was not childish faith. This was doctrine. This was the load-bearing wall of her whole interior architecture. Somewhere in nineteen years of believing, *missing* had hardened into *alive*, because *alive* meant he might come back and explain everything himself, and *dead* meant the explaining fell to the living, and none of us wanted that job. "He's missing. There's a difference, Mama. A difference you made sure I learned."

Ruth rose. She gathered her sewing with hands that shook only slightly, which for her counted as composure, and she said, "It's late," in the voice that ended conversations in that house for twenty years, and she went to her room, and the door clicked shut with terrible gentleness.

Ada and I sat looking at the ugly lampshade.

"She'll buy it for me," Ada said finally. Not bitter. Just certain, the way you're certain about tides. "She'll sell something she hasn't told us about and buy it for me and put me on that boat with a parcel and a lie about visiting an aunt. Watch her."

"I'm watching her," I said. "I've been watching her my whole life. She's better at this than we are."

"And what will *you* do, Jude?" She turned and looked at me straight on, and I saw our father in her face for a moment — everyone always said Ada had Ruth's coloring, but it wasn't true, it was his jaw, his level gray stare, the look of a person auditing your soul and finding minor discrepancies. "You sit in that observatory with your files and your oil cans, and you fix everything except the one thing broken. One berth, Jude. Three of us. Someone has to do arithmetic in this family, and Mama can't, and I won't accept the answer she's going to give."

"What answer should I give, then?" I asked. Wryness was my armor but it doesn't work on little sisters; they've seen you without it. "Go on. You've clearly got the ledger balanced."

"I've got it balanced," she said, picking her book back up, opening it to her place in somebody else's life. "But nobody's going to like my figures."

* * *

I walked home along the river road because I am drawn to my own misery the way gears are drawn to mesh. The ice had come further out from the banks overnight. In the middle distance the channel still ran black and open, a single vein of moving water perhaps forty yards wide, narrowing visibly as I watched — well, not visibly, but I could feel it narrowing, the way you can feel a clock running down when you stand close enough.

By morning the ferry office would sell that berth to somebody. By week's end, maybe, the *Larkspur* would sound her whistle one last time and slide away down the last open water, carrying whichever member of my family had won the terrible lottery nobody had agreed to enter. After that: white silence until April, and behind the white silence, waiting with the patience of paperwork, a magistrate with a reopened file and a list of names, three of which rhymed suspiciously with Galloway.

I stopped at the rail where the road ran nearest the water and looked at the channel. Cold enough now that my breath hung around me like a bad decision. And I thought — not for the first time, but for the first time seriously — about the clock.

About Meridian. About the sabotaged escapement and the filed tooth and the work done by a hand trained exactly as mine had been trained, and about the chime barrel I had not yet fully disassembled because there had been, until this moment, no reason to disassemble it. My father built half her works. Men like him — careful men, secretive men, men who hid things in clocks — they did not hide things without leaving a trail. They left trails the way other men left signatures.

The channel in front of me shuddered. A slab the size of a barn roof calved off the far shelf and swung grinding into the current, and the open water lost another few feet, and somewhere downstream the ice was doing the same thing at every bend, closing like a fist around the last road out.

Three of us. One berth. And beneath the chime barrel of a hundred-year-old clock, possibly, a fourth option that nobody had priced yet.

I turned up my collar and walked home fast, and behind me the river creaked like a house full of secrets, settling in for the winter, closing its accounts.
