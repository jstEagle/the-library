## 8. A Thief's Arithmetic

Miriam Ashe kept her office above the chandler's, in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and she received me with her census book already open on the desk — which told me she'd been expecting me since before I'd decided to come.

"You have something," she said. Not a question. The woman had a way of turning statements into doors you walked through whether you meant to or not.

I set my father's ledger on her desk between us. I had wrapped it in flannel against the cold, the way you'd carry an injured bird, and unwrapping it felt like a small surgery. "Everything," I said. "The funds he was accused of taking. The patron drew them out himself — there are signatures, dates, a second book of accounts my father kept because he didn't trust the first one. And a letter." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "He knew what would happen to his name."

Miriam read for a long time. Snow ticked against the window like a patient apprentice tapping on glass. I watched her face for the thing people look for when they hand over the truth: relief, vindication, some sign that the weight is about to lift.

What I saw instead was arithmetic.

"This changes things," she said finally. "It doesn't change them the way you think."

"Explain that to me."

She closed the ledger gently, as if it might bruise. "Jude. Magistrate Corven reopened this case because a file sat open eleven years and an inspector general asked why. He wants it *closed*. Closed files make quiet careers. A posthumous exoneration makes noise — letters from the Ashworth family, questions in the capital about why a magistrate spent public money raking up old scandal." She said the name Ashworth the way you'd say weather coming in off the water. "The patron is dead, but his family isn't. They still own half the shipping concern your father worked for. They still dine with the men who appoint magistrates."

"So the truth isn't enough."

"The truth is a stone," Miriam said. "It matters who throws it and at whom. If this goes to Corven here, in this town, it goes into a drawer. If it goes anywhere near the Ashworths' lawyers, it goes into a fire. There is exactly one version of events where your father's name comes out clean, and it is not a version that happens in this county."

I sat with that. Outside, the river made its frozen sounds, the groaning of ice that thinks about becoming land.

"The berth," I said.

"Sails day after tomorrow. One passenger. After that, the ice takes the river until spring, and spring is too late — the summons will be served by then, and once it's served, your family answers in *this* jurisdiction." She slid the ledger back across the desk toward me. "You should decide tonight who carries it."

"I thought you were the one carrying it. That was the bargain."

"The bargain was cooperation, and you've cooperated. But I'm a census taker, Jude, not a courier. If I'm seen ferrying evidence to the capital on behalf of a fugitive family—" She stopped herself, and for a moment I saw past the official to the person, tired, forty-ish, doing her best inside machinery she hadn't built. "Take it yourself if you can. Or give it to whoever leaves."

"Nobody's leaving," I said. "We're all staying together. That was the whole point of—"

But even as I said it, I heard how it sounded. Like a boy insisting the clock will keep running if nobody looks at the mainspring.

* * *

Ada found me at the observatory that evening. She came up the tower stairs two at a time, breath smoking, cheeks red as apples, and thrust a folded paper at me before I could say hello.

"She bought it," Ada said. "Mother bought the berth. For herself."

I took the paper. It was the ferry ticket, made out in careful ink: *R. Hale.* Of course. Even now, even buying passage off a dying river, Ruth Galloway protected the false name like a coat against the cold.

"When?" I asked.

"Tomorrow morning. First light. She thinks I don't know. She left the ticket out where I'd find it, which means either she wanted me to find it or she's losing her touch." Ada dropped onto the workbench stool among my scattered files and pinwheels. In the lamplight she looked fourteen instead of seventeen, then suddenly much older than both. "She's going to turn herself in, Jude. She's going to sail downriver, walk into whatever court will take her, confess everything, and spend however many years they give her so we can stay here under our fake name with our clean records. That's her plan. I read her whole heart in three lines of ferry ink."

"What did you say to her?"

"Nothing. What was there to say? You can't argue with someone who's decided to be a saint. Saints don't listen." She picked up a small brass escape wheel and turned it over in her fingers. "You taught me what this does. Lets the power out one tooth at a time so nothing runs away with itself. She never learned that lesson. She just winds herself tighter and tighter and calls it duty."

"Ada—"

"Don't *Ada* me yet. Because here's mine." She set the wheel down and looked at me straight, and I saw our father in the set of her jaw, which was strange and painful, given everything. "If Mother gives herself up to clear his name, then his name gets cleared by her ruin. And if nobody clears his name, then he died a thief and she ruined herself for nothing. Either way the arithmetic comes out wrong. Unless somebody changes the sum."

"How?"

She stood, and now she was neither fourteen nor seventy but exactly seventeen, ferocious, certain, terrifying. "Somebody has to go. Somebody has to take that ledger to the capital where the magistrate can't bury it. And it can't be Mother, because Mother going means confession. And it can't be you, because you're the one who found it and the magistrate will want *you*, and if they take you they take all of us down asking how the Galloways came to be Hales in the first place." She held out her hand, palm up. "Give me the ledger, Jude."

"No."

"Yes."

"You're seventeen years old."

"I'm seventeen years old and I'm the only one of us whose name isn't attached to anything. No records. No testimony. No history. I'm a blank page, and blank pages travel." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, I could see her hating it, and she pushed through anyway. "You always said a clock is honest because every gear does only what it was made for. Well, I was made for this. Let me do what I was made for."

I looked at my sister and understood that love, in our family, was indistinguishable from sacrifice. We each kept trying to pay the whole debt alone, and called the attempt devotion.

"It's dangerous," I said.

"Everything is dangerous. Staying is dangerous. The ice is dangerous. Being a Hale forever is dangerous — it's just the slow kind, so nobody notices." She wiggled her fingers. "The ledger, Jude."

"Not tonight. Tomorrow. At the dock." I closed my hand over hers and pressed the fingers shut. "If we're doing this, we do it right. Nobody slips away alone. Nobody becomes a martyr in secret. We tell Mother, or we don't go."

Ada's eyes went bright and furious. "She'll forbid it."

"Then we'll have one last argument as a family," I said. "We've been putting it off for eleven years."

* * *

We told her that night in the kitchen on Ferry Lane, by lamplight, with the wind worrying at the shutters. Ruth listened without interrupting, hands flat on the table, the way she listened to bad news about other people — composed, courteous, immovable.

"No," she said, when Ada finished.

"Mother—"

"You are not sailing to the capital alone with stolen evidence and a false name and seventeen years of sense. Absolutely not."

"Then come with me," Ada said. "Both of us. Leave the berth empty for the truth."

"And leave Jude here alone when the summons comes?" Ruth turned to me, and her composure finally cracked along an old fault line. "That is what this is, isn't it. You've both done sums. Everyone in this room has done sums, and every sum ends with someone being spent." She laughed, a terrible dry sound. "Your father used to do arithmetic like that. Everything balanced, everyone paid. Look where his arithmetic landed us."

"His arithmetic was wrong because the numbers were forged," I said quietly. "Someone else entered the figures. We're just copying his mistake, Mother — letting other people write our ledger. The Ashworths wrote his. Corven will write yours. When do we get to hold the pen?"

Ruth looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — not surrender, not yet, but the first honest doubt, a pallet dropping into place after years of misalignment.

Outside, far off, the river boomed like a struck bell. Ice shifting. The sound traveled through the floorboards and up through our chairs, and all three of us felt it in our teeth.

Day after tomorrow, the ferry sailed. Tonight, none of us slept.

And before first light, before any of us could lock down our separate plans, there came a knock at the door — three measured raps, unhurried, official.

Through the window, lantern raised against the dark, stood Miriam Ashe.

With a second man beside her, wearing the grey coat of a magistrate's officer, holding papers that needed no lantern to be recognized.
