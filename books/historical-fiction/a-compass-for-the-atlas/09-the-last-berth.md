## 9. The Last Berth

The morning of the departure, the cold came down the river valley like a debt collector — patient, thorough, and unimpressed by excuses.

I woke before dawn in my workshop above the horologist's shop on Tallow Street, and for a full minute I lay there listening to the master clock in my mind, ticking through its repaired heart, keeping time the way my father had designed it to keep time: honestly. Then I remembered what day it was, and my stomach performed a small mechanical failure of its own.

One berth. One berth left on the *Meridian*, the last ferry that would run before the river locked itself shut for the winter. The ice was already thickening along the banks, white teeth growing inward from both shores toward a meeting point somewhere in the middle of the current. By week's end there would be no river. Only a road made of nothing, going nowhere, that a man could walk out onto and die with great confidence.

Ruth had bought the berth. I'd found the receipt tucked into her prayer book, because my mother hid things the way other people lost them — badly, and in places where God could see. She meant to take it herself. She meant to walk onto that boat, sail downriver to the capital, put herself in front of the magistrate, and confess to falsified census records in exchange for whatever mercy might be spared for her children. She'd been saying her goodbyes all week in the language of casseroles and mended coats, and she thought none of us had noticed.

Ada had noticed. Ada noticed everything about our father and nothing about danger.

And Miriam Ashe had noticed all three of us, because noticing people was her profession, and she was very good at her profession.

So: one berth, three claimants, and me — the man who'd spent six years being nobody, scheduled to become somebody in front of the entire town before the first bell of morning.

My father would have appreciated the arithmetic of it. He taught me that every escapement is a negotiation between forces that want to run away and forces that want to stand still. Let the runaway win and the clock screams itself to ruin. Let stillness win and it dies in place. The art, he said, tapping the brass with his pipe, is in the letting-go. Controlled release. You don't stop the wheel. You decide when it turns.

I got dressed, put the ledger inside my coat against my chest, and went down to the dock to stop a ferry.

* * *

The *Meridian* sat low and sullen in the grey water, funneled steam rising from her stack and freezing into a ragged banner. A modest crowd had gathered on the dock — it wasn't every day the ferry company sold its final passage before ice-out, and there is nothing a small town loves more than a departure it doesn't have to make. The harbormaster had set up a little table with a ledger of his own, because this was a transaction, and transactions must be witnessed, preferably by everyone.

I saw Ruth first, standing near the gangplank in her good black coat, her hands folded like a woman waiting for a sermon. She had told Ada she was going into town to settle accounts with the cooper. My mother, who had lied to a census taker for six years with flawless discipline, was a catastrophically bad liar in person.

Ada stood apart from her, arms crossed, chin up, wearing the stubborn expression she'd inherited from a man we weren't supposed to mention. She knew what Ruth intended. Of course she knew. They'd been circling each other for days like two clock hands destined to overlap at some terrible hour.

And Miriam Ashe stood at the edge of the crowd with her census book under her arm, watching the dock the way I watched an unfamiliar movement — not judging it yet, only reading it.

The harbormaster called the berth holder forward. "Passage for one, paid and sealed! Mrs. Hale, if you'll step up."

Hale. Even now, even here, the false name, spoken aloud over the water where our real name had once been spoken with pride.

Ruth stepped forward. And Ada moved too.

"No," Ada said, loud enough to carry. "No, Mama. You're not taking it."

"Ada—" Ruth's voice cracked on the second syllable, and the whole town heard the fracture.

"You'll confess to something you didn't do," Ada said. "You'll hand yourself to that magistrate and he'll close the file and Papa's name will stay buried under the patron's money forever. That's not sacrifice, that's surrender, and I won't watch it."

"The berth is mine, love. It's paid."

"Then I'm buying it from you." Ada pulled out the little canvas purse she kept tied inside her coat — the fund she'd been building for four years toward, I now understood, some escape or pilgrimage I'd never asked about. "Whatever it cost, I'll pay it back over the crossing. I'll wash decks. I'll do the books. But it should be me, and we both know why."

"Why?" Ruth said, and there was anguish in it, real anger. "Because you're twenty-two and you think the world keeps promises?"

"Because I'm the one who still believes he was worth clearing."

The crowd had gone very quiet in the way crowds do when they smell blood in a family. Somewhere behind me, someone whispered *Hale* to someone else, and someone else whispered back, and I heard the word travel through the gathering like a spark down a fuse: *Galloways*.

Miriam Ashe took a step forward, opened her mouth —

— and I stepped past her, onto the gangplank, and put my hand flat on the ferry's mooring line.

"Nobody's taking the berth," I said. "Not yet."

Every face turned. I want to tell you I felt brave. What I actually felt was seasick on dry land, and aware that my voice was coming out steadier than I had any right to expect, the way a clock runs smoothest right before you find out what's wrong with it.

"Jude." Ruth's voice, very small. "Jude, go home."

"Mrs. Hale," I said, deliberately, loudly, "is my mother."

The word went through the crowd like the filed tooth through the clockwork — small damage, enormous consequence. I saw faces change. I saw old Mr. Prewitt, who'd bought watch springs from me for six years, take off his hat without seeming to know he'd done it. I saw Miriam Ashe close her census book with great care, as if closing a door gently so as not to wake anyone.

"My name is Jude Galloway," I said. "My father was Elias Galloway, keeper of this observatory, and he did not steal the expedition funds. I can prove it. This—" I drew the ledger from inside my coat, and my breath smoked around it, and I held it up so the crowd could see the oilcloth wrapping, my father's own hand on the seal, "—is his accounting of every shilling that passed through the expedition, kept in duplicate and hidden in the master clock he built, in a chamber of his own design, where it has waited eleven years for someone to look."

"That's a claim," said a voice, and it was Miriam's, and it was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "It's a good one. But it's a claim, Jude. Papers in this town go through this town's court, and this town's court goes through the patron's family, same as it always has."

"I know," I said. "That's why the papers aren't staying here."

I turned to Ada. She was staring at me with her whole face, and I saw my father in it, and my mother, and something that was entirely hers.

"You're taking the berth," I said. "Not running. Delivering. Downriver to the capital, to the magistrate's principal clerk — his name is in the front leaf; your father wrote it there with instructions. You give the ledger into the central registry, sworn and stamped, before any local writ can touch it. That's the one door they can't close, because it isn't theirs."

Ada's hands were shaking. "Jude, if you've claimed the name, they'll come for you the moment I'm gone."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the design."

Because here was the thing I'd worked out in the dark hours, turning it over like a broken movement: the magistrate didn't need all of us. He needed *a* Galloway, someone to prosecute for the false records, someone to hang the shame on so the file could close tidy and the patron's family could go on pretending. For six years we'd hidden, and hiding had fed them — every hidden year was evidence of guilt. But a Galloway who stood in the open, who handed over the proof and then stood still to be arrested, gave the local court everything it wanted right up until the moment the capital's stamp came down and made the arrest a scandal instead of a verdict.

An escapement, after all, is just a machine for being caught and released, caught and released, in exactly the order you choose.

"The berth is Mrs. Hale's," I said to the harbormaster, "and Mrs. Hale is giving passage to her daughter, Miss Ada Galloway, who is carrying documents under seal to the central registry." I looked at him steadily. "Witness it in your book. Date and hour. All of you — witness it. That matters more than any of you know yet."

The harbormaster, bless his bureaucratic soul, wrote it down. The crowd pressed closer. Somebody repeated *Galloway* like tasting it.

Ruth took my arm. Her glove was trembling against my sleeve. "You're giving up the berth," she said. "You're giving up everything. Six years, Jude. We built a life on quiet."

"We built a life on holding our breath," I said. "Mama, you were going to walk onto this boat alone and confess to a crime of paper to protect children who aren't children anymore. Ada was going to chase a ghost downriver alone. And I was going to keep winding other people's clocks and calling it living. Every one of us sacrificing separately so the others wouldn't have to. It's the family disease. I'm curing it."

"How?" she whispered.

"Together, but not here." I nodded at Ada, who was already moving up the gangplank, the ledger clutched to her chest, crying and grinning at the same time in a way that would have made our father put down his pipe and laugh. "She carries the truth. We hold the ground. When the ice breaks, it breaks all at once. You know rivers, Mama. You taught me that."

The harbormaster shouted for casting off. The *Meridian*'s bell rang out over the water, one bright stroke, and the mooring lines came loose, and the ferry slid away from the dock with my sister aboard, carrying eleven years of my father's patience downriver at eight knots.

I stood at the end of the dock until the funnel smoke thinned to nothing against the grey sky. Behind me, the town murmured my name — my true name, strange in their mouths, getting less strange by the syllable. Beside me, Miriam Ashe opened her census book to a fresh page, licked her pencil, and regarded me with an expression I couldn't fully read: part apology, part appraisal, part something I suspected she'd deny under oath was respect.

"Well, Mr. Galloway," she said. "You've just made yourself the easiest man in this county to find. I hope you understand that the magistrate's summons will be drawn by nightfall. There will be officers at your door within the week."

"Then let's not waste the week," I said, and turned toward the observatory hill, where my father's clock was keeping perfect time above the sleeping town. "Walk with me, Miss Ashe. You have a book to fill, and I have a great deal to tell you about a hidden compartment — starting with the part your magistrate's patron really doesn't want written down."

She fell into step beside me. Above us, the first snow began to fall, fine and deliberate, like dust settling over a mechanism that had finally, after long years of silence, started to move.

* * *

The officers came on the fourth day, as promised. Two of them, red-nosed with cold, boots crunching the frosted ruts of Ferry Lane, a warrant in a leather folio and no pleasure in their faces.

They found the workshop unlocked and empty, the lamps still warm, the bench swept clean, every tool laid out in careful order — a craftsman's farewell. On the bench, weighted under my smallest screwdriver, was a note in my neatest hand, addressed to the magistrate himself:

*I have gone up to the observatory to finish repairing the master clock. It will keep true time from this day forward, whatever happens to its maker. Come and arrest me there, if you must — but come at the top of the hour. I'd hate for you to miss it striking.*

What the officers did not know — what the warrant could not say, because the ink on it was already drying — was that three hundred miles downriver, a girl named Galloway was stepping off the *Meridian* onto the capital's stone quay, with a sealed ledger under her coat and the whole machinery of justice, slow as any clock, beginning at last to turn.
