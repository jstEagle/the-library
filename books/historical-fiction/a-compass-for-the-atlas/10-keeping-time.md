## 10. Keeping Time

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, with less ceremony than a drunk leaving a tavern. One day the water lay flat and white as a sheet of unmarked paper; the next it had heaved itself apart with a groan you could feel in the soles of your boots three streets away, and the whole town trooped down to the banks to watch the floes grind and tilt and carry off a section of somebody's fence like a tithe.

I watched too. I'd earned the right, I felt. I had spent the winter learning that ice is only water that's made up its mind, and that minds — rivers', towns', magistrates' — can be changed.

Ada was gone by then, four days downriver on the *Coriander*, with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and my mother's good shawl around her shoulders and an expression on her face that said she would argue with God Himself if He stood between her and the capital. She had the berth. She had the proof. She had our name.

I had stayed behind and told a ferry dock full of frozen strangers who I was, which at the time had seemed like the harder half of the bargain, though in fairness nobody at the dock had to sail the *Coriander* through the spring break-up.

* * *

The magistrate's letter arrived in April. I knew what it was before Ruth opened it, because she held it the way you hold a bird that might be either singing or dying.

She read it standing in the doorway of the house on Ferry Lane, and she read it twice, and then she handed it to me without a word, which for Ruth Galloway — Hale, formerly; Galloway again now, by decree of no less an authority than the county record office — was as much as a speech.

*The expedition funds of 1841, alleged to have been misappropriated by Elias Galloway, keeper, were in fact diverted by Ambrose Fell, patron and treasurer, as documented in the enclosed ledger and corroborated by correspondence recovered from the Fell estate. The charges are hereby struck. Mr. Galloway's service to the expedition is entered into the record posthumously, with the thanks of this office.*

Posthumously. There was a word I turned over for a long while, sitting on Ruth's step while she went inside to put the kettle on, because grief and vindication arriving in the same envelope apparently called for tea.

My father had been dead seven years, drowned or lost or simply gone wherever men go when the river takes them, and here was a government clerk informing us that he was now officially innocent. It struck me as a great deal of administrative effort spent on a man who could not read it. And yet when Ada's letter came a week later — triumphant, blotched, written at some capital inn with more enthusiasm than ink — and reported that the Fell family had declined to contest the matter, their solicitor having "advised discretion," I found I cared very much about official words after all. Dead men keep poor ledgers but excellent lawyers, it turns out, if they have children stubborn enough.

Ruth cried at the memorial. I want that set down plainly, because my mother had not cried in front of me in seven years, and I had begun to suspect she'd traded the capability away along with the name.

They unveiled the amended plaque at the expedition monument on the first warm Saturday of May — the same stone, the same list of names, one line re-cut by the mason, who charged the town nothing and told everyone about it for free. ELIAS GALLOWAY, KEEPER. The word *keeper* restored where *thief* had never been carved but might as well have been.

Ruth stood before it with her hands folded and her chin up, the way she did everything, and wept without making a sound, tears running straight off her jaw onto her Sunday collar. Ada wasn't there to see it — still in the capital, negotiating with the Geographical Society about her father's papers, because my sister does nothing by halves and has lately decided she will be the family's historian whether the family requires one or not — so I stood beside my mother and offered her my handkerchief, and she took it, and neither of us said anything, and it was the finest conversation we'd had in years.

"He taught you the escapement," she said at last, eyes still on the stone. "He used to say you'd be a better hand than him by fifteen."

"He flattered himself," I said. "I was better by fourteen."

She laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh — and squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt, and we stood there in the sun while the town filed past the stone and read the new-old name, and old Mrs. Prewitt, who hadn't spoken to Ruth since 1842, stopped and said, "It's a fine day for it," which in the currency of this town amounted to a full pardon issued both directions.

* * *

The master clock took me until June.

Not because it resisted — once I'd cleared out Fell's sabotage, the filed tooth replaced and the pallet trued and the whole train cleaned of whatever spite had been dripped into its oils — but because I kept finding my father in it, and I was in no hurry to finish finding him.

His design was everywhere once I knew to look. The chime barrel pins were cut in a rhythm I recognized the way you recognize a walk heard from another room. The hidden compartment behind the barrel — the one that had started all of this, that had cost a family its name and given it back — bore his maker's mark scribed inside the lid where no one would ever see it: *E.G., for whoever is patient.* He'd built a hiding place into the heart of the town's time, and then he'd left the truth inside it and gone off down the river, trusting that someday someone patient would come looking.

Or maybe not trusting. Maybe just hoping, in the way of men who've run out of the other thing. I sat on the observatory floor with that little scribed mark under my thumb for the better part of an afternoon, deciding how I felt about being hoped at, and concluded finally that it was no worse than being inherited, which I had been doing my whole life regardless.

The compartment I left intact, but empty, its door swinging freely. Some things shouldn't be hidden twice.

On the last day I adjusted the pendulum — a half-turn of the rating nut, no more, the smallest correction and the largest — and then I climbed down from the works and sat in the observatory gallery with my hands dirty and my coat off and waited for noon.

The clock struck twelve. All eight bells, clean and true, each note landing exactly where its brother had promised it would land, the beat steady as a pulse in a sleeping man's wrist. Below me the town went about its business — carts and gulls and the ferry company's new spring timetable chalked on the board by the dock — all of it moving to a time this machine was keeping, and I sat there grinning like a fool in an empty room, because nobody else was going to do it.

"You'll go cross-eyed, staring at it like that."

Miriam Ashe stood in the gallery door with her census book under her arm — the same book, I'd have sworn, though she claimed the original had gone to the magistrate and this was merely its respectable successor. She had a way of appearing in doorways that suggested she practiced.

"The town rolls need updating," she said, coming to stand beside me. "Galloway, Jude. Occupation: clockmaker. There's a blank where your residence should be."

"I'm aware of how forms work, Miss Ashe. You held one to my throat all winter."

"And yet." She opened the book, uncapped her pen, and waited.

I looked out the window. The river ran high and brown and busy, carrying the last of the ice south toward the sea, going about the business of being water again. Somewhere down that river Ada was turning my father's papers into a legacy. Somewhere up Ferry Lane my mother was taking down the false name from the door lintel with a scraper and a bucket of lye, muttering to herself, doing the job properly because she did every job properly. And here was I, being asked to put myself down on paper in a town that had spent a winter learning exactly who I was and had, remarkably, decided to keep me anyway — the observatory committee had already asked, in the shy way committees ask, whether I might consider taking the keeper's position, seeing as how I understood the clock better than anyone living.

"Jude Galloway," I said. "Clockmaker. Ferry Lane — the house with the scraped lintel. You can write *keeper's apprentice retired* under occupation, if you've room."

"There's room," Miriam said, writing. Her pen scratched, small and certain, in the quiet under the bells. "Welcome to the town, Mr. Galloway."

"I've lived here eleven years."

"Not officially." She closed the book. Then, softer, in the voice I'd heard exactly once that winter, the night she'd told me she was sorry for the trouble and had meant it: "Your father kept good time, from what I read in those papers. Every entry dated. Every figure checked twice."

"He'd have liked the clock running true," I said. "He'd have complained about my pallet angles first."

"Naturally."

Above us the master clock ticked on into the afternoon, unhurried, keeping the only kind of time there is — the kind that goes forward, and holds everything, and forgives nothing and everyone in the same steady beat. I put on my coat and picked up my tools, and Miriam Ashe held the door, and together we went down out of the tower and into the town that knew my name.

The kettle would be on at Ferry Lane. My mother would pretend she hadn't been watching the road. And come autumn, when Ada came home with a trunk full of her father's letters and opinions on the Geographical Society, there would be a place at the table for all of us — Galloways, every one, on the record at last, and late for dinner as likely as not.

But that's another season's story, and this clock isn't going to wind itself.
