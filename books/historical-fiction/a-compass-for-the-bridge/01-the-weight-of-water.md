## 1. The Weight of Water

The pearls came up from the water cold, and they left Ines's hands warm. That was the first thing she had learned at eight years old, kneeling on the sorting bench with her knees going numb against the salt-bleached wood: everything the sea gave up was cold, and everything the company touched turned warm, and the difference between those two temperatures was the whole of her life.

She worked the third bench from the window, where the morning light came in slanted and forgiving. Forgiving mattered. A pearl's value lived in its luster, and luster was a liar—it could make a flawed stone sing under the right light and go dead as a fish eye under the wrong one. So she had learned to turn each pearl three times against the velvet pad, tilt it toward the window, tilt it away, and read it honestly. The company did not pay her to be deceived. The company paid her—well. The company did not pay her. The company fed her, housed her, and deducted the cost of both from a debt so old she had stopped being able to see its bottom, the way divers said you stopped being able to see the surface if you went deep enough.

*Four hundred and eleven.* The number arrived in her head unbidden, the way numbers did now. Lot 411, graded second-quality, one hundred six shells, consigned to the Meridian Trading Company, San Ildefonso, in the year of Our Lord 1898. She hadn't tried to memorize it. That was the trouble with numbers—they stuck to her like burrs to a fishing net, and no amount of shaking freed them.

"Slower," muttered Odalys beside her, not looking up. "You're making us look bad."

Ines slowed. This was the arithmetic of survival that mattered more than any ledger: work quickly enough to be useful, slowly enough to be unremarkable. Be neither the best nor the worst on any bench. When the overseer walked the rows, be the girl he had to squint at to remember.

*Be useful. Be invisible.* Her father had taught her the first half before the sea took him. Eight years of indenture had taught her the second.

The sorting hall smelled of brine and oyster rot and the tallow candles the company burned even in daylight because light through glass was, apparently, also something that could be deducted from a debt. Forty women worked the benches, most of them daughters of divers, all of them bound the way their fathers had been bound—to boats they didn't own, for debts calculated by men who never got their boots wet. Ines knew every woman here by the sound of her breathing. She knew which ones wept silently into their grading cloths when a letter came from inland, which ones had children lodged in the company nursery whose fees would keep those children bound past childhood, which ones had simply gone quiet one season and never come back to loudness.

What she did not do was speak of any of it. Speaking was a current, and currents carried you places.

"Ines." The overseer's voice, from the end of the row. Not angry. Overseers here were never angry; Captain Voss had taught them that anger was a confession of disorder. "Counting house wants a pair of hands for the afternoon. Okonkwo. You."

Her stomach performed its small practiced drop. The counting house. She stood, wiped her hands on her apron, kept her eyes at the neutral middle distance—chin, collarbone, nothing higher—and went.

* * *

The counting house was the warmest building in San Ildefonso, which was to say the most dangerous. It sat on stilts above the tide gate, close enough to hear the water slap the pilings, and inside it the company's whole truth lived in ledgers: what came up from the beds, what went out on ships, what was owed and by whom and forever. Clerks moved between the shelves like men wading in shallow water, pens scratching. Nobody looked at her except to hand her things. A sorter's girl fetched buckets of fresh water for the ink, swept the shell grit from between floorboards, carried bundles from shelf to shelf. Useful. Invisible.

And within reach of the ledgers.

It had started innocently, the way all her transgressions started: as competence. Two years ago a clerk with gout in his foot had asked her to fetch the debt rolls, and she'd brought the wrong volume, and rather than admit she couldn't tell them apart—rather than be useless—she had opened the spines and read the labels herself. Reading. Silently, alone, in the half-second it took to find the right book. Reading aloud was forbidden to her kind; an ordinance posted at the compound gate said so in words she had taught herself to decode anyway, in secret, letter by letter, over years, using shipping manifests and hymn sheets and the names on crates. But reading with your mouth shut—that was nobody's business. That was just eyes.

Since then she had been requested for counting house duty eleven times. Each time, she read what passed under her hands. Each time, she forgot none of it. She told herself this was practical: a girl who understood the ledgers made fewer mistakes fetching them, and mistakes were dangerous, and danger was the enemy. She told herself she wasn't building anything, tower by tower, inside her skull. She told herself a lot of things.

Today's task was simple: carry the completed dive rolls from the intake desk to the binding shelf, in order, without dropping them. The dive rolls were her least favorite documents and her most compulsive reading. Each roll recorded a day's diving—the boats out, the shells brought up, the men who had descended. And then, in a column down the margin, in the clerk's tidy copperplate, the word that appeared too often: *Lost.*

*Lost* meant drowned. Everyone knew that. What everyone pretended not to know was what else *lost* meant: the man's debt did not die with him. It transferred. She had seen the entry format a hundred times now—*debt of J. Abad, deceased, assumed by Company against future wages of household*—which meant his widow's wages, his daughter's wages, docked until the impossible sum was paid or until another generation knelt at a sorting bench. The sea took the men once. The ledgers took them again, slower.

She was halfway across the room with roll 47-B under her arm when she felt it: the particular silence that meant Captain Voss had entered the building.

He didn't announce himself. He never did. He simply became present, the way a change in pressure announces a storm—you felt it in your ears before you saw anything. The clerks' pens scratched a little faster. A bucket-carrier near the door found urgent business elsewhere. Ines kept walking, kept her pace exactly what it had been, because the one thing worse than being noticed by Voss was being noticed *changing* because of Voss.

He stood by the intake desk talking to the head clerk, Herr Brandt, in that low level voice of his, the voice of a man reading figures aloud. She caught fragments as she passed at her obedient distance. A boat missing from the moorings at Punta Salina. Questions about whether anyone had come ashore in the night. Brandt saying he'd inquire, Voss saying, "No. I'll inquire," in the tone of a man who intended to inquire personally and thoroughly and with tools Brandt would rather not imagine.

Ines reached the binding shelf, set the roll in its place, and turned back. Her path would take her along the far wall, past the intake desk, past Voss, at ten paces. Ten paces was nothing. Ten paces was a lifetime. She fixed her gaze on the floorboards, on the grain of the wood, on anything, and walked.

"Girl."

She stopped. The word had not been loud. It didn't need to be.

She raised her eyes to the permitted altitude—his collarbone, brass buttons, the edge of a jaw shaved clean as a ledger line—and waited. Around her the room resumed its scratching, deliberately indifferent, every clerk praying the same prayer: *not me, not me.*

"You work the sorting benches." Not a question. Voss knew everything that happened under the company's roofs the way a ledger knew its own entries.

"Yes, Captain."

"How long?"

"Eight years, Captain."

"And before that?"

The question hung there. Before that was a boat called *Mariposa* and a man who sang low off-key coming up the ladder with his mask fogged, telling her the sea was generous today, wait till you see. Before that was a father. She put none of this in her face.

"I was born here, Captain. Compound side."

A pause while he considered whether the answer contained anything worth keeping. Then, mildly, terribly: "Brandt says you know where things go. Shelves, volumes. That you're quick."

"I try to be useful, Captain."

"Mm." He looked at her then—a full look, eye level, brief as a knife laid flat on a table and just as complete. Whatever he found there was evidently unremarkable, because he turned back to Brandt and said, "Punta Salina. Tonight," and dismissed her existence.

She walked out of the counting house with her heart going like a shorebird's wings and her face doing absolutely nothing at all. Useful and invisible. Eleven times in the counting house and never once a full look from him, until today. Because of a missing boat. Because of whatever had come ashore in the night.

She filed it where she filed all dangerous knowledge, in the tower inside her skull, and went to fetch fresh water for the ink.

* * *

Evening came down on San Ildefonso the way it always did: gold on the water, then rose, then a bruised violet, then the lamps of the compound flickering on one by one like a slow dishonest constellation. Ines carried her supper ration—rice, dried fish, a spoonful of oil—in its tin to the bench behind the cookhouse, where the wall still held the day's heat and the frangipani dropped its flowers onto the sand.

Mama Rosa found her there, as Mama Rosa found everyone eventually, drawn by some cook's sixth sense for hunger that was about more than rice. She was broad and gray-braided and she moved through the compound like weather that had decided, on balance, to be kind. She sat down without asking, which was her way, and divided Ines's fish portion in two and put the bigger half back on Ines's tin.

"You're thin."

"I'm the same as always."

"That's what I said." Mama Rosa chewed, watching the water. Beyond the compound wall the tide gate clanked its evening chain, and the drying nets hung in long dark veils, and the last skiffs came home trailing the smell of oyster and kerosene. "Voss was asking after workers today. Brandt's girl told the wash-house. He has a list started."

"He always has a list."

"This list is new." Mama Rosa said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her eyes stayed on the nets. "Boat gone missing. Men asking questions at the dock. You were in the counting house."

"For ink buckets."

"Mm." Mama Rosa finished eating and dusted her palms. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the sound of the tide gate chain filled the silence, rhythmic as a pulse. Then, because she was Mama Rosa, because she had pulled Ines out of the nursery line at seven years old and fed her first real meal and never once explained why, she said, "Your papa dove the east beds the day he died. Did I ever tell you the water was wrong that day?"

Ines's whole body went still. "Don't."

"It was green-black, sitting wrong in the channel, and the old divers said wait, and the foreman said the quota doesn't wait." Mama Rosa's voice stayed even, gentle, terrible. "I'm not telling you this to hurt you, niña. I'm telling you because you're grown now, and grown women get to choose what they carry. But you should know the sea didn't take him careless. The sea was only the instrument. Hands on ropes and pens on paper took him, same as they take all of them."

"I said don't." Ines heard her own voice come out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. Eight years she had kept her father in a sealed room inside herself—not mourned, not examined, just *kept*, the way you keep a lamp lit in a house you never enter. If she opened that door—

Mama Rosa stood, unhurried, and pressed a palm briefly to the crown of Ines's head, the old blessing, the one that meant *live, live, live*. "Lock your door tonight," she said. "New lists mean new questions, and questions mean somebody's already answered wrong."

She gathered the tins and went, singing something low and tuneless toward the cookhouse, and Ines sat alone behind the warm wall with her father rising in her throat like water she couldn't swallow down—the ladder, the fogged mask, the song, and then the other thing, the thing she had seen from the sorting bench at twelve years old and never once let herself see since: the rope going slack. The boat that didn't move for a long time. The foreman writing in his book before the body was even cold on the deck, pen moving fast, as if recording.

*As if recording.*

The phrase arrived whole, cold as a pearl from the deep, and would not leave. She had watched a man write at that moment and thought, at twelve, *he is writing my father's name*, and had treasured the thought like a relic: somewhere, in ink, he exists. But she was twenty now. She had read hundreds of dive rolls. She knew what clerks wrote at speed in the margins of dive rolls, and it was never names.

It was *lost*, and then it was arithmetic.

Ines sat until the lamps burned low, until the compound slept, doing the one thing she had trained herself eight years never to do: opening the door. And when she finally lay down on her mat in the dark of the women's quarters, sleep came thin and shallow, and the last thing she heard before it took her was not the tide gate or the chain but her own thought, repeating with the patience of water:

*Before you can be invisible, you must know exactly what they are trying not to show you.*

She dreamed of ledgers. And in the morning, crossing the yard before first bell with her bucket, she took the short path along the drying nets—the path she never took, because it was exposed and because the nets held nothing for her—and that was where she saw, tangled in the shadow of the folds, a hand.

A living hand, she realized, as it moved. Attached to a man lying still among the nets, salt-crusted, bleeding through a makeshift bandage, watching her with the fixed desperation of someone who had been watching for a long time. His lips parted. What came out was barely breath.

"Please. Are you alone?"
