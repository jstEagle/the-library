## 2. Ledger of the Drowned

The counting house smelled of ink, salt rot, and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled too often by too few hands. Ines Okonkwo entered it the way she entered every room in the Meridian compound: sideways, quietly, already working.

It was Thursday, which meant the ledgers of the western beds came back from the counting desk for rebinding, and Mr. Calloway—the company's chief accountant, a man whose skin had turned the color of old parchment from thirty years indoors—expected the shelves dusted before the tide turned. She had learned his schedule the way other girls learned songs. Thursday, dusting. Monday, mending bindings with paste she boiled herself from rice flour. Every second Friday, the floor scrubbed with brine so the boards wouldn't warp in the wet season.

She never touched the ledgers themselves. That was the first rule, older than her indenture: a diver's daughter did not put her hands on company paper. Her hands were for buckets and brushes and the hem of her own apron, which she kept wrung dry because wet cloth left marks on wood, and marks invited questions.

She was sixteen now, eight years into a ten-year contract signed in a language she could speak but not read, and she had survived those eight years by understanding one thing better than anyone else in the compound: that survival was a discipline of the eyes. Look without appearing to look. Know without being known to know. The compound rewarded usefulness the way the sea rewarded divers who knew when to come up—generously, but only if you surfaced before the air ran out.

So she dusted. And while she dusted, she counted.

That was her secret, or half of it. Not reading—reading aloud was forbidden to workers, and a girl caught sounding out words on company paper would be beaten first and questioned after—but counting. Numbers were safe. Numbers were just things, like shells on a sorting table, and no one had ever told her that knowing how many shells there were amounted to theft. By eight years old she could tally a pearl lot at a glance and get within three of the true count. By twelve she knew the rhythm of ledger numbers the way she knew tides: which pages held the good beds, which held the depleted ones, which years had been kind and which had drowned men.

She did not think about that last category. She dusted past those pages the way you step over a grave you'd rather not name.

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles did in San Ildefonso, with weather.

Rain had come in off the gulf two days early, fat warm drops that turned the compound's chalk paths to paste and drove every clerk with sense indoors. The counting house filled with men escaping it—three junior clerks playing at work, Calloway at his desk with a fan he never used, and the door propped open so the smell of wet earth could argue with the smell of ink. Noise meant cover. Cover meant Ines could move through the shelves without the usual ceremony of becoming furniture.

She was halfway down the eastern rack, the one holding the death rolls and debt transfers—the dullest volumes, bound in scuffed black calf nobody bothered to open between audits—when her brush snagged.

A thread. One of the brass corner-fittings on Volume 41 had worked loose, leaving a wire edge that caught her brush bristles and held them. She freed them carefully, annoyed at herself, and was reaching to dust around the fitting when the volume fell open.

It fell open because the binding was broken. That was all. A loose book, a weak spine, gravity doing what gravity does. Later she would tell herself this many times—that she hadn't gone looking, hadn't pried, that the book opened itself like a mouth.

The page it showed her was a transfer record, written in Calloway's small upright hand, each line as neat as fence posts:

*Diver: Anselm Okoye. Status: deceased, March 1893. Outstanding debt to company: 41 pesos, 12 reales. Debt transferred to company account, non-collectable. Wages accrued at date of death: forfeit.*

Below it, another. *Diver: Paulo Mabika. Status: deceased, June 1894.* Below that, a third, a fourth. Page after page of the same formula, the same tidy hand, deaths arranged like beads on a string. Forty-one pesos owed by a dead man, forgiven by a generous company. Wages accrued, forfeited. Forfeited. Forfeited.

Ines stood very still, brush in hand, rain hammering the roof.

Here is what she knew about dead divers, the way everyone in the compound knew it: they died owing. The company advanced a man his boat share, his gear, his rice; the sea took him; the debt died with him. It was the shape of the world, like the moon pulling water. Nobody wept over arithmetic.

But arithmetic has a grammar, even to a girl who cannot read it fluently, and something in these lines was wrong the way a current is wrong—you feel it in your legs before your mind can say why.

Wages *accrued*. That word appeared on every line. Accrued meant gathered, meant earned, meant sitting somewhere in a company drawer waiting. And then: *forfeit*. Not paid to a widow. Not credited against the debt—which would have been simple, would have been clean, would have erased forty-one pesos with wages the man had already earned. Instead both columns simply closed, like hands folding.

She looked down the page. Eleven names. Eleven dead men whose earned wages exactly covered—or more than covered—what they owed. And instead of one number canceling the other, both numbers had been moved. Transferred. Swallowed.

Her father's last dive had been in '93.

The thought arrived uninvited and hit her harder than any wave ever had, and she shut it out immediately, the way she had practiced shutting it out for five years, the way you press a thumb over a leak in a boat. Not now. Not here. She was in the counting house with three clerks ten feet away and Calloway's fan ticking, and her face was the only thing she owned outright in this world, and it would show nothing.

She lifted the brush. She dusted the shelf above the fallen volume. Then, with a motion so small it barely qualified as motion, she tipped the black book closed with one finger.

Except—her eye caught the next line before the covers met. Not a name. A figure in the margin, circled twice in pencil: *1893 total: 1,140 pesos retained.*

Retained. Kept. Eleven dead men's wages, kept by the company, and totaled at the bottom like fish in a crate. And 1893—

One thousand one hundred and forty pesos. She turned the number over in her head and felt it grow teeth. A good diver earned perhaps sixty pesos in a season, less his debts. Eleven men. It fit. It fit perfectly, the way a lie fits when it has been fitted carefully.

"Ines."

She did not jump. She had trained herself out of jumping years ago; jumping cost a beating in some houses. She turned with the brush still moving and found Mr. Calloway watching her from his desk, his pale eyes the color of water left standing in a bucket.

"The eastern shelf," he said. "Volume forty-one. Its spine went bad last audit. You'll bring it to me for the repair list. Nothing else on that rack."

Nothing else on that rack. As though he had watched her eyes travel. As though he knew the exact weight of what sat on that shelf, and guarded it not with locks—he didn't need locks—but with instructions.

"Yes, sir," she said, in her flat careful voice, and carried Volume 41 across the room and set it on his desk and went back to her buckets, and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady; it was only underneath, in the place where she kept her father's face, that something had begun, quietly and catastrophically, to bleed.

* * *

She finished the counting house by lamplight. The clerks left when the rain slackened, then Calloway, locking the door behind him with the ceremony of a priest. She let herself out through the cleaning entrance with her bucket, nodded to the gate guard, who did not nod back because acknowledging her was beneath acknowledgment, and walked home along the seawall in the last grey light.

And all the way home, she counted.

She couldn't help it. That was the poison of numbers—they kept working after you set them down. Eleven names on one page. How many pages in Volume 41? She had dusted that shelf for six years; the volume was as thick as her forearm, maybe eighty leaves front and back, and not all of it was transfers, but suppose half were. Suppose half were eleven names each. Four hundred names. Four hundred dead divers across the years, each one closing like folded hands, wages forfeit, debts swallowed, totals circled in pencil at the bottom of every page like signatures.

Four hundred families. Four hundred widows who had come to the compound gates asking after wages and been turned away by men quoting rules from books the widows couldn't read. She had seen them there, standing in the sun with their papers clutched wrong-side up, and she had thought, in the blind way she permitted herself to think about such things, that they were being refused because paper was cruel. She had not understood that the cruelty was the paper's whole purpose.

Because that was the thing her mind kept circling back to, the thing she wanted desperately not to have understood: why would a company forge a death? No—she corrected herself, walking the seawall, waves breaking white below her—not forge. *Falsify*. There was a difference, and the difference was the trap. A forged death roll was a crime committed once, in haste. But these entries were neat. Consistent. Totaled. They had a system's patience. A man dies at sea, and the company writes *deceased*, and transfers his debt, and forfeits his wages, and keeps the difference—and does it four hundred times, in the same tidy hand, year after year—and at some point the falsification stops being an act and becomes a policy, becomes the quiet engine under the whole compound, under the pearls on the sorting tables, under the wages she herself had never once seen itemized.

Her own contract. Ten years, signed by her mother's mark and a company agent's flourish when her father died, because a dead diver's family owed his debts, and a child's labor was legal tender. She had always assumed the terms were fair, in the way you assume the sea is deep—as a fact of nature, not a decision anyone made. Now she stood on the seawall in the dark and wondered, for the first time, whose decision it had been. Whether somewhere in a black calfskin book there was a line with *Okonkwo* in it, wages accrued, forfeited, and a total circled twice in pencil.

She stopped walking.

Below her the tide was turning, the water sliding out over the oyster beds in long silver sheets, and she made herself do the thing she had spent five years refusing to do: she thought about her father's last dive. Properly. All at once, instead of in the small safe doses she allowed.

He had gone down in March, in weather the older divers called dishonest—clear on top, cold and treacherous below. He was forty-one pesos in debt that season; she knew this because Mama Rosa had said it once, years later, in the flat voice she used for facts that hurt. He went down anyway, because men in debt to the company did not refuse the water. He came up without air in his lungs, and the company sent a clerk to the house with a paper, and her mother pressed her mark to it, and within a season Ines was sorting pearls for wages she never saw itemized.

Forty-one pesos. The same figure at the top of the page. Anselm Okoye's debt had been forty-one pesos too, and Anselm Okoye had died in March 1893, and his wages—earned, accrued, sitting in a drawer—had been *forfeited* rather than applied.

Two men. The same debt. The same month. One of them was her father, and the other was a stranger whose name she now knew forever, and between them lay a question so large she could not look at all of it at once, only its edges: whether the sea had really taken her father, or whether the paper had gotten there first—whether *deceased* in that book was a record of what happened, or an instruction.

No. That was too far. She pulled back from it hard, the way you surface fast when the pressure starts to sing in your ears. Divers died. The gulf killed men every season; that was true and honest and terrible. It didn't need a conspiracy to drown Anselm Okoye. The sea managed that on its own.

But the sea did not write *forfeit*. The sea did not circle totals in pencil. Whatever had happened to her father at the bottom of the gulf, what happened afterward—in the counting house, in the tidy hand—had been done by men, on purpose, for money. That much was certain now, printed in her memory in letters as clear as fence posts, and it could not be unprinted.

* * *

Mama Rosa had soup on when she came in, and questions nowhere in sight, which was Mama Rosa's particular genius: she fed people as though feeding required no explanation. Ines ate at the little table by the window, and Mama Rosa stirred a pot that didn't need stirring, and neither of them spoke, which was also a genius, and usually a mercy.

Tonight the silence had edges.

"You're eating slow," Mama Rosa said at last, not turning from the pot. "You eat fast when something's chasing you and slow when you're doing the chasing. Which is it?"

"Nothing." Ines set down her spoon. "The counting house. Just tired of the smell of it."

"Mm." Mama Rosa ladled herself a bowl and sat opposite, and her eyes, which missed nothing that happened anywhere in the compound, rested on her granddaughter's face with a weight Ines could feel like weather. "Calloway keep you late?"

"A while."

"He's careful about his shelves, that man. Especially the east rack." Mama Rosa said it lightly, tasting the soup, but Ines felt the sentence land on her chest like a placed hand. "Old paper, the east rack. Some of it should've gone to ash years ago, if you ask me. Nobody asks me." She blew on her spoon. "Best thing about old paper, mi'ja, is forgetting which shelf it lives on."

It was a warning. Ines understood it as one, delivered obliquely, in the grammar of pots and shelving that older women used when saying a thing straight would be dangerous. Mama Rosa knew something. Had maybe always known something. And was telling her, in the only code that kept them both safe, to forget.

"I don't know what you mean," Ines said. "I dusted. I went home."

"Good girl." Mama Rosa reached across and squeezed her wrist, brief and hard, and her hand was trembling, just slightly, just enough to feel. "Eat your soup."

Later, lying awake in the dark with the tide audible below the window, Ines conducted the inventory she performed every night—names, faces, tasks done, tasks owed, threats observed and catalogued. It was how she kept herself alive; the inventory was her prayer. But tonight the inventory ran against a wall. Every entry ended in the same image: a page of neat upright lines, eleven names, a total circled twice.

She tried to do what Mama Rosa had asked. She tried to fold it away, dust it, shelve it on the east rack of her mind where dangerous paper lived. Forgetting was a skill like any other, and she was good at skills.

But noticing, she was discovering, is not like touching. You can take your hand off a hot iron. You cannot take your mind off a fact. The page stayed open in her head, and every time she closed it, it fell open again, like the book itself had wanted this—like six years of dusting had been leading her down that aisle to that shelf for exactly this fall of a broken spine.

Somewhere out in the dark, the tide gate groaned against its chain, the old sound of the compound closing for the night, and Ines lay listening and understood that something in her life had permanently changed, and that the change was invisible, and that invisibility had always been her country—and now there was a border inside it she had crossed and could not cross back.

She had noticed. Noticing was the unforgivable thing. Noticing was the first entry in a ledger of its own, and God help her, she had already begun wondering what the total would be.

Two days later, at dusk, among the drying nets below the tide gate, she found the answer's first installment: blood on the ropes, and a man breathing shallow behind them, who knew her name.
