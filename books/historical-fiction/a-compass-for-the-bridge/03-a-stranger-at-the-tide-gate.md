## 3. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The tide gate closed at dusk, and so did Ines.

It was one of the few rules of San Ildefonso that everyone respected, because the tide gate was less a gate than an argument between the harbor and the sea—a stone throat where the water came in twice a day and took back whatever it wanted. Boats went out with it. Boats came in with it. Between those breaths, the docks belonged to nobody, which meant they belonged to whoever was willing to stand on them in the dark.

Ines was not that person. Ines had spent eight years learning exactly where the daylight ended for her, and she stepped over that line every evening like a dancer who knew her marks. Counting house to cook shed. Cook shed to the row of lean-tos the indentured families called the Rows. The walk took four hundred steps if she took the long way around the drying racks, three hundred and ten if she cut through, and she always took the long way, because cutting through meant passing the net lines where the men mended canvas by lantern light, and being seen after hours was a debt she could not afford.

But tonight the kitchen had sent her out anyway.

"The Voss wants oysters," Mama Rosa said, in the flat tone she reserved for things that were not really requests. "Oysters and the white wine from the good crate, and he wants them before the tide turns, and he wants me to shuck them, which means somebody walks to the beds and somebody carries them back, and my knees have made their position on walking known."

"Why me?"

"Because Consuelo has the fever and because you move quiet and because I asked." Mama Rosa was already grating something, her back a wall of shoulders and steam. "Take the small pail. Take nothing else. Be back before the lamps."

Being sent out at dusk was itself a small transgression, and Ines felt it like a stone in her shoe—the whole walk down to the beds, a wrongness she couldn't shake. The company had rules about women moving alone after the tide turned, not out of concern but out of bookkeeping: a woman on the docks after dark was either looking for trouble or selling it, and both kinds required forms. But Captain Voss's dinner arrived when Captain Voss's dinner arrived, and no clerk in San Ildefonso was going to put *oysters, late* into a report.

So she went.

The oyster beds lay past the last warehouse, past the chandlery with its shuttered windows, past the point where the packed shell of the road gave up and became mud and broken coral. She filled the pail by feel, the water cold to the wrist, the shells gaping at her like small mouths. Above her the sky did what it always did here—went from copper to violet in the space of a prayer, the first stars pricking through before she'd finished the second one.

She was halfway back when she heard him.

Not a sound, exactly. The absence of one. The nets hung along the tide line on their long frames, drying in the salt wind, and where there should have been the tick of wind-stiffened cordage there was instead a stillness, a held-breath quality, as though one patch of night were denser than the rest. Ines stopped. Her first instinct—the instinct of eight years' training—was to walk on. Whatever it was, it wasn't hers. Her second instinct, slower and older, was that whatever hid in the dark was more afraid than she was, and fear that big usually meant trouble worth knowing about.

She stood in the mud with a pail of oysters and let the two instincts fight it out.

Curiosity won, as it always did, by promising to be quick.

She left the pail by a mooring post and moved toward the nets. Up close the drying racks were a country of their own: long poles lashed into trestles, nets draped over them like the skins of enormous sleeping animals, and the smell of old fish and tar so thick it was nearly a taste. She ducked under the first frame and there—wedged into the shadow between two hanging nets, folded up like a letter someone had tried to cram back into its envelope—was a man.

He was not from here. That was the first thing, before the blood, before anything. His skin had none of the port's particular weathering; he was pale under the tan, sunburned across the cheekbones, and his hair was the color of wet sand. A foreigner, then, off one of the deepwater ships—and yet his clothes said no such thing. They were local cotton, cheap and salt-ruined, the clothes of a fisherman or a deckhand, worn long enough to be honest. He'd been trying to look like the port. He'd failed at the face and succeeded at everything else.

And he was bleeding. Not dramatically—not the bright theatrical spill she'd imagined blood to be when she was small and her father was alive—but steadily, sullenly, from somewhere high on his side, soaking through the cloth in a darkness that even the starlight could read. He'd pressed a wad of net-cord sacking against it and tied it crudely with a length of twine, and his hands, she noticed, were shaking in a controlled way, the way a man shakes when he is spending effort on not shaking.

Ines did not scream. Screaming was loud, and loud was the opposite of everything she was.

"You're bleeding on the company's nets," she said, very quietly.

His eyes found her. Even in the dark she could see they were light-colored, gray or green, fever-bright, and they went through a fast sequence—surprise, assessment, calculation, and then something that might have been relief, arriving last and unwelcome.

"Spanish," he rasped. Or tried to. His mouth was dry and the word cracked.

"Trade language," she corrected. Everyone in San Ildefonso spoke the trade language; it was the one thing the company had given freely, because it made them easier to use. "Whose boat?"

"None." He swallowed. "I swam."

She looked at him. The tide gate was a half mile of open water from the shipping channel, and the current through the stone throat could take a strong man and fold him against the rocks like laundry. Nobody swam the gate. Nobody swam the gate and then climbed, bleeding, into the company's own net racks, unless—

Her eyes went to his wrist without her permission. It was raised, braced against the trestle pole, and in the thin light she saw it: the brand. The Meridian mark, the little three-pointed star inside a circle, burned into every diver and deckhand and dockworker the company owned, inked into the skin of everyone whose name lived in its ledgers. She wore hers on the same spot. Every morning she saw it when she washed.

Except his was scarred over. Deliberately, brutally scarred—burned again and cut, worked over until the mark was a rough pale knot of tissue, unreadable. Someone had tried to erase themselves from the company's inventory, and had done it with fire and pain rather than paper.

Ines took a step back.

The movement was involuntary and he read it perfectly. "No," he said, low and fast. "No—listen—"

"Stay there."

"Listen. Please." He shifted, winced, and kept his voice at her own frequency, that hushed register below the hearing of the world. "You work for them. You know what they are."

"I know how to carry oysters," Ines said. "That's all I know."

"That's a lie," he said, without heat. "That's the first lie everyone learns here. I've been in this port two days and I've heard it forty times."

Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanked, slow and irregular, counting the swell. Ines became extremely aware of the distance to the pail, to the path, to the lamplit safety of the compound. She also became aware, with equal unpleasantness, that she had not yet walked away.

"Who are you?" she said.

"A courier."

"For whom?"

And then he did the thing that changed everything, though she would not understand how much until later, turning it over in the dark of her cot like a stone found in a pocket. He looked at her—at her wrist, at the brand she shared with half the port—and he said a name.

"Tomas Reyes," he whispered.

The name went into Ines like cold water.

Everyone in San Ildefonso knew Tomas Reyes, or had. The company clerk. The quiet man at the high desk in the counting house who recorded the pearl weights in his narrow upright hand, who never raised his voice, who once—she remembered this with the strange clarity of childhood—had looked at her across the scales and said, *You count well, little one*, and gone back to his figures while her heart glowed for a week. The clerk who had taken a boat out to the outer beds during the storm season two years ago and drowned, officially, formally, entered in the ledger in black ink: *Reyes, T., lost overboard, wages to date settled with next of kin: none.*

Dead two years. Dead and paid-out and filed.

"I saw him buried," Ines said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "There was no body. They buried the—his effects. A box. I remember, because—" Because she had watched, a girl of fourteen, and thought how small a box for a man. "He's dead."

"Yes," the stranger said. "That's what the ledger says."

"And ledgers don't lie."

Something moved across his ruined face—not quite a smile, more bitter than that. "Is that what they teach you here? God." He pressed his hand harder against his side, and she saw fresh darkness seep between his fingers, and hated herself for noticing it the way she noticed ledger numbers, automatically, as data. "The ledgers are the lie. All of them. Reyes isn't dead. Reyes is—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, some survival instinct overriding the fever. He shook his head slowly. "He told me one name to give, if it all went wrong. Only one. Yours, he said, if I could find the girl who counts."

The mud sucked at Ines's feet. The bell buoy clanked. Somewhere behind her, back toward the compound, a dog began to bark, and she thought of Captain Voss sitting down to his dinner of oysters that had cost her this errand, this moment, this crack opening in the floor of her life.

"He doesn't know my name," she said. "Nobody knows my name."

"He does," the stranger said. "He said: the daughter of Okonkwo. The one who counts. He said you'd know what a falsified line in a death roll looks like, even if you've never been allowed to say it aloud."

*Okonkwo.* Her father's name, spoken on the company's docks by a bleeding foreigner among the company's nets. Eight years of careful erasure—of becoming *the girl*, *the sorter*, *her*—and this man had reached into the dark and pulled out the one word she'd buried deepest, and now it hung in the salt air between them, exposed, glowing.

She should run. She understood this with total clarity. She should pick up the pail, walk back to the compound, deliver the oysters, and never come near the tide gate again, and in a year the moment would be worn smooth and deniable as sea glass.

Instead she heard herself ask, "Where is he hurt? You. Where are you hurt?"

"Under the ribs. It's stopped mostly. It needs—" He exhaled. "It needs a day. Maybe two. Then I'm gone."

"Voss's men dragged the chandlery yesterday," she heard herself say. "Looking for a stowaway, they said. And the harbor watch doubled tonight. I counted six extra lanterns coming down." She stared at him, at the scarred-over brand, at the blood. "They're hunting you."

"They're hunting what I carry."

"What do you carry?"

He was quiet a long moment. The fever was working on him now; she could see it in the shine of his eyes, the slight lag in his focus. When he spoke, each word seemed chosen and carried up from somewhere deep.

"Enough," he said. "Enough to drown this company. Enough that when Voss finds out it exists—and he will find out—he will tear this port apart down to the keels. And anyone standing near it." His gaze fixed on her, apologetic and merciless at once. "Which now includes you, I'm sorry to say. He said your name to me. I said yours to him, in a manner of speaking. That's a thread now, whether you pull it or not."

Ines looked down at her own wrist, at the neat unbroken brand she had never once resented out loud, and felt—for the first time in eight years—the full weight of what it was. Not a mark of belonging. An entry in an account. And accounts, she understood suddenly, could be balanced however the holder wished. A life could be a credit or a write-off. Her father's had been written off. Tomas Reyes had been written off. And she was standing in the mud at the edge of the company's world, holding a pail of oysters going cold, talking to proof that the writing-off was a knife.

"If I help you," she said slowly, "and they find out, they'll void my indenture into prison. If they find you here, they'll ask who spoke to you. Who brought food. Who walked past these nets." She shook her head. "Do you understand what you've done? You've taken invisible and made it visible. There is no worse thing you could have done to me."

"I know," he said simply. "I'm sorry."

And because he was sorry, and because he was feverish, and because he had crossed half the world carrying something heavy enough to drown a company, and because underneath eight years of trained blankness Ines Okonkwo was still the daughter of a man the ledgers had erased—instantly, efficiently, without a single line item out of place—she crouched down beside the net rack, and pulled the pail of forgotten oysters closer, and said:

"Eat one. You look half-dead, and dead men can't answer questions. And I have questions."

She fed him oysters in the dark among the nets, raw and brine-slick, opened with the little knife from her apron pocket, and he ate like a man remembering that food existed. Between oysters he gave her pieces—just pieces, rationed, a starving man hoarding his last bread.

A ship. A channel crossing. A bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest that he touched once, unconsciously, the way other men touched knives. Men who had trusted him with it and were now scattered, caught, or silent. A name he refused to repeat. And Reyes—alive, hidden, working, patient as tide.

"He sends letters," the stranger said. "Years of letters. Signed, dated, witnessed. What the company did. What it's still doing." His eyes closed briefly, opened. "Paper, girl. It's all paper. Their whole kingdom is paper. And paper can be stolen."

The tide turned while he talked. She felt it before she knew it—the subtle reversal in the air, the change in the buoy bell's rhythm—and with it came the sound she had been dreading: voices on the dock road. Lantern light swinging loose and purposeful, not the steady patrol pace but the searching kind, beams raking along the waterline, sweeping under warehouses, probing into places where things hid.

Ines rose without a sound and peered through the net fringe. Four men. Harbor watch, plus one in a company coat walking slightly apart from the others, and her stomach dropped through the mud, because she knew the shape of that coat and the particular unhurried way it moved. Voss didn't hurry. Hurrying was for people whose time was costing them something.

"They're coming along the tide line," she breathed. "Can you walk?"

"Not fast."

"Not fast isn't a direction. Where?"

He pointed downstream, past the racks, to where the mudflats gave onto a drainage culvert under the seawall—a black slot she'd known since childhood, that every child of the Rows knew and every adult pretended not to. "There. If it's not grated."

"It's never grated. The company keeps meaning to and never spends the money." She was already moving, dragging a spare folded net from the end of the rack, shaking it out. "Get into the culvert. Stay under the arch until the water covers your boots, then follow the flow, don't fight it. Don't come out till dawn. Don't come to me—I'll find a way to find you, near the chandlery, tomorrow after the midday sort."

"And if we're seen—"

"We won't be. Because you're going to be underwater and I'm going to be invisible." She paused, one hand on the netting, and the question came out of her before she could weigh it, small and terribly human. "Why me? Of everyone in this port. Why does a dead man know my father's name?"

The stranger looked at her from the ground, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his ruined side, and for a moment he seemed about to answer honestly. Then the lantern light swung toward the racks, and there was no time for honesty.

"Culvert," she hissed. "Now."

He went—slowly, gritting his teeth, folding himself out of the nest of nets and staggering down toward the seawall with her steadying him for three steps before she made herself let go. He slipped beneath the arch just as the light reached the drying racks, a smear of darkness swallowed by darker darkness, and Ines turned, pail in hand, expression smoothed to its usual blankness, heart going like a diver's pulse at depth.

The lantern stopped six feet away.

Captain Voss stood at the edge of the net racks, lamp raised, and looked at her the way he looked at everything: as an entry awaiting classification. His face was calm and pale and completely without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Behind him the watchmen waited, breathing loud in the quiet.

"Late for oysters," Voss said.

"The beds were slow, sir. Tide was turning."

A silence. His eyes moved over her—apron, pail, the mud on her hem, the nets above—and she stood inside her practiced stillness and prayed it fit her as well as it had every day for eight years.

"You came down alone," Voss said. "In the dark."

"Yes, sir."

"No one on the tide line?"

"No one, sir."

He regarded her a moment longer. Then, mildly, almost kindly, he said: "Strange. We pulled a man from a boat this morning who says a courier reached this port by water two nights ago." He lowered the lamp. "Swimmers leave traces, girl. Wet footprints dry slowly on a cool night."

Ines's mind moved with terrible speed, back along the tide line, to the mud, to the black slot of the culvert, to the trail of disturbed silt any fool with a lamp could read like writing.

"Mine, likely, sir," she said. "From the beds."

Voss looked at her. Then he turned and looked, very slowly, down the length of the drying racks, toward the seawall, toward the dark arch where the drainage water ran, and Ines understood that the next sixty seconds would decide everything she had ever been.

"Wait there," said Captain Voss, and started toward the culvert.

* * *

She did not wait there.

She did the only thing her eight years had trained her to do: she made herself useful, loudly. "Sir—the oysters," she called after him, holding up the pail, letting it slop, letting her voice do what voices weren't supposed to do after the tide turned: carry. "Mama Rosa will skin me, they're for your dinner—they'll be cold, sir, the ice is back at the compound—"

The watchmen glanced at her. Voss did not. He was five steps from the arch, lamp extended, and she could see the beam sliding along the seawall, catching the slick stones, the disturbed silt, heading for the black slot—

And then the bell buoy began to clang. Fast, hard, urgent—the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one—and a shout went up from the harbor watch on the far pier. Lights bloomed across the water. Something was loose in the channel: a skiff, adrift and burning its own lantern, sliding unmanned on the ebb with the current, straight toward the rocks of the gate.

Every lantern on the tide line swung toward the water. Including Voss's.

"Diversion," Voss said quietly, to no one, and for one strange heartbeat he did not move at all—stood perfectly still with the lamp dripping light, recalculating, a man watching his evening's arithmetic collapse. Then he turned. "You. Girl. Go home by the compound road. Straight there. Anyone on the tide line tonight goes on the list."

Yes, sir, said Ines Okonkwo's mouth.

Her feet carried her up the compound road at exactly the pace of an exhausted oyster-girl, neither hurrying nor lingering, past the watchmen running the other way, past the burning skiff reflected in a thousand shattered pieces on the black water. She did not look back at the culvert. She did not look back at Voss, standing motionless at the seawall with his lamp, watching the fire drift out to sea.

Only when she reached the compound gate, when the bolt shot home behind her and the familiar dark of the cook shed swallowed her whole, did she let herself stop. She set down the pail. Her hands, she discovered, were shaking in a controlled way, the way a man shakes when he is spending effort on not shaking.

From the inner kitchen, Mama Rosa's voice: "Forty minutes for oysters. The man will have eaten his own boots."

Ines breathed in. Salt, smoke, the ordinary smell of ordinary evening. Behind her eyes: a scarred brand, a whispered name, a dead man who wrote letters, a burning skiff drifting toward the gate like a promise.

*Tomas Reyes.*

Tomorrow, after the midday sort, near the chandlery. She had promised it out loud, in her own voice, to a stranger—which meant that somewhere in the great ledger of her life, a new line had been entered, in ink she could not unspill.

"Mama Rosa," she said, stepping into the kitchen light, reaching for the ice. "Tell me about my father."
