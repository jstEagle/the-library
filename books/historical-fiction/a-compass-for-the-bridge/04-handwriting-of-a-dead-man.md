## 4. Handwriting of a Dead Man

He was worse in the light.

I had seen him only in the blue half-dark of the tide gate the night before, when everything about a man can be imagined into something safer than it is. Now, crouched behind the stacked drying racks where the nets hung stiff with salt, he was just a man—thirty maybe, hollow-cheeked, his foreignness plain in the bones of his face and the way he held himself like a man who had learned that stillness was cheaper than running. His wrist, where he'd pushed back the rag wrapped around it, showed the brand: the Meridian star, half-obscured by a cruder scar burned over it, as if someone had tried to erase him the way one erases a figure from a column.

"You said you knew him," I whispered. The nets moved around us in the wind off the water, and through their mesh I could see the compound laid out in its morning order: the sorting tables, the counting house with its shuttered windows, the long shed where the divers' families queued every second Friday to be told what they were owed, which was never much, and to be told it gratefully, which they always were. "You said the name like it meant something. Tomas Reyes."

"It means everything." His voice was sandpaper. He'd taken water into his lungs two nights ago, and he hadn't shaken it yet. "Señora—"

"Ines." I didn't know why I gave him my name. It felt like handing a pearl to a stranger on an open street. "Not señora. Nothing about me is señora."

"Ines, then." He looked at me the way no one at Meridian had ever looked at me, which is to say he looked *at* me and not through me, and not past me toward whatever task I could perform. It was unbearable. "I need you to understand what I'm about to show you before you decide anything. Because after you see it, you can't unsee it. And after you unsee nothing—" he almost smiled, and the smile cracked his dry lips, "—you'll have to choose what kind of person you are. I won't make you choose. I only want you to understand that not choosing is also a choice. That's what Reyes taught me."

"Reyes taught you," I repeated. My heart was doing something stupid and loud against my ribs. Around us the compound woke: the clatter of the kitchen, Mama Rosa's voice rising and falling over the fire pit, somewhere a diver's boy laughing at gulls. The ordinary world, going on. I wanted it to keep going exactly as it was, with me tucked inside it like a bead in coral, unnoticed, unclaimed, alive. "Tomas Reyes has been dead for two years. There was a service. The company paid for candles. They read his debts aloud and forgave them, which everyone said was generous."

"The company paid for candles," Elias agreed. "Because a funeral is the cheapest lie there is. You buy flowers, you buy a priest, you buy silence. Cheaper than wages. Wages go on forever. A funeral ends."

Something cold walked down my spine, wearing shoes that fit too well.

"What do you mean, wages go on?"

Elias reached inside his shirt. I flinched back, already composing the story I would tell if Captain Voss materialized out of the morning—that I had found this man, that I had shouted for help, that I had been on my way to do so—and then his hand came out holding not a knife but a packet. An oilcloth bundle, small, tied with cord gone soft from sweat. He unwrapped it on his knee with the care of a man handling eggs.

Letters. Perhaps thirty of them, bound in three stacks, the paper foxed with damp, the ink faded but legible even from where I crouched. I knew how to read—I had been taught by candle stubs and stolen moments before the company forbade literacy among the indentured, before the rule became *no reading aloud*, before even silent letters became contraband of a sort—but I had never seen handwriting like this. Most company documents were written in the round, obedient script of clerks trained to produce columns that never argued. This hand leaned forward. It hurried. It slanted down the page like a man walking fast against wind, and it crossed its t's with little slashing strokes, impatient, alive.

"This one," Elias said, and selected a page, and held it where the light through the netting fell across it in bars. "Dated the fourteenth of March, this year."

I read it. I have read it ten thousand times since; I will read it until I die.

*They have listed another eleven this quarter. Bautista's boys among them. I copied the roll myself, in my old position, before they found reason to move me to the warehouse—the irony being that they promoted me closer to the papers I intend to steal. Eleven men entered the water and did not come up, and the roll says six, and the roll says the five who are missing were never hired, though their fathers signed their indenture papers in this very office and their mothers queue at the shed on Fridays asking after wages owed to sons the company swears it never employed. The debt transfers to Meridian. The wages transfer to Meridian. The grief stays where it is, free of charge. Voss calls it tidying. He said it to my face at supper, tidying, as one says sweeping. I am done being tidy.*

The nets swayed. The gulls screamed. Somewhere a bell rang the change of shift, and my body, trained by eight years, wanted to rise and go to the sorting tables where my hands were expected, because my hands being expected was the whole architecture of my survival.

"He wrote this in March," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through water. "Reyes died in the autumn two years ago. Drowned crossing to the northern islands. They rang the bell."

"They rang the bell," Elias said. "And then they paid me to carry his letters south, one at a time, to a lawyer in the capital who takes payment in secrets instead of coin. Reyes has been dead officially for two years and unofficially not at all. He lives inland, in the high country, with a widow who owes her husband's name to one of these rolls—a man who drowned so completely on paper that the widow got to keep the name and the hut and nobody questioned it. From there he collects testimony. Copies of rolls. Signatures of witnesses. For three years he has been building a case, and now the case is nearly built, and now I have nearly gotten him hanged by falling off a boat."

"Nearly?" The word snagged. "Nearly gotten him hanged?"

"I don't know where he is precisely. Nobody does. Not even me—not his location, not his face anymore, since he let his beard grow and took to calling himself by another man's name. I carry letters to a waystation and others carry them onward. If Voss breaks me, I can tell him truthfully that I couldn't lead him to Reyes if I wanted to." Elias's jaw tightened. "Which is fortunate, because I will want to, before he's finished with me. Everyone does, eventually. It isn't weakness. It's arithmetic. A body can only hold so much before it gives up its contents."

* * *

I should have run then. Let me say that plainly, because for years afterward I asked myself why I didn't, and the honest answer is that I didn't run because I was already caught—caught not by Voss or by Elias but by a date on a piece of paper. March the fourteenth. Two years dead. Alive.

Eight years I had navigated San Ildefonso by a map so familiar I had stopped seeing it as a map. Here were the safe channels: walk with your eyes down, count pearls without counting them aloud, never let your shadow fall across an officer's path. Here were the deep waters to avoid: the counting house after dark, Voss's office, any question beginning with *why*. I had charted all of it, refined it, survived by it, and the map had one fixed point at its center, immovable, load-bearing: the company told the truth about deaths. Men went into the sea and did not come up, and this was tragedy, but it was *true* tragedy, honest tragedy, the sea's own arithmetic, and the company merely recorded what the sea decreed.

My father had gone into the sea. Eight years ago, almost to the month. The rope parted—or so they said—and the water closed over him, and the roll recorded his death, and the roll recorded that his debts, alas, exceeded his earned wages, and therefore nothing was owed to the widow and child he left, and my mother was dead by then anyway, and I was nine, and the company generously absorbed my father's remaining debt by taking me into indenture, and everyone called this mercy, and I believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative, and because I was nine.

Now a slanting, hurrying hand told me the sea's arithmetic had a co-signatory.

"How many," I heard myself ask. My hands had gone cold around my knees. "How many names on the rolls are lies?"

Elias looked at the letters rather than at me, as if the number were written there and he needed to check.

"On the master rolls? Over the life of the company—hundreds. In the last decade alone, since Voss systematized it—" he said *systematized* the way a man spits, "—Reyes believes more than two hundred. Two hundred people who dove and drowned or dove and lived and were drowned anyway, on paper, because a dead diver costs one funeral and a live diver's family costs wages for forty years. Some of them aren't even dead, Ines. Some are working other compounds up the coast under new names, erased here so the company could keep both their labor elsewhere and their silence here. The founder's fortune—the whole of it, the warehouses, the ships, this compound, the governor's friendship—all of it rests on a column of names that were crossed out so money wouldn't have to move."

The world tilted. That is the only way I can describe it, and I have tried many times. Not like fainting. Like standing on solid ground your whole life and looking down to find that the ground was painted onto glass, and beneath the glass, machinery. Everything I had trusted—the bell tolling for Reyes, the candles, the forgiving of debts, the solemn faces at the shed on Fridays telling mothers that alas, the sea keeps what it takes—it was all set dressing over the machinery, and the machinery's only product was the transfer of numbers from the poor column to the rich one.

Even my fear reorganized itself. All my life I had feared the wrong things in the right proportions: feared Voss, feared hunger, feared the brand. Now I understood the fear itself had been curated, rationed out to me like rice, kept at exactly the level where I would work and not question. A terrified woman runs. A suitably frightened woman sorts pearls.

"Ines." Elias's voice pulled me back. The sun had climbed; the nets threw moving shadows over the letters like fingers trying to pull them back into hiding. "I need to tell you the rest, and then I need you to tell me no, and then, possibly, I need you to tell me yes."

"The rest of what?"

"Why I came to you. Why I was watching the compound at the tide gate instead of lying quiet in the hills until my shoulder mended." He shifted, and I saw how bad the shoulder was, the joint sitting wrong under the borrowed shirt. "Three days ago I had the full packet. Thirty-two letters plus the copied rolls, sealed for the magistrate in the capital—Judge Ferrero, the one man on the bench who's never taken Meridian money, we think. My boat was boarded off the point at night. No lights, no challenge—Voss's method. I went over the side with the letters in oilcloth and left the boat and poor Tomás the fisherman to explain what he couldn't. I've been in the water and the mangroves for two days. And when I finally got close enough to the port to plan a way out, I discovered the problem."

"The checkpoints," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. But there it was: every indentured worker in San Ildefonso carried the route to the capital inside them like a hymn they'd never be allowed to sing. The tide gate, where the company guard checked every leaving boat and every leaving body. The mountain customs house, where colonial inspectors stamped cargo manifests and turned back anyone whose papers smelled of the compounds. And the river ferry, last crossing before the capital road, commanded by one Mateo Voss, the captain's cousin, a man who had reportedly once turned back a bishop for having insufficiently humble luggage.

Elias blinked. Then he laughed, softly, painfully, holding his ruined shoulder.

"There it is," he said. "That's the mind I need. You recite the walls of your prison like scripture, and you're right, they're exactly that. Three checkpoints, and I cannot pass one of them. I am a branded man with a scarred-over brand, no papers, no face anyone in authority will look at kindly, and a wound that will fever again within the day. Every hour I stay in this compound, the net tightens. Voss will know by tomorrow at the latest that someone came ashore—he always knows—and when he starts pulling threads, this thread leads to you. You pulled me from the water. Someone saw, or will claim to have seen. So you're already in the current whether you swim or not."

"That isn't true." My voice came out flat, hard, the voice I used on the younger girls when they cried at the sorting tables. "Nobody saw. I told no one. I am careful. Being careful is the entire—"

"Being careful," Elias said gently, "is what Reyes was, for eleven years. He was the most careful man in this compound. He counted the falsified rows with his own hand because they made him, because he was useful and accurate and invisible in the way good clerks are invisible. And one day the carefulness itself became the trap, because the only person who could testify to the fraud was the person best hidden inside it." He gathered the letters back into their oilcloth with those slow, egg-careful movements. "Careful is a fine strategy, Ines, right up until the moment it stops being a choice and becomes a habit you can't break even when the house is burning. I'm not asking you to decide today. I'm telling you the fire has started, and showing you where the doors are, and noting—with respect—that you're the only one who's memorized the layout of this particular house."

I stood up too fast. The nets brushed my hair. Through the mesh, the compound lay in its morning order, the sorting tables filling with bent heads, the counting house squat and certain, and I thought: *every window in that building looks out over water that took my father, and every ledger inside it may hold a line with his name crossed out by a living hand.*

"I have to be at the tables," I said. "If I'm late, it starts. Questions. Questions are worse than beatings; questions follow you."

"Then go," Elias said. "Sort your pearls. I'll be here tonight, or I'll be in Voss's cellar, and either way you'll know by the quality of the morning whether you chose the door or watched it burn."

I turned to go. Then turned back, because there was one more thing, and it came out of me before I could weigh it, the way the truest things do.

"The letters. If they take you—they can't find them here. Wherever here is. Swear it."

"They won't find them," Elias said, "because starting tonight, I can't be the one who has them. A courier carrying letters is evidence. A courier carrying nothing is just a vagrant with a bad shoulder." He held the oilcloth bundle out across the space between us, resting it on the lowest net-cord, so that accepting or refusing was entirely my act, not his.

"A dead man's handwriting," he said. "Heavy as ballast and worth more than every pearl in that building. I can't carry it through three checkpoints. Neither can he." A nod toward the compound, toward the counting house, toward something I didn't yet understand he meant. "But you told me yourself, the first night, without knowing you were telling me: nobody searches the things the company trusts."

I had said no such thing. But I would say it, later, and mean it.

I took the letters. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed more than the sea.

Behind me, at the far end of the compound, the counting house door opened, and Captain Voss stepped out into the morning light, unhurried, drying his hands on a cloth, and stood a moment looking out over the water with the expression of a man checking figures that never disappoint him.

Then he turned, slowly, and looked at the drying racks.
