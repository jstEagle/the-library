## 5. What Voss Knows

Captain Voss did not believe in anger. Anger was expense. It burned fuel, it clouded arithmetic, and in thirty-one years of keeping Meridian Trading Company's accounts balanced between men, he had never once seen rage produce a profit.

So when the fisherman Mateo Cruz stood dripping on the dock planks at six in the morning, swearing before God and the Virgin that he had not seen a boat that Voss knew for a fact had been seen, Voss simply opened his ledger and waited.

The waiting was the whole art. Men filled silence like water fills a hole. They poured out names and times and half-remembered faces just to stop the stillness pressing on them. Voss had learned this from an auditor named Herr Brandt, back in Hamburg, who could make a shipping clerk weep by turning a page slowly. Brandt used to say that every man's life was an account, and every account had an error somewhere, and you needed only patience to find it. Voss had thought about that sentence for twenty years. He considered it the finest education he ever received.

"Again," he said now. "Tuesday night."

"I told you. I was mending nets."

"Mending nets until when?"

"Late. The tide came in late, the moon was—"

"The moon was full," Voss said. "Full moons are good for mending. They're also good for rowing a man up the channel without lights." He turned to the next page. "You were seen at the tide gate at half past eleven, Mateo. You were seen speaking to someone. The watchman remembers your lamp."

Mateo's face did something complicated—the small muscular collapse of a man whose lie has been weighed and found lighter than the truth. "A stranger," he said finally. "Wet through. Paid me two pesos to say nothing."

"Two pesos." Voss wrote it down. The scratch of the pencil was the loudest thing on the dock. "For what? Rowing him?"

"He was already across. He only wanted—I don't know. Directions. Which buildings were company buildings. Where the counting house stood."

Voss closed the ledger. That was enough for the morning. Not everything—never everything at once; a net hauled too fast tears—but enough.

* * *

He walked the port as he always walked it, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, nodding to workers who nodded back with the particular brightness of men hoping not to be noticed. This was the paradox of his position and he understood it perfectly: everyone watched him constantly, and no one looked at him. They saw the uniform, the rank, the reputation. They did not see the man doing sums inside it.

Here is what Voss knew, laid out in his head like entries awaiting posting:

Item one: A boat missing from the southern moorings since Tuesday, recovered Wednesday dawn two miles downcoast, hull wet, oars shipped neatly, no sign of violence. Someone had borrowed it carefully. Careful borrowers were professionals or desperate men, and desperate amateurs left messes.

Item two: Herr Reyes, clerk of the San Ildefonso counting house, officially deceased these two years—drowned, so the roll said, in the spring survey run. A convenient death. Voss had signed the notification himself. He had also, because thoroughness was cheap insurance, kept one eye open ever after. Dead clerks who stayed dead were fine. Dead clerks who sent letters were a liability class all their own.

Item three: Eleven days ago, a letter had gone out from the capital office under audit seal, announcing the colonial magistrate would review Meridian's books this season. Routine. Audits happened. But audits made paper move, and paper moving meant paper could be touched, swapped, added. And item four, posted just last night: word from a paid ear in the mountain villages of a traveler asking after the road schedule, a foreigner by his accent, traveling light, asking questions about which offices handled company correspondence.

Put the items side by side and they summed themselves. A courier had reached San Ildefonso. He carried something. And whatever he carried concerned the death rolls, because nothing else in the company's books was worth carrying anywhere.

Voss did not feel alarm. Alarm was for amateurs. What he felt was the mild displeasure of a bookkeeper finding a smudge on a column—a smudge that might mean one wrong digit or might mean the whole page was false, and there was only one way to find out, and it was the same way regardless: find the entry, trace it back, strike it through.

One loose thread, cut, cost a morning's work. One loose thread, unraveled, cost the company everything. The mathematics admitted no other reading.

* * *

At midmorning he convened the dockworkers—not all of them, just the twelve whose shifts overlapped Tuesday night—and conducted the interviews in the drying shed among the hanging nets, where the smell of salt and rot kept men uncomfortable and talkative.

They gave him what they had, in fragments. A stranger seen near the tide gate. A stranger asking after the counting house. A stranger, said one boy too young to lie well, limping, holding his left arm against his ribs like a bird with a broken wing.

Limping. Wounded. Good. A wounded man moved slowly, hid badly, and needed help, and help was the one commodity in San Ildefonso more expensive than pearls. Every person who aided him became a thread, and threads could be counted.

"You'll each receive one peso," Voss told them, "for your time."

They blinked. Fear curdled into confusion. This was not how the Captain behaved.

"For your time," he repeated, "and for remembering anything else. Memory improves with compensation. If anyone recalls further details—a face, a voice, a place where a tired man might sleep—you'll bring it to me directly, and there will be another peso. No one gets punished for helping the company keep accurate records."

He watched the word *accurate* land. He always used accounting language with the workers. Ledgers, records, accuracy, balances. It confused them pleasantly and told them nothing, and confusion plus ignorance equaled compliance. Herr Brandt again. The old man would have been proud.

One peso per worker, perhaps three pesos total in follow-up payments. Against that, consider the cost if the courier reached the capital with proof of falsified rolls: investigations, restitution claims, wages owed to families of drowned divers going back years, the founder's name dragged before a magistrate, the board's credit collapsing like a rotted pier. Voss had once estimated, privately, what the death-roll savings totaled. It was a handsome figure. But against ruin, even a handsome figure was a rounding error. You spent three pesos or you lost the company. Any fool could do that sum.

Which was why, walking back along the seawall, he permitted himself a moment of genuine professional satisfaction. Because there was a fifth item he had not yet written down, one that narrowed the field beautifully:

Item five: whoever helped the courier would have to be someone invisible. Ports were full of loud people demanding things, and loud people got remembered. But a wounded foreigner hiding among nets, needing food, needing silence, needing passage—such a man would seek out precisely the sort nobody saw. A servant. A laborer. Someone whose presence registered on no one's mind at all.

Voss paused at the rail and looked down at the compound yard, where the divers' families moved about their work like slow fish in a tank. Women sorting. Children hauling. The girl Okonkwo crossing the yard with her basket, spine straight, eyes down, exactly as she had crossed it every day for eight years.

He noticed her the way he noticed all of them: as a line in an inventory. Useful hands, quiet disposition, no debts of consequence, no family beyond the cook. She was, in the strictest sense, beneath suspicion—which was itself a category he kept, because in his experience *beneath suspicion* merely meant *not yet audited*.

He filed her away where he filed everything, and moved on.

* * *

By afternoon he had set the machine running. Two men watching the tide gate. A man at each road out of town—the mountain track and the river path—ostensibly checking cargo seals, actually checking faces. Word passed quietly to the ferry master at the river crossing, who owed Voss the kind of debt that didn't appear in any book but was honored with perfect regularity anyway. And a standing order with the gate guards: any worker requesting papers, passes, or travel chits was to be reported to him within the hour, regardless of rank.

Then he sat down at his desk in the enforcement office, took out a clean sheet, and began the exercise he trusted more than any informer: the list.

Every person with access to the counting house. He wrote the names in pencil, ranked by opportunity. Clerks first, obviously, though Reyes's old post sat empty and the junior men were watched around the clock by their own ambition—if one of them had found letters from a dead predecessor, he'd have sold them to the board for a promotion, not smuggled them anywhere. Then the cleaners. Then the errand-runners. Then the cooks, because food went everywhere in a compound and food was never searched.

He worked down the page, adding annotations in his small precise hand. Access patterns. Habits. Debts. Who owed the company, who hated it, who was too stupid to be dangerous, who might be smart enough.

Near the bottom, in the section marked *servants and laborers*, his pencil paused over a name.

Okonkwo, Ines. Diver's daughter. Eight years indenture. Counting house cleaning detail, three days weekly. No recorded incidents. Father deceased, spring run, year of '92. Mother none known. Guardian: Rosa Ferreira, head cook.

His pencil hovered. There was nothing there. That was the trouble. Eight years of nothing was either innocence or discipline, and discipline looked exactly like innocence until you peeled it. The girl cleaned the room where the ledgers lived. The girl read—yes, he'd noted that once, years ago, a small anomaly: caught glancing at pages she shouldn't, corrected herself instantly, never repeated the offense. Corrected herself *instantly*. Amateurs froze when caught. Professionals adjusted. He had logged it and dismissed it, because a child memorizing numbers was a curiosity, not a threat.

But that was before a dead man's courier came ashore asking directions to the counting house.

Voss drew no circle around her name. Circles were conclusions, and conclusions drawn early were expensive. Instead he wrote a single notation beside it—*watch*—and turned the page.

Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the channel, lifting the boats. Somewhere in the compound a wounded man was being hidden, or fed, or bandaged, by someone whose name he would extract in time. Time was the one resource Voss possessed in abundance and spent without sentiment, because sentiment, like anger, was expense.

He sharpened his pencil, squared his papers, and settled in to wait for the next figure in the column to present itself.

It always did. Accounts, Herr Brandt used to say, always balance—in the end, someone pays.
