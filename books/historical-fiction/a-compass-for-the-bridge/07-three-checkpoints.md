## 7. Three Checkpoints

The counting house kept a wall map of the company's routes, yellowed at the corners and pinned with brass tacks gone green, and for eight years I had dusted around it without once letting my eyes rest on it longer than a servant's eyes should rest on anything.

Now I stood before it in the dark after the lamps were banked, and I let myself look.

* * *

I had told Elias no. I wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in the ledger of my own skull: when he begged me in the net-loft to carry his letters north, I said *no*, and I meant it, and I went back to the compound and slept—or lay still pretending to sleep—beside three other girls who breathed like tired animals and dreamed of nothing worse than pearls.

But Mama Rosa's story had cracked something, and cracks do not close because you wish them shut. Water finds them. Truth finds them. I lay awake doing the one thing I had trained myself never to do: I noticed.

So the next evening, when the compound settled and the guards changed shift and the counting house stood empty except for its ghosts of arithmetic, I slipped in through the door I had oiled months ago without knowing why, and I looked at the map.

Three hundred miles, port to capital. The road left San Ildefonso through the tide gate—that was not even a checkpoint, really, just a gate, a guard, a habit of stopping everyone who passed with anything larger than a basket. Then the road climbed into the mountains for two days to the customs house at Puerto Cielo, where every crate and satchel was weighed, opened, and stamped. Then down along the river to the ferry crossing at Vado Bravo, where the water was too deep to ford and every soul crossing was logged by name.

And at Vado Bravo, according to the map's neat annotation in faded ink—*station master: A. Voss*—the ferry answered to the cousin of the man whose boots I could hear in my sleep.

I traced the route with one finger, not quite touching the paper, the way you trace a scar.

Three doors between here and the magistrate. Three places where a girl with no papers and no name worth writing would be stopped, turned over, searched. And inside whatever they searched would be a bundle of letters written in the hand of a dead man, each one heavy enough to hang us both.

"You're looking at it wrong."

I nearly came out of my skin. Elias stood in the doorway, moving like a man whose ribs argued with every step, and he closed the door behind him as softly as a tide coming in.

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm supposed to be dead," he said. "Resting is an improvement." He came to stand beside me, following my finger up the map. "You've got it in your head that you need to hide the letters. Somewhere dark. Somewhere clever."

"Somewhere they won't look."

"They'll look everywhere," he said. "Voss doesn't search. He *unpacks*. I watched him go through a courier's belongings once, piece by piece, patient as a surgeon. He found a letter sewn into a coat seam, another rolled inside a candle. He is very good at finding things that are hidden." He paused. "What he has never once done, in all the years I carried paper for Reyes, is read anything he didn't think was meant for him."

I turned that over. "He can't read?"

"He reads fine. He just doesn't read what bores him." Elias tapped the map, right on the counting house square that sat at the center of San Ildefonso like a spider in its web. "You work in there. You told me. Ledgers, duplicates, audits. What goes north this month?"

"The audit copies," I said, before I remembered I wasn't supposed to know the schedule. Too late; it was true anyway. "Every year after harvest season. The pearl ledgers—the real ones stay here, but duplicate fair copies go to the capital for the magistrate's auditors. Company property, sealed, escorted."

"Escorted by whom?"

"Company men. Voss picks them himself."

"And when the escort hands those ledgers to the auditors," Elias said slowly, "does anyone open them on the road? Check them against the originals?"

"No. That's the point of the seal. The seal says the contents are exactly what the cover claims." Something was happening in my chest, a warmth I didn't trust yet, the feeling of numbers beginning to line up in columns. "The seal isn't protection from inspection. It's a promise that none is needed."

Elias smiled for the first time since I'd fished him out of the nets, and it made his bruised face look almost young. "Reyes always said the best hiding place isn't darkness. It's boredom."

* * *

We worked by feel more than light, whispering over the map while the compound slept its exhausted sleep, and what we built that night was less an escape plan than an argument—a proof, the way Reyes's letters were a proof.

The tide gate first. I walked it twice a week carrying wash or messages; the guard there, Ferro, had known me since I was small enough to steal mangoes off the company boats. He had never once looked inside my basket. He looked at my face instead, and my face was a document he had already filed under *nothing to worry about*. The gate would cost us nothing if I went alone, carrying something ordinary.

"That leaves me," Elias said. "I'm a foreigner with a burned brand on my wrist. I can't walk anywhere."

"You can't walk anywhere *yet*. Can you ride?"

"Barely."

"Then you don't exist until the last door. I carry everything that matters."

"And if they take you at the second door?"

"Then the letters are lost and so am I, and Reyes should find a better courier than a dead man and a diver's daughter." I said it flatly, and heard how much of Papa was in the flatness. "But they won't take me at the second door. Listen."

Puerto Cielo, the mountain customs house, was run by inspectors who lived on bribes and boredom in equal measure. Every traveler was searched—but searching was not the same as seeing. I had cleaned the counting house long enough to understand the difference. A clerk's errand girl, traveling with company chits, carrying nothing but a satchel of paperwork, would be waved through by men whose hands were busy with the cargo carts behind her. The danger was papers I didn't have. Chits, waybills, a letter of passage.

"I can write them," I said, and the words felt like stepping off a boat into deep water. "I've copied ten thousand entries. I know the format of a waybill the way you know your own hands. The countersignature is the hard part—I'd have to imitate a supervisor's hand."

"Can you?"

I thought about it honestly. "I learned to write by copying other people's handwriting in secret. It's the only kind of writing I do well."

Elias laughed, then winced and pressed his ribs. "God. Reyes would love you."

"Don't say things like that."

"Why not?"

Because it made the plan warm, and warm things make you brave, and brave things get people killed. But I didn't say that. I said, "The third door. Vado Bravo. That's not boredom, that's blood. Voss's cousin runs the ferry, and if word travels ahead of us—and it will, Voss will send word the moment he suspects—the cousin won't be bored. He'll be hunting."

"So we don't arrive at Vado Bravo as a girl with a satchel. We arrive as something he's afraid to touch." Elias studied the map again. "What does the audit schedule say about deadlines?"

"Fair copies must reach the capital registrar within the month or the company forfeits its trading license for the quarter. Everyone knows it. The ferry master knows it better than most—he gets blamed when company freight sits at his crossing past deadline."

Elias's smile came back, slower this time. "Then you arrive at Vado Bravo carrying the thing his own neck depends on, and you make sure he knows it. Power obeys power. If you can't be powerful, borrow the shape of it."

I stepped back from the map and looked at the whole route at once, and for the first time it did not look like a wall. It looked like a ledger. Entries and counter-entries. Each checkpoint a column, each guard a figure who balanced his own books of fear and greed, and every column had a weakness if you read far enough down.

That was the terrible discovery of that night—not that the route was passable, but that it was passable *because* it was corrupt. The system that had erased my father was a system built on men trusting paper more than truth. And paper, unlike truth, could be forged, folded, sewn, smuggled. Paper took orders.

I had spent eight years learning to be invisible inside that system. It had never occurred to me that invisibility was a skill, and skills could be aimed.

* * *

"There's still the question," Elias said near midnight, when the oil in our stolen lamp had burned low, "of where the letters actually ride."

"Not on me."

"Not on either of us, if we're smart. If Voss takes me—and he may, he's close, I can feel it—he searches me down to the stitching. If he takes you—" He stopped, because neither of us wanted the sentence finished. "The letters need a carrier that can't be interrogated. Something that moves under escort, passes every checkpoint without being questioned, and arrives in the capital whether we live or die."

"And what moves like that?" I asked, though I already knew. I had known since he said *boredom*.

"Company property," Elias said. "Sealed company property. What's going north this month that nobody on God's earth would dare open?"

The lamp flame leaned in the draft, and in its light the whole plan assembled itself the way a page balances: columns closing, totals agreeing.

"The pearl ledgers," I said. "The audit copies. We hide the letters inside Meridian's own books, and we let Meridian carry its own condemnation to the magistrate's desk."

Silence. Even the sea outside seemed to hold its breath.

"It's madness," Elias said at last, reverent.

"It's bookkeeping," I said. "It's the only language this company respects. So we'll speak it."

There was a duplicate fair copy already begun in the counting house—started by old Manolo before his hands shook too badly to rule lines straight, set aside unfinished, scheduled to be completed and bound before the audit escort departed. No one touched it but me. I cleaned the room; I squared its papers; I was furniture with fingers. Over the next days I would finish the binding work myself, and no one would question the invisible girl doing visible labor, because that was the one miracle this place believed in.

One problem remained, and I said it aloud because saying it made it manageable, made it an entry rather than a fear: the binding was thick cord-stitched leather, and the letters would change its thickness, and Manolo might notice, and Voss might count pages.

"Sew them flat," Elias said. "Against the spine boards, under the endpapers. Books are judged by their faces, not their spines. And Ines—" He caught my arm, gently, wincing. "If this goes wrong. If Voss takes you. You don't know where they are. You finished a ledger, that's all. Swear it."

"I don't lie well."

"You lie beautifully. You've been lying since the day you were born—it was called surviving. Just aim it outward for once."

Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it knocking at the seawall like a creditor. Two days, maybe three, until the audit copy was due to travel. Two days to finish a book that would testify for the drowned.

And below us, faint across the compound, I heard boots on gravel stop beneath the net-loft window, pause a long moment, and move on. Patient. Unhurried. The walk of a man doing arithmetic.

Elias heard it too. He blew out the lamp, and in the dark we stood very still, two figures in a room full of paper, waiting for the count to come out even.
