## 8. Pearls and Paper

The counting house smelled of camphor and old glue, and by lamplight it looked like what it was: a room where men had learned to make drowning profitable.

Ines sat at the low table with the duplicate ledger open before her, a bone folder in one hand and a pot of wheat-starch paste cooling at her elbow. Binding repair was her work now—had been for two months, ever since she'd made the mistake of fixing a cracked spine in front of Don Aurelio's assistant instead of pretending not to know how. *Useful*, Mama Rosa had always said. *Useful is safe.* But usefulness had a way of pulling you deeper into rooms you should never have entered, and tonight the room held three ledgers, one of them scheduled to travel to the capital under armed seal, and a bundle of letters hidden in the false bottom of her mending basket that could hang every man whose name appeared on these pages.

She had not slept. She did not expect to sleep again until this was finished or she was dead, and between those two outcomes there was very little daylight left.

The letters lay against her hip where the basket sat close, and she could feel them the way you feel a coal wrapped in cloth—a heat that wasn't temperature. Eleven sheets in Reyes's hand. Dated after his death. Naming ships, naming divers, naming sums: *José Alcantara, drowned June 1889, wages accrued 41 pesos, transferred to company account per standing order.* Eleven sheets, and somewhere in the middle of them, in a clerk's tidy cursive that had never once trembled:

*Ibarra, Emilio. Died on the third dive. Debt forgiven as deceased. Wages forfeit.*

Her father.

She had read that line forty times since Elias pressed the bundle into her hands, and every time her eye snagged on it like a nail in cloth. Forty-one words. Her father's whole death, reduced to an entry that balanced.

*Stop.* She made herself look away from the basket. *Work.*

The duplicate ledger was called the audit copy, and it was a beautiful thing if you liked lies bound in calfskin. Every pearl lot from the season, graded and weighed and priced; every diver's tally entered in the company's favor; every debt carried forward with compound interest computed by hand in Don Aurelio's office while the divers who owed it slept on rope pallets twelve to a room. The magistrate in the capital required a certified copy each year—it was law, some distant law written by men who had never seen a luggers' deck—and the copy traveled south with an escort, sealed in oilcloth, stamped twice, handled by clerks who believed what they read because reading was their whole religion.

This year the audit fell in nine days. And this year, Ines Okonkwo was the only person alive who knew both how the ledger was bound and what needed hiding inside it.

She turned to the back board. The binding was French-style, sewn over raised cords, with a hollow between the spine lining and the boards where a careful binder sometimes tucked reinforcing strips of linen. A careful binder could tuck other things.

Eleven letters. Folded small—they were already folded small; Reyes had been a courier's master before he'd been anything else—eleven folded sheets would make a bundle perhaps the width of two fingers, thin as a razor if pressed flat. The hollow would take them. The spine lining would cover them. And over the lining, fresh leather dyed to match, and over the leather, paste, and over everything—

The company's own stamp. Meridian Trading Company, San Ildefonso Station, in violet ink, applied by Don Aurelio's own hand because he trusted no one else with the seal. He would hold the finished ledger in his hands, turn it over, satisfy himself that the binding was sound, and stamp it. He would certify his own condemnation with his own ring.

Something moved through her chest then, hot and frightening, and she recognized it after a moment because she had felt it so rarely she barely knew its name.

It was laughter.

* *

She worked by stages, because haste was the one thing she could not afford.

First the measurements. She cut a strip of scrap paper to the exact dimensions of the folded bundle and slid it into the hollow, testing the lie of it, marking with a pinprick where the spine lining would need to sit proud by a hair's width. Too tight and the boards wouldn't close true—Don Aurelio had handled a hundred ledgers; he would feel the difference the way a diver feels a shifted weight in his chest. Too loose and the pages would whisper when they moved. Paper talked. She had spent eight years learning what paper said about the people who kept it, and she knew it whispered when it was frightened.

Second the letters themselves. She unwrapped them at last, all eleven, and laid them face-down on clean linen so she would not have to read them again—not because she couldn't bear it, but because she couldn't afford to. Reading slowed the hands, and the hands had to be perfect tonight. Tomorrow night too, if the paste took slowly. Three nights of work compressed into whatever darkness remained.

But before she folded them into their final shape, she allowed herself one last look—not at the words but at the hand itself. Reyes wrote the way water runs downhill: economical, inevitable, every letter leaning slightly forward as if hurrying toward something. Eight years of sorting pearls beside the counting house window had taught her the clerks' hands through the glass. She knew Don Aurelio's crabbed capitals, the junior clerk's flourishes, Voss's blunt initials on punishment orders. And she knew that any auditor worth his salt would recognize this forward-leaning script as a company hand, trained in this company's school, which meant the letters would read as authentic before anyone parsed a single word.

That mattered. She hadn't understood why until she'd lain awake composing the argument she would never get to make. If the letters looked planted, they'd be dismissed as planted. They had to look like what they were: paperwork that had escaped. A dead man's filing, surfacing.

*Paper testifies,* she thought, folding the first sheet along its original creases. *You just have to bind it properly.*

Third—the sewing. This was the part her hands loved even as the rest of her shook, because sewing was the oldest thing she knew. Her mother had died with a needle still warm; her father had re-laced net by lamplight while Ines, six years old, passed him the twine. Her stitches were famous among the sorters, though none of them knew it, because famous was another word for visible. Tonight fame could go to hell. Tonight her stitches would be invisible or nothing.

Linen thread, waxed. Curved needle borrowed from the sail-mender's kit and returned without his noticing, because noticing things was how people got hurt in San Ildefonso. She started at the headband and worked down the spine, catching the new lining over the hollow, and above the hollow her needle passed through sound binding, and below it the same, so that the only evidence of her work was a section of thread that ran *behind* the letters rather than through them—two inches of stitch that anchored nothing, served nothing, and existed solely so the sewing pattern would look unbroken to a man who ran his thumb along the spine and counted threads.

She had invented that trick herself, lying awake. She thought of it now as the liar's stitch, and she hated it, and she was prouder of it than of anything she had ever made.

Halfway down the spine, the lamp guttered.

Ines went absolutely still.

It was nothing. Wind through the shutters, the cheap coconut oil doing what cheap oil does. But for three full seconds she sat with the needle suspended and listened to her own heart count, and in those three seconds she saw the whole architecture of her ruin: Voss in the doorway, the basket overturned, eleven sheets fanned across the floor like a hand of cards she could not take back. Voss didn't rage—that was the terrible thing about him; the dockworkers' stories agreed on it. He *counted*. He would pick up each sheet and read it in order and then he would look at her the way he looked at a ledger that didn't balance, and he would say something quiet, and that would be the end of Ines Okonkwo, and the letters would burn in the courtyard stove before breakfast.

The lamp steadied. The flame stood up straight again, yellow and ordinary.

She put the needle back in the leather and drove it home.

* *

Near the third hour, Mama Rosa came.

Ines heard her before she saw her—the particular drag of the left foot, the soft clink of the keys at her belt that every child in the compound had once used as a clock. Mama Rosa crossed the counting house without asking why her foster daughter was awake at the hour when even the cats had given up, set down a covered bowl of cassava porridge, and stood looking at the ledger with an expression Ines couldn't read.

"You're repairing it," Mama Rosa said. Not a question. Nothing was ever a question with her; questions invited answers, and answers were dangerous currency.

"The spine was cracked."

"Mm." Mama Rosa picked up the bone folder, turned it over, set it down exactly where it had been—to the inch, Ines noted; the old woman missed nothing and performed missing nothing even better. "Don Aurelio wants it finished Thursday. Sealed Friday. On the boat Saturday."

"I know the schedule."

"Of course you do." Mama Rosa looked at her then, really looked, the way she had not looked since the night she'd told her about the falsified roll—the night the ground had opened under eight years of careful blindness and shown Ines her father's name in a dead man's ledger. The look lasted two seconds. It contained a question Mama Rosa would never ask aloud, and an answer Mama Rosa would never want spoken, and beneath both of them, plain as a brand: *I know what you are doing, girl, and I am telling you I know, because if I can see it then others might, and you had better be more careful than you think you are.*

"Porridge," Mama Rosa said. "Eat it while it's hot. Cold porridge is an insult to the cassava." And she gathered her empty bowl from yesterday, clinked her keys, and dragged her left foot out into the dark.

Ines ate the porridge. It was the best thing she had tasted in a year, and she tasted almost none of it.

* *

Dawn found the ledger closed.

She had done everything except the finishing: the matching leather over the repaired section, the paste, the final pressing under weights overnight so the boards would set true. That remained—two hours' work tomorrow night, no more, and then the ledger would go to Don Aurelio's desk and out of her life forever, carrying its secret south like a swallowed hook.

She wrapped the tools. She buried the letters' wrapping paper in the ash bin and stirred the ashes. She wiped the table down with camphor so the smell of fresh paste wouldn't reach anyone's nose before the leather covered the evidence. Then she stood in the gray light with her palms flat on the closed cover and let herself feel it, fully, for the first time.

The ledger weighed maybe four pounds. It had taken her most of a night, and it would take nine days to travel, and inside its spine rode the only true account of eight years of drowning that existed anywhere on earth. The company had built a machine for making deaths disappear, and she had fed the deaths back into it, stitched into its own flesh, dressed in its own skin, sealed with its own seal. Let it carry them. Let it deliver them. Let some clerk in the capital crack the spine and find the dead waiting in the binding, patient as pearls.

Her hands were trembling. She watched them tremble with a kind of detached interest, as if they belonged to another woman—one who had done something enormous and irreversible while Ines herself had simply followed the stitches.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and could not land on: it had felt *good*. Not righteous—she didn't trust righteousness; righteousness was loud, and loud got people killed. Good the way a held breath feels good when it finally leaves. For eight years her whole self had been organized around a single instruction—*be unseen, be useful, survive*—and last night she had used every skill that instruction had built her, every stolen hour of watching, every memorized number, every invisible stitch, and aimed them all the other way. The wall was still a wall. She had just found out she was the mortar.

Elias had begged her to run the letters to the capital himself, wounded as he was, and she had refused him, and he had looked at her like a man watching his last coin roll into a drain. Now she understood what she hadn't been able to tell him: she hadn't refused because she was afraid. She'd refused because a wounded foreigner with a branded wrist couldn't cross three checkpoints, but a ledger could. The company trusted its paperwork more than it trusted any living soul—paperwork didn't bleed, didn't flinch, didn't remember. Well. This paperwork remembered.

She blew out the lamp. Gray light filled the counting house, and in it she checked her work one final time, running her own thumb along the spine the way Don Aurelio would, counting threads. Even. True. Silent.

Only one flaw anywhere: at the base of the spine, where her needle had made its last pass, a single thread-end protruded less than a hair's width past the leather. Invisible unless you were looking. Invisible unless you were her.

She decided to leave it.

Let there be one sign, she thought—some mark only she would know, so that wherever the ledger traveled, however many hands it passed through, one person on earth could put her finger to that spot and say: *I did this. The dead are in here. I put them there myself.*

Outside, the compound bell rang the morning shift, and somewhere beyond the walls Captain Voss was already awake, already counting, already hunting a courier who was lying feverish ten feet from where Ines stood. Nine days until the boat. Nine days of being exactly what she had always been—useful, silent, blank-faced—in a world that had just changed shape around her without changing appearance at all.

She squared the ledger on the table, ready for Don Aurelio's inspection, and went to wake Elias, because the fever had broken in the night and fever-broken men asked questions, and every answer she gave him now was a checkpoint of its own.

The first one came before she reached the door.

"Ines." His voice, rough from the nets' shadows behind the drying shed. "The audit copy—did you finish it?"

She stopped. Through the shutter slats the sea was turning from gray to gold, and she thought of her father swimming up through exactly that light, twenty-two years ago, toward a surface that had already erased him.

"It's finished," she said. "Now we have to survive it."
