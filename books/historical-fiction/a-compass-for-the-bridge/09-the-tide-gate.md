## 9. The Tide Gate

The ledger weighed nothing and everything.

That was the trick of it—Ines had spent the whole night telling herself so. Fourteen letters, sewn flat into the spine of a duplicate audit book, pressed between signatures and sums, and the whole thing fit under her arm like any other burden she had carried since childhood. It was lighter than a bucket of shells. It was lighter than the pearl sacks she'd hauled at seven years old, before anyone thought to teach her that girls who read were girls who talked, and girls who talked were girls who drowned.

But when she lifted it from beneath Mama Rosa's floorboard at first light, her arms shook as if it were made of iron.

*It's paper,* she told it silently. *You're paper. Paper can't hang you.*

Paper could. Paper had built Meridian's fortune out of names written twice—once alive in the wage columns, once dead in the rolls—and Ines knew now which entries were lies because she had memorized them the way other people memorized prayers. Diver 114, Anselmo Okonkwo. Status: deceased. Debt transferred to company. Wages forfeited.

Her father, filed away in an accountant's handwriting, eight years before she ever learned to notice.

She wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, then in the faded blue shawl Mama Rosa pressed on her at the door—the same shawl she wore to market every week for six years, so worn into habit that no one would look at it twice. That was the plan entire: be the girl with the shawl, be the errand girl, be nobody. The audit copy was scheduled to travel to the capital on the Thursday packet, escorted by a company rider, because the company trusted its own paperwork the way drowning men trusted driftwood.

All she had to do was get it through the tide gate.

* * *

The tide gate stood where San Ildefonso narrowed to meet the sea, a stone throat between the counting house quarter and the working docks. Everything left the compound through it—pearls, shell, salt fish, ledgers, and once a year, if the tides were cruel, a body on a plank. The gate had two guards by day and four by night, but only one of the day guards mattered this morning.

Old Fermin.

He had known her since she was small enough to ride her father's shoulders down to the water. He had been the one who fished her out of a riptide when she was nine, hauled her up by the collar, and said nothing about it to anyone, because reporting it would have meant questions about why a bonded child was swimming unsupervised, and Fermin did not believe in questions. He believed in tides, in tobacco, in the slow dignity of standing in the same spot for thirty years watching everything and stopping nothing that mattered.

He was there now, leaning on his pike the way he always leaned, hat brim low against the morning glare, and Ines felt her heart begin to hammer before he'd even seen her.

This was the part no one warned you about. Not the danger—that was clean, a thing with edges you could grip. It was the *performance*. Eight years of being invisible had given her a face like still water, and now she had to wear that face while carrying a fire inside the binding of a book, and the gap between the two was where she might die.

*Be useful,* she reminded herself. *Be boring. Be exactly what he expects.*

She adjusted the ledger under her arm—carefully, naturally, the way clerks' errand girls adjust things—and walked toward the gate at the pace of someone thinking about nothing more interesting than breakfast.

* * *

The morning helped her. Mornings at the tide gate were always a chaos of ordinary commerce: shell carts rattling through, divers' wives arguing over stall placements, the packet boat's crew shouting for their cargo manifest, a knot of company men in clean shirts pointing at things they didn't understand. Noise was cover. Motion was cover. A girl with a ledger was furniture moving through furniture.

Ines let the current of the crowd carry her to within ten paces of Fermin before he noticed her, and when he did, his face did what it always did—nothing much. A nod. The smallest lifting of the chin.

"Errands early," he said. Not a question. Old men at gates made statements instead of questions; it saved effort.

"The audit copies." She heard her own voice come out flat and correct, and marveled distantly that it could. "For the packet. Señor Alvear wants them checked aboard before the tide turns."

Alvear was the second clerk, a soft man with ink-stained cuffs who genuinely did want things checked early, which made him perfect to invoke, because invoking him cost him nothing and confirmed nothing. This was another thing the ledgers had taught her without meaning to: the best lie is a true sentence about someone else.

Fermin grunted. He looked past her at the crowd, at the carts, at the sea doing its eternal arithmetic against the stones.

"Tide's already turned," he said. "Packet'll sit till midday."

"Then I have time." She smiled—a small, tired, market-day smile, the smile of a girl whose feet hurt—and it terrified her how easily it came. "Unless you'd like to carry it back and explain to Alvear why it sat in the sun."

Something moved behind Fermin's eyes. Not suspicion. Amusement, maybe, or the particular patience of a man who has watched children grow into their cleverness and knows better than to encourage it. He stepped aside half a pace, waving her through with the pike.

"Go on, then. Mind the cart tracks, they've mud on them again."

"Thank you, Fermin."

"Ines."

She stopped. One foot past the shadow of the gate, one foot still inside it, and her pulse went from hammering to something worse—silent, total, like the moment between a wave breaking and the water arriving.

* * *

He didn't turn her around. He didn't even look at the ledger. He looked at her, and that was almost harder, because Fermin had never in her life looked *at* her—he had looked near her, around her, through her, the way everyone sensible did. Being seen by him now, of all mornings, felt like standing in shallow water and feeling the sand pull away.

"You've grown into your mother's walk," he said.

"My mother died when I was four."

"I know it." He resettled his hat. "I carried the news to your father. Down at the diver's quarters. He was rigging lines and he didn't stop rigging, just kept working while I told him, and when I finished he said thank you and kept working. That's how I knew he was a man who'd drown himself before he'd quit." Fermin spat tidily into the channel below, where the tide ran brown and quick. "Anselmo's daughter. Walking out the gate with company papers."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped stone. She had not heard her father's name spoken aloud in years—not by anyone. On the rolls he was a number. In the compound he was a cautionary story told to new divers in lowered voices. In her own skull he was a locked room she walked past daily at speed.

"Fermin," she said carefully, "I have to get these checked aboard."

"I know you do." And then, quieter, with the gate's noise washing over them both: "Strange season, this one. Voss had men out all night asking after boats. Somebody came in off the water who wasn't meant to. You hear anything, you keep your head down and your mouth shut. Same as always." His eyes flicked, just once, to the ledger under her arm—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—and came back to her face. "Same as always, girl."

Did he know? Could he know? No. Impossible. He was making conversation the way old men at gates did, wrapping warning in weather talk, and if his gaze had touched the ledger it was only because ledgers were the most interesting thing passing through his gate all day.

Or because he'd been standing at this stone throat for thirty years watching everything, and things that passed through throats had a way of passing through him too.

"Same as always," she echoed.

"Go on, then."

She went.

* * *

On the far side of the gate, the world opened. The harbor spread out gray-green and glittering, the packet boat riding at anchor with its lazy smoke, the hills beyond stacked up in morning haze going gold at the edges. The capital lay somewhere past all of it—two days' travel, three checkpoints, one magistrate. The ledger burned against her ribs like a coal wrapped in wool.

She made herself walk to the quay, make the handoff arrangements with the packet man—Thursday, midday, manifest signature, all of it ordinary—and only when she was walking back, empty-armed in the way that was suddenly, achingly wrong, did she let herself feel it.

She had done it. The first gate. Passed with a smile and a dead man's name in her mouth—no. Not a dead man's name. Her father's name, alive in Fermin's mouth after eight years underwater. *Anselmo's daughter.* As if the roll that erased him couldn't reach past the counting house walls. As if memory were a country Meridian's ledgers had never conquered.

She stopped at the edge of the quay and breathed, and the breathing hurt, and she understood something with terrible clarity: she could not do this by being invisible anymore. Invisible had gotten her through Fermin. But somewhere ahead was a customs inspector with sharp eyes, and a ferry master who shared blood with Captain Voss, and a hunter already circling the nets for a stranger. Sooner or later invisibility would fail her, and when it did, whatever was underneath it would have to be enough.

Underneath it, apparently, was a girl who could lie fluently in the language of ledgers and walk on shaking legs and think about her father's hands on rigging lines while her heart tried to claw its way out of her chest.

It would have to be enough.

* * *

She was halfway back along the net-drying racks when she saw the company men.

Three of them, in Voss's livery, and they were not patrolling. Patrolling had a rhythm—walk, look, walk—and these men had none. They were moving through the drying racks in a line, methodical as comb teeth, tipping over baskets, running hands along the hanging nets, pulling apart the little shelters of sacking where the beach people sometimes slept. Where Elias had slept, two nights ago, before she'd moved him at dusk to the cooperage loft because the nets suddenly felt too open, too watched, though she hadn't known why she felt it.

Now she knew.

One of the men straightened up holding something—a scrap of cloth, gray, foreign weave—and showed it to the others, and even at forty paces Ines could read their body language the way she read ledgers: the lean-in, the quick conference, the way one man broke off at a run toward the counting house quarter.

Toward Voss.

Ines did not run. Running was visibility; visibility was death; eight years of training held her spine straight and her stride even while her mind screamed. She walked—walked, *walked*—past the mouth of the lane, past the racks, cataloguing everything in the corner of her eye the way she'd once catalogued debt transfers she was never meant to see. Three men. Cloth found. Runner dispatched. Nets searched on the seaward side.

Which meant the landward side would be next. Which meant the cooperage loft—her loft, her hiding place, her own bed of sacking where a wounded courier lay feverish under a false floor—had hours, maybe less.

And the Thursday packet was two days away.

She reached the corner of the cookhouse and let herself pause, one hand on the wall, a tired errand girl catching her breath. From here she could see the cooperage roofline, unbroken, innocent. From here she could also see, at the far end of the compound, a figure she knew by silhouette alone, standing in the doorway of the counting house with his hands clasped behind his back, looking out over his territory with the calm of a man checking figures that had finally, agreeably, begun to add up.

Captain Voss had found thread.

Ines pushed off the wall and walked on, even-paced, blank-faced, while inside her the plan and the panic tore loose from each other and began to race.

Two days. She had promised Elias two days, and the packet, and the mountain road beyond. Now she had perhaps until nightfall, a sick man upstairs, and a choice assembling itself in her chest with the cold click of an abacus: save him and lose the letters, or save the letters and learn what her silence was truly worth.

Voss turned his head, slowly, and looked down the length of the compound—past the racks, past the cookhouse, past her.

Ines kept walking.
