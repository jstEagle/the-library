## 10. Elias Taken

The ledger was safe. That was the first thing I made myself count, the way I counted pearls, the way I counted everything, because numbers did not tremble.

It lay where I had left it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the third flagstone past the broken jetty, under six inches of sand that no one swept because no one cared about the ground beyond the tide gate. The company's own audit ledger, bound now around a second spine of folded letters, each one a dead man's wages, each one signed by a clerk who had been two years in his grave. It would sit there until Thursday, when the supply cart took the northern road and I went with it, an errand girl carrying nothing worth searching for.

Safe. I said the word to myself like a password. Safe, safe, safe.

And then I turned back toward the compound, because Elias was still in the drying nets, because a promise is also a thing you can lose, and because I had not yet learned — God help me, I had not yet learned — that some things you carry are safer than any person you love.

I smelled the search before I saw it.

* * *

Fish, tar, and lamp oil. That was what the net yard smelled like every evening of my life, the smell I could have navigated by blindfolded, the smell of my whole narrow world. Tonight there was something else underneath it, sharp and official: whale-oil lanterns, the kind the guards carried, burning hot enough to sting.

The drying racks had been pulled apart.

Not searched — *dismantled*. Nets that took four men to haul had been dragged off their frames and thrown down in gray heaps. The bamboo spreaders were snapped, two of them, snapped the way you snap kindling when you are not being careful because none of it belongs to you. A barrel of cork floats had been overturned and rolled across the yard, leaving a trail through the wet sand like the tracks of some enormous stupid animal.

Three guards stood at the far end with lanterns. They were not looking at the nets anymore. They were looking at a fourth man kneeling between them, and the kneeling man was not resisting, because his hands had been lashed together in front of him with a length of codline.

Elias.

Even from thirty paces, even in that bad light, I knew him by the way he knelt — not cringing, not collapsed, but settled back on his heels like a man waiting out weather. His shirt was torn at the shoulder where they'd grabbed him. His bared wrist, where the brand had been scarred over, caught the lantern light.

And standing over him, with his hands clasped behind his back and his head tilted like a man reading a column of figures, was Captain Voss.

I had seen Voss a hundred times and never once seen him hurry. He moved through the compound the way the tide moved, inevitable, uninterested, arriving everywhere he intended to be. Now he stood in the wreckage of the net yard and did not raise his voice, and somehow that was worse than shouting. I heard him clearly anyway, because the yard was silent. The whole compound had gone quiet the way it goes quiet when someone falls from a mast — everyone listening, everyone glad it isn't them.

"You came ashore at Point Salmeda," Voss said. "The boat is accounted for. You are not. Someone put you there, and someone fed you since. Those are two someones I want."

Elias said nothing.

"Here is what I know about couriers." Voss crouched, unhurried, so that his face was level with Elias's. "They are given only what they can afford to lose. It is the first rule of the trade, and it is the reason couriers are so cheap. So whatever you were carrying, you have already handed it off, and whoever took it from you is, at this moment, the most valuable thing in San Ildefonso." He paused. "You are not. You are the cheapest thing here. Do you understand what that makes tonight?"

Elias lifted his head. I was too far away to see his face properly, but I saw the shape of what he did with it — the slow, deliberate arrangement of a man making himself stupid, making himself empty, giving them the dull stare of a foreigner who barely speaks the trade tongue. I had worn that face my whole life. I knew every seam of it. And I knew, watching him, that it was not entirely a performance, that pain and fear were doing half the work of flattening him.

But he did not look toward the tide gate. He did not look toward the broken jetty. Whatever they did to him between those lanterns, he kept his eyes on Voss, on nothing, on the middle distance, and never once let them drift toward the one place in this port where the truth was buried under six inches of sand.

*Guards, take him inside,* I thought at him, uselessly. *Look at nothing. Be nobody.*

Voss straightened. "Take him to the coal store," he said to the guards. "Not the cells. The cells have windows, and windows invite sympathy." And then, almost gently, the way a man closes a ledger at the end of a day's accounting: "We will continue in the morning."

They marched him out. The lanterns went with him. The net yard dropped into darkness, and I stood behind the rack of spreaders with my heart going like a pump handle and my mind already — this is the part that shames me, if shame is the right word — already working.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers taught me, before I ever understood I was learning it: when something goes wrong, do not ask who is to blame. Ask what the number now is.

The number now was this: Elias knew the letters existed. Elias knew they had come out of Reyes's hand and gone somewhere in this port. Elias did not know where, because I had made sure of it — not out of cleverness, at first, but out of the old habit, the deep-water habit of telling no one anything. When he'd pressed the bundle into my hands among these same nets, three nights ago, he had begged me to keep them one night while he found his land legs, and I had taken them and hidden them before dawn without telling him where, and by the time we sewed them into the duplicate ledger's binding, I had done it alone, in the counting house, after hours, and sent him to watch the door. He knew the letters went into *a* ledger. He did not know which ledger, which shelf, which room. He did not know about the audit copy. He did not know about Thursday's cart.

He knew me.

That was the flaw in the number. That was the line item that would not balance. Elias had been seen with me — Mama Rosa had seen him, the night cook had seen him, and now Voss had him in a coal store with all the hours between now and morning to make him describe every soul who had ever looked at him sideways. A wounded stranger does not survive in a company port alone. Voss knew that better than anyone. Voss had built his whole career on the fact that nobody survives alone here.

By morning he would have my description. By tomorrow noon he would have my name.

Unless.

I stood in the dark net yard among the ruined nets, and I felt something happen in my chest that I can only describe as a door closing. Not slamming — closing, quietly, the way I closed every door of my life. Behind that door was the Elias of three nights ago, feverish and laughing softly at his own jokes, showing me the letters with hands that shook, saying *you don't have to help, Ines, just don't betray me*, and meaning it, meaning even that I owed him nothing. Behind the door was the man who had taught me the word *magistrate* like it was a spell that worked on more than children. Behind the door was everything in me that wanted to run to the coal store, to do something, anything — to get myself taken too, as if two prisoners were better than one, as if pity ever balanced a column.

Pity would doom us both. That thought arrived with no emotion attached at all, flat and clean as a stamped receipt, and I understood that it was true, and I understood that I was going to act on it, and I understood that the person who acted on it was still me, which was perhaps the worst discovery of the night.

If Elias gave me up — and everyone gives something up eventually; the coal store has ways; I had heard the stories and so had he — then Voss would come looking for a pearl-sorter with access to the counting house. There were eleven of us with keys or borrowed access. Eleven names, and mine among them, and I could not change that.

But I could change everything else.

* * *

I did not go back through the main yard. I went the long way, along the sea wall, through the gap in the salt-store fence that the children used and that the guards pretended not to know about because the head guard's own nephew slipped rum through it. Old paths. Invisible paths. My feet found them without instruction, and I let them, and I watched myself move through my own life like a clerk reviewing entries made by someone else.

At the counting house I used my key.

Eight years I had held that key, and never once thought of it as power. It was a chore. It was *be useful*. Sweep the floor, oil the hinges, carry the ledgers to the press, memorize the columns because reading aloud was forbidden but remembering wasn't, remember everything, tell no one. Tonight the key turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking and the room opened up around me, dark and familiar, smelling of dust and ink and the faint brine that comes off every paper in this port eventually.

The shelves stood in their ranks. On the third shelf, second row, between the copra manifests and the diving-season summaries, sat the audit ledger's twin — the fair copy I had built the false binding from, the one whose stitching pattern I had copied stitch for stitch so that no inspector's thumb would ever find the difference. It sat there looking exactly like what it appeared to be: company property. Boring. Trusted. Untouched.

That was the whole plan, reduced to one sentence now: the company trusts its own paperwork more than it trusts any living person.

So let them interrogate. Let Elias hold out until morning or fail at dawn, it made no difference I could control. What I controlled was this: when Voss compiled his list of eleven names, every name on it had to look the same. No favorites. No anomalies. If I was visible — if I had been seen running, if my lamp burned late, if my key turned at odd hours — I would be the obvious entry, and obvious entries get pulled first.

So I did what the ledger would do. I made myself ordinary.

I lit my lamp. I took the broom and swept the counting house floor, thoroughly, exactly as I did every Thursday eve before the audit cart, because it was Thursday eve before the audit cart. I oiled the hinge on the record cabinet that squeaked. I carried yesterday's spoiled drafts to the burn bin, and while I was at the bin, out in the open yard where anyone could see me, I stood a moment in plain sight, yawning, a tired girl doing a tired chore, and I let two different people see me do it. Anomalies hide in crowds; crowds are made of witnesses. Every witness who could say *she was sweeping, she was boring, she was where she always is* was a brick in the wall between me and the coal store.

Then I went back in, and I sat down at the clerk's desk, and I allowed myself ten minutes — I counted them, I am always counting — to think the thoughts I had locked behind the door.

They will hurt him. Not kill him, not yet; killing is waste, and Voss does not waste. But they will hurt him, and he will lie awake in the dark with his wrists raw, and at some point in the small hours he will think of me, and he will not know whether I ran. That is the cruelty I chose. Not the coal store's cruelty — mine. Because the alternative was to go to him, and if I went to him, the letters died with both of us, and the drowned stayed drowned, and Papa's name stayed erased in ink that was written over his actual body in the actual sea.

A name on a death roll is just ink, Mama Rosa says. But ink is the only immortality this company ever granted anyone, and they had granted none.

I would not trade Elias for nothing. I would carry him forward. That was the arithmetic I could live inside: his capture buys the letters' silence; his silence buys me until Thursday; Thursday buys the capital; the capital buys — maybe — everything. It was cold. I checked it twice, the way I check a column, and it balanced, and I hated that it balanced, and I let it stand.

* * *

I was banking the lamp when the shadow crossed the window.

Not a guard's stride. Too quick, too bent, too familiar. A woman's walk with a basket on her hip, cutting along the salt-store wall where no one with honest business walks at this hour.

Mama Rosa.

She reached the counting house door and knocked — three soft knocks, then one, our old signal from when I was nine and she used to smuggle me fried plantain past the matron. Eighteen years of signals, and she spent one of them tonight, which told me before she spoke how bad it was.

I opened the door and she pushed in fast and shut it behind her, and up close her face was wrong — not frightened, worse than frightened. Resolved.

"They came to the kitchen," she said, low. "Two of Voss's men, asking which workers had counting house access. Not asking Voss-style, shouting. Quiet-asking. Writing names." Her hand found my wrist and gripped it, hard, flour-dusted fingers pressing my pulse as if checking whether I was still alive. "Ines. The list exists already. Your name went on it an hour ago."

"The coal store—"

"Is where the stranger is, yes, and the whole compound knows it, and by morning the whole compound will know a pearl-sorter was friendly with him, because Rosa the washerwoman's boy saw you two talking among the nets Tuesday and children sell cheaper than anyone." She said it all in one breath, the way she recited recipes, and then she stopped, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There is a man coming to dine at the captain's table tonight. From the mainland office. Voss has to host him. Voss has to sit at his own table and pour wine and smile for two hours whether he likes it or not."

I stared at her. "How do you know—"

"Because I volunteered to cook it," Mama Rosa said. "Thirty years I have fed captains and inspectors and owners, and every one of them owes me a small kindness they don't know they promised. I am calling in three of them tonight, and they will buy you until dawn. After dawn—" she shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved her whole body, the shrug of a woman who had buried husbands and daughters to this port and outlived the grief by cooking, "—after dawn, girl, you are on your own, and you had better already be gone."

"Gone." The word sat in the air between us. "The cart doesn't leave until—"

"Then you'll leave before the cart. Or you'll be on the cart early. Or you'll be creative, Ines, because you are the most creative invisible child I ever raised, and I raised you specifically for the day the visible world came looking." She pressed something into my hand, wrapped in cloth and still warm. Food, and under the food, folded small, coins — real ones, not company scrip. "Now listen. One thing, and then I go back before the fish course is missed."

She took my face in both hands, and for a moment she was not logistics at all.

"Whatever you hear from the coal store tonight — whatever you imagine — you do not come back here. You hear me? You do not turn around. Grief is a luxury for women who are free. You are not free yet. So be cruel now, and be kind for the rest of your life."

Then she picked up her basket and was gone, and I stood alone in the counting house with the lamp banked low, holding warm bread and stolen coins and the coldest, clearest sentence anyone had ever given me.

Dawn. I had until dawn.

I unwrapped the bread, ate it without tasting it, and pulled the audit schedule toward me across the desk. The supply cart. The mountain road. Three checkpoints, and the first one already passed, and a name on a list an hour old, and a man in a coal store keeping my secret with his teeth.

Thursday had just become tonight.

I blew out the lamp, and went to steal my own future back one hour ahead of Captain Voss — knowing that somewhere below the compound, in the dark, Elias was paying for it in advance.
