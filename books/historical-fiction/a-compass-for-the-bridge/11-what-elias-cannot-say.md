## 11. What Elias Cannot Say

Captain Voss did not believe in anger. Anger was expense without return. Anger made men break things they would later need, and say things they would later regret, and Voss had spent thirty years in the company's service learning that the world ran on arithmetic, and everything else—rage, mercy, love—was just bad accounting.

So when they dragged the stranger into the back room of the harbor office and dropped him onto the wet boards, Voss did not shout. He poured himself a cup of coffee from the pot on the stove, sat down across the table, and opened a fresh page in his notebook.

The room had one lamp. He had chosen it for that reason years ago. Not darkness—the eye needed something to fix on—but narrowness. A man in a narrow light could not see his interrogator's face, only the pen moving, only the ink recording what he said. Men feared the record more than they feared the man. The man might be reasoned with. The record could not.

"Name," Voss said.

The stranger lifted his head. They had worked him over on the boat, before Voss arrived—one of the deckhands had a habit of starting early—and his left eye had swollen shut, his lip split. His wrists were lashed to the chair arms with codline. Salt had dried white in his beard. He was perhaps forty, weathered like a man who'd spent his life on decks, and there was nothing remarkable about him at all except his stillness.

He looked at Voss the way men looked at weather. Something to be endured.

"Name," Voss said again.

"Voss." The voice was hoarse but steady. "I know who you are. Everyone between here and the capital knows Captain Voss. The company's broom."

"Then you know I sweep clean."

"I know you sweep *quiet*." The stranger smiled with his ruined mouth. "That's different."

Voss wrote something down. It didn't matter what. He had learned long ago that the first minutes belonged to the notebook—that giving a man time to watch you write established who held the ledger in this room. He let the silence run while he finished his coffee. Outside, the tide gate groaned against its chains, and somewhere down the dock a woman was singing to a child, an old diving song about the mother of pearls, and the sound came through the wall thin as thread.

"You came into this port four days ago on a stolen skiff," Voss said. "The skiff belongs to Meridian. Its pilot is missing. You were found in the drying nets at the tide gate with a wound in your side that somebody stitched badly. And you had this in your coat."

He set the compass on the table. Brass, old, the glass cracked. On its case, engraved: *T.R.*

The stranger's breath changed. Only that. A hitch, half a second, then smoothed over like a hand passing over a wrinkle in a sheet. But Voss saw it. He always saw it.

"T.R.," Voss said. "Tomas Reyes."

Nothing.

"The clerk. Died two years ago. Fever, according to the rolls. Buried at sea, according to the rolls. Except here's a strange thing—" Voss turned the notebook toward himself, though he didn't need it; he knew every line by heart. "Two weeks before he died, Reyes signed out forty reams of company paper. Forty reams, for a clerk keeping accounts on a station of sixty men. That's a lot of letters to nobody."

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"So either he was writing the longest farewell in maritime history," Voss said, "or he was writing to someone. And now here you are, with his compass, bleeding in our nets. Which makes you a courier. Which means somewhere in this port—or leaving it—is a message worth killing a pilot and stealing a boat for."

The stranger raised his good eye and studied Voss with something that was almost professional interest.

"You talk like a clerk yourself," he said.

"I started as one."

"A clerk with a machete."

"A clerk with tools." Voss set the notebook down flat. "Let me explain how tonight goes, so you can spend your strength wisely instead of on heroics. There are two ways this ends. In the first, you tell me what you carried and to whom, and I put you on a boat north as a bonded prisoner, which is unpleasant but survivable. In the second, we keep going all night, and I learn it anyway—because I always learn it—and you're a corpse in the channel before the gulls are up. I don't prefer either. I'm indifferent to both. But the first costs me less, and I am a man who watches costs. So. What did you carry?"

The stranger was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed—a dry, cracked sound, more cough than laugh.

"You've got it wrong," he said. "You think I'm the courier because of the compass. But the compass isn't cargo. It's a keepsake. It belonged to a diver named Okonkwo, years ago. Reyes kept it after." His swollen eye narrowed. "You should ask yourself why a dead man's compass was buried in a warehouse drawer for two years, Captain, and why your founder lets a dead clerk sign out forty reams of paper without asking questions. You're sweeping a floor somebody dirtied on purpose."

It was a good try. Voss noted the technique: redirect, implicate the hierarchy, sow doubt upward. Couriers were trained in it. Which confirmed the courier theory even as the man tried to dissolve it.

"Noted," Voss said, and wrote it down.

* * *

He worked the stranger for three hours.

He began with facts, because facts were cheaper than force. Where had he embarked? The man said he didn't remember. Voss believed him—not out of mercy but because concussed men genuinely didn't remember, and the deckhands had been thorough. Where was the message? The man said there was no message. Voss didn't believe that, but he filed the answer's shape: the denial was too calm. A liar performing innocence performed it too hard—eyes wide, hands loose, voice pitched cooperative. This man's innocence was flat, bored, almost insulted. Either he was the best liar Voss had ever met, or—

Or he genuinely didn't have it.

That thought arrived around midnight, sitting in the room like a third person, and Voss made himself look at it squarely, because the worst mistake in interrogation was wanting the answer to be in the room when it wasn't.

If the courier didn't have the package—if it had already left his hands—then the clock wasn't hours anymore. It was days at most.

"Who did you give it to?" Voss asked quietly.

The stranger's face did something small and fatal. Not much. A tightening at the corner of the undamaged eye, a swallow that came a beat early. But after three hours of watching a man hold still, movement was a shout.

"There it is," Voss said softly. "There's the tell."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You gave it away. Here, in this port. Before we took you." Voss stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at the black water of the harbor, thinking in lists, because lists were how he kept the world from becoming chaos. Who moved through this port unremarked? Who could take delivery from a stranger in the nets at dusk and vanish by morning? Not the pilots—they were watched, including by him. Not the merchants—they were watched by everyone. The courtesans, the priests, the smugglers—all catalogued, all priced.

Which left the invisible ones. The compound workers. The people whose names appeared in his ledgers only as numbers against debt.

"How many divers' daughters can read?" he asked the window.

No answer. He hadn't expected one.

He turned back. "Here's what happens now. You sleep. In the morning we continue. I have time—you should understand that. Time is the one thing the company pays me in without limit." He paused at the door. "What's your name? For the record."

A long silence. Then, hoarse: "Elias."

"Elias what?"

"Just Elias. The rest drowned."

Voss nodded to the two men waiting outside and went into the corridor, where he stood a moment under the whale-oil lamp, doing sums.

* * *

The counting house list took shape in his own quarters by candlelight, because he did not trust the office walls and he did not trust his subordinates' memories, and because there was a particular clarity that came from writing a thing in his own hand at three in the morning when the port was asleep and every name was only a name.

Every worker with access to the counting house. That was the perimeter. Whoever the courier had passed it to had to have entered or approached that building within the last four days. Voss started from memory, then pulled the duty rosters and reconciled them, and the exercise—which should have taken twenty minutes—took two hours, because the compound's bookkeeping was worse than he'd tolerated knowing. Names misspelled across pages. Entries dated wrong. One diver listed on the August roll who had, according to three other documents, died in June. Sloppiness everywhere, the ordinary rot of any large operation—and yet, looking at it, Voss felt the first cold thread of something he refused to call doubt.

Reyes had kept these books. Reyes had signed out forty reams. Reyes was officially dead, and dead men did not send couriers with cracked compasses unless they intended something, and everything the courier had said about the founder letting a dead man sign out paper—

No. Voss struck the thought flat. His business was threads, not tapestries. Find the thread. Cut it. Get paid.

By four he had a working list: eleven names with routine access to the counting house. Four clerks, all male, all watched. Two senior sorters. Mama Rosa, who delivered meals and was too old and too shrewd to bother—though he wrote her down anyway, because shrewdness was exactly what made people dangerous. The foreman Obi. Two tally boys.

And the girl.

Ines Okonkwo. Diver's daughter, indentured at nine, eight years in the sorting sheds. The entry was thin, as entries for girls always were: value assessed, debts carried, skills noted. *Literate (concealed). Reliable. Unremarkable.* He remembered adding that last word himself, years ago, with something close to approval. She was the best kind of worker—present, silent, accurate. He had seen her memorize ledger columns at a glance and reproduce them without error, and had never once wondered what use such a memory might be put to outside the shed.

Now he sat looking at her name, and beneath it he wrote a single question mark, and the question mark seemed to expand until it filled the page.

Because here was the problem with invisible people: you couldn't find them when you needed them. Watchers watched the loud ones. The loud ones were almost never guilty. Somewhere in his compound was a person quiet enough to take delivery from a bleeding stranger among the drying nets, and he realized, with genuine professional irritation, that he had no idea where she slept, who she spoke to, or what she wanted—because he had spent eight years priding himself on never having to wonder.

The lamp guttered. Down the hill, the tide gate chains clanked in the pre-dawn swell, and Voss thought about the girl who walked past it every morning with her eyes down, and about the stranger strapped to a chair in the harbor office who had given up nothing—not from loyalty, he was nearly sure, but from ignorance. The package was already gone. Already folded into some crack in the port he had never bothered to map.

He dipped the pen and wrote at the top of the page, underlining twice:

*Question all eleven. Start with the girl.*

Then, after a moment, he added the detail that had been nagging at him since the nets—small, procedural, the kind of thing he recorded precisely because it refused to fit:

*She was seen near the tide gate at dusk. Alone. No task assigned.*

He did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what sloppy accountants called variance they hadn't traced.

Outside, the first grey came up over the water, and somewhere between the compound and the shore, a girl who had been invisible for eight years was about to discover that the ledger had turned, and her name was on the wrong page of it.

Voss capped the ink, gathered his list, and went to wake the clerks.
