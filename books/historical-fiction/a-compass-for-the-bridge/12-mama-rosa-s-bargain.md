## 12. Mama Rosa's Bargain

The summons came at midday, in the form of a boy.

He was one of the tally-runner children who carried slips between the compound buildings—barefoot, quick, unremarkable—and he stood at the door of the sorting shed with a folded paper he would not hand to anyone but me. I knew what it was before I touched it. There was only one kind of paper that traveled with that kind of care.

*Ines Okonkwo. Counting house. After the fourth bell.*

No reason given. No reason needed. In San Ildefonso, a summons from Captain Voss required no more explanation than the tide.

I folded the slip once and put it in my apron pocket, where my hands could find it, and went back to sorting pearls the way you keep walking when a dog is watching you: evenly, without hurry, as if your legs have no opinion about anything at all.

Around me the shed went on with its small sounds—the click of shell against shell, the murmur of graders calling out sizes, the sea doing its endless arithmetic against the seawall. Nobody looked up. That was the first mercy of invisibility: when one of you is called, the others have learned not to see it, because seeing it means imagining their own names on a slip.

But Mama Rosa saw it. Mama Rosa always saw everything.

She was not in the shed—she was never anywhere she wasn't needed—but by the time the fourth bell rang and I crossed the yard toward the counting house, she was standing at the kitchen door with flour on her forearms and an expression I had known since I was six years old, the expression that meant *come here and let me look at you before the world does.*

"You've been summoned," she said. Not a question.

"After the fourth bell."

"I know." She wiped her hands on her apron slowly, buying seconds the way she bought everything else—patiently, with whatever was at hand. "Voss takes his meal in the counting house anteroom on audit nights. He thinks none of us notice his habits. He thinks a man who notices everything cannot be noticed himself."

"Mama Rosa—"

"He likes the snapper when it can be had fresh. He likes it better when someone remembers he likes it without being told." She looked past me, toward the water, and her voice did something I almost never heard it do: it became quiet in a calculating way, like a ledger being opened. "I will serve him tonight."

"No." The word came out of me fast and flat. "You don't go near him. You don't put yourself in that room."

"Child." She said it the way she said it when I was small and trying to carry a pot too heavy for me—not mocking, just factual. "I have been putting myself in rooms with dangerous men since before you were born. It is how I am still here. A cook is invisible even when she is standing in front of you. You taught me nothing; I taught you."

"That's not—" My voice caught on something. I made it flat again. "If he decides to take someone tonight, I'd rather it be me."

"And if he decides to take someone, which of us do you think he'll find easier to explain?" She took my chin in her floured hand, gentle as anything. "A diver's daughter with a strange accent and no family anyone can name? Or Rosa Ferreira, who cooked for old Meridian himself before the founder's hair went grey, who fed half the company's clerks at their weddings, whose debts and favors are strung through this port like the drying lines? He may suspect you of something. He cannot suspect *me*. I am furniture, mija. Furniture that has been in this house longer than he has."

I wanted to argue. I want to tell you I fought her, that I had some plan of my own worth protecting her from—but the truth is that everything I had was already spent on forged chits and sewn bindings and a route north I could recite in the dark. Everything I had left was the thing she was offering to spend for me: time.

"How long can you give me?" I asked.

"As long as a dinner lasts," she said. "And a dinner with Rosa Ferreira lasts as long as she wishes it to. Now listen." Her grip firmed. "When you're in there, answer only what's asked. Say 'sir' the way the clerks say it, not the way the divers say it—he'll hear the difference. And when you look at me across that room, you look at me the way you've looked at me every evening of your life. Bored. Hungry. Nothing else. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Liar," she said fondly, and pushed a lock of hair from my face with a floured thumb, leaving a pale streak above my brow like a mark of some kind. "Go. Wash that out before they see you looking marked."

* * *

The counting house at night was a different animal than by day.

By day it was all motion—clerks calling figures, runners dodging between desks, the abacus clatter like rain on a tin roof. By night, after the fourth bell, it emptied down to lamplight and paper and the particular silence of a room full of records nobody was allowed to read aloud. I had cleaned this room for eight years. I knew which floorboards creaked under the third desk from the window. I knew the smell of the sealing wax and the way the tide changed the draft through the shutters.

Tonight the room knew me back, or felt like it did. Every ledger spine on those shelves seemed turned toward me like a face. Somewhere among them—or soon to be among them—was the duplicate audit ledger with its stitched secret riding inside the binding like a swallowed letter. I had checked yesterday that it sat where it should sit, third shelf, second row, waiting for the cart south. Tonight I could not so much as glance at that shelf. Voss would be here. Voss missed nothing except what he had decided in advance not to exist.

He arrived at the fifth bell.

Captain Voss entered the counting house the way cold enters a room—not dramatically, not with violence, just a change in the temperature that everyone's body understood before their minds did. He was not a large man. He didn't need to be. He moved like a man who had never once in his life been surprised by anything, and that was worse than size.

"Ines Okonkwo." He said my whole name, both parts, the way officials said names—weighing them, testing them for hidden compartments. "Sit."

There was one chair placed facing his desk. One. The arrangement itself was a question, and the question was whether I would sit in it like a person being questioned or stand like a person with something to hide. I sat like a girl who had been on her feet since dawn and was grateful for any chair at all. That part wasn't even acting.

"You work the counting house," he said.

"Eight years, sir. Sorting, cleaning, carrying ledgers where they're asked to be carried."

"You read?"

Here it was, then. The first real tooth of the night. Reading aloud was forbidden to indentured workers—it kept us useful and illiterate in the eyes of the company, though the truth was more complicated and more dangerous, because I could read, and had learned in secret, and had used that secret to copy chits two nights ago with my heart going like a drum.

"A little, sir. Numbers mostly. Numbers are allowed."

"Numbers are allowed," he repeated, without inflection, and I could not tell whether I had passed or failed. He opened a slim folder on his desk. I did not look at it. Looking at it would have told him the folder mattered. Instead I looked at the lamp flame, the way a tired worker looks at anything.

"There was a boat," Voss said. "Two weeks ago. It came into the tide gate at night and left before dawn, and its pilot has stopped reporting to anyone. Before that, a courier died on the mountain road with letters in his boot. Before that—" he closed the folder softly, softly, a man handling other people's futures "—a clerk named Tomas Reyes drowned. Officially. Do you know what *officially* means, Ines Okonkwo?"

"It means it's written down, sir."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not anger. Interest. A fisherman's interest in movement on the line.

"It means it's written down," he agreed. "Now. You clean this room. You handle these ledgers. Has anyone asked you to carry anything out of it? Anyone spoken to you about papers, about the magistrate, about Reyes?"

"No, sir."

"Has anything strange reached your hands lately? Anything at all."

My mind presented me, unhelpfully, with a complete inventory: a half-drowned foreigner bleeding among the nets; letters in a dead man's handwriting; a needle and thread and three hours of work by candlelight; Elias's face the last time I saw him, before the nets were searched and the searching took him. I set all of it behind the blankness I had polished for eight years, the blankness that was not emptiness but discipline, a room swept clean every morning so that nothing showed on the floor.

"Only pearls, sir. Pearls and dust."

Voss studied me for a long moment. I let him study me. Underneath the stillness my pulse was doing things I refused to acknowledge, because acknowledgment shows in the throat, in the wrists, in the wetness of the palms, and I had learned long ago to file my fear somewhere my body couldn't reach it.

Then the door opened, and Mama Rosa came in with dinner.

* * *

She did not knock. Cooks of her standing did not knock; knocking was for people whose presence required permission. She came through the counting house door with a covered tray held level on one arm and her shawl pinned with the company's old service brooch—the one the founder's wife had given her twenty years ago, back when favors were currency and Mama Rosa had been rich in them—and the room changed around her the way a room changes when a lamp is lit.

"Captain Voss." Her voice wrapped around his name like cloth around something breakable. "They work you through supper. Old Meridian was the same. Brilliant men, terrible habits. Sit, sit—you'll forgive me, I don't wait to be told in my own kitchen's territory."

Voss blinked. I watched a man who was never surprised experience a small, controlled surprise, and I understood then exactly what Mama Rosa was doing: she was making herself memorable in precisely the ways that made me forgettable. While his attention was on her—on the tray, on the snapper done in the coastal style with lime and chili, on the brooch and the history attached to it—I ceased, for the length of that dinner, to be interesting.

"You served the founder," Voss said. Not a question either. His questions and statements sounded alike; it was one of the unsettling things about him.

"I fed that man for eleven years. I fed his clerks, his captains, his wedding guests." She lifted the cover off the dish and the smell filled the room—lime and char and the sea underneath, the smell of San Ildefonso at its best, the smell of before. "I remember when you came to this port, Captain. Thin as a gull and twice as hungry-looking. You ate at the common table and you thanked me for it. Do you remember?"

"I remember," Voss said.

"Most men forget who fed them when they were nobody." Mama Rosa arranged the plate before him with the ease of long ownership. "I don't hold it against them. But I notice. Noticing is my trade, same as yours."

I sat very still in my chair, a fixture, a girl waiting to be dismissed. But across the lamplight Mama Rosa glanced at me—one heartbeat, no longer—and what passed between us was not words. It was older than words. It was the look from a hundred suppers: *eat while it's hot*, *mind your manners*, *I am here*, *you are not alone in this room*. Love, expressed as logistics. Sacrifice dressed in an apron and called small talk.

"The girl," Voss said, without turning to me. "How long have you known her?"

"Since she was four years old and cried for a week straight when her father's boat came back without him." Mama Rosa said it plainly, and I understood that plainness was the armor—that grief offered openly looks like grief, but grief offered as household fact looks like history, and history belongs to everyone. "I raised her the way I raised half the compound. On rice and rules."

"She's been useful, they tell me. Quiet. Unnoticed."

"The best ones are," Mama Rosa said. "You want to worry about the noticed ones, Captain. The loud ones, the clever ones who make speeches. The quiet ones just want to get through the day like anybody. Isn't that right, mija?"

"Yes, Mama Rosa," I said, and it was the truest sentence I spoke all night.

Voss ate. He ate well—Mama Rosa's food had that effect even on men who suspected everyone—and while he ate he talked, or rather he let Mama Rosa talk, and I began to see the shape of her bargain. She was spending her capital. Every anecdote about old Meridian, every remembered debt—a clerk she'd nursed through fever, a harbor master whose wedding feast she'd cooked for nothing, a debt of gratitude owed her by the harbormaster's wife who happened to be Voss's cousin by marriage—every one of them was a coin laid on the table, and the purchase was simple: *this girl is mine, and touching what is mine costs you more than it gains you.* She never said it. She didn't have to. Twenty years of favors spoke for her, fluently, in a language Voss understood perfectly because it was his own: obligation, leverage, price.

"Your snapper is better than the club's," Voss said at last, which from him was a speech.

"The club pays boys to cook," Mama Rosa said. "I pay attention." She gathered his emptied plate and stood, and for a moment she was framed in the lamplight with the tray on her arm, and she looked—there is no other word—regal. A queen of kitchens, negotiating treaties in gravy and lime. "You'll want your coffee. I'll send the girl to bring it, and then she'll help me with the dishes, because tomorrow is audit day and we all have our loads to carry. Don't we, Captain?"

"We all have our loads," Voss agreed.

Mama Rosa went out. The door closed behind her, and the room got colder by degrees, and I understood that the treaty had a term limit: one cup of coffee, one stack of dishes. After that I was alone with him again, and the folder on his desk, and whatever he had decided about me before I ever walked through the door.

Voss looked at me over the lamp.

"You may go," he said. "For now. Tell Rosa her coffee is the only honest thing in this building."

I rose, curtsied the way the clerks' daughters curtsied, and walked to the door with my heart hammering so hard I was certain he could hear it, certain each step would be my last as a free-in-the-small-ways woman.

At the threshold I paused—because leaving too fast is also information—and looked back across the room one final time. At the shelves. At the third shelf, second row, where a duplicate ledger waited in the dark with a dead man's words sleeping in its binding, patient as a seed.

Tomorrow the cart would come for the audit ledgers. Tomorrow the road south would open, and the mountain customs house beyond it, and the river ferry where Voss's cousin commanded, and after them the capital, where truth waited to be unwrapped like a package nobody had ordered.

Behind me, Voss turned back to his folder. Ahead of me, in the yard, Mama Rosa stood waiting with the coffee pot and the flour still on her arms, and when she saw my face she did not ask what had passed in that room. She only nodded once, toward the kitchen, toward the dishes, toward the ordinary work that was now the most extraordinary cover either of us would ever have.

"Wash up," she said softly. "Audit day comes early."
