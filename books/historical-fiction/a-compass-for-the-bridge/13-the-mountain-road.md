## 13. The Mountain Road

The chit in my pocket said I was Marta Ilustre, errand girl to one Señor Baltazar Quimpo, junior ledger clerk of the Meridian Trading Company, San Ildefonso branch. The chit was a lie stitched together from memory: the clerk's signature I had watched a hundred times across the counting house floor, the company stamp whose worn left edge always printed a hair too light, the serial numbering system that ran red for freight and black for personnel. I had written it by candlelight in Mama Rosa's kitchen with a hand that did not shake until the ink was dry.

Three days up the mountain road, the lie was beginning to fit me like a borrowed dress—loose at first, then warming to my shape.

I had never been beyond the tide gate. Eight years of indenture, and my world had measured exactly as far as my legs could carry me between the drying racks and the counting house, between the compound gate and the shore where my father's boat had gone out and, once, not come back. Now the road unspooled ahead through terraced slopes green as wet silk, and every step was distance I had not been permitted, purchased with a name that was not mine.

The satchel rode against my hip. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a change of clothes and two honest apples, lay the duplicate pearl ledger—the company's own quarterly audit book, bound in blue goat leather, its spine freshly resewn in thread I had colored with soot to match the original stitching. Between its boards, sewn flat against the binding where no auditor's thumb would ever press unless he knew to press, were eleven letters in the handwriting of a dead man.

I did not let myself think of them as letters. I thought of them as ballast. Cargo. Numbers in a column. If I thought of them as what they were—Tomas Reyes's patient, damning arithmetic of the drowned—my hands would start to sweat, and sweating hands fumble things, and fumbled things get searched.

Be useful. Be invisible. And now, be fluent.

* * *

The customs house at Paso Real sat at the shoulder of the mountain like a tollbooth at heaven's gate. You could see it for an hour before you reached it—a squat stone building straddling the road's narrowest point, flag limp in the heat, mules queued in the yard, and above the door the colonial crest with its motto about justice and weight. I read it as I approached because reading aloud is forbidden to indentured girls and I was no longer, technically, one, and because I wanted the practice of being someone allowed to read.

The queue moved slowly. Ahead of me a farmer argued over a crate of chickens; a Chinese merchant with ledgers of his own—real ones, heavy with tariffs paid—waited in the shade of a tamarind. I studied all of it the way I had once studied the counting house: who held power, who performed it, which clerks were careful and which were lazy, and where the lazy ones kept their eyes.

The inspector working the pedestrian line was young, perhaps twenty-five, with a new uniform still creased from its folding and an abacus he handled like a weapon he wasn't sure how to load. His senior colleague, gray and bored, processed carts. The young one got people like me.

He took the farmer's chits, frowned at them, took mine.

"Name."

"Marta Ilustre. Errand girl for Señor Quimpo, Meridian Trading." I had rehearsed the sentence until it came out with the particular boredom of someone asked this question forty times a day. Boredom, I had learned watching Voss's men work the docks, is the accent of innocence. Fear has an accent too. So does eagerness. Only indifference passes without comment.

"Purpose of travel."

"Company property to the capital for audit. Under seal." I turned the satchel so he could see the wax stamp on the flap—Mama Rosa had contributed candle stubs; I had pressed it myself with the brass button from Elias's coat, carved with a knife into something close enough to the company mark. Close enough for a tired man at dusk. This was midmorning, though, and this man was not tired yet.

"Open it."

There it was. The word I had walked three days to hear, dreading it, rehearsing my answer to it. In my rehearsals I had produced a letter of instruction—forged, of course—instructing the bearer to surrender the seal only to the audit office intact, on penalty of the clerk's personal liability. Paper fights paper. But paper also invites scrutiny, and scrutiny was the country I could not afford to visit.

So instead I heard myself say, in the flat voice of a girl who has grown up around clerks, "It's under company seal, señor. I'm not authorized. Señor Quimpo would have the amount docked from my wages." A pause, precisely measured—not too quick, not too slow—and then, because usefulness is a better key than any document: "Unless you want to note it as an official refusal? You'd need my name spelled right. It's I-l-u-s-t-r-e. And the ledger number, or the audit office will reject the manifest and your supervisor will write to ours asking why the quarterly books were opened at a roadside station, and that correspondence takes weeks, and—" I stopped, as if catching myself being tiresome. "Beg pardon, señor. I only know what happens when paperwork goes back wrong. They make me copy the corrections."

He looked at me the way men look at weather. An inconvenience, familiar, survivable.

"Ledger stays closed," he said, and wrote something on his pad. "Chit."

He examined my forged chit while my heart performed its business somewhere high in my throat, steady as a diver's pulse before the drop. The signature. The stamp. The serial in black ink, personnel series, fourth-quarter format. I had copied them from memory because memory was the only library permitted me, eight years of memorizing numbers I was forbidden to read aloud, and it turned out all that stolen literacy had been quietly building a locksmith.

He stamped my travel pass. He waved me toward the archway.

And that is when I saw the error.

* * *

Behind me, the farmer was back, chickens squalling, holding out a second sheaf of papers. The young inspector had his totals spread on the plank table—three columns in a child's careful hand—and I saw the figures upside down from four paces, because four paces and upside down was how I had learned to read the count house books, standing behind my betters, forbidden to look directly, looking anyway.

His duty calculation on the chicken crates was wrong. Transposition error, third row—he'd carried the figure wrong, and the tax came out six pesos high, and the farmer knew it was wrong but couldn't prove it because the farmer, like my father, like half the port, had never been taught the numbers that governed him.

The old me—the me of eight days ago, the me whose entire strategy of survival was built on not existing—would have walked under that archway and been through it already. That girl knew that noticing is dangerous. That girl had watched divers listed as deceased and said nothing, had carried the knowledge like a stone swallowed whole, and the stone had nearly killed her before Elias washed ashore.

But there was another thing I had learned in the counting house, in the years of being useful and unseen: invisible people are furniture, and nobody interrogates furniture—but everybody asks furniture to hold things. The clerk forgets his spectacles and the errand girl finds them. The inspector mislays his totals and—

"Señor." I was already turning, already speaking, and some detached part of me watched in astonishment, as if from the ceiling, at what I was doing. "Forgive me—I think row three carried wrong. The crates are twelve dozen, not thirteen—you counted the empty coop twice. At the duty rate posted, it should come to—" I named the figure. Correct. To the centavo. I had done it in my head in the time it took him to lift his pen.

The yard went quiet in the way small yards do when something mildly interesting happens. The farmer stared at me. The gray senior clerk lifted his head for the first time all morning.

The young inspector looked at his own figures. Looked at mine, spoken aloud, standing in air where he could not pretend he hadn't heard them. Then he recomputed, lips moving, abacus clicking—and arrived where I had already arrived.

"Huh," he said.

He corrected the entry. He told the farmer the lower sum, and the farmer blessed me with a look I felt land on my back like sun. And the inspector—this is the part I would turn over and over for the rest of my life—smiled at me. Not suspiciously. Gratefully.

"You've got a head for figures," he said. "Meridian trains you?"

"Yes, señor." Which was true. Meridian had trained me in everything I was using against it, though he didn't need to know the second half of that sentence.

"Some of those company girls are wasted in the pearl sheds," he said to his senior, who grunted. He waved me through the arch with something almost like courtesy. "Safe roads, errand girl."

And I walked under the stone gate of the colonial customs service, past its motto about justice and weight, carrying eleven letters that would hang half its merchants if they were found, and no one stopped me, because I had balanced a chicken farmer's taxes.

* * *

I made two miles before my legs stopped obeying orders and I sat down under a mango tree at the roadside and shook.

Not fear, or not only fear. Something worse. Triumph, and underneath the triumph, cold and rising like water in a hold, horror.

Because here was the thing the mountain road had shown me, plain as the ledger line itself: the system had not resisted me. It had *received* me. I had spent three days expecting walls and found doors, and the doors were not even locked—they were manned by boys who smiled at anyone who spoke their language. Arithmetic. Procedure. The correct boredom in the correct voice. I had crossed the empire's spine by being good at the empire's homework, and the same fluency that saved me was the fluency that had condemned my father—because the death rolls were falsified by men equally fluent, equally smiling, whose errors were never transpositions but intentions, and no farmer at any table would ever be able to say *row three carried wrong* against them, because the falsification was the correct figure. It balanced. That was the obscenity of it. Voss's cruelty, the founder's theft—they weren't failures of the system. They were the system functioning perfectly, efficiently, and the only people who could catch the error were the ones the system had forbidden to read aloud.

I thought of my father then, on the road where I had sworn not to think of him, in the water where I never let myself see him—his last dive, the rope going slack, the surface closing smooth as a ledger line over the place he'd been. Eight years I had refused to look at that moment because looking meant grieving and grief was a luxury the indentured could not carry. But Mama Rosa had told me the truth five nights ago in her kitchen, her hands flat on the table as if to hold it down: his death roll entry was signed by a man who had never met him, dated three days after the debt was transferred, witnessed by two names belonging to dead men already. My silence had not protected me from the company. My silence had *been* the company, working through me, cheap as arithmetic.

A girl with a head for figures. Wasted in the pearl sheds.

I laughed, or something near laughing, alone under the mango tree, and it came out wrong, and I let it.

Then I got up, because the waystation at Kilometer Forty was another four hours' walk and the audit clock did not care about my revelations. I checked the satchel strap. I adjusted my shawl. I became Marta Ilustre again, bored, useful, invisible—the mask that had just passed its hardest test fitting easier now, almost comfortable, which was its own small terror.

The road bent north along the ridge, and below me, far off, I could see the river valley—the ferry crossing, silver in the haze, third and final checkpoint, where they said the ferry master shared Voss's blood and something of his eyes.

I was still two ridges from Kilometer Forty when I saw the smoke.

Waystations cooked at midday; smoke was ordinary. But this smoke stood too straight and too dark, a single column off the road's shoulder, and as I came down the switchback I saw what burned: papers, a small heap of them, fed page by page into a fire by a man in traveling clothes while another stood watch on the path.

They were not burning their own papers. Burning your own papers you do indoors, quickly, ashamed. These men were burning them in the open, unhurried, methodical, the way you destroy evidence you want seen destroyed—or the way you mark a trail for someone coming behind you.

The watchman turned as I approached, and I dropped my eyes fast, becoming furniture, a girl with a satchel and no curiosity, and I was nearly past when the wind turned and carried across the road a scrap of burning paper, curling, half alight, and one line of ink showed itself to me for the half-second before it became ash.

I read it the way I read everything—fast, upside down, forbidden.

It was dated after the writer's death.

Tomas Reyes had been on this road. Or someone wanted very badly for me to believe he had.
