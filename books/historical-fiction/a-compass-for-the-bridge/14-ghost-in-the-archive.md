## 14. Ghost in the Archive

The waystation at Kilometer Thirty-One was not on any map Ines had ever seen, which she understood now was a feature and not an accident. Maps were for people who mattered. This place existed only in the bodies of those who stopped there: mule drivers, chit-bearers, errand girls with company property strapped to their backs.

It was a long, low building of gray timber, half swallowed by the mountain's knee, with a stable on one side and a cook shed on the other. Smoke stood up from the chimney as straight as a plumb line in the windless evening air. Ines counted six mules in the paddock, two carts under tarpaulin, and one man on the porch who looked up from his pipe, catalogued her—a small brown girl, ledger satchel, company chit pinned to her collar—and went back to his pipe. She had never been so grateful for boredom in her life.

Inside, the station keeper's wife took her coin without looking at her and gave her bread without looking at her and pointed her toward the corner nearest the stove without looking at her. Ines ate slowly, the way she had learned to eat: enough to be unremarkable, not enough to be remembered. The ledger rode between her feet, its binding pressed against her ankles through her boots. Every hour she had spent on the mountain road she had been aware of it the way you are aware of your own heartbeat—constantly, uselessly, and with mounting dread that one day it might simply stop.

She had sewn the letters into the binding herself, in Mama Rosa's kitchen, by the light of a single lamp, with a needle pulled from the hem of her own sleeve. Nine letters. Two years of a dead man's hand. The stitches ran along the spine where the leather was already cracked and doubled, and once finished, the ledger looked exactly like what it was supposed to be: a fat, boring, water-damaged accounting book bound in the cheap stock Meridian always used for audit copies, the kind of object no one opens twice if they can help it.

That was the whole idea. That was the entire architecture of her hope. A thing too dull to look at.

She slept that night on the bench by the stove, satchel under her head, and woke twice—once to the sound of a mule stamping, once to nothing at all, which was worse—and both times she lay still and listened until the dark became just dark again.

* * *

In the morning there was fog. The road east ran down out of the mountains toward the river country, and the fog sat in the switchbacks like milk in a bowl, so that the world ended ten paces from the porch rail. The station keeper told her the ferry road would be clear by midday and not before, and that she could sweep the common room if she wanted credit against her supper, because he had eyes and could see she was the sort who'd rather work than sit.

He was right. Uselessness made her itch. She took the broom and worked from the corners outward, the way Mama Rosa had taught her before she could reach the handle standing up, and while she swept she listened, because sweeping is the most invisible form of listening ever invented.

There were four other travelers stranded by the fog: the pipe-smoker from the porch; a fat man in merchant's cotton who complained about everything; a young priest going down to the river missions; and a woman in traveling gray who kept to herself by the window, sewing something small and white.

It was the priest who mentioned the name.

"—Reyes," he said, in answer to some question of the merchant's about the audit. "Tomas Reyes. The clerk who drowned. Half the coast swears he's writing letters from the grave."

"Superstition," said the merchant. "Dead men don't write."

"Then someone is forging a dead man very well." The priest smiled around his cup. "They say he has proof of the death rolls. Falsified. Whole families cut off their wages because the company wrote their men into the sea."

Ines swept past them. Her broom did not slow. Her face did nothing at all, and this cost more than anything she had ever done, because inside her chest something was ringing like a struck bell and she had to keep it silent, keep walking, keep the bristles moving over the boards.

They said he was alive. Elias had said he was alive. But hearing it in a stranger's mouth, on a mountain, three days' travel from the sea—it made it real in a way that frightened her. Real things could be lost. Rumors couldn't drown.

* * *

It was the woman in gray who left the letter.

Ines did not see her leave it. That was the point, and later Ines would understand that she had been meant to find it the way a fisherman means for the fish to find the hook—by making the finding feel like accident.

She found it when she went to remake the bench by the stove after breakfast, lifting the cushion to beat the dust out, and there on the frame, half tucked into the gap between slats, lay a folded paper. Plain paper, unmarked. She should have left it. It was none of her business; strangers forgot things; the keeper would return it or keep it, either way it wasn't hers.

Her hands picked it up anyway. Eight years of being useful had taught her hands to move before her caution caught up.

The fold was sealed with nothing but its own crease, and inside, in a small, precise, clerkly hand—the same hand she had studied by lamplight until she could have drawn it from memory, the same hand that dated its own death—was written:

*To the one carrying what I could not. If my courier reached you, then you are already on the road, and I am sorry for it. No one should carry this weight who has not already been made to carry heavier.*

*You will want to know what the rolls are, truly, since you risk your life for them. So: the founder, Aldous Wren, learned early that a dead diver is worth more to the company than a living one. A living diver draws wages. A dead one draws nothing—but his debt remains, transferred by clerical order to the estate, to the family, to whoever can be made to pay it. And the insurance bonds taken against the boats pay out on paper deaths that never happened, divers written off as lost so the company could collect twice. My own signature appears on forty-one rolls. I signed them because I was told to sign them, and then I copied every one before I let them bury my name.*

*Now the warning, and I beg you to believe it: do not trust that the magistrate is the end of the road. He wears the law like a coat, but the law in this colony has pockets, and Meridian fills them. The magistrate's own brother, Emil Duarte, sits on Meridian's board of directors. Whatever you carry will pass through the hands of a man whose family fortune is braided into the very ledgers you are trying to hang. I do not say turn back. I say know what you are walking into. There may be no honest pair of hands waiting at the end of this road. There may only be hands.*

*I chose you—or Elias chose you, which comes to the same thing—because you have survived what these papers describe. Remember that they cannot erase you the way they erased us on paper unless you let them write your silence into the record.*

*T.R.*

Ines read it three times. Then she folded it along its existing creases, walked to the stove, and fed it to the fire, watching the hand of a dead man curl and blacken and go up the flue.

Then she sat back down on the bench and put her face in her hands—not weeping, though her eyes burned—because there was a shape in her chest now, cold and expanding, and she recognized it. It was the feeling of a floor giving way. It was the feeling of learning that the compass you have trusted points not north but toward whatever magnet someone else has buried.

* * *

She thought: *I could stop here.*

The thought arrived quietly, reasonably, dressed in the voice of survival, the oldest voice she knew. The waystation sat at a crossroads. One road went down toward the river, the ferry, Voss's cousin, the capital, the magistrate whose brother wore Meridian's colors. Another road—there was a track behind the stable, the keeper said, that wound west to the coast fishing villages, where nobody asked names and pearls were traded off blankets and nobody had ever heard of an indenture.

She could walk away. She had the ledger. She could drop it in the river from any bridge on the mountain road and nine letters would become fish bedding and Ines Okonkwo would become a girl again, invisible, alive, useful somewhere far from San Ildefonso. Voss might even stop hunting. A loose thread cut itself, sometimes, if you gave it slack enough.

She sat with that thought for a long time, turning it like a pearl, examining it for flaws.

And beneath it, surfacing slowly, came her father's face. Not the memory she usually permitted—the last dive, the boat coming back empty, the roll call where his name was read and answered by silence—but an older one. Him teaching her numbers in the sand behind the compound, drawing them with a broken shell because teaching an indentured child to read was forbidden but arithmetic was just play. *Numbers don't lie,* he used to say. *People lie with numbers. But the numbers themselves remember.*

The numbers remembered. That was the whole of it. Forty-one signatures. Forty-one families paying debts for men the company had already collected insurance on. Her father's line in a roll she had not yet seen but now knew existed, knew with the certainty of bedrock: *dived, drowned, deceased, debt transferred.* Four words standing where a man should be.

If she stopped here, the numbers would still be true. They would just go on being true in the dark, forever, and she would be one more person who knew and said nothing, which was the job description she had held for eight years, and it had not saved her father, and it would not save anyone else.

But—

*The magistrate's brother sits on the board.*

She turned that fact over and over, and every angle of it was bad. If the letters reached the magistrate's desk and the magistrate was honest, his own family's wealth was implicated and honesty would cost him everything he had, and men rarely paid that price voluntarily. If the magistrate was not honest, then she was delivering the evidence directly to the enemy's door, wrapped and stitched by her own hand, and Voss need only wait at the other end.

Either way, the ledger would pass through the hands of the people it condemned. That was not a flaw in her plan. That was the plan. She had hidden the truth inside the company's own paperwork precisely because the company's paperwork moved freely where a girl could not. But Reyes's letter peeled the comfort off that logic. The machinery she was exploiting was the same machinery that ground them all. She was trusting the machine to malfunction honestly.

What if it didn't?

What if she stood in the audit hall in the capital and watched an official flip past the stitched spine without opening it, sign the audit sheet, and hand her ledger back with instructions to return it to the counting house? What if the truth traveled all that way and arrived and was simply filed?

She tried to imagine it and found, to her surprise, that she could—easily, vividly, in detail. Eight years in the counting house had given her an imagination for bureaucratic indifference that most revolutionaries must lack.

And yet.

There was a knock of boots on the porch boards outside, and she heard the keeper greet new arrivals, and among the voices—one of them was describing, with the relish of a man trading novelty for drinks, a search party on the mountain road. An enforcer from the coast. Asking after a girl traveling alone with company property. Offering payment for word of her.

The fog was thinning. By midday the road would be clear, and whatever direction she moved, she would be moving through a countryside with ears.

Ines took out the ledger and set it on her knees and opened it to the middle, where the columns ran in their neat faded rows—pearl weights, diver credits, debt deductions, all the honest arithmetic of a dishonest enterprise—and she looked at the stitching visible in the gutter of the spine, her own crooked thread, and she made her decision the only way she knew how: by treating it as a problem with a solution rather than a grief with a duration.

If the magistrate might be bought, then the truth needed witnesses beyond the magistrate. Not one pair of hands—many. Reyes's letter assumed a single delivery. Fine. She would change the assumption. She was, after all, becoming fluent in the language of records.

She had memorized the letters. All nine, every date, every figure, every damning clause, laid down in her memory the way she had once memorized ledger numbers because reading aloud was forbidden. Memory could not be confiscated. Memory could not be searched at a ferry crossing. If the ledger was taken, burned, filed, or forgotten, the truth would still be walking around inside a small brown girl whom nobody bothered to remember.

And if she reached the capital and the magistrate's hands proved to be only hands—she would speak anyway. Loudly. Publicly. In front of the auditors, the court clerks, whoever was present. Paper could be pocketed. A scene could not. Visibility was the one currency Voss could not confiscate, because spending it destroyed its value for everyone who watched her spend it.

It was a worse plan than the original. It relied entirely on her, which was the thing she had spent eight years training herself never to do.

She closed the ledger and buckled the satchel.

Outside, the fog was pulling apart like wet wool. The road down to the river showed itself in patches—dark pines, white limestone, a gleam of water far below. Somewhere on it, behind her, a man who believed cruelty was efficient bookkeeping was riding with her description in his pocket.

The woman in gray was gone. Ines searched the common room, the stable, the privy, the paddock. Gone, and no one recalled when, and no cart missing. She stood a moment where the woman had sat, by the window, and saw on the sill, caught under the latch, one small white thing: a pearl button, the cheap kind, drilled for thread.

Sympathizers, then. A network. Reyes had not been hiding alone all this time; there were hands passing things along the roads, leaving letters under bench cushions, wearing gray and vanishing into fog. She was not the first courier. She was not even, probably, the last. Something old and patient was moving through this colony's blind spots, and she had stumbled into its current.

It should have comforted her. Instead the undertow pulled harder: if there was a network, Voss would eventually find its edges. Every sympathizer was another name on another list. She had seen how lists worked.

Ines put the button in her pocket, settled the satchel strap across her chest, and walked out onto the road heading down.

The river was two days away. The ferry master was Voss's cousin. And the letter had told her the truth she would carry past him, past the checkpoints, past the gates of the capital itself: there might be nowhere honest to deliver what she carried.

So she would decide, when she got there, whether honest was a place or a thing she made.

Behind her, the fog finished burning off, and the mountain stood revealed in hard morning light, every switchback of the road visible for miles—visible, she realized, to anyone watching from above.

Someone was watching. She didn't turn around. She lengthened her stride, small and steady and unremarkable, and descended toward the river with a dead man's warning folded into her memory and her father's numbers keeping time with her steps.
