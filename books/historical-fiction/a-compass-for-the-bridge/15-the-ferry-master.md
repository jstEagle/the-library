## 15. The Ferry Master

The river announced itself before I saw it—a low brown roar threading through the cane fields, wide as a street and twice as busy. Two days on the mountain road had taught me the rhythm of walking as someone else: shoulders down, eyes forward, the ledger satchel riding my hip like it weighed nothing, like I carried company property every day of my life. Which, in a sense, I now did.

The crossing point was exactly where Reyes's map said it would be, and so was everything else: the customs shed with its corrugated roof, the queue of traders and their boxes, the flat-bottomed ferry nosing against the pilings while a deckhand coiled rope with the boredom of a man who had done it ten thousand times. And standing at the foot of the gangplank, checking papers with the leisure of someone paid by the hour and not by the mercy—

A tall man in a white linen coat gone gray at the seams. A jaw built on the same template as Captain Voss's, wider at the hinge. Eyes that did arithmetic on every face that passed.

Voss's cousin. The third checkpoint. The one I had been dreading since the mountain road began to descend.

I joined the queue behind a spice trader whose crates smelled sharp enough to sting, and I did what I had practiced on the road for two days: I became a clerk's errand girl. Not a diver's daughter. Not a woman carrying letters that could unmake a fortune. A girl named for nothing, sent by nobody important, whose whole existence was a line item in someone else's day.

The queue shortened. My turn came.

"Papers." He didn't look up.

I handed him the chits—the ones I had copied from memory at the waystation, Elias's forged letterhead under my own careful hand, the audit seal reproduced well enough to pass at arm's length. He took them the way Voss took ledgers: thumb along the edge, a glance worth less than a second.

"Meridian property?" He nodded at the satchel.

"The audit ledger, señor. Bound for the magistrate's office in the capital."

That got his eyes up. They were paler than his cousin's, watered-down versions of the same instrument, but they worked the same way. They measured me—my sun-darkened skin, my road dust, my sex, my age—and arrived at a sum he didn't like.

"Alone?"

"My employer prefers speed to ceremony."

"Your employer." A smile without warmth in it, like a ledger entry without a number. "And your name is on this manifest where?"

My stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, and I made my face disagree with it. This was the moment I had rehearsed on the road, walking and muttering to the cane fields, and here it was arriving early, before I'd finished breathing.

"I'm not on the manifest," I said. "Property doesn't list its carriers. It lists its custody chain." I held out my hand, palm up, patient as an accountant. "May I? The seal has to stay intact."

He didn't give me the papers. He turned them over slowly, reading the back the way illiterate men read—with great care and total concealment. Behind me the spice trader sighed. Somewhere in the queue a child started crying, thin and rhythmic, like a pulley that needed oil.

"Custody chain," he repeated.

"Three signatures. Counting house, mountain customs—" I pointed at the stamp on the second chit, the real one, earned honestly two days ago by correcting an inspector's addition errors, "—and yours is meant to be fourth. Third, rather. The ferry is the last stop before the capital road."

"And if I don't sign?"

The question hung there. Around us the dock went on with its business—rope creaking, water slapping pilings, the deckhand spitting into the current—but for me the world narrowed to the width of one man's hesitation.

I had spent eight years learning what people were afraid of. Divers were afraid of the deep and hid it behind jokes. Overseers were afraid of the founder and hid it behind shouting. Mama Rosa was afraid of nothing except for me, which she hid behind soup. And this man, this pale copy of a dangerous original, standing in his faded coat at the end of nowhere collecting signatures from strangers—

He was afraid of being blamed.

It came off him the way heat comes off stone at dusk. You could see it in how carefully he held the chits, as if paper could testify against him. You could hear it in the word *don't*, which he pronounced like a man testing whether a floor would hold his weight.

"You'll sign," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. Somewhere behind me, the spice trader stopped sighing.

"Because the audit closes Thursday," I went on, and my voice did something I hadn't authorized—it dropped into the flat, unhurried register of the counting house, the voice of clerks reciting figures that ruin people. "The magistrate's office expects the Meridian ledgers by Wednesday night. If they arrive Thursday, the delay gets recorded. And when a delay gets recorded, someone asks whose signature was last on the custody chain." I let that sit for exactly one breath. "Captain Voss will be asked. Captain Voss does not enjoy answering questions. And you are—" I glanced at his jaw, his hands, the family resemblance doing my work for me, "—his cousin. Señor Ferro, isn't it? The manifest mentions the ferry master by station."

I had never heard the name before yesterday, when Elias, feverish and half-laughing in the nets, had told me: *Voss's cousin runs the river. Ferro. The family keeps everything in the family.* I watched the name land on him like a hand on the shoulder of a sleeping man.

"Who told you my name?"

"The manifest," I said again, calmly, because repetition sounds like documentation. "And Captain Voss will want to know why the audit ledger sat at the river while the magistrate's office wrote *delay* next to Meridian's name. He doesn't blame upward, Captain Voss. Ask anyone who's worked under him."

That was true, and truth travels farther than any forgery. Every worker in San Ildefonso knew that Voss's cruelty had a specific architecture: it flowed downhill, always, and pooled on whoever stood lowest. Ferro knew it too. I could see him knowing it, the arithmetic running behind his pale eyes—one loose girl against one angry cousin, one signature against one recorded failure.

Power obeys power. That was the lesson of my whole life, written in every compound roll call and every wage entry never paid. Nobody in San Ildefonso had ever obeyed *me*. But I had learned, sorting pearls in the dark, that authority is mostly costume, and the trick is not to sew your own—you borrow. You walk into a room wearing the fear of someone bigger than you, and people assume the fear is yours.

I was nobody. But I was nobody carrying Captain Voss's deadline, and therefore, for the length of this conversation, I was somebody Voss would answer to. It was a lie shaped exactly like the truth, which is the most durable kind.

Ferro looked at the chits a long moment. Then at me. Then past me, down the road I had come from, as if the mountains might produce a better explanation than the one standing in front of him.

"A girl," he said, mostly to himself, "traveling alone, with company property."

"An errand," I said. "Company property doesn't require an escort. It requires a signature." I held out my hand again. "The tide won't hold the ferry, señor, and Thursday won't move."

Something shifted in his face—not trust, nothing like trust, but the particular surrender of a man deciding which risk he'd rather explain later. I had seen that exact expression on overseers approving impossible dive quotas. Fear makes its choices the same way everywhere.

He signed. The pen scratched, and I felt the sound go through me like a fishhook—third checkpoint passed, three signatures, one chain of custody, and inside the satchel against my hip, sewn into the binding of a duplicate pearl ledger, forty-one letters in a dead man's hand waiting to testify.

"Next," Ferro called over my shoulder, already bored again, already safer for having surrendered.

I walked the gangplank. My knees held, which surprised me; some part of me had expected them to announce everything. The deckhand took my coin without looking at me. The ferry pushed off into the brown water, and San Ildefonso's river took my weight the way it takes everything—without comment, without memory.

* * *

The crossing took half an hour. I stood at the rail and let myself feel it arrive in pieces.

First came the shaking, delayed from the dock, my hands performing what my face had refused. Then came something worse and better: laughter, small and awful, pressed through closed teeth. I had done it. The tide gate guard who'd known me since I was small had looked through me and seen nothing; the mountain inspector had accepted me because I was useful; and now a Voss had signed his name beneath mine on a chain of custody that ended in his own destruction. The company's paperwork was carrying its condemnation downriver, sealed and stamped and *on schedule*, and no one had stopped it because no one could imagine it. Invisibility had kept me alive for eight years. Today it had moved evidence.

Then, as the far bank grew near, the laughter died and left the other thing, the thing I had been outrunning since the nets.

Elias.

Two days on the road, and I had permitted myself exactly one image of him: the way he'd looked at me when I left, trying to smile with cracked lips, saying *you're faster than I am now, they'll chase me longer.* He had made himself the slower target. He had walked out along the shore road in plain view, limping deliberately, letting himself be seen, buying me hours. And the hours had worked. I was here. He was wherever Voss had taken him.

Pity, I had told myself at the mountain customs house, pity would doom you both. Hardness was the price of the plan. I had paid it and kept walking.

But on the water, with no checkpoint ahead for a full day and no performance required of anyone, the hardness leaked. I thought about his wrist, the brand scarred over with a newer scar, a man who had tried to erase what the company had made him and only added to the record. I thought about him refusing to give Voss anything—not because he knew where the letters were, he didn't, only I did—but because giving up nothing was the last dignity available to him.

And then I thought the thought I had been keeping in the bottom compartment of my mind, below the letters, below the plan, below even my father:

If Voss broke him—and Voss broke everyone eventually, it was his profession—then Elias would tell him about the girl. The errand girl. The ledger. The capital road.

Not the hiding place. Never that; he truly didn't know it. But the route, the direction, the deadline. Enough to make Voss understand, in his cold bookkeeping way, exactly what had left his port sewn inside his own audit papers. Enough to start him down the mountain after me.

I looked at the water sliding past and did the arithmetic I had been avoiding. Ferro's signature had cost perhaps four minutes of hesitation. A rider leaving San Ildefonso the morning after Elias was taken—two days ago, maybe less—would already be on the mountain road. Would have passed the customs house. Would reach the river.

Would reach the ferry.

I turned and found the deckhand.

"How often does the boat cross?" I asked, casual, a girl making talk.

"Hour on the hour, light permitting." He squinted upstream, where the water flattened into glare. "We're due back directly. There's another party waiting on the far side, matter of fact—came down the mountain road this morning, fast as sin. Paid double to cross first."

The river kept moving. My heart did not.

"Traveling alone?" My voice came out level. Eight years of practice, and I spent all of it on those two words.

"Big fellow. Coat wrong for the weather." The deckhand shrugged, coiling his rope. "Said he was meeting the ferry master. Family business, he said."

Family business. On the far bank, small with distance, a figure stood at the landing where the mountain road came down to meet the water, still as a post in the glare, and even across half a river of brown noise I could feel the arithmetic in it, measuring the approaching boat, measuring me at the rail, arriving at a sum.

I had borrowed power at the dock and it had worked, because Ferro feared someone above him. But the man waiting on the far bank was the top of the hill. There was no one above Captain Voss to hide behind.

Except, I thought, turning the satchel's strap tight against my ribs, feeling forty-one letters press against my hip like a heartbeat—

Except whoever opened the ledger next.
