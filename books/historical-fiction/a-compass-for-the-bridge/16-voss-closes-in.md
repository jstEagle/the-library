## 16. Voss Closes In

The boy broke at dawn. Not from the fists—Voss had learned long ago that flesh was a poor ledger to keep accounts in; flesh forgave too easily and remembered too much. The boy broke when Voss stopped hitting him and started talking instead. Quietly. Reasonably. The way a man reads figures aloud.

"Your name is Elias Varga," Voss said, sitting on the stool across from the cell, wiping his hands on a clean cloth as though he'd just finished gutting fish rather than two hours of questions. "Born in Puerto Cabello. Courier for the Meridian house there until you were dismissed for theft. You came north on the *Santa Inés*, you lost your boat at the tide gate two nights ago, and you were carrying nothing worth mentioning." He folded the cloth. "That's your story. It's tidy. I almost believe it myself."

The man hanging by his wrists in the netting shed didn't answer. His breathing had the wet rattle of ribs that would need months to mend, if anyone let him live that long.

"Here's what I believe instead," Voss continued. "You're not a thief. Thieves are lazy, and you are the most patient man I've met all year. You waited at the tide gate like a spider who knew the fly was coming. You were carrying something for Tomas Reyes."

The name landed. Voss watched it land—the smallest contraction around the prisoner's eyes, gone in half a heartbeat, but Voss had spent thirty years reading faces the way clerks read columns, and a half-heartbeat was more than enough.

"There it is," he said softly. "Reyes."

* * *

He had never met Reyes. That was the joke of it—two years of hunting a dead man, and Voss could not have picked him out of a church congregation. He knew only the hand: the cramped, upright script that had filled the company's correspondence for eleven years before it supposedly drowned off Punta Salina. Voss had signed the death roll himself. He remembered the morning clearly: the widow Reyes standing in the counting house doorway with two children clinging to her skirts, receiving the standard condolence and the standard nothing, while Aldous Meridian himself looked on from the inner office with an expression of mild satisfaction, as though death were a line item that had finally balanced.

Voss had thought nothing of it then. Death rolls were his trade. He had written more of them than any priest in the province had absolved.

But Reyes had not drowned, and for two years—two years, while Voss collected his salary and burned the occasional rumor and told himself the ghost stories would die down—the dead clerk had been collecting paper. Statements. Copies. Names. And now he had sent a courier into San Ildefonso, which meant the collection was nearly complete, which meant—

Which meant it was already moving toward somewhere, and Voss did not yet know where.

"You gave the letters to someone," Voss said. "The boat was found empty. The oilskin packet was gone. So either you sank them—and men like you don't sink proof—or you passed them. Who works the tide gate? Who moves freely through the compound?"

Silence. But it was a different silence than before, and Voss heard it for what it was: not defiance but calculation. The man was protecting something. Protecting *someone*. Which meant the letters had a living keeper, here, in the port, close enough to touch.

Voss stood, buttoned his coat. "Feed him," he told the guards. "Don't mark the face. If I need to show him around town, I want him recognizable."

* * *

The counting house smelled of ink and camphor and, beneath it, the faint brine that no amount of scrubbing ever lifted from a building this close to the water. Voss walked the aisles between the shelves of ledgers with his hands clasped behind his back, and felt—for the first time in years—the particular cold that comes not from fear of a man but from fear of arithmetic.

He had always understood the arrangement. He wanted that recorded, if anything about him was ever recorded: he had never lied to himself about what the work was. Aldous Meridian built a pearl empire on the backs of divers who could not refuse, and when the divers died—as they did, at a rate the sea set and no one else adjusted—the company faced a choice. Honor the debts owed to widows and orphans, or erase the men entirely and keep the wages as margin. Erasure was cheaper. Erasure required only a clerk with steady handwriting and an enforcer with a steady temperament, and Voss had both. For eleven years the rolls had come through his desk, and he had stamped them, and the stamped names had vanished into the general silence of the sea, and the money had flowed uphill, and Voss had received his share of the flow, and he had slept well.

He had slept well because he believed the system held. That was the entire architecture of his life: not loyalty, not cruelty for its own sake—he had no taste for cruelty, whatever the dockworkers whispered—but confidence in the load-bearing walls. Papers moved where papers were supposed to move. Men who noticed things kept their noticing to themselves because noticing without power was just a slower form of drowning. The magistrate in the capital took the company's donations and asked no questions, and the magistrate's brother sat on the board, and so the law itself was a wall in the same building.

Every wall holds, Voss had believed, until enough weight leans on it.

And now a dead man was leaning.

"Audit schedule," he said, and the junior clerk came scurrying with the book. Voss ran his finger down the column of dates. The provincial audit was fixed—had been fixed for months, published in the gazettes, unremarkable, routine. The magistrate's assessors would arrive within the fortnight. All company records for the fiscal year would travel under seal to the capital for reconciliation. Standard practice. He had signed the transport order himself, back when it meant nothing, back when paper was only paper.

The duplicate ledger—the fair copy prepared for the audit, bound and sealed and scheduled to leave with the next mounted courier—was due to depart in four days.

Four days. And a courier of Reyes's was in port, and his packet was missing, and somewhere between the tide gate and the mountain road there was a keeper of those letters whose face Voss could not yet see.

He closed the schedule book with hands that were perfectly steady, and noted, in some cold back room of himself, that steadiness was starting to cost more than it used to.

* * *

By noon he had the list. Every worker with access to the counting house: fourteen names. He cut it down by the hour of Elias's arrival—the tide gate logs, the kitchen rotations, the net-menders' schedules—and arrived at six. Then five. One of the sorters had been seen near the drying racks at dusk two nights past; a deckhand swore a figure had moved among the nets after curfew and sworn, when pressed, that he'd been drinking and couldn't be sure. The descriptions conflicted. Small. Quick. Moved like someone who belonged there.

Small and quick described half the compound.

Voss stood at the window of his office overlooking the sorting floor and watched them work below: bent heads, quick brown hands, the endless sifting of shell and grit. He had looked at these people for eleven years the way he looked at weather. Useful. Unremarkable. The girl at the third table—Okonkwo's daughter, the diver's child, the one Mama Rosa had raised out of some stubborn tenderness he'd never troubled to understand—her fingers moved over the pearls with a fluency he had occasionally, idly, admired. She had memorized grades and weights without ever being taught to read them aloud, because reading aloud was forbidden and she had never once needed it forbidden twice.

She had also, he recalled with a slow tightening, been the one who found the stranger among the nets. Her own account, given flatly and correctly, the night the boat was found. She'd reported the intruder, raised the alarm, done exactly what a careful worker should do.

Exactly what a careful worker should do. Or exactly the story a clever one would tell.

He turned the thought over and could not put it down. It fit too well. The discovery was convenient; her account was flawless; and she was one of the few souls in the compound who moved everywhere without ever being noticed moving anywhere. Eleven years of watching her be invisible, and only now did he understand that invisibility was not a trait but an *accomplishment*, and accomplishments could be put to use.

Still. Suspicion was not proof, and Voss had survived this long by refusing to act on anything less. He wrote her name on his list anyway—not at the top, not circled, just present, just weighed—and the writing of it felt strangely like an apology offered in advance.

* * *

The message came up from the ferry station at dusk, and it was the cousin's handwriting, and the cousin never wrote unless something was wrong.

Voss read it twice by lamplight. A girl traveling south with company property—a sealed document case, audit papers, proper chits—had been detained briefly at the river ferry and released on the strength of the audit deadline. Small. Quick. Traveled alone. The cousin had checked the chits against the registry and found them, in his words, *flawless*, and it had evidently not occurred to him until afterward that flawless was its own kind of warning. He wrote asking whether such a courier had actually been dispatched.

She had not. No courier had been dispatched. The duplicate ledger was still in the counting house, still sealed, still waiting—except Voss made them break the seal that night, right there on the long table, with the junior clerk's trembling hands doing the work, and the binding came apart under the knife and the spine split open along the stitching that was not original stitching, that was new thread, small and even and strong, and inside the hollow of the binding, wrapped in waxed cloth, lay a packet of letters in a cramped, upright hand.

Dated months after Tomas Reyes drowned.

Voss read three of them standing up. He read the fourth sitting down, because his legs had opinions of their own. Names. Dates. Roll numbers. Divers erased, wages absorbed, signatures—including, on the fourth page, in the fourth line, his own name, written in another man's hand, describing an arrangement Voss had never spoken aloud in his life: *the enforcer receives per head a commission of eight reales, paid quarterly through the office of A. Meridian, as attested by the bearer of this letter, T.R.*

Proof. Not suspicion, not testimony that could be bought or beaten away—proof, sewn into the company's own skin, traveling under the company's own seal, addressed by the company's own schedule to the magistrate's own audit table.

He had built his whole life on the belief that corruption was safe because it flowed one direction: upward, into pockets deeper than any court could reach. Standing in the lamplight with the letters fanned before him, Voss understood—with the clarity of a man who has stepped off a stair that isn't there—that he had been wrong about the direction. Corruption was a current, and currents turn. The same machinery that erased the drowned could erase him. A signature on a death roll was a signature on his own.

"They carried it out," the junior clerk whispered. "Captain, the case was logged—it went south yesterday morning, with the escort as far as the river—"

"I know where it went." His voice came out level, and he marveled at that, distantly, the way a man marvels at the calm sea an hour before the storm makes landfall.

The girl. Okonkwo's daughter. Small, quick, invisible, flawless chits copied from a memory he himself had sharpened by forbidding her to read. She had walked the letters past his checkpoints wearing his own company's authority, and every guard she passed had waved her through because she was useful and unseen, because he had trained the whole coast for eleven years not to see people like her.

He had built the road she was escaping on.

"Ride tonight," Voss said. "Wake the garrison. Six men, fresh horses, the mountain road behind her—I'll follow at first light." He was already moving, pulling the keys, the pistol, the heavy coat. "And send word ahead to the ferry master. If she doubles back—"

"And the prisoner, Captain?"

Voss stopped with his hand on the door. Somewhere outside, the tide was turning against the pilings, patient as arithmetic.

"Hold him," he said. "If I bring back the ledger and the girl quietly, we hang him for theft and none of this happened. If I don't—" He heard the sentence fail, heard what lived inside the failure, and finished it anyway. "If I don't, he's the only witness who can testify he never delivered anything. Keep him alive."

Then he was out into the dark, and the mountain road ran south before him like a column of figures he could no longer make balance, and for the first time in thirty years Captain Voss rode faster than his composure, chasing a girl who had learned invisibility at his own instruction, toward a courtroom where his own name was already sewn shut inside a book.

Behind him, in the netting shed, Elias began—very softly—to laugh.
