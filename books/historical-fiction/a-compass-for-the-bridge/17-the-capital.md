## 17. The Capital

The city announced itself by smell before sight—coal smoke and horse dung and hot stone, layered over the salt she had breathed for eighteen years until it was no longer a smell at all but a condition of being alive. Then the road crested the last hill, and there it was: the capital, terraced down toward a harbor larger than the whole of San Ildefonso's, its rooftops red in the late-morning light, its church spires needling a sky gone pale with heat.

Ines stood at the roadside with the satchel strap crossed over her chest and let herself look. That was the strange part. For nine days she had permitted herself only glances—at roads, at faces, at the horizon for riders—and now, arriving at the place where everything would be decided, she stood in full view of the sky and stared at a city like a girl on holiday.

*Not yet*, she told herself. *You are not finished being invisible. One more day.*

The ledger rode against her hip inside the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth that had once protected dried fish, because Mama Rosa said nothing masked the smell of honest work better than fish. Inside its binding, sewn flat between the spine's cloth and the leather cover with thread pulled from the ledger's own hem, lay forty-one letters in the hand of a dead man. Tomas Reyes had counted drowned men the way an accountant counts coins, and he had written their names down where no one thought to audit: in letters to a courier, hidden in a net loft, carried over a mountain by a boy who was now sitting in a room without windows answering Captain Voss's questions with silence.

She did not let herself think about Elias. Thinking about Elias was a door she had nailed shut on the mountain road, somewhere past the second checkpoint, when pity rose in her like tide and she pushed it back down with both hands. Pity would slow her feet. Pity would put her on a boat home. Pity would kill them both and bury the letters with her.

She walked into the capital.

* * *

The magistrate's court occupied a block of pale stone near the harbor, fronted by columns and guarded by two soldiers who were more interested in the shade than in visitors. Ines circled it twice before she understood what she was seeing.

She had expected a fortress. What she found was a market.

The audit drew people the way a drawning drew gulls. Clerks hurried in and out with armfuls of documents. Merchants waited on the steps with petitions. A woman sold sugarcane from a cart at the bottom of the stairs, and two boys argued over a spilled basket of limes not twenty paces from the doors through which the colony's accounts flowed. The machinery of judgment, Ines realized, ran on the same fuel as the machinery of pearls: men in good coats who needed to be somewhere else by evening.

That was either the most terrifying thing she had ever learned or the most useful.

She bought a stick of sugarcane she did not want, because a girl standing still on the steps of the magistrate's court was a question, and a girl chewing sugarcane while she waited was furniture. From there she watched the traffic. Company men came out around midday—she knew the cut of Meridian's coats the way she knew the sound of Voss's boots—carrying document cases and wearing the particular blankness of clerks who have been yelled at. One of them stopped on the steps, mopped his face, and complained loudly to his companion about *ledgers upon ledgers upon ledgers, as if the man thinks paper breeds.*

"Which man?" his companion asked.

"The auditor. Reyes—" The clerk caught himself, glanced around, lowered his voice. Whatever he said next was lost to the harbor wind.

Ines chewed her sugarcane and felt the letters press against her hip like a heartbeat.

Reyes. Even here, even now, the name made men lower their voices. She wondered if the clerk meant the auditor or the ghost, and decided it didn't matter. Names had weight. She had spent her life learning how much weight could be carried by someone with no name anyone bothered to say aloud. Now she was carrying the opposite: too many names, all of them drowned, all of them waiting.

At the second bell of the afternoon, a company wagon arrived with the audit materials—the ledgers summoned from every Meridian station for inspection. Ines watched them carry the boxes up the steps and understood, with a lurch, that hers might already be inside.

* * *

Getting in took less than she feared and more than she liked.

The public gallery of the audit chamber sat above the main floor, reached by a stair behind the building, open to any citizen who could pass a soldier's bored glance. Ines went up in the middle of a knot of petitioners, head down, satchel clutched in front of her like every other bag in the crowd. No one looked at her. Nine days of practice had refined her invisibility into something close to art.

From the gallery rail she could see the whole chamber below: a long table where three auditors sat with stacks of paper rising around them like levees, a second table for the company's representatives—two lawyers and a senior factor whose face wore the expression of a man enduring a tax—and rows of lesser desks where clerks copied, compared, and initialed. Ceiling fans turned without conviction. Light fell in dusty shafts from high windows.

And there, on the auditors' table, in a stack marked for the San Ildefonso station, lay a ledger bound in green leather.

She recognized it the way a mother recognizes a child in a crowd of children. Her stitching was invisible—she had checked a hundred times on the road, in waystation privies, by candlelight, by daylight, running her thumb along the spine feeling for the faint thickness of paper where no thickness should be—but she knew. Forty-one letters. Eleven names of drowned men whose wages had fed the company instead of their widows. Her father among them, entered in Reyes's careful hand as *Domingo Okonkwo, diver, lost with full debts outstanding*—a lie written so neatly it had passed for truth for eight years.

An auditor drew the green ledger from the stack, opened it, and began to check figures.

Ines gripped the rail.

This was the moment she had crossed mountains for, and it was nothing. It was a tired man in a good coat turning pages with a licked thumb, comparing columns, making small marks in pencil. He did not know what rode inside the object in his hands. He saw pearls and prices. He could hold the proof of eleven murders—no, she corrected herself, not murders; erasures, which the company considered a gentler crime, though the drowned were equally dead—and set it aside in favor of checking whether the 1896 harvest tally matched the customs declarations.

Truth delivered, but not yet spoken. It sat in that chamber like a seed under snow.

She watched for an hour. The auditor worked through the green ledger methodically, page by page, and Ines tracked his progress the way a sailor tracks weather. The letters were sewn into the binding—front and back boards, distributed so no single bulge would show. He would find them only if he opened the covers fully, stripped them, or if the binding failed. Ledgers did not fail. That was the entire point of ledgers. They were built to outlast the hands that made them.

Unless—

A junior clerk came to the table, spoke to the auditor, and pointed at the stack. There was a brief exchange. Then the auditor closed the green ledger, squared it, and passed it—not back to the stack, but across to the copying desk, where a row of clerks sat transcribing key figures into summary books.

Transcribing. Not examining.

Her stomach dropped through the gallery floor. If the ledger was copied and returned to the company's custody—sealed, crated, shipped back to San Ildefonso with its secret still sewn inside—it would sit in the counting house forever, one arm's length from the truth, exactly as invisible as she had been. All of it for nothing. The mountain road, the ferry, Elias in his windowless room, her father's name still owned by the men who had erased him.

*Think like the ledger*, she told herself, the words she had used since the night she threaded her first needle. *What does a ledger do? A ledger gets audited. A ledger gets signed. A ledger gets filed.* And sometimes—a cold thought, arriving fully formed—sometimes a ledger gets disputed. Disputed ledgers were not copied. They were examined. Line by line. Cover to cover.

She needed the audit to doubt this book. She needed one figure wrong enough to demand scrutiny, and she needed the scrutiny to reach the binding.

There was a way. It had been in her hands for eight years. Every number in that book she had memorized in the counting house, cleaning floors in the dark while reading aloud was forbidden—every harvest total, every debt transfer, every suspicious zero where a dead man's wages should have been. She knew that ledger better than the man copying it. Better, perhaps, than the auditors. If a voice from the gallery called out a discrepancy—if an indentured girl, impossibly, corrected the record in front of the colony's accounting officers—they would have to look. They would have to open the book wide.

They would also arrest her. Or Voss would, the moment word reached him. Visibility, chosen at last, in its most dangerous form.

Her heart slammed against the letters. *Wait*, they seemed to say. *Not yet. Not unless you must.*

Below, a clerk licked his thumb and turned another page of the copy. The green ledger lay open under his hands, patient as a grave.

Ines moved along the gallery rail, closer to the stair, and made herself breathe.

* * *

She was still deciding when she saw the soldier come up the outside steps and speak urgently to the officer at the chamber doors. Distance stole most of the words, but not the shape of them—she read *gates* and *immediately* and *captain* off the man's mouth, and the officer's face tightened in a way she had seen before, on dockworkers the day Voss walked the pier.

She descended the inside stair ahead of the spreading murmur and came out onto the side street, walking fast without running, because running was a confession. At the corner she stopped in the shadow of a shuttered doorway and looked back toward the harbor road.

The city gates were half a mile off, where the mountain road funneled between customshouses and into the streets. Even at that distance she could see the dust of horses.

Three riders. Moving fast enough to raise a plume. The lead rider sat his saddle the way iron sits in a fire—straight, square, inevitable. She did not need the distance closed to know him. She had known that silhouette since she was ten years old, the way you know a storm by the color under the clouds.

Voss.

He should not have made it this fast. The mountain road was two days by horse for a rider who loved his horse, and Voss did not love anything. Which meant he had changed mounts at every station, which meant he had known—since at least yesterday—what the green ledger was. Elias had finally given him something. Not the hiding place; Elias didn't have it. But enough. Enough to make Voss ride his own men into the ground, because for the first time in his efficient, arithmetic life, Captain Voss was afraid.

He was riding to seize the ledger before the auditors understood it—or to denounce it as stolen company property, thief in hand, and watch them burn it in the courtyard for tidiness. Either way, the letters died in the attempt. Either way, so did she.

Ines pressed back into the doorway and let the riders pass on the harbor road, counting heartbeats to keep from counting the distance between them. Dust settled. Hooves faded toward the gates. Somewhere ahead of him, in the chamber she had just left, a clerk was copying figures from a book that contained, folded into its very bones, the testimony of forty-one dead men.

She had minutes. Perhaps fewer.

The sugarcane seller had moved his cart to the corner by the court's front steps. Petitioners still queued. Clerks still hurried with their cases. The whole apparatus of judgment ground on, indifferent, unaware that its verdict was currently being carried by a tired man with a licked thumb.

Ines stepped out of the shadow.

She thought of her father's last dive—the memory she had kept locked for eight years, the splash she had refused to hear again—and unlocked it deliberately, like opening a ledger to a marked page. *Domingo Okonkwo, diver.* If the book was going to be opened, she would be the one to open it.

She crossed the street toward the front steps, and the letters kept time against her hip like a second pulse, faster now, faster—

Behind her, at the far end of the avenue, the gates swallowed three riders and a cloud of dust.
