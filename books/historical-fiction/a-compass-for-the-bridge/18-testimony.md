## 18. Testimony

The audit hall smelled of beeswax and fear.

Ines stood at the back with the other company retainers—clerks' assistants, a cook's girl from the magistrate's own kitchen, two porters who kept their eyes on the floor the way she had taught herself to do for eight years. She stood among them and did not stand among them. That was the trick. You arranged your body so that a man looking straight at you would see only a wall of servants, and you would be one stone in that wall, indistinguishable, unremarkable, safe.

The ledger sat on the long table between the auditors like a guest of honor.

She could not stop looking at it. Three days on the road with that book strapped against her ribs, she had learned its weight the way a mother learns the weight of a sleeping child—the shift of it when she turned in the night, the press of the binding into her breastbone when she bent over a washbasin at the waystation, pretending to scrub a collar while her heart counted the hours until the ferry. Now it lay open under lamplight, and strangers' hands moved across its pages, and Ines Okonkwo discovered that being parted from a thing can feel worse than carrying it.

The elder auditor was a dry stick of a man named Corvel, with spectacles that magnified his eyes to the size of a child's. He read the pearl tallies aloud in a voice like sand poured slowly from a jar. His junior checked sums against shipping manifests. Neither of them knew they were reading a confession. They thought they were doing arithmetic.

*So did I*, Ines thought. *For eight years.*

The Meridian men sat to the left of the table—Herr Brandt, the company factor, gray-suited and sweating though the hall was cool; and beside him a lawyer from the capital whose face was composed of polite denials not yet spoken. Brandt had already answered six questions with variations of *the records speak for themselves*. He believed they did. That was the beauty of what she had done, and the terror of it: the company's whole architecture rested on the assumption that no one reads past the columns.

The letters were sewn into the spine beneath the marbled endpaper, forty-one pages of Tomas Reyes's small furious hand, each one dated after the day the ledgers said he drowned.

Corvel turned a page. Turned another. Stopped.

Ines saw it before she understood it—the stillness that comes over a man when the numbers stop adding up. His finger traveled down a column, returned to the top, traveled down again. He took off his spectacles, breathed on them, put them back on. His junior leaned over. Their heads came together in a whisper that had no sound but that Ines heard anyway, because she had spent eight years learning to hear the things counting houses did not say.

"What," Corvel said quietly, "is this binding?"

"The standard company binding," Brandt said. "Double-stitched calf over board."

"Then someone has stitched *letters* into our calf."

* * *

It happened the way she had imagined it a hundred times on the mountain road, and nothing like any of those imaginings.

The junior auditor took a knife to the endpaper. The hall went silent except for the sound of thread parting, stitch by stitch, a small ripping like something being unstitched from the world itself—and then pages slid out onto the green baize, fanned loose, and Corvel picked one up, and his lips moved as he read, and stopped moving.

"Factor Brandt." His voice was different now. Drier. Colder. "Who is Tomas Reyes?"

"A clerk," Brandt said. "Deceased. Two years ago. Lost overboard during—" He stopped. Ines watched him understand, watched the sweat go from damp to running, watched a man realize in real time that the records he trusted had been speaking against him all along.

"These letters are dated fourteen months after his death," Corvel said. "Either the dead write posthumously in San Ildefonso, or your death rolls are forgeries. Bring me the master rolls. All of them. Now."

Chairs scraped. The lawyer was on his feet saying something about jurisdiction, about the sanctity of commercial documents, about how an audit of pearl tonnage could not possibly extend to personnel files—and then the doors went open hard enough to crack against the wall.

Captain Voss walked in like weather.

He had come down the mountain road faster than any man should have been able to, and it showed: coat dusty, jaw stubbled, one knuckle split. But his eyes were as she remembered them from the sorting floor—not angry, never angry. Counting. They crossed the hall, touched the scattered letters, touched Brandt, touched Corvel, and then found her.

Eight years of practice held her face still. Eight years, and it nearly failed anyway, because Voss looked at her the way a man looks at a sum that has finally refused to balance.

"There," he said. "That girl. She is Meridian property—an indentured diver's daughter, third class. She stole company documents. Arrest her and this farce ends."

"Farce," Corvel repeated, without looking up from the letter in his hand.

"The woman is a thief and possibly mad. The documents are forged—"

"You said they were stolen," the junior auditor observed mildly. "You cannot have both. Either they are genuine company papers taken by a thief, or they are forgeries, in which case there is nothing to steal. Choose, Captain. I find I am beginning to keep a list."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—the first crack Ines had ever seen in them, hairline, swift, sealed again at once. She understood it even if no one else in the room did. He had raced down a mountain to cut one loose thread and had instead walked into a room where every thread was coming undone in public, where his own efficiency was becoming evidence.

"Captain Voss," Corvel said, "you will sit down, or you will explain to the magistrate why an enforcer of the Meridian Trading Company attempted to interrupt a colonial audit by force of presence. Those are the options. I am told the company values clear accounting."

Voss did not sit. But he stopped walking forward, which was its own kind of defeat, and the porter nearest Ines shifted half a step away from her, as if proximity to marked goods might mark him too.

And then Corvel said the words she had been waiting three days to hear, the words she had rehearsed answers to in the dark of the ferry hold:

"Does anyone in this room know what these letters allege?"

Silence. Brandt studied his shoes. The lawyer studied his notes. Voss studied her, and she felt the look land between her shoulder blades like a hand.

Ines Okonkwo stepped out of the wall.

* * *

Later she would not remember crossing the room. She would remember only the moment before it—her own heartbeat, absurdly loud, and a strange clarity, as though she had lived her whole life underwater and had finally, without noticing the exact instant, broken the surface.

Every eye in the hall came to rest on her and did not slide away. That was the thing no one warns you about, invisibility ending. It does not lift gently, like fog. It tears, like the endpaper off a binding, and underneath there is nowhere to hide, ever again, and the light hurts.

"I can read," she said.

Brandt made a small sound of outrage. Someone laughed—one of the porters—and strangled it.

"I sorted pearls for eight years by grade and by debt-code, and I memorized every number in the counting house because I was forbidden to read them aloud." Her voice shook. She let it. "I cleaned the ledgers. I know their weight better than anyone in this building, including the men who wrote them. And I know what is written in the margin of page four hundred and eleven of the 1893 master roll, because I dusted it every week for six years and was never once allowed to ask about it."

Corvel had drawn out the master rolls now—summoned, confiscated, spread open along twenty feet of table. He looked at her over his spectacles. "And what is written there?"

"A name." Her father's name rose in her throat like a swallowed stone coming back up. "Emeka Okonkwo. Diver, second grade. Recorded dead on the ninth of March, 1890, drowned off the Anegada beds with debts transferred to the company and wages forfeit." She made herself say the next part plainly, without decoration, because decoration was for people who had the luxury of being believed. "My father did not drown on the ninth of March. Nine divers came up that day. Mama Rosa fed nine men at the compound table that night. The roll says eight. My father was the ninth. He was erased so the company would not owe my family eleven months of wages—eleven months he had already earned, diving for pearls I was later made to sort with my own hands."

The silence had a texture now. She could feel the whole hall leaning toward her, officials and clerks and porters alike, and she understood suddenly that this was what power felt like when it finally arrived at the bottom of the world: not force, not money. Attention.

"The letters in that binding were written by a man your records call dead," she said to Corvel. "Tomas Reyes, company clerk. He copied the falsified rolls himself, year after year, because he was ordered to, and then one day he could not do it anymore. Forty-one letters. Names. Dates. The true count of the drowned against the false count in the books. Every diver erased so his family could claim nothing. I carried them here inside your own audit ledger because I knew—I *knew*—that Meridian trusts its paperwork more than it trusts any living person. You were never going to search the one thing you believe in."

"Where is Reyes?" Corvel asked.

"Gone to ground. Alive. Ask Captain Voss—he hunted his courier through my port for two weeks." She did not look at Voss. She could not afford what her face would do about Elias, about the nets, about the price already paid. "Ask him about a man called Elias Marsh. Ask him what happened at the drying nets."

Voss's voice cut across the hall, level as a ruled line. "This woman is fabricating—"

"Page four hundred and eleven," Ines said, still watching Corvel. "Read it aloud. If I'm lying, the roll will prove it in ten seconds. That is the thing about records, Captain—you taught us all to trust them."

Corvel found the page. His finger moved down the column of names, and Ines watched him count divers the way she used to count pearls: quickly, silently, accurately. His finger stopped. Went back. Came down again slower.

"Junior Auditor Reyes," he said, "will you read the entry for March 1890 aloud?"

The young man read it: *"Okonkwo, E., diver, second grade. Deceased 9 March. Effects: one compass, retained. Debt of 14 pesos 60 transferred to Company account."*

"One compass, retained," Ines repeated. "He dove with a compass he built himself, brass, with a bone ring on the chain. It guided the boat crews home through the night channels. A dead man's effects, retained—that means someone kept it. Who keeps a dead man's compass, Factor Brandt? And who keeps a dead man's wages?"

Brandt's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

Corvel removed his spectacles and set them down on the table with terrible gentleness, the way a man puts down a weapon he intends to pick up again. "I have audited thirty years of colonial accounts," he said to the hall, to no one, to everyone, "and I have never once been handed the proof inside the thief's own ledger. Junior Auditor—seal the master rolls. All volumes, 1885 to present. Sergeant, take names. Every name in these letters goes on a list tonight, and tomorrow morning every family on that list will be summoned here, and we will read the true counts against the false ones line by line until the arithmetic is settled."

"And her?" Voss said.

His voice was quiet. That was the worst of it. Ines finally turned and met his eyes, eight years of dread rising all at once, and found that the dread had changed shape somewhere on the mountain road. It was not larger than her anymore.

"Her," Corvel said, "is a witness. She stays where I can see her, under the protection of this office—which, Captain, given what I have read in the last ten minutes, I would describe as the safest place in this entire colony for her, and the most dangerous for you."

Voss smiled. It was a small, correct, bookkeeping smile, and it frightened her more than shouting would have, because men like Voss did not smile when they were finished. They smiled when they had found the remaining entry that no one had audited yet.

"Of course," Voss said. "The law must take its course." And his eyes went past her, briefly, to the doors—to the street beyond, to whatever messages were already riding west toward the capital, toward shareholders, toward a founder whose fortune was built on the ninth of March and nights like it.

The sergeant began writing names. The junior auditor stacked letters into evidence packets, sealing each with wax. Outside, the evening bell rang across the capital, and Ines stood alone in the middle of the brightest room she had ever been in, visible, exhausted, undeniable—and she thought of Elias, who had taught her that a courier's job is not done when the package is delivered, but when someone reads it.

They were reading it now. Line by line. Name by name.

Then a clerk hurried in from the outer office, whispered to Corvel, and the old man's face changed.

"Captain Voss," Corvel said slowly, "your cousin commands the river ferry at Paso Hondo, does he not? The magistrate's office has just received a report of an irregular passage—a girl traveling alone, carrying company property, three days ago. It seems the ferry log has already been... amended. Twice." He looked up. "Before you tell me what I should believe about this witness, you may wish to explain what your family believes about that ferry."

Voss's composure did not break. But Ines, who had spent eight years reading what counting houses did not say, watched his hands—watched them close, very slightly, into fists, and knew that the reckoning had only begun.
