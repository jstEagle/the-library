## 1. What Can Be Carried

The Ministry of Roads and Rivers had been a school once, before the war ate the children, and it still smelled faintly of chalk and wet wool. Quentin Kavanagh climbed its marble stairs for the last time on the fourth of Frostmonth, past windows papered over against the shelling, past a porter who no longer looked up from his stove. The republic's capital was dying by inches — a tram line closed here, a bakery dark there, the gas lamps lit later each evening as if the city itself were trying to sleep through something.

They had given Quentin the third-floor room with the cracked mirror, because it was the room they always gave to people whose names were about to stop mattering.

Inside waited a man Quentin had met four times in six years and would not have recognized on the street, which was rather the point of him. He was small and gray and wore the uniform of nothing at all. On the desk between them sat a packet wrapped in oilcloth, a needle, a spool of thread the color of Quentin's coat, and two cups of tea that had gone cold an hour ago.

"Sit," he said, though there was only one chair and Quentin was already taking it.

Quentin sat. Outside, somewhere below the papered window, a tram bell rang — one of the last, perhaps; the lines were going dark district by district like lamps.

"You'll carry the dispatches yourself," the gray man said. "No courier chain this time. The chain has links missing." He said it without drama, the way a man reports weather. "You'll take the coastal packet from Callowmark. The *Meridian*. You know her."

"I've never seen her."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "That's what knowing her looks like."

He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay the dispatches: three documents, closely written, folded to the size of a prayer card, and beneath them a single sheet of cipher key so fine it might have been a page torn from a missal. Quentin did not touch them yet. There was a discipline to this — you let the objects be objects until the moment came when they had to become something else.

"The codes go into your coat lining," the man said. "Left breast, over the heart, because sentiment is the best hiding place there is. No one searches where they expect to find something precious. They search where they expect to find something hidden."

"And if they search everywhere?"

"Then you're dead, and the codes are a tailor's problem." He slid the documents across. "Read none of them. That's not cruelty, Kavanagh. It's mercy. A courier who knows what she carries will flinch when someone says the wrong word in a tavern. A courier who carries paper and thread will not."

Quentin had been called many things in the course of the work — clerk, attaché, the cousin, the accountant, once memorably *the widow's nephew* — but the gray man used their true name now, quietly, the way you set down something breakable before picking up something sharp. It was the closest thing to honor the service offered.

"Say it back to me," he said.

Quentin said it back. The ship, the port, the tide, the consulate in Callowmark, the fallback address if the consulate burned — *when*, the man corrected, not *if*. The words went in clean and settled somewhere below thought, in the place where the body keeps its instructions: how to walk unhurried under a curfew siren, how to look bored at checkpoints, how to answer questions with answers so dull that the asker's eyes slide away before the sentence ends.

Being nobody was a trade like any other. It had apprenticeships. It had tools. Quentin had learned them young enough that they had grown into the bone, and there were mornings — more of them lately — when Quentin woke and could not immediately locate the person underneath all the nobodies, and had to lie still and wait, the way you wait for a name remembered too late.

*What can be carried,* the old instructor at the academy used to say, pacing before them in the winter light. *Not papers, not weapons, not gold. What can be carried through any border, any search, any interrogation?* The recruits had offered answers. Silence. Patience. A good memory. He shook his head at all of it. *A reason to come home,* he said finally. *Everything else gets taken from you eventually. A reason walks through.*

Quentin had written the phrase in a notebook then, young and earnest, and had spent the years since quietly failing to understand it.

Now the gray man pushed the needle across the desk.

"You sew. I don't trust anyone else's hands near my stitching," he said, which was a compliment in his language. "Small stitches. Turn the coat inside out. When you're done, put it on and wear it out of here. Don't fold it again."

Quentin turned the coat inside out on their knees. It was a good coat — gray wool, unremarkable, cut in the fashion of two years ago so as not to seem newly bought. The lining was linen, soft with wear. Quentin threaded the needle in the cold tea because wax was for people who expected to live long enough to be tidy, and began.

The first document went in along the left seam, flat against the ribs. The stitches were small as insect legs. Through the wall came the muffled sound of a wireless — the state broadcaster playing the same march it had played every evening since Autumn, as though repetition itself were a fortification. Somewhere farther off, the low cough of artillery, so routine now that neither of them remarked on it, the way farmers do not remark on wind.

"You've done this before," the gray man observed.

"Twice."

"And both times delivered?"

"Both times delivered."

He nodded slowly, watching Quentin's hands. "There's a version of this work where you believe in the thing you carry for," he said. "And there's a version where you carry because stopping would mean admitting what it cost you to start. Most of us end up in the second country and pretend we live in the first." He drank his cold tea. "I'm telling you this because you won't remember I told you, and because it's the only inheritance I have."

Quentin kept sewing. The second document went into the hem-weight pocket, folded around a strip of lead salvaged from the old weight, so the coat would hang the way it always hung. The cipher key — the dangerous thing, the thing that turned three pieces of paper into orders — went last, into the left breast, over the heart, exactly where he'd said. Sentiment was the best hiding place there was.

When it was done, Quentin turned the coat right-side out, shrugged it on, and stood. It hung correctly. It weighed what it had always weighed. In the cracked mirror across the room stood a person of no consequence whatsoever: mid-thirties, forgettable face, the posture of someone on an errand, the eyes of nobody at all.

"Well?" the gray man asked.

"Nobody," Quentin said.

"Good. Practice being him all the way to the coast." He gathered the oilcloth and burned it in the stove with the ceremony of a man lighting a cigarette. Then, at the door, without turning around: "Kavanagh. If the tide doesn't wait, don't make it. The republic has made a great many promises lately it cannot keep. Don't add yours to the pile."

He left. Quentin finished the cold tea, because waste was a sin the war had taught everyone equally, and looked for a long moment at the chair where the packet had been, at the small scattered threads on the desk like the shed skin of some transformation already complete.

Then they buttoned the coat, over the codes, over the heart, and went down the stairs of the school that was no longer a school, out into the dying capital, rehearsing being nobody with every step.

* * *

Three days later, waiting for the coastal train in a station where half the departure board had gone dark, Quentin slept badly in a wooden chair and dreamed — as one does, as one always did, the mind prying up floorboards the moment the watch relaxed — of tea.

Not the ministry's cold tea. Real tea, poured properly, in a real room, four years ago, in the brief green season when the war had paused to catch its breath and everyone had pretended the pause was peace.

Ilse Marchetti had kept rooms above a bookbinder's off the Weilerstrasse, in the quarter where the embassies thinned out and the translators lived, because translators were needed everywhere and trusted nowhere and so made their homes in the seams. Her table by the window was buried under drafts — treaties, communiqués, a novel someone in the foreign office was translating for reasons never explained — pages weighted down with whatever came to hand: an ashtray, a boot, a bronze hare with one ear worn smooth.

"You sit there," she had said, pointing Quentin to the only chair not holding paper, "and you touch nothing, and you tell me if this sentence sounds like a threat or a promise. In your language it can be either. That is the whole tragedy of your language."

She had read aloud. Quentin had listened, chin on hand, and rendered judgment — threat, probably, though the phrasing allowed deniability — and Ilse had laughed, a short, surprised sound, as if laughter were a document she hadn't cleared for release.

"That's what they pay me for," she said. "To hear the difference. And half the time even I can't hear it." She poured the tea — dark, bitter, sweetened with plum jam the way her grandmother had done it, wherever that grandmother had lived; Ilse belonged to that whole border-stratum of people who spoke five languages and were natives of none of them. "So. The famous silent cousin. Do you know, in six years you have never once told me anything true about yourself?"

"I told you I liked the jam."

"The jam is true." She considered this, tapping her pen. "Fine. One truth for one truth. That's fair rates."

And Quentin — why? Even now, even with the codes stiffening against their chest like scar tissue, Quentin could not fully account for it except that the tea was warm and the window light was green and Ilse Marchetti looked at people the way no one in the service ever did: as though they were worth reading closely. So Quentin gave her a truth. Not a large one. Not the name, not the work. Just a small one, some ordinary grief from before, held out flat on the palm like a coin.

Ilse took it seriously. That was the thing. She took it as seriously as a treaty clause.

"When this is over," she said afterward, refilling the cup, "when they finally sign whatever it is they're failing to sign — I'm leaving. There are universities in the neutral ports. Someone will want a translator who can hear threats in four languages." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. "You'll come find me. You owe me a visit where I'm not translating anything."

"It's a promise," Quentin said, and heard, even as the words left, how easily promises were made in those days — currency in a country that believed it would keep existing.

"One promise, then, on the books," Ilse said, and wrote something on the corner of a draft and tore it off and handed it over: her initials, and a date, and the words *one visit owed.* "Keep it. When peacetime comes, present it for redemption."

Quentin had kept it. That was the trouble with keeping things — the notebook, the scrap of paper pressed flat in the lining of a different coat, years ago — you carried them through every search and checkpoint and forgot, eventually, that carrying is also a form of intending. The scrap had survived three apartments and two aliases. The promise had survived the war's resumption, the fall of the northern front, the evacuation of everything with wheels or sails.

It had survived because Quentin had never once let themselves think directly about it, the way you don't look straight at a star.

In the station chair, half-dreaming, Quentin watched the tea steam rise off a memory, watched Ilse tap her pen and laugh her short surprised laugh, and felt the old armor settle into place — duty, schedule, the next hour's small necessities, all the cold comfortable machinery of purpose that kept grief at arm's length. The armor fit well. It had fitted for years.

But armor, Quentin knew — had always known, in the place below thought where the body keeps its truths — armor is just a way of deciding which wounds you'll allow.

The departure board clacked. A single new entry crawled up in letters of yellow light: COASTAL PACKET SERVICE, CALLWMARK, ONE FIRST-CLASS BERTH, DEPARTURE 06:40. The *Meridian* waited at the other end of that line of light, and beyond her, the sea, and beyond the sea, whatever the codes said — the orders Quentin would carry unread, over the heart, into a future the republic might not survive to see.

Quentin stood, shouldered the bag, and joined the thin line of travelers shuffling toward the platform: a nun, a merchant counting money he clearly didn't trust to banks, a young man in a soldier's coat two sizes too big who stared at his boots as if they might explain something.

Nobody looked twice at Quentin. Nobody ever did. That was the craft, and the craft held, and the coat hung right, and the codes rode quiet against the heartbeat they hid behind.

Only — as the train pulled out into the gray morning, past coal yards and burned tram sheds and a field where refugees' carts stood parked in patient rows like a caravan awaiting a decision — only once did Quentin's hand move without permission, rising to rest flat against the left breast, over the papers, over the cipher key, over the small folded ghost of a promise made in peacetime to a woman who translated threats for a living and could not, perhaps, hear this one coming:

that the republic she had served, the borders she had trusted, were closing around her even now, while Quentin rode away from the capital carrying orders sealed against their own heart.

One visit owed. Presented for redemption.

The train ran south through the frost, toward the tide.
