## 3. Callowmark Harbor

The storm that had battered us for two days broke at dawn, and Callowmark rose out of the mist like a promise neither of us had made.

Quentin Kavanagh stood at the Meridian's rail and watched the neutral port assemble itself out of the gray: a crescent of pale stone quays, forested hills behind, the masts of forty ships standing against a sky the color of old linen. Gulls wheeled and screamed. Somewhere ashore a bell was ringing — not a signal bell, not an alarm, but the slow, patient tolling of a clock tower marking the hour, as though the world beyond Vessany's borders simply went on keeping time without reference to what was happening inside them.

That was the thing about neutrality. It looked like peace. It sounded like peace. Quentin had been raised on maps in which Callowmark was a thin ribbon of green between the republic and the sea, a country too small to fight and too stubborn to be conquered, and they had always imagined it the way one imagines the far side of a wall. Now they were about to step through it, and the wall turned out to have doors everywhere, and through every door came people.

The harbor was full of them. Not ships — people. Even at this distance Quentin could see them on the quay: clusters of travelers with too many bags, families arranged in the rigid geometry of those who have waited a long time for something, a line snaking from a long low building with a flag Quentin didn't recognize hanging slack above it. And scattered among the dockworkers' drab canvas, colors that did not belong here. A coat of republican blue. A scarf in the black and gold of the Vessany civil service. Someone had hung a banner from a warehouse window — Quentin couldn't read it from the water, but the letterforms were unmistakably home.

*Refugees,* they thought, and then corrected themselves, because the word implied the republic had fallen already, and it hadn't. It was merely ending, the way a candle ends: still giving light, still warm to a cupped hand, but guttering in a draft everyone could feel and no one would name.

"Kavanagh." Captain Oduya's voice came from behind, flat as slate. She stood with her hands clasped behind her back, her coat collar turned up against the residual spray, and she did not look at Quentin so much as aim herself in their direction. "We'll make the outer mole within the hour. The rudder stock wants proper attention and Fenwick says the plating forward is weeping worse than he can solder. We'll be here six days. Perhaps eight."

"Understood."

"You'll want to go ashore directly."

It wasn't a question, but Quentin answered it anyway. "There are dispatches for the consulate."

"There are always dispatches for the consulate." Oduya's gaze moved across the harbor, unhurried, cataloguing. She had spent the entire storm like that, Quentin had noticed — standing on her quarterdeck in weather that put grown men on their knees, watching the sea as if it were a subordinate who might yet surprise her. "Things have changed in Callowmark since our last call. The consular district has new rules. Papers at every corner past the customs house. You'll carry your letter of passage where you can show it quickly."

"I will."

"And Kavanagh." She paused, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes that was not command and not quite warning. "Whatever you see there — whatever state it's in when you find it — your instructions don't change. Deliver what you're given. Receive what you're given. Come back to my ship before the evening gun. That last part isn't the Republic's order. It's mine."

"Yes, Captain."

She nodded once and turned away, and Quentin understood that this was the closest Oduya came to kindness: converting concern into procedure so it could be issued without embarrassment.

Below, the ship woke into arrival. Tamsin's voice carried up the companionway, high and imperious, directing Fenwick about coiling lines that Fenwick, by the sound of his grumbling, had coiled perfectly well already. Quentin descended to their narrow cabin — a converted storeroom amidships, granted grudgingly because dispatch couriers slept badly among sailors who asked questions — and took the packet from its locked case.

It was heavier than it had been in Vessany's capital. Physically it could not have changed; it was oilcloth over lead-foil over paper, sealed with the chancery's double eagle pressed into black wax, and wax does not gain weight at sea. But Quentin felt the weight differently now, after the storm, after four days of galley smoke and Fenwick's terrible songs and Tamsin's laughter ringing off the bulkheads. In the capital the packet had been armor. Here, approaching a shore where the republic's name opened some doors and closed others, it was beginning to feel like a target painted on the chest.

*You are nobody,* Quentin reminded themselves, dressing. Plain coat, no insignia, the courier's letter folded flat inside the breast pocket. Hair oiled back, face arranged into the mild blankness of a clerk traveling on dull business. They had rehearsed being nobody for years; it was the first discipline the service taught, before codes and ciphers, before anything. *Be the man carrying invoices. Be the woman visiting a sick aunt. Be so unremarkable that the eye slides off you like rain off glass.*

But the storm had cracked something. They knew it as they climbed to the deck. Two days hauling salt pork and coffee for the cook while the Meridian groaned through mountainous seas, laughing once — actually laughing — when a wave came down the hatch and soaked Tamsin to the skin and the girl had stood dripping and declared herself a fountain. Quentin had laughed. Couriers did not laugh where crews could hear them. Couriers were vessels themselves, sealed and anonymous, and a vessel that begins to enjoy its contents spills more easily.

Quentin pushed the thought down with the practiced motion of long habit, and stepped onto Callowmark soil.

* * *

The quay smelled of fish, tar, and woodsmoke — and under all of it, faintly, of burning paper.

Quenton noticed it before they consciously identified it: a dry, acrid sweetness threading through the harbor air, thin enough to dismiss, persistent enough to follow. They followed it. It was, they would reflect later, the first honest thing they did in Callowmark — before the consulate, before the lies, before any of it — they simply walked toward a smell, the way any person would, instead of away from attention, the way a courier must.

The line outside the low building resolved as Quentin approached. It was a processing office; the unfamiliar flag belonged to the Callowmark border service. And the line was made almost entirely of people from home. An old man in a railway uniform thirty years out of fashion, holding a leather grip with both hands as if it might be confiscated. A woman with two children and a third wrapped against her back, her accent carrying even at this distance — the flattened vowels of the southern provinces, where Quentin had done their first run at nineteen. A young man with the particular posture of a deserter, shoulders up around his ears, flinching whenever a uniform passed.

They waited in silence, most of them. That was what struck Quentin hardest. No wailing, no argument. The patience of people who had already lost the arguments, who had learned that queues and forms were the last machinery still running, and who fed themselves to it docilely, hour after hour, hoping to be processed into safety.

A boy — twelve, perhaps thirteen — worked the line with a tray of rolls for sale. He stopped near Quentin, took in the plain coat with a professional glance, and said nothing, which marked him as more discreet than half the adults in Vessany's chancery. Quentin bought a roll they didn't want just to have done something, and the boy moved on, and the line breathed and shifted and waited.

"How long have they been coming?" Quentin asked a dockhand leaning against a bollard, jerking his chin at the queue.

The dockhand shrugged with the whole geography of his shoulders. "Trickle, all autumn. This last month—" He made a gesture like water rising over a sill. "Boats, mostly. Fishing boats, ferries, anything that floats. Some swim the estuary at Vellport, if you can believe it. February water." He spat, philosophically. "Your lot must be losing."

"My lot," Quentin said carefully, "sell rope."

"Course you do." The dockhand grinned without warmth and returned to his bollard, and Quentin walked on with the roll going stale in their pocket, hearing the sentence repeat itself: *your lot must be losing.* Not *is losing.* Must be. A deduction from evidence, made by a stranger, in a neutral port, casually, the way one remarks on weather.

The burning smell strengthened toward the eastern quarter, where the consular buildings stood behind wrought-iron fences in a district of plane trees. Quentin registered it fully only when they rounded the last corner and saw the smoke.

Not the black boil of a building afire. A steady, disciplined column of gray rising from behind the consulate's garden wall — smoke with documents in it, smoke that curled with the particular flutter of burning paper, sheets of it sometimes lifting on the thermals and sailing up over the wall, char-edged ghosts spiraling over the plane trees before the wind took them. One drifted down onto the cobbles ahead of Quentin. They picked it up without thinking.

It was a visa application. Half burned. A name gone, a photograph gone, only the questions remaining, printed in neat bureaucratic rows: *Purpose of travel. Duration of stay. Persons dependent upon applicant.* And in the space for answers, in careful schoolroom handwriting, someone had written: *Any place that will have us.*

Quentin stood in the street holding a dead woman's or living woman's hope, half of it ash, until a consular guard at the gate said, "State your business," and Quentin remembered their own name.

"Dispatches. From the Meridian. Captain Oduya's compliments."

* * *

Inside, the consulate was eating itself.

The chancery hall still functioned — a clerk at a desk, a queue of three, the republican flag standing in its bracket by the window with ceremonial stubbornness. But the doors along the corridor stood open, and through each one Quentin saw the same scene in different arrangements: filing cabinets pulled out and gutted, drawers stacked in corridors like open graves; clerks feeding ledgers into iron bins already roaring; the First Secretary himself, a man named Aldous Renn whom Quentin had met twice and disliked moderately both times, standing at his office window holding a single sheet to the light as though checking a banknote for forgery, deciding, and dropping it into the flame of his desk lamp.

"Courier," Renn said, without turning. "Oduya's boat. Good. Sit. No — don't sit. Give me what you have and give it to me quickly, because in approximately three hours none of this exists and I intend to be at the Hotel Terminus with a drink in my hand when it stops existing."

Quentin did not sit. They produced the packet, laid it on the desk, and recited the receipt formula they had known longer than they had known their own handwriting. Renn broke the seals with a paper knife, scanned the contents with the speed of a man reading his own obituary, and initialed the receipt without expression.

"You'll take a reply," he said. "Sit down. It'll take ten minutes to draft."

While he wrote, Quentin allowed themselves to look — really look — at the office. The map of the republic on the wall, pins in every city Renn had responsibility for. Someone had begun removing the pins and stopped halfway, so that the western provinces stood bald and denuded while the east still bristled, a country dissolving unevenly, like ice on a thawing pond. The bookshelves gaped. The portrait of the President had been taken down from over the mantel, and the pale rectangle it left on the wallpaper was the cleanest thing in the room.

"They told us we'd have orders by spring," Renn said, still writing. He said it to the page rather than to Quentin, in the tone of a man who has discovered that complaining to subordinates costs nothing because subordinates cannot promote you. "Reinforcements for the archive staff. A cipher clerk. Instead I've received eleven separate instructions to destroy things and no instructions to do anything. Do you know what I've learned in thirty years of service, courier?" He signed the reply, blotted it, sealed it. "I've learned that when a state starts burning faster than it writes, it has already died, and the fire is just the estate sale."

He held out the reply packet. Quentin took it and began the receipt formula again, and Renn waved it off.

"Formula later. Listen to me instead, because I'm about to commit the last act of my career and it's called honesty." He came around the desk, lowered his voice. The clerk in the hall had drifted closer to the doorway, either to listen or to protect himself from being the only one who hadn't. "The naval attaché's office received a directive last week. If the situation deteriorates further — their phrase, as if anyone knows how to measure deterioration anymore — vessels departing Callowmark for republican waters will be inspected. Manifests checked against lists. There are lists now, courier. Persons barred from protection. Barred from passage. Persons whose presence aboard a republican vessel constitutes espionage." His eyes rested on Quentin's plain coat, mildly, terribly. "You'd be surprised who's on them. Translators. Schoolteachers. People who typed the wrong memorandum twenty years ago."

"The Meridian carries no passengers," Quentin said. "Diplomatic packet only."

"Of course." Renn smiled, and it was the smile of a man handing someone a heavy package and walking away quickly. "Then you have nothing to worry about, and neither does Oduya, and everything will proceed smoothly, and I will drink to all of it at the Hotel Terminus." He clapped Quentin once on the shoulder — a gesture that in another life might have been warmth. "Deliver that to her personally. Not to a clerk. To her hands. Tell her Renn said: sail light, sail early, and trust no manifest that anyone else has touched."

At the door, Quentin paused. The question came out before the discipline could catch it, small and naked:

"The refugees on the quay. Does anyone process them? Does anyone—"

Renn had already turned back to his window, where the smoke rose steadily from his own garden, where his own clerks were feeding his own files to the flames. "There's a form," he said. "There's always a form. Whether there's anyone left to stamp it—" He lifted one hand, let it fall. "Go home, courier. Whatever home is this month."

* * *

The walk back to the harbor took forty minutes because Quentin kept stopping.

They stopped at a tram stop where a notice board had been papered with three layers of announcements: ferry schedules, curfew advisories, and then, newest, printed on cheap gray stock that was already curling in the damp, proclamations in two languages. Quentin read them without appearing to read them, the courier's sideways scan, taking in whole lines at a glance. Regulations concerning foreign nationals. Regulations concerning assembly. And then a longer one, headed with the double eagle and the words *BY DECREE OF THE PROVISIONAL COMMITTEE FOR PUBLIC ORDER*, listing categories of persons whose movements were subject to restriction, whose assets were subject to seizure, whose presence in certain districts constituted — the phrase was exquisite, bureaucratic, obscene — *a burden upon the protective functions of the state.*

Names were to be published separately, the notice said. Lists posted weekly. All loyal citizens were encouraged to assist the authorities in locating persons failing to declare themselves.

*Loyal citizens.* Quentin stood at the tram stop feeling the ground tilt gently, the vertigo of a man on a stationary train watching the adjacent train move. Serving a state meant serving its continuity; that had been the whole architecture of Quentin's faith, the load-bearing wall. You served through governments because governments passed and the state endured. But this was not a government passing. This was the state turning on the idea of the state — devouring its own definition, posting notices about loyalty in a hand already shaking. And Quentin was carrying its mail. Had sewn its secrets into their coat. Was, at this moment, wearing it against their skin like a second spine.

A tram rattled past. Through its windows, ordinary faces, going somewhere ordinary. Behind the tram, on the far pavement, a woman in a gray coat was helping an old man post a bill of some kind — a notice of a missing person, maybe, or a shop sign, Quentin couldn't tell — and the ordinariness of it hurt worse than anything in the consulate. Life insisted on itself. That was the unbearable discovery of the afternoon. People posted signs. Children sold rolls. The line at the border office breathed and shifted and waited, and the republic died in the background like weather, and everyone kept making tea.

By the time Quentin reached the waterfront the evening light had come up brass-colored through the haze of burning, and the Meridian lay at her berth with her wounded bow toward the town, rigging alive with gulls. Fenwick was on the gangway, black-armed, arguing with a harbormaster's man about the price of plate steel. Tamsin sat on a bollard mending a net she had no business mending, and she watched Quentin come up the quay with that unblinking attention she had, the attention of someone who had been young in ports before and knew what a person looked like when the town had gotten into them.

"Evening gun in twenty minutes," she said. "Captain's orders. You're cutting it fine, courier."

"So I've been told."

"You've got smoke on your collar." She said it without accusation, just fact, and went back to her net, and Quentin climbed the gangway carrying the consulate's reply and the half-burned visa application, which they had kept, folded now in the same pocket as their own papers, though they could not have said why.

In the cabin, alone, Quentin set the two pieces of paper side by side on the bunk. The reply packet, sealed, official, containing whatever Renn's dying chancery wished to say to Oduya's able hands. And the scrap of ash-bordered hope: *any place that will have us.*

Six days in port, Oduya had said. Perhaps eight.

Through the porthole, across the darkening water, the smoke from the consular gardens still rose, thinner now, nearly done. And below it, along the quays where the refugees gathered against the cold, other fires had been lit — small ones, domestic, cooking fires, warming fires — dozens of them, trembling in the wind off the sea.

Tomorrow, Quentin decided, they would find cheaper lodging ashore. A ship was no place to wait out repairs; that was the reason they gave themselves, and it was even true. But underneath the reason, in the margin where Ambrose Pell would later write his careful notes, another truth sat quietly: Quentin Kavanagh had seen a city full of people from home, and could not bear to sleep behind a locked door while they slept in doorways.

They did not yet know that the cheapest lodging near the harbor was a boarding house called the Saltglass.

They did not yet know whose name was already inked on a list, waiting for the morning postings.

But the tide knew. The tide brings everything in, eventually.
