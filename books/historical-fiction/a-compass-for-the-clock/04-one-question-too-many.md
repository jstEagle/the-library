## 4. One Question Too Many

The census office of Callowmark Harbor occupied a former chandlery at the foot of the Customs Stair, and it still smelled of its old life — tarred rope, tallow, the ghost of dried herring — beneath the newer smells of ink and damp paper. Quentin found it on the second morning after the Meridian came into the graving yard, because Captain Oduya had said, in the flat voice she reserved for things that were not suggestions: *No one leaves this port without a census mark. Neutral harbor or not, they count everyone now.*

The queue outside began forming before the office opened, and it was unlike any queue Quentin had seen in Vessany's capital. There were no clerks in livery here, no merchants with ledgers under their arms. There were dockhands with their caps wrung in their fists. There was a woman in a nightgown beneath a borrowed coat who had clearly been pulled from a burning building two days ago and had not yet found another dress. There was a boy of perhaps eleven standing alone with the practiced stillness of someone who had learned that adults ask fewer questions of children who stand very quietly.

Refugees. The word had been an abstraction to Quentin until Callowmark. In the capital it had appeared only in dispatches, a column of figures, *displaced persons: 40,000, rising.* Here it was a smell — wet wool, fear-sweat, the particular staleness of people who slept wherever they were permitted — and a sound: the low murmur of names being rehearsed. Quentin listened to it as they waited, that murmur, and understood that half the line was practicing. Not their names. Their *other* names. The ones they would give the man behind the desk.

Quentin did not need to practice. Quentin Kavanagh of the diplomatic service had died neatly in a fire three years ago, along with a woman named Sera Ilves who had existed for six months and then been folded away like a chart no longer needed. What remained was a courier with no country visible on them, carrying codes sewn into their coat lining, and the trick — the whole trick, the only trick — was to be nobody so thoroughly that even you forgot to look for yourself.

*You are gray,* their instructor had told them, years ago, in a room without windows. *Gray is not invisible. Gray is what the eye slides past. Practice being forgotten before you ever leave a room.*

Quentin had gotten very good at it. They had not realized, until this queue, how much it cost.

* * *

Inside, the chandlery had been partitioned into eight booths with sailcloth dividers that reached neither floor nor ceiling, so that the room held eight simultaneous interrogations and none of them private. A clerk at the door stamped hands with a number in violet ink. Quentin's was 44. The ink smelled of anise and would not wash off; Quentin noticed three people in line already scrubbing at theirs with spit and sleeve.

They were called to booth six at mid-morning. The taker there was a thin man somewhere between forty and sixty, dressed with a precision that seemed almost aggressive amid the general dishevelment of the harbor — a gray suit brushed clean, a collar fastened to the last button, hair combed flat and parted with a ruler's exactness. His nameplate read **A. PELL, CENSUS TAKER, THIRD DISTRICT**, and beneath the nameplate, squared to the edge of the desk like a man squaring a grave, lay his ledger.

"Sit," he said. Not unkindly. That was the first thing Quentin noted about him: nothing he did would be unkind or cruel in any way that could be complained about. "Name."

"Sarel Voss." The Vessanyi merchant identity, the one with genuine papers behind it, bought years ago from a family that had genuinely emigrated. It was the safest lie because most of it was true. "Clerk, by trade. Traveling on ship's business."

"Which ship?"

"The Meridian. Packet vessel, out of Vessany. She's in the graving yard for hull repairs."

Pell wrote. His hand was small and exact, each letter identical to its brothers, the handwriting of a man who had never once hurried. He did not look up when he asked the next question.

"Born where, Sarel Voss?"

"Callowmark," Quentin said, and let a small, apologetic shrug follow it. "Though I haven't lived here since I was nine. My Vessanyi is better than my Callowmark dialect, I'm ashamed to say."

It was a calculated offering — a small flaw, freely admitted. Liars who were flawless were memorable. Pell took the flaw, weighed it, wrote something. Quentin could not see what.

"And your purpose in Callowmark. Ship's business," Pell repeated, in the tone of a man placing a specimen under glass. "What business does a clerk have aboard a packet ship?"

"Manifests. Cargo tallies. The Meridian carries mail and light freight; someone has to keep the numbers honest." Quentin smiled the smile of a person mildly bored. "It's dull work. I'd sooner tell you about it than you'd sooner hear it."

Something flickered at the corner of Pell's mouth. Not amusement. Recognition. *A man who has heard ten thousand dull answers and catalogued which ones are offered to end conversations.* He turned to a fresh line on his form.

"Kinship. Parents living?"

"My mother, in the interior. My father died when I was small."

"Her name and district."

Quentin gave them — a real woman, in a real district, chosen long ago precisely because her existence could be verified by anyone persistent enough to check, and because she had been paid enough never to be surprised by a visitor claiming to be her son's acquaintance. The instructor had called this *planting the orchard.* You did not build a cover story out of words; you built it out of verifiable things scattered across a province, and then your story was simply the path that connected them.

But Pell did not write the name down where Quentin could confirm it. He wrote it — Quentin saw the pen move — and then wrote *beside* it, a short notation in a shorthand Quentin could not read, and moved on without transition:

"Debts?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Debts." Pell's voice did not change at all. "Owed by you, owed to you. The Republic of Vessany is in default on seventeen international obligations as of this month. Anyone holding republican paper is holding losses. We record it. It is nothing personal, Master Voss. Nothing in this office is personal; that is rather the point of it."

"Then no. No debts worth the ink."

"Worth the ink," Pell echoed softly, and wrote that down too, or something like it.

The sailcloth divider rippled. From booth five came a woman's voice rising into something ragged — *"I told you, I told you four times, my husband went down with the* Andromeda *off—" *— and then a clerk's soothing monotone, and the sound of a stamp coming down. Quentin kept their eyes on the middle distance, on a water stain on the far wall shaped like a running dog. Gray. Be gray. But some animal part of them had begun keeping count, the way it did in bad rooms: two exits, one window barred, one taker, one clerk, seven strangers within earshot, and the nearest constable post — they had checked yesterday, out of habit — forty paces up the Customs Stair.

"Associations," Pell said.

"That's a wide question."

"It is a precise one." He folded his hands on the ledger. For the first time he looked directly at Quentin, and his eyes were the pale, patient gray of old dishwater, eyes that had looked at ten thousand faces and filed every one. "In ordinary years we count heads. This year we count what heads carry. Loyalties, Master Voss. Kinships, debts, associations. The Assembly has directed all neutral ports to maintain registers of persons arriving from republican territory, because a great many people are arriving from republican territory, and a great many of those people are not merely fleeing it. Some are carrying it. Some are carrying *orders* from it." He unfolded his hands. "Which are you carrying?"

"Nothing," Quentin said, and made the word come out tired rather than smooth. "I carry cargo tallies and my mother's disappointment in my handwriting. If I were carrying anything worth having, Master Pell, do you imagine I'd be queuing in the rain at the foot of the Customs Stair for the privilege of telling you my mother's name?"

Silence. The stamp thudded twice in booth five. Somewhere behind the partitions a kettle whistled and was ignored.

"No," Pell agreed mildly. "Probably you would not." He drew the ledger toward himself and began, with unhurried strokes, to close out the entry. Name, trade, vessel, purpose — the ordinary architecture of any census, the kind of page that had existed in every empire since empires began. Quentin watched him write and felt the familiar loosening in the chest, the small victory of a lie landed cleanly, already rehearsing the walk out the door.

Then Pell's pen stopped. He dipped it again — deliberately, Quentin would think later; he wanted to be seen doing it — and wrote one more thing. Three or four characters, maybe five, in the left margin of the entry, beside the name *Sarel Voss*, in that same tidy shorthand he had used once before. Then he blotted it, closed the ledger, and produced the stamp.

"What's that?" Quentin asked. Their voice stayed level. They were almost proud of how level it stayed. "The margin note."

Pell pressed the stamp to the back of Quentin's hand beside the violet 44. When he spoke, he did not look up from cleaning the stamp.

"Marks are for the door clerk. Margin notes are for me." He set the stamp in its cradle with a small click, like a clock part seating home. "Every taker keeps his own system. Names go in the column. Judgments go in the margin. Yours says that you answered every question correctly, Master Voss. Fluently. Comfortably. Better than comfortable, if it comes to that — you answered like a man who has answered before." At last he raised his pale eyes, and there was nothing in them but arithmetic. "Honest people are clumsy. I find I miss the clumsiness when it isn't there."

"The census doesn't test honesty," Quentin said. "It counts heads. Your own clerk told us so at the door."

"The census counts heads," Pell agreed. He tore the carbon copy of the entry free, squared it, and held it out. "The margins are mine. Keep your certificate, Master Voss — no ship sails without presenting it, and the Meridian will be inspected before departure. Routine. Everything here is routine." A pause, measured to the length of a heartbeat, no longer. "Give my regards to your captain. And to the crew. All of the crew. I'll want the full manifest by Thursday — passengers especially. Neutral harbors have been generous lately, and generosity attracts stowaways the way lamplight attracts moths."

He turned, without ceremony, to the queue, and his voice lifted into the same mild, tireless register: "Next."

* * *

Quentin walked out through the violet-inked crowd and did not stop walking until the Customs Stair was behind them and the fish market opened ahead, loud and gross and gloriously indifferent, gulls screaming over the gutting tables. Only there, among barrels and ice, did they permit themselves to think it through, standing as if studying mackerel.

Three errors, possibly four. First: too fluent. Pell was right; the fluency itself was the tell, and Quentin had known that rule for fifteen years and broken it anyway, out of the arrogance of practice. Second: the manifest. Passenger manifests could be requested; Quentin had assumed the Meridian, a packet ship with no passenger berths, would never draw one. Third — and this was the one that sat cold in the stomach — the margin note. Not *what* it said; Quentin would never know that. But *that* it existed. Somewhere in a locked drawer in a former chandlery, next to ten thousand rehearsed names, was a small cluster of shorthand characters that meant *Sarel Voss: watch.*

And fourth, the error that wasn't an error yet, only an opening: *all of the crew.* Why say it that way? Unless the census taker had reason to believe the Meridian's crew list might not match the Meridian's souls.

There was no reason. There was no stowaway. Not yet. Quentin stood among the fish barrels and understood, with the peculiar clarity that arrives only alongside dread, that the sentence had simply arrived early — that Pell was the kind of man whose suspicions preceded their causes, and that living in the same harbor as such a man meant every future choice was already half-made against you.

They bought a paper cone of fried smelts they did not want and ate them standing up, because a person with nowhere urgent to be was a person worth remembering, and walked the long way back toward the waterfront, taking the route past the cheap lodging houses on the salt side of the ridge — the ones whose windowsill notices promised *BED & BOARD, REASONABLE, NO QUESTIONS.* The first two had queues of their own, refugees spilling down the steps. The third was a narrow gray house with a blue door and a sign in careful painted letters: **THE SALTGLASS.** On the sill, behind the notice, someone had arranged a row of clocks, all ticking, none of them agreeing on the hour.

Quentin stood across the lane looking at those mismatched clocks for longer than a clerk with cargo tallies had any reason to. Then they crossed the street, and knocked at the blue door, and prepared — for the second time that day — to lie well.

This time, to their lasting confusion, nobody asked them anything at all.
