## 5. The Saltglass Boarding House

The consulate clerk had looked at my requisition for lodging funds the way a man looks at a coin he suspects is counterfeit.

"Repairs," he said, tapping the word with one blunt finger. "How long?"

"I couldn't say. The yard won't know until they open her hull."

He wrote something in his ledger — I would learn in Callowmark that everyone wrote something extra in ledgers here; it seemed to be a civic pastime — and counted out eleven days of per diem at a rate that assumed I would sleep in a doorway and be grateful for it. "Harbor district," he said. "Nothing above Copper Lane. Nothing with a sign out front."

I thanked him in the flat voice of a man who has no opinions, and went out into the rain to find a room that did not exist according to any official record.

The harbor district of Callowmark was built on the assumption that everything would eventually fall into the sea. Streets descended in wet stone stairs; houses leaned against each other like tired travelers sharing a load; laundry hung between buildings in gray banners that never quite dried. It was, I realized within an hour of walking it, a district made entirely of people in transit who had stopped moving. Every window held someone watching the street. Every doorway held someone who stepped aside just far enough to let you pass, and no farther.

I asked at four houses. At the first, a woman looked at my coat — good wool, capital cut — and shut the door while I was still speaking. At the second, the landlady wanted papers, references, a name I was prepared to give but not a signature to hang beneath it. The third had no rooms but suggested, with a look I could not parse, that I try the Saltglass, "if you don't mind your neighbors." The fourth was a brothel, which at least was honest about what it sold.

I found the Saltglass by its smell before its appearance: onions, coal smoke, and tar, the scent of a kitchen that cooked for more mouths than it could afford. The house itself stood where Copper Lane gave up and became stairs, three stories of soot-dark brick with a bowing porch and no sign at all, which the consulate clerk would have approved of. A hand-painted board by the door read simply: *ROOMS. ASK FOR WIDOW SEFU.*

I stood in the rain a moment longer than I needed to, rehearsing.

This is the part of the trade no one warns you about: not the danger but the rehearsal. Before every door, every checkpoint, every customs shed, I assembled myself — name, history, accent sanded down to nothing, hands roughened by a fictional trade. It took thirty seconds and cost something I could not name, because each time I finished building the false man, the true one went somewhere I could not follow. I had been Meridian's courier for six years. I had been nobody for six years. Somewhere along the way, the nobody had eaten the rest.

I knocked.

The woman who opened the door was tall and broad-shouldered, with gray threading through close-cropped hair and the forearms of someone who had hauled children, furniture, and possibly a boat out of harm's way. She looked at me the way the first landlady had looked at my coat — but where that woman had seen money and slammed the door, this one saw the whole of me, head to boots, and appeared to reach some rapid conclusion.

"You're soaked," she said. "Come in before you drip on the porch all day."

"I'm looking for a room."

"And I'm looking for help carrying a mattress down from the third floor. These things resolve themselves." She stepped back to let me in. "Name?"

Here it came. The assembly, the false man, the—

She waved a hand. "No, don't tell me yet. Names are like boots in this house. You leave them at the door and you'll find them again when you need them. Kitchen's through there. There's bread if the deserters haven't murdered it."

And she went upstairs.

* * *

I stood in the hall of the Saltglass boarding house with rain running off my coat onto clean-scrubbed floorboards, experiencing a form of disorientation I had not trained for.

In Vessany's capital I had passed through checkpoints where men with lists took my name, compared it against other names, and stamped me onward — and that had been comfortable, in its way, because being measured meant the measurer's world still functioned, and I knew how to live inside functioning machinery. This was different. This woman had declined to measure me. She had left the door of the false man standing open behind me with no one guarding it.

Through the kitchen door: warmth, onion steam, a long table, and five people eating bread and soup with the focused silence of the genuinely hungry.

They looked up. I looked back. Nobody asked anything.

A young man nearest the stove — I would learn he was called Roshen, and that he had walked out of a republican garrison with his kit and his conscience in the same bag — pushed the breadboard toward me without a word. An older woman with ink-stained fingers nodded once over her cup. Two others barely glanced up; in a house of exiles, a new face was weather, not news.

I sat. I ate. The soup was thin and excellent.

It occurred to me, somewhere in the second bowl, that I had not been fed by anyone else's hands since leaving Vessany — that aboard the Meridian I had eaten standing, in the galley's margins, taking care to owe no one and be owed nothing. Invisibility had rules, and the first rule was: accept nothing, because accepting creates a thread, and threads can be followed back. I broke the rule twice more before the bowl was empty, accepting tea and then a spoonful of honey from the ink-fingered woman, and felt the threads attach themselves one by one like small, warm hooks.

Widow Sefu reappeared as I finished, wiping her hands on an apron that had surrendered to its work. "Third floor, end room. Window faces the water. Stove works if you buy your own coal, works better if you steal your neighbor's, but Bram will know, Bram always knows." She named a price that was half what the consulate had budgeted and did not ask why a man with a good coat wanted the cheap room. "Supper's at seven. Late means cold. Doors lock at ten, not because we're afraid, but because locks keep honest habits."

"I can pay for the week," I said, and heard how the sentence ended too soon — *in case I have to leave quickly* — and wondered if she heard it too.

She took the coins without counting them. "Week, month, whatever kind of time you need. Towel's on the bed." And then, at the foot of the stairs, she paused and looked back at me with an expression I would spend chapters trying to translate. "You eat like someone who's been alone too long. Around here that's not unusual. Around here it's practically the qualification."

* * *

The room was narrow and clean, with a iron-framed bed, a washstand, and the promised window, through which the harbor lay in its gray evening light: cranes, masts, the Meridian small and competent among fishing boats, dry-docked with her ribs open to the yard's lanterns. Fenwick would be working late. Captain Oduya would be arguing with shipwrights. Tamsin would be wherever useful things were happening. For eleven days — perhaps fewer, perhaps more — the ship could do without me, and the thought arrived not as freedom but as a kind of vertigo, standing on solid ground with no orders ahead of me and only my own hours to fill.

I unpacked what could be unpacked and hid what could not. The codes stayed sewn in the lining, their small hard ridges pressing against my ribs whenever I moved, a private arithmetic riding close to the heart. Eleven days of being nobody in a city where nobody was apparently the local costume. I could do it. I had done longer.

At seven the smell of onions pulled me downstairs whether I willed it or not.

Supper at the Saltglass was served family-style, which is to say it was an ambush. I took the last chair, at the corner where I could watch the door — old habit, unkillable — and found myself beside the man Sefu had called Bram.

He was perhaps sixty, with white hair swept back from a forehead built like a dock, and he did not look up when I sat, because he was looking through a jeweler's loupe at the open body of a mantel clock, his tools laid out on a cloth with surgical precision. His hands moved with a patience I associated with people who had made peace with very slow work.

"He's deaf," said the ink-stained woman across the table, not unkindly. "Not that it slows him. He reads lips like a magistrate reads confessions, so if you're going to lie to him, do it facing away."

"Bram," she said, louder, and touched his elbow.

He looked up, removed the loupe, and turned to me fully, and I understood at once that I had never in my life been looked at so completely. Deafness had given him back all the attention the hearing spend on listening. He studied my face — mouth, eyes, the set of my shoulders — the way I imagined he studied a movement inside a watch: what is this mechanism for, and what has it been forced to endure?

Then he smiled, reached into his waistcoat, and set a small object beside my bread plate.

A pocket watch, running. Silver case worn to brass at the edges. It ticked against the table with a sound like a patient pulse.

"It's not a test," the ink-stained woman translated, amused. "It's a hobby. Or a religion. He mends watches nobody claims — pawned ones, dead men's ones, ones the tide brings in, he doesn't ask. When they're fixed and no one comes, he gives them to whoever sits next to him at supper. You've been chosen. Take it before he changes his mind."

I looked at the watch. I looked at Bram. My mouth opened to produce the refusal — *I can't accept, thank you, I don't take what I haven't earned* — the litany of invisibility, the first rule.

Bram watched the refusal assemble itself on my face and pushed the watch half an inch closer to my plate.

His expression held no pity and no challenge. Only a plain, enormous certainty, the kind you find in men who repair things: *this works now; it should go to someone who needs it.* No questions about where I'd come from or what I'd done or whose war I carried in my coat lining. Just a watch, ticking, offered across forty centimeters of pine table as though the entire apparatus of nations, borders, manifests, and codes did not exist — as though the only relevant facts in the world were that the watch ran, and I was hungry, and here was supper.

I picked it up. It was warm from his waistcoat.

"Thank you," I said, and made sure he could see my lips say it.

He nodded once, satisfied, and went back to his clock, and that was the whole ceremony. Around us the table talked — Roshen arguing cheerfully with a fisherman about whether the republican fleet could still put to sea (it could not; both agreed); the ink-stained woman, whose name was Corvel and who copied letters for illiterate dockworkers, complaining about a client who dictated love notes to three different women in one afternoon; Sefu presiding with the weary omniscience of a lighthouse keeper. Ordinary talk. Dangerous talk, in the capital. Free talk, here, where the republic's writ ran thin as harbor fog.

I sat in the warm noise with a stranger's watch in my palm and felt something in my chest come loose that I had not known was fastened.

Six years of being nobody. Six years of doors, lists, stamps, names given and discarded, of measuring every human contact for the thread it might spin back to me. And this household — exiles, deserters, a deaf man and a widow and a letter-copier and a boy who had abandoned his uniform — had taken me in over the course of one hour without asking a single question, and the terrifying thing, the thing I kept turning over like contraband, was that no question had been necessary. They had looked at me and seen the only fact that mattered: another person who could not go home. Everything else was detail.

After supper I helped carry dishes, because Sefu looked pointedly at my idle hands and then at the basin, and the grammar required no translation. Roshen dried beside me, humming something off-key. Bram carried his clock back to the parlor, where a shelf of them stood — a dozen cases in various states of undress, ticking out of rhythm with one another, a small disorderly democracy of hours.

"Leave yours on the sill tonight," Corvel advised, nodding at the pocket watch. "He winds them all at midnight. Don't ask how he knows the time. That's theological."

Upstairs, I set the watch on the windowsill facing the harbor, next to the window where the Meridian's masthead lamp burned steady green.

I lay in the strange bed and listened to the house settle around me — footsteps, a pipe drawn through a wall, Bram's clocks beginning their midnight argument below, a dozen hearts disagreeing about the hour and none of them wrong. In the capital, silence had been safety. Here the noise was safety, the clatter of lives overlapping so thickly that no single one could be pulled out and examined.

I slept without rehearsing. I did not notice until morning that this had happened, and when I noticed it, I lay still a long moment, unsettled as a man who finds his own door unlocked and nothing missing.

* * *

On my second morning, coming down the outside stairs with the harbor wind combing rain sideways, I stopped at the lane's mouth where a crowd had gathered around the customs-house notice board — a permanent fixture I had passed without interest twice already, and which now held a knot of readers so still they might have been painted.

Notices in Callowmark were routine: tariffs, tides, quarantine flags. But the paper at the center of the cluster was fresh, the ink still glossy, posted under the double seal of the harbor authority and — my stomach dropped before my mind caught up — the consular stamp of the Republic of Vessany, which had no right to be sealing anything anymore except that paperwork outlives the states that issue it, as I had good reason to know.

Someone in the crowd was reading aloud. Their voice had the flattened carefulness of a person handling hot metal.

"—declared enemies of the Republic, being persons barred from its territory, its protection, and its papers—"

Roshen stood at the edge of the crowd, and I saw his face change as the reader worked down the list, saw him count names the way a man counts waves between him and a storm. Beside me, Corvel had gone very quiet, her ink-stained fingers pressed to her mouth.

I should have walked on. A courier's first law: never be found reading enemy lists; never let your face do arithmetic in public. I stayed. Because the list was alphabetical, and because somewhere in the M's there was a name I had carried for six years alongside the codes, lighter than the codes and heavier than everything else, belonging to a woman to whom I had once made a promise over cold tea in a capital that still believed it had a future.

The reader reached the M's.
