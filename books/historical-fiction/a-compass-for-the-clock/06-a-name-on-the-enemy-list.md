## 6. A Name on the Enemy List

The proclamation went up on a Wednesday, pasted to the customs house wall in the gray hour before market, and by noon the crowd around it had thickened into something that could not be called a queue because no one wanted to admit they were waiting. They drifted, they smoked, they argued about fish prices two feet from the paper that was deciding their lives. Quentin understood the choreography. It was the same dance people did outside sickrooms and courtrooms — pretending proximity was accidental.

Quentin had come to the harbor district for an honest reason: the Meridian's carpenter needed pitch and canvas, and Captain Oduya had sent her passenger ashore with coin and a list precisely because a courier buying ship's stores attracted less attention than a courier doing nothing. That was the theory of invisibility Quentin had lived by for eleven years. Be about a small errand and you become the errand. A man carrying rope is rope.

But the crowd had gathered at the wall where the proclamation hung, and crowds have gravity, and Quentin had been three weeks among people who asked nothing of them and so had grown careless about the pull.

The paper was long — four columns of dense official script under the double-headed seal of the Republic of Vessany, the wax already softening in the harbor damp — and it began, as such documents always did, with *Whereas*. Whereas the security of the Republic requires the enumeration of persons whose conduct has forfeited their claim to territory or protection. Whereas treason wears many coats. Whereas, whereas. Quentin read the way they'd been trained to read, fast and flat, eyes moving in sweeps, looking for names they knew, and for most of the first column there were none — just lawyers and railway men and a colonel whose only crime seemed to be having lost a battle the republic had needed him to win.

Then, second column, halfway down, between a customs auditor named Voss and a whole family of Vrany brothers printed in a single line like livestock:

**MARCHETTI, ILSE — translator, formerly of the Foreign Ministry. Charges: unauthorized disclosure of state documents; aiding persons designated enemies of the Republic. Barred from all republican territory. Stripped of protection. To be detained if found.**

The harbor noise went on around Quentin — gulls, winches, a man swearing at a mule — but it arrived from a great distance, as though the sound had to cross open water first. Quentin read the entry again. Then a third time, not because the words were difficult but because some part of the mind kept insisting that repetition would change them, the way you knock on a door harder the more certain you are that no one is home.

*Aiding persons designated enemies of the Republic.* Ilse Marchetti, who had spent nine years rendering other people's lies into six languages with perfect, deniable neutrality. Who had once told Quentin, over tea gone cold in a ministry canteen, that a good translation was like a good witness — it changed nothing, it only made sure the truth arrived intact. What documents could she possibly have disclosed? Whom could she have aided? The charges were so unlike her that they circled back around to being exactly like her: she was the sort of person a dying government accused when it needed to accuse someone who had once been trusted. Treason needs a familiar face. It proves to everyone else how deep the rot goes.

*To be detained if found.*

Quentin stepped back out of the crowd, one body among bodies, face arranged into the mild boredom of someone reading shipping notices, and walked — did not hurry, never hurry, hurrying is a confession — down the quay toward the chandler's. The errand got done. Pitch, canvas, a coil of tarred line, the correct coins counted out with the correct small talk. The chandler's boy joked about the weather. Quentin laughed in the right places, the laugh of a person entirely unoccupied, and all the while something behind the ribs was doing arithmetic.

She was alive. The list said *to be detained if found*, which meant she was findable, which meant somewhere her heart was still beating and her hands were still translating. In the ledger of griefs that Quentin had been keeping since the capital, this was the first credit in months. And against it, immediately, the debit: she was in Callowmark. Somewhere in this city of consulates and informers and census takers writing things in margins, within however many miles Quentin could walk in a day, Ilse Marchetti was hiding under a false name from a republic that had just declared her naked to the wind.

*Barred from all republican territory. Stripped of protection.*

There is a particular cruelty in stripping protection. It does not authorize anything; it merely withdraws objection. Anyone may do what they like to her, and the republic will simply decline to notice. Quentin knew the phrase. Had carried dispatches sealed with that phrase. Had once believed it was the price of serving any state at all — that you swallowed its cruelties whole and called it loyalty, the way you swallow a pill with water you don't taste.

Quentin paid for the stores, signed the chandler's book with a name that belonged to nobody, and carried the goods back toward the harbor stairs very slowly indeed.

* * *

The promise had been made in peacetime, which is to say it had been made carelessly, the way all the best promises are — before either party understood what they would someday cost.

It was spring then, in the capital, the year the war still had borders. The Foreign Ministry kept a translators' room on the third floor where the windows faced an inner courtyard full of lilacs that no one was allowed to pick, and Quentin had been sent there to collect a decoded summary and had found the room half-empty because of some holiday, one lamp lit, one woman working over a draft with a pencil held the way surgeons hold probes.

Ilse Marchetti looked up, saw the seal on Quentin's folder, and said, without preamble, "You're the invisible one. They warned me about you." Then she pushed a chair out with her foot and went back to work, which was, Quentin would later understand, the single most disarming thing anyone had ever done to them. No questions. No performance. Just the assumption that Quentin was a person who could sit down.

They talked while she worked. Or rather she talked and Quentin discovered, with something close to alarm, that they were answering. She complained beautifully — about diplomats who thought fluency was translation, about a minister who wanted his speeches to sound "inevitable" in every language, as though inevitability were a dialect. She translated a sentence three ways to show Quentin how a single verb tense could start or prevent a war. She had ink on her left ring finger and a habit of reading aloud under her breath in whatever language she was leaving, and Quentin sat there holding their sealed folder and breaking, clause by clause, every rule of detachment they had ever been taught.

Near the end of the afternoon — Quentin could never afterward remember why the afternoon ended, only that it did, that everything ended that year — she said, stretching, "When this is all over, I want to go south. Somewhere with bad roads and good oranges. Nobody translates anything."

"You'll need papers," Quentin said. "South means borders."

"Then I'll need someone who knows how papers move." She said it lightly, a joke shaped like a request, and then — Quentin watched her do it — she made it real. "Would you? When it's peace. If I ask. Would you get me through?"

And Quentin — invisible, professional, sworn to nobody — heard themselves say: "You won't need me. Peace will let you walk through."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Quentin said. "That's not what you asked." A pause, the length of one lilac shadow moving across the courtyard floor. "Yes. If you ask, I'll get you through. Whatever it costs."

She had smiled then and gone back to her draft, and Quentin had walked down three flights and out into the spring and told no one, and had spent four years telling themselves the promise was void — because the peace never came, because she never asked, because a promise made in sunlight doesn't survive contact with winter.

Now here was winter, and here was her name nailed to a wall in wet ink, and the promise stood up inside Quentin's chest like a debt collector who had waited out the statute of limitations and knew it.

* * *

Back aboard the Meridian, Captain Oduya took inventory of the stores and Quentin's face at the same time, and said only, "You saw the wall."

"The wall," Quentin agreed.

"It goes up every market day now. Longer each time." Oduya coiled the tarred line herself, testing it hand over hand; she was a woman who trusted her own hands more than anyone else's eyes. "You knew someone on it."

It was not a question, and Quentin considered lying, and found — this was new, this was the Saltglass house working on them like water works on stone — that lying to Oduya required more effort than the truth. "A translator. From the ministry years. Ilse Marchetti."

Oduya's hands paused, resumed. "Marchetti. She translated for the armistice talks at Corvel, before your time or mine. Best in the service, they said." A beat. "Which makes her either very guilty or very convenient."

"She isn't guilty."

"I didn't say she was. I said the list doesn't care." The captain set the coil down and looked at Quentin directly, the way she rarely did; her attention had weight, like a hand on the shoulder. "Courier. We repair another week, maybe ten days. Then we sail on the morning tide, and after Callowmark there is nowhere neutral left to stop. Whatever you are going to do about what you saw on that wall, you should begin knowing that."

"There's nothing I can do," Quentin said. "She's one name on a list of forty. I don't even know where in the city—"

"But you intend to find out."

Quentin said nothing, which was itself an answer, and Oduya nodded slowly, unsurprised, the way captains are unsurprised by weather.

"My deck stays clean," she said. "Understand me. Whatever moves through this port moves on shore, not aboard my ship. I carry dispatches, cargo, and passengers with papers. Nothing else, no one else." She turned back to her manifest. "Get some sleep. You look like a man about to do something stupid, and stupidity wants rest."

* * *

At the Saltglass house, supper was beans and salt pork and Bram's bread, and Widow Sefu had somehow obtained an orange, cut six ways, and everyone pretended not to notice that she took none of it herself. Tamsin recounted, with relish and considerable invention, a fight she'd witnessed on the fish quay. Fenwick — who appeared most evenings now, ostensibly to see Tamsin, actually, Quentin suspected, to sit somewhere warm where nobody was drowning him in engine grease — argued mildly about whether the fight had involved two men or four.

Bram ate in his silent way, watching everything, and afterward he drew Quentin aside to the workbench at the back of the parlor, where the unclaimed clocks hung on their pegboard like a wall of stopped hearts. He tapped Quentin's sleeve, and when Quentin turned, pressed a scrap of paper into their hand. His handwriting was a draftsman's — precise, unhurried:

**You walked past the customs house twice today. Once going, once coming back the long way. People only walk past a thing twice when it has taken hold of them.**

Quentin stared at the note. Of course. Of course the clockmaker had seen; a man who repairs time for a living notices what passes twice.

"I read the proclamation," Quentin said quietly.

Bram nodded, unsurprised, and wrote:

**Someone on it?**

Quentin hesitated. The old reflex said: reveal nothing, trust no one, you are a vessel and vessels don't confide. But Bram was waiting with his pencil ready, patient as pendulums, and around him the room sounded with the ordinary music of the household — Sefu scolding Tamsin about the orange rinds, Fenwick laughing, the sea beyond the shutters — and Quentin heard themselves answer, in a voice rusty with disuse, the truth.

"A translator. Years ago she asked me for a favor, in peacetime, when it was an easy thing to promise. Her name went up on the wall today. Barred from the republic. Stripped of protection. Detained if found."

Bram read this — Quentin had spoken slowly, and he read lips well but liked the safety of confirming — and his face did something complicated and gentle. Then he wrote for a long moment, and turned the page:

**My brother was on a list once. Different country, different war. A neighbor tore the notice down before anyone official read it, and hid him in a hay loft for a year. He is alive now because one person decided a promise mattered more than a wall.**

He tapped the paper. Then he added, beneath:

**Print shops. If she is a translator hiding in this city, she will find work near words. Printers, scriveners, the pamphlet trade. Words want her the way ships want water. Ask at the small presses off Cordwainer Row. Ask badly, and they will tell you nothing. Ask as someone who also loves words, and doors open.**

Quentin looked at the note, then at the old man's face — seamed, calm, absolutely certain — and felt the last argument inside themselves go quiet. Not the fear; the fear would stay, it had a lease. But the argument, the reasonable voice that had spent the walk home explaining why forty names on a wall were none of Quentin's business, why the dead past should keep its promises to itself.

"How do you know about print shops?" Quentin asked.

Bram smiled, wrote one word, and showed it:

**Exiles.**

* * *

Quentin did not sleep. They lay in the narrow Saltglass bed listening to the house settle and the tide turn, and behind their eyes the proclamation burned in the dark, second column, halfway down, wedged forever between Voss and the Vrany brothers.

Eleven years of invisibility, and it had taken one printed name to make them visible to themselves. Because here was the thing Quentin could no longer deny, lying awake in a borrowed room in a neutral port while the republic that owned their name rotted across the water: the promise had never expired. It had only been waiting. Promises made in sunlight are contracts with the dark — you sign them in daylight and forget, and then the dark comes to collect, and it knows exactly what you owe.

*I'll get you through. Whatever it costs.*

She hadn't asked. But the list had asked for her, and Quentin had come to believe — could feel it settling into them like ballast — that a promise doesn't wait on the asking. It waits on the need.

In the morning, before the Meridian's boats were lowered, before the captain's coffee or the harbor bells, Quentin dressed in the coat with the codes sewn flat against their left side — the republic's last orders riding warm over their heartbeat — took Bram's note from under the pillow, and set out through the waking streets toward Cordwainer Row, following the smell of ink and hot metal deeper into the city.

Somewhere ahead, under a false name and a stranger's roof, Ilse Marchetti was translating her way toward survival. Quentin meant to find her before the census did, before Pell's neat margins closed around the harbor like a net — and to stand in front of her, visible at last, and hear what four years of silence had done to the easiest promise they had ever made.
