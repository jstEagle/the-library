## 7. Bram's Clocks

The trouble with hearing people talk about you is that they say so much and mean so little.

Bram Voss had been forty-one years old when the fever took his ears — a long winter of ringing, a spring of muffled voices, and then silence, total and clean as swept glass. That was thirty years ago. In those thirty years he had learned what most people never learn: that the mouth lies before the eyes do, and that hands are the last part of a person to learn deception. A liar's face can hold still. His thumbs will twitch at his seams.

So when the new lodger came to the Saltglass house — the one who signed the register as *K. Merle, clerk* and then sat at supper like a person listening to their own funeral — Bram watched the hands first.

The hands were wrong for a clerk. Too scarred across the knuckles. Too quick to close around the cup, the way a sailor closes around a rail without thinking about it. And when Widow Sefu asked, in her gentle way, whether *Mr. Merle* had family in Callowmark, the answer was no, none, but the shoulders said: *don't ask me again,* and the eyes went to the window, to the harbor, where a gray packet ship sat at anchor like a held breath.

Bram said nothing. He never said anything. It was the great advantage of his life, and for thirty years it had also been the loneliness of it.

He sat at the end of the supper table with his notebook and his pencil, and he wrote: *New one. Clerk. Lies with his mouth, tells truth with his shoulders.* He underlined *shoulders* twice, and Sefu, passing behind him with the bread, read it upside down the way she had learned to do, and smiled, and put an extra heel of bread on the new lodger's plate without comment.

That was how the Saltglass worked. Questions were a currency nobody spent here. Everyone at that table had paid enough.

* * *

The clocks told Bram when the clerk left the house each evening.

Not because they chimed — though they did, all seventeen of them, staggered through the hour like a choir that had never agreed on a key — but because he felt them. He kept the shop on the ground floor of the Saltglass, a room that had once been a parlor and now was a country of ticking. He felt the house breathe through them. When a door opened at the front, the draft moved through the hall and the pendulums shivered, all together, a small cold chorus. He knew Sefu's tread by the way the floorboards answered her heels. He knew Tamsin's by its hurry. And within four days he knew the clerk's: measured, soft-soled, always after dark, always alone, always out until the lamps were being doused by the city men with their long poles.

Every evening. Out into a city that was filling up with refugees and soldiers' widows and men selling papers off handcarts, out past the harbor office where the census takers sat behind their little windows like spiders who had learned to wear waistcoats. Out and out and out, and back again near midnight with salt wind in his coat and nothing found in his face, because there was nothing in his face ever, which was itself a finding.

Bram sat at his bench under the lamp with a dead carriage clock open on its side, gears fanned out like a surgeon's instruments, and he thought about the clerk's shoulders.

People searched cities for three reasons, in his experience. Love. Money. Fear.

The clerk walked like love. Fear walks fast and keeps to walls; money walks with its chin up, appraising. But love — love goes out every night into the same streets and comes home empty and goes out again the next night, because hope is the only engine that runs on nothing. Bram had walked that walk himself once, in another country, in another language, looking for a brother who had already drowned. He remembered the particular quality of the lamplight on those nights. You could see it in a person afterward, if you knew where to look. It never entirely washed off.

On the fifth evening he followed him. Not far, and not closely — an old man with a cane and a deaf man's habit of walking in his own weather draws no suspicion — but far enough. The clerk checked a wall by the fish market where the proclamations were posted, stood before them a long time, and did not move for so long that a gendarme drifted over to look at him too, and only the clerk's perfect blankness saved him from a question. Then he walked on, down toward the print district, where the shops shuttered early and the air smelled of ink and lamp oil, and there he stopped at a corner and looked at something small in his hand — a scrap of paper, a printed thing — and looked up at a building across the street with its upper windows lit.

Bram saw his face in that moment, lit by the shop window opposite. It was not the face of a spy casing a target. Bram had seen spies. Spies look at doors and rooflines and the intervals between patrols. This man looked at a lit window the way you look at a grave you're not permitted to mourn at.

Then the clerk made his mistake, or perhaps it was only his humanity showing: he turned, scanning the street out of habit, and Bram was already bent over adjusting his bootlace, and when he rose again the clerk had gone, swallowed by the print district's crooked lanes.

But Bram had seen. And Bram, who had spent thirty years learning to speak without sound, understood exactly what kind of message a man sends when he stands under a stranger's lit window and cannot make himself knock.

* * *

The watches began the next morning.

It was Sefu's idea, though she would have denied the framing. "Your bench is drowning," she said over his head, tapping the tray of unclaimed repairs — the sad museum of it, the watches whose owners had fled or died or simply forgotten, each tagged with a date and a name in his careful hand. "You mend them and no one comes. Mend them and give them away. Let them go be useful somewhere."

It was true. The tray had become an argument he was having with time itself: *someone will come back for this.* Thirty years of that argument. Some mornings he won. Most mornings the watch stayed in the drawer, running faithfully for no one, which seemed to Bram a crueler fate than stopping.

So he chose the first one deliberately. Not the fine ones — the gold repeater with the cracked enamel, the hunter-case with someone's portrait worn to a shadow inside the lid. He chose the plain one: a steel-cased pocket watch, soldier's issue from some army or other, the crystal starred but the movement sound beneath. It had come in two years ago with a deserter who'd traded it for a week's lodging and a lie about his name, and it had run ever since on Bram's bench, faithful as a dog tied to a post.

He cleaned it properly. New mainspring. Fresh oil in every pivot. He regulated it against the regulator clock — his master, the only clock in the room he permitted to be right — and when it held true through a full day and a night, he polished the case until the steel showed the lamp back at him like a small dark eye.

Then he wrote the note. He sat for a while over it, pencil hovering, because writing to a stranger is a different craft than writing to friends, and he had not done it in a long time. Finally he wrote:

*This ran for two years on my shelf, keeping perfect time for nobody. It seemed a waste. It is yours. Wind it daily. — B.*

He climbed the stairs at dawn, when the house was still blue-dark and the harbor fog had come up the hill to sit on the rooftops like a patient animal. The clerk's door was unlocked — careless, or trusting, or both. Inside, the room was bare as a held breath: bed made with military corners, one bag, one coat hung with a strange lumpy carelessness that Bram's eye snagged on and filed away without understanding. On the pillow, nothing. No books, no letters, no photograph. Men who keep rooms this empty are either honest or very good.

Bram set the watch in the center of the pillow, square, deliberate, the way you'd set down a card face-up.

He was back down the stairs before the house woke, and at his bench, and working, when the clerk came down for breakfast and Bram heard — no. Felt. When the floorboards above announced a pause at the top of the stairs, a stillness, and then the particular slow descent of a man carrying something carefully in his closed hand.

At breakfast the clerk said nothing. Of course. But he sat differently. He sat with the watch in his pocket — Bram could see the shape of it against his thigh, the way his thumb kept finding it through the cloth, touching it, turning it, reading it like braille. And when Sefu poured the coffee and remarked to the table that Bram fixed things, that it was his whole character and his whole tragedy, the clerk looked directly at Bram across the table — held his eyes, which people rarely did, unsure how to address a deaf man and often choosing not to try — and nodded. Once. Slowly.

Thank you, said the nod.

Bram nodded back. *You're welcome,* said his. And underneath both nods, the other conversation, the one neither of them could afford to have aloud: *I know you're lying about your name. I don't care. Here is proof I don't care. Tell me what you're looking for.*

The clerk's thumb moved on his thigh, over the watch, and did not tell him anything. Yet.

* * *

The second watch went up on the fourth morning, after Bram had followed him again.

This time the clerk had gone to the harbor offices, and Bram watched through the fogged window of the chandler's next door as he emerged with that particular rigidity of a man who has been measured and knows it. There was a census taker with him partway down the steps — a thin man with a ledger and a smile like a filing cabinet — talking, gesturing with his pencil, and the clerk nodding at everything with his face shut like a shop at closing. When the thin man finally peeled away, the clerk stood a moment on the quay, and his hand went to his coat, to the lining of it, and pressed there — once, hard, checking — and then dropped.

Checking something sewn in. Bram knew that gesture. He had made it himself, once, crossing a border with his brother's real name written inside his own collar.

So: the clerk carried something worth checking. The clerk had been counted by the state and disliked the arithmetic. And the clerk was still walking, every evening, toward the print district, standing under that lit window, not knocking.

The second watch was a woman's — a small silver thing with a chain of woven hair, which had always struck Bram as either the most romantic or the most macabre fashion ever devised, and which he had decided, long ago, was both. Its owner had been a singer at the opera house before the troubles, and she had left Callowmark in a convoy of carriages one gray morning and never sent for it. The movement was delicate and slightly temperamental, like its late owner, and it took him three days to bring it to rights.

Under the note he wrote:

*You walk past the same window every night. Knocking costs less than watching. Trust me — I have done both. — B.*

He hesitated over it. It was forward. It was possibly foolish. A spy would report it; a frightened man might bolt. But he had watched the clerk eat breakfast for eleven days now, and he had seen him pass the butter to Tamsin before she asked, and refill Sefu's cup when her hands were full, and sit a half-second longer than necessary at the table each morning as if memorizing it. Spies performed belonging. This man was practicing it, badly, hungrily, like a language learned too late in life.

He left the watch on the pillow beside the first one, the small silver one next to the steel soldier's watch, two timepieces side by side like two people sharing a bed.

That evening the clerk did not go out. Bram felt the absence the way you feel a missing chime. The house settled into its usual noises — Tamsin arguing cheerfully with Fenwick the stoker, who was lodging temporarily while the packet ship took on repairs, their voices a warm blur above the tick of the shop — and the clerk stayed in his room, and Bram, sitting at his bench with a jeweler's loupe screwed into his eye, tracked the slow pacing overhead. Four steps, turn. Four steps, turn. A man composing a decision.

At midnight the pacing stopped. At ten past midnight, footsteps came down the stairs, quiet as apology, and paused outside the shop door.

Bram did not look up. He let the silence stretch until it became an invitation, and then he raised one hand from the work and beckoned, still studying the escapement in front of him, making room in the world without making a fuss of it.

The clerk came in. Stood in the doorway a moment, taking in the room — the clocks, the drawers, the old man bent over the light — and then crossed to the bench and stood at its edge, and took out a piece of paper, and unfolded it, and laid it on the corner of the bench beside the lamp.

Bram finished the adjustment he was making. He gave the courtesy of completed work; it was a courtesy he valued beyond almost any other. Then he set down his tweezers and looked.

It was a pamphlet page. Printed cheaply, on bad paper, the kind of thing handed out in markets and stuffed into doorways: a poem, or half of one, translated into Vessanyan from some other tongue, the translation good enough to sting. At the bottom, the printer's mark — a small anchor wrapped in ivy — and below it, in tiny type, the name of a print shop in the Tanner's Lane district.

Bram read it twice. Then he looked up at the clerk, whose face was doing what it always did, which was nothing, except that tonight the nothing had cracks in it, and through the cracks Bram could see the thing he had suspected since the first supper: grief. Old, patient, load-bearing grief.

The clerk tapped the printer's mark. Once.

*Is this place real? Do you know it? Is she there?* — any or all of these, in a single tap of one finger, the way deaf men ask questions and the way desperate ones do, the vocabulary reduced to essentials.

Bram considered him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Around them the seventeen clocks ticked their uneven congregation, and somewhere in the middle of the noise, the regulator marked the quarter hour, steady as a heartbeat under all the others.

He knew the mark. Anchor and ivy — the Lindqvist press, Tanner's Lane, run by exiles who printed in six languages and had been raided twice and reopened both times. He knew it because everything in Callowmark that was hunted eventually passed through his shop to be hidden in plain sight, and once, two winters ago, a woman with ink-stained fingers and an accent from the north had brought him a mantel clock to repair and had waited while he worked, and they had talked — she talked, he wrote — about translation, about how every language was a country you could defect to. She had paid too much for the repair and he had let her, and she had said, at the door, in careful written words in his notebook: *A man who mends time must believe it can be repaired.* He had kept that page. It was still in the notebook, third from the back.

He got the notebook now. He opened it to that page, and turned it around, and slid it across the bench to the clerk, and watched him read.

He watched the clerk's hands close slowly on the edge of the bench, knuckles whitening, the whole body going still the way water goes still just before it decides to be ice. And then he took his pencil, and under the dead woman's — no, not dead, only gone — under the translator's old sentence, Bram wrote one line of his own:

*Lindqvist's press. Tanner's Lane. Ask for the night compositor. Go at opening, not at closing — a man standing outside a lit window looks like a thief. A man waiting at the door when it opens looks like what he is.*

The clerk read it. Read it again. Then he did something Bram had not seen him do in twelve days: he breathed out, all the way, like a man surfacing, and pressed his fist briefly to his own sternum — thanks, in a hundred languages, in none — and folded the notebook page with the care of a man folding a map of the only country he has.

He started for the door. Stopped. Came back, and from his pocket he took the small silver watch, the woman's watch with the woven-hair chain, and set it gently on the corner of the bench, and pushed it toward Bram.

*No,* said the gesture. *I can't take this one. This one should wait for her.*

And he was gone, up the stairs, and the house ticked, and Bram sat a long while with the silver watch in his palm, feeling its small heart beat against his lifeline.

In the morning, the clerk was gone before dawn. Bram felt the front door's draft move through the pendulums at first light, and smiled at his bench, and reached for his notebook, and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in letters larger than his usual:

*K. Merle, clerk. Real name unknown. Looking for a translator. Going, today, to get her.*

And below it, after a moment:

*About time.*
