## 8. The Translator's Lodging

The pamphlet found Quentin before Quentin found it.

It happened on the fourth evening of searching, in the fish market off Salt Lane where the stalls closed early and the cobbles stayed slick with brine until morning. A boy had been handing out papers at the mouth of the lane — not broadsheets, nothing so official, but thin quarto sheets printed on the cheap grey stock that came out of shops too small to afford better. Most people took one, glanced at the first line, and dropped them again, so that by dusk the gutters of Callowmark were papered with a drifting confetti of words.

Quentin picked one up because they had learned, in three days of walking this city with their collar up and their eyes down, to read what other people discarded. Discarded things told you what a place feared and what it had already given up on.

The pamphlet was in Vessanyan — not the language of Callowmark at all — and it was titled, in small careful type, *On the Meaning of Borders*. Quentin read the first paragraph standing under a lamp that smelled of burning whale oil, and by the second paragraph their hands had gone cold despite their gloves.

Because the prose was Ilse's. Not her name — there was no name anywhere on the page except a printer's mark, a small anchor above the letters *V&K* — but her sentences. Quentin would have known them anywhere. Ilse Marchetti translated the way some people sang: she kept the bones of the original language visible beneath her own, let the foreign rhythm show through like a scar showing through silk. She had once explained it to Quentin over tea, years ago, turning a draft back and forth: *A translation should not pretend the journey was easy. If the reader feels no resistance, I have lied.* No one else in Vessany or its scattered diaspora built sentences quite like that — clauses that leaned slightly against the grain, prepositions placed with the precision of someone who had weighed every one of them against five alternatives.

Quentin read the whole pamphlet twice, then folded it into their coat with hands that were not entirely steady, and asked the boy where the papers came from.

The boy looked at the coin, then at Quentin's face, and said, "Voss and Ketterle. Printer's court, behind the chandlery on Harrow Street." Then he was gone into the crowd, and Quentin stood a moment longer in the fish-smell and lamplight, feeling something dangerous open in their chest that they recognized, after a moment's confusion, as hope.

Three days of searching, and the city had simply handed her over.

* * *

Harrow Street was narrow, crooked, and lit badly, the kind of street that existed in every port city precisely so that things could happen on it without being witnessed. The chandlery announced itself by smell before signage — tallow and tar and rope-end — and behind it, through an archway barely wide enough for a cart, lay Printer's Court: a cobbled square the size of a drawing room, hemmed in by leaning gables, where three shopfronts glowed with lamplight against the early dark.

The middle one said *VOSS & KETTERLE, PRINTERS* in gold letters gone brown with age. Through the window, past bottles of ink arranged like apothecary's wares, Quentin could see a press — an iron hand-press of respectable age — and beyond it, in the back of the shop, a figure bent over a composing stick at a slanted desk.

The breath went out of Quentin slowly, deliberately, the way they had taught themselves to breathe in waiting rooms and border offices: exhale first, empty yourself, become the vessel that any room can pour anything into. *You are nobody. You are a person who noticed a pamphlet and followed it out of idle curiosity. You are a customer.*

They rehearsed it twice more under the archway, in the shadow between one lamp and the next, and then pushed open the door.

A bell rang — a cheap brass thing, the only careless object in the room. The rest of the shop was meticulous. Type cabinets in ranked order, galley trays stacked square, the floor swept clean of paper dust, everything smelling of oil and metal and the faint vinegar sharpness of fresh ink. It was the orderliness of people who worked with their hands and could not afford mistakes, and Quentin felt a sudden absurd tenderness toward the whole room.

The figure at the back desk did not look up. "Closing," she said, in Callowmarkan, flat as a shutter. "Come back tomorrow."

The voice did the thing Ilse's voice always did when she spoke a language that wasn't hers: it simplified. It flattened itself on purpose, sanding away her music, giving the listener less than she had. Quentin had watched her do it a hundred times in ministry anterooms, playing the modest translator, invisible beside whatever ambassador needed her. They had once told her it was wasted talent, that performance. She had laughed and said, *It is not hiding if everyone is looking somewhere else.*

"I'm sorry," Quentin said, also in Callowmarkan, keeping their voice level, ordinary, bored. "I read one of your pamphlets. Down by the fish market. I wanted to know where I could find more."

The pen stopped.

For a long moment the shop held perfectly still — the lamp flame steady, the press black and patient, the woman at the desk with her back to the door — and Quentin watched the stillness itself and understood it. It was not the stillness of someone deciding whether to make a sale. It was the stillness of someone counting exits.

Then Ilse Marchetti turned around.

* * *

She had changed in ways that had nothing to do with time. Her hair was cut short and practical, pinned back without ceremony; her hands were stained at the knuckles with ink that looked like it lived there now, permanent as freckles; she wore a printer's apron over a dress two seasons out of fashion. Three years since the tea and the drafts and the promise, and the years sat on her lightly, but something else sat on her heavily. She had been beautiful in the effortless way of people who forget to check mirrors; now she watched mirrors everywhere, all of them made of windows and doorways and other people's eyes.

That was what had changed. She had been a woman who moved through rooms expecting them to be glad of her. Now she moved through rooms expecting them to close.

Her eyes went over Quentin the way a customs officer goes over luggage — face, coat, hands, the space behind them — and Quentin stood inside that inspection without flinching, because flinching would answer the question before she asked it.

"More," she repeated. Her voice had lost the flattening. It was careful now instead, which was worse.

"The one about borders. *On the Meaning of Borders.*" Quentin reached into their coat slowly — everything slowly, in this shop, in this whole year — and drew out the folded pamphlet, rain-wrinkled at one corner, and set it on the counter between them. "I don't read Vessanyan well enough to be sure, but it seemed to me the translator loved the original more than was strictly professional."

Something crossed her face and was gone before Quentin could name it. "Anyone can buy the stock," she said. "Four deniers each. Ten for the set. There are six."

"I'd like all six."

"Then you're either a collector or a policeman." She said it lightly, testing the weight of the word in the air between them, and her eyes never moved from Quentin's face. "Policemen in this city have developed a taste for our stock. They take copies for evidence. They come back and ask who wrote what. They ask very polite questions in very bad Vessanyan." A pause, precise as a compositor setting type. "Your Vessanyan is good. I remember that."

"Ilse—"

"*Frau Kessler.*" The correction came fast and quiet and absolute. "In this shop I am Frau Kessler, widow of a typesetter from Brunnstadt, no history, no politics, no languages worth mentioning. The man who owns the chandlery believes it. The landlady believes it. If you know another name for me, you will not use it here."

The bell's echo had long died. Outside, somewhere up Harrow Street, a cart rattled past and faded. Quentin put both hands flat on the counter — an old gesture, surrendering the hands, and knew she'd recognize it for exactly that.

"I didn't come here to arrest you," they said. "I came because I've spent three days looking for you, and I was afraid the city would find you first, and it wouldn't be me."

Ilse looked at their hands on the counter. Then at the pamphlet lying between them, its title facing up like an accusation or a summons. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and the simplification was gone from it entirely; this was the real voice, the one with the grain in it, the one Quentin had carried across three winters like a coal banked in ash.

"Quentin Kavanagh," she said. "Sworn courier of a republic that is eating its own children. What exactly happens if I believe you?"

* * *

They drank tea in the back room because Ilse refused to go anywhere public and refused, equally, to stand in the shop like suspects in a tableau. The back room was hardly a room at all — a closet of shelving and spare rollers with two stools, a spirit stove, and a window high in the wall that opened onto the neighboring roof, which Quentin noted, and saw Ilse note them noting. Old habits greeting each other across the table like hostile ambassadors.

She poured the tea without ceremony and sat opposite, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence was so familiar it hurt. It was the silence of the ministry library, of long afternoons translating treaties while rain worked at the windows, of the particular companionship of two people who could be quiet together without anyone wondering why.

"You look well," Ilse said finally, which was a lie delivered kindly, because Quentin knew exactly how they looked: thinner, grayer, with the permanent slight squint of someone reading faces for weapons. "The republic still dresses you well, at least. That coat has good lining."

"Good lining matters in my trade."

"Your trade." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small precise motion. "Say what your trade is, Quentin. Out loud. In this room. I want to hear how it sounds."

"Courier. Cipher clerk. Bearer of dispatches." Quentin met her eyes. "I carry sealed things for a state that is dying, and I have just watched its consulate burn its own memory in a courtyard, and I have spent three years telling myself that carrying is not the same as choosing."

"And is it?"

"That's the question I cross the harbor every night to avoid."

Something eased in her shoulders — not trust, Quentin thought, nothing so generous as trust, but the settling of a weight into a position from which it could be aimed. She had been a translator in a ministry; she understood that answers were ammunition, and she was deciding whether Quentin was worth arming.

"You should know what you walked into," she said. "So you can decide whether to walk back out." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "In the spring, my colleague Denz — you remember Denz, the one with the laugh like a drain — submitted a report on my work. Translations I had done, correspondence I had handled, a seminar I chaired. He framed it as concern. By summer it was an accusation. By autumn it was a decree. My name is on a list now. You may have seen it posted."

"I've seen it."

"Then you know what the word next to my name is." Her voice stayed level; only her thumbnail, pressing white against the cup, betrayed her. "*Traitor.* For translating. For making sure that when two governments lied to each other, at least they lied in grammatically correct sentences. That was my crime, Quentin. Accuracy. Someone decided accuracy was a weapon, and everyone who owned one became an enemy."

The stove hissed. Somewhere beyond the wall, the press stood in the dark like a sleeping animal, and Quentin thought of Bram's bench of unclaimed watches, of Widow Sefu's table set for whoever arrived, of the whole quiet economy of sheltered people that the republic's decrees called treason.

"Why here?" Quentin asked. "Why printing?"

Ilse almost smiled — the ghost of the old laugh, the one like a door opening. "Because a translation dies in a drawer," she said. "Mine all did. Thirty years of treaties and communiqués and nobody outside the ministry ever read a word I made. And then Denz denounced me, and I ran, and I discovered something the ministry never taught me." She looked toward the dark doorway and the press beyond it. "Words want to be *carried*. All those years I thought I was serving language. Language was waiting for me to serve it properly. Six hundred pamphlets a week, Quentin, in Vessanyan, going home in sailors' kitbags. Your dying republic reads me at breakfast whether it likes me or not."

"There were soldiers collecting them on the quay yesterday," Quentin said quietly. "Burning them in a brazier by the customs house."

"I know. We print faster than they burn. It is the only arithmetic left that favors us."

*We.* Quentin heard it and didn't comment. Voss, presumably. Ketterle, whoever Ketterle was or had been. A household of two or three, bound by ink instead of blood — another Saltglass, another small raft.

But Ilse's eyes had come back around, and the warmth banked itself again, deliberately, the way a lamp is turned down to save oil. "Now," she said. "My turn to ask, and you will notice that I am asking the questions policemen ask, and you will forgive me for it, because the last man who sat where you're sitting also said he wasn't a policeman, and I believed him for eleven minutes too long."

Quentin waited.

"What ship brought you to Callowmark?"

"The packet steamer *Meridian*. Diplomatic mail. Repairs at Oduya's yard — we struck weather crossing."

"When does she sail?"

Quentin hesitated one heartbeat too long, and Ilse saw it, and her face closed another degree.

"Quentin. When does she sail?"

"The morning tide," Quentin said. "Day after tomorrow. Tomorrow, now — it's past midnight."

"Mm." Ilse set down her cup and stood, moving to the high window, and stood there with her back to them, looking at nothing, at the black shape of the neighboring roof. When she spoke again her voice was stripped of everything except structure, load-bearing words only, the way she talked when feeling threatened the sentence itself.

"A republican courier walks into my shop four days before the *Meridian* sails. He knows my true name. He knows my decree. He carries sealed dispatches whose contents he will not speak of — yes, I saw your face when I mentioned the consulate, don't pretend otherwise. And he tells me a story about pamphlets and fish markets and finding an old friend." She turned. The lamp caught half her face and left half in shadow, and Quentin thought: *she is translating me right now, from enemy into friend or friend into enemy, and she cannot yet tell which direction is faithful to the original.*

"In my old life," Ilse said, "I would have known within a sentence whether a text was genuine or forged. I have lost the gift, or the texts have gotten worse. So I will ask you only one question tonight, Quentin Kavanagh, and you may lie to me if you must, but understand that I will spend the rest of this night and probably the rest of this war deciding whether you did."

The stove ticked. The bell over the shop door hung silent. Somewhere far off, toward the harbor, a ship's bell counted a quarter-hour that neither of them marked.

"If your dispatches," Ilse said, "say what I think they might say — if somewhere in that good lining of yours there is a list, or an order, or a name — and if my name is in it—" Her voice did not break. It narrowed, which was worse. "Then you are not here because you found me. You are here because they sent you. And everything else you have said tonight is a translation with one word changed."

The room waited. The whole city seemed to wait — the press, the roofs, the tide pulling at the ships in the harbor, the great slow machinery of Callowmark grinding toward morning.

And Quentin, who had lied in nine languages to border guards and ministers and mirror-glass, sat with the truth and the lie balanced on their tongue like a coin on its edge — knowing whichever way it fell, it would fall across the new border that had opened between them, the one no treaty had drawn and no army defended, the width of a kitchen table and deep as the sea Ilse had to cross before it sailed.

"Ilse," Quentin began, and stopped, because for the first time in their life they could not find a sentence that was both safe and true — and she watched them stop, and read the stopping like a page.

"So," she said softly. "There *is* a list."
