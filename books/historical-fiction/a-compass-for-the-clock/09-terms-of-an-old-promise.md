## 9. Terms of an Old Promise

Ilse Marchetti had learned to read people the way she read drafts: first for structure, then for the lies between the lines.

The girl at the print shop counter called herself Anna Ferro, and she set type with clean hands and a journeywoman's indifference, as though words were lead and nothing more. That was the performance. Ilse had built one herself, brick by brick, in the eight months since Vessany had put her name on a list. You learned to move like furniture. You learned to let your voice flatten into boredom. You learned that the safest person in the room was the one who seemed to have nothing worth taking.

So when the stranger came in on a grey Tuesday asking after pamphlets — asking too specifically, with a courier's flat vowels carefully roughened at the edges — Ilse did not look up from her proof sheet. She checked her margins. She let three seconds pass like a slow tide.

"We do almanacs," she said. "Seed tables. A very good calendar."

"I was told you handled other material."

"People are told many things."

And then the stranger said her name. Not *Anna*. Not the false patronymic she'd bought from a smuggler in Callowmark's west quarter. Her real name, spoken quietly, in the accent of the republican foreign service, and Ilse's hands went still on the proof sheet because there was only one person alive who would use that voice to find her, and only one reason.

"Quentin," she said.

They looked older than she remembered. Thinner. They stood in the print shop doorway with harbor light behind them and their coat buttoned to the throat despite the mild air, and they had the particular stillness of someone who has spent months practicing not being seen. She knew that stillness. She'd worn it like a borrowed coat herself.

"You shouldn't be here," she said in Vessene, then, catching herself, in the trade tongue. "You especially shouldn't say my name."

"No one's following me."

"Everyone is always following you. That's what you are." She set down the proof sheet, wiped her inking knife though it didn't need wiping, and called to the back that she was taking her meal hour. The girl at the counter didn't look up. In Callowmark, not looking up was a civic virtue.

* * *

She took them to her lodging by three turnings and a courtyard, doubling back once without explaining why, and Quentin followed without complaint, which told her their tradecraft hadn't rusted. Her room sat above a chandler's shop, narrow as a coffin standing up, with a window that looked onto the chimney pots of the harbor district. She lit no lamp. The grey afternoon gave them enough.

They sat on the single chair; she took the bed's edge. Between them lay four feet of floorboards and eleven years.

"You look well," Quentin said, which was such an obvious lie that it functioned as a kind of tenderness.

"I look like a woman who sets almanacs for a living." She pulled off her cap and let her hair down, because hiding hurt her neck and she was tired of pain that served no one. "Ask what you came to ask."

"I came to see you."

"No." She said it gently, the way you correct a student who has almost grasped the grammar. "You came because my name is on a list. I know it's on a list; I've known since spring. Someone in this city reads proclamations and remembers old debts. So. Ask."

Quentin's hands were in their lap, fingers laced, the same nervous knot they'd made over translation drafts a lifetime ago in the ministry annex, when the republic had still believed it deserved the word *republic*, when tea went cold on the windowsill while the two of them argued about whether a treaty clause could be rendered faithfully or only approximately. Ilse had argued for *approximately*. She had built a whole life since on the truth of that argument: nothing crosses a border intact.

"The list," Quentin began. "It's not a warrant yet. It's—"

"It's everything. Don't soften it for me, I translated those instruments for six years. *Barred from protection* means anyone may report me and be paid for it. It means no consulate will stamp my papers. It means if the Meridian's captain found me at his gangway he'd be obliged to hand me over." She watched Quentin flinch at the ship's name, and filed the flinch away. "Yes, I know which packet you serve. I've always known. It's why I never wrote to you."

"Why did you keep translating, then? After you ran?"

"Because words don't stop being true just because the men who commissioned them are monsters." She heard her own voice rise and brought it down. "I'm sorry. That wasn't fair. Sit — you're sitting. Good."

* * *

Here is what happened, and she gave it to Quentin plainly because she wanted them to carry it out of the room, wherever they carried everything else:

Her colleague Aldo Renzi had occupied the desk across from hers for nine years. He had a wife who was ill and a gambling debt that was worse, and when the Ministry of Public Instruction began requiring loyalty attestations from all contracted translators, Aldo had looked at Ilse's draft of the Corvane trade protocols — a draft that softened, in three places, the language by which Corvan dockworkers could be conscripted — and he had seen in those three softened clauses either treason or salvation, depending on which side of the ledger he stood.

He chose the ledger. Of course he did. Everyone chose the ledger eventually; Ilse had simply believed, with the arrogance of the principled, that she wouldn't be worth anything on it.

The hearing lasted forty minutes. She was permitted no translator of her own, which was its own dark joke — the state's best translator, denied language, required to answer questions posed deliberately in dialects she read but had never spoken. They stripped her position. They did not strip her citizenship, not at first; they suspended it, a word doing labor it was never built for. And then a clerk — some second-floor pen-pusher she had never met, whose name she would never learn — had added her to the schedule of persons barred from republican protection, and suspension became exposure, and exposure became the knock on her door at four in the morning that she escaped by eleven minutes because a neighbor who had never spoken to her in six years stood in the stairwell blocking the corridor with a laundry basket and a great deal of improvised slowness.

"So," Ilse said. "That's the fall. Denounced by a tired man with debts. Stripped by a committee that misspelled my name in the record — *Marcheti*, one T — so that legally I am hunted under a version of myself that doesn't exist. There's a freedom in that, actually. The woman they condemned isn't quite me. Sometimes I let her take the punishment and I stay here, setting almanacs."

Quentin was quiet a long moment. Harbor sounds came through the window — a winch, a gull, the flat cry of a stevedore counting bales.

"I didn't know," they said finally. "When I got the posting I didn't know you were—"

"On the list? How could you. Nobody knows the list until it touches them." She leaned forward. "But here is my question, Quentin. Here is the question I have been turning over since you walked through that door saying my name like a key. Why are you here?"

"Because I promised you passage. Years ago. In peacetime."

"A promise made over cold tea by two people who worked for the same flag."

"It wasn't conditional on the flag."

"No?" She studied them. "Then answer me something else. Something harder."

She stood, went to the window, and looked down at the chimney pots rather than at their face, because there were things easier to ask a window than a person.

"What's in your dispatches?"

Silence. She let it stretch until it creaked.

"The codes sewn into your coat — yes, I know about the coat; I helped write the manual section on lining-codes, before you were weaned onto them. Every courier thinks they've invented the trick of carrying orders against their own heart. So tell me, courier. What does the republic want with neutral Callowmark? What does it want with its exiles? Because there are hundreds of us in this city, and lately the ones who watch the harbors have noticed that ships arrive with paper and leave with paper, and none of the paper is for us. Is your cargo about us? Are you carrying the sentence we haven't been handed yet?"

"Ilse."

"Answer the question."

"I can't."

"Not *won't*. Say it precisely. You of all people should love precision. Can't, or won't?"

Quentin's jaw worked. "Can't. The codes aren't mine to open. They're sealed for the consul's hand, or whoever holds the office when—" They stopped.

"When the office stops existing," Ilse finished. "Yes. I saw the smoke over the consulate square yesterday. Your masters are burning their own memory, Quentin, and you're swimming after them with more memory stitched into your ribs."

She turned from the window then and looked at them properly, and what she felt was not anger, or not only anger. It was the old grief of the translator: standing between two languages, knowing exactly what was being said in each, forbidden to make them match. Quentin sat in her chair with their duty buttoned up to their chin and their love showing at every seam anyway, and Ilse understood that she was asking them to commit treason in the next five minutes, and that they could not, and that they might anyway, and that whichever way it broke she would have to live in the wreckage.

"Let me tell you what I think," she said softly. "I think you don't know what you carry. I think that's how they design it — the honest couriers are kept ignorant so their faces stay honest. And I think some part of you came here hoping I'd never ask, because as long as I didn't ask, you could pretend the promise and the dispatches pointed the same direction."

"And if that's true?"

"Then you're the most dangerous man to love in this city, because you'd burn down a country for a promise but you won't open a seam for a question."

The word *love* landed between them like a dropped plate. Neither moved to pick it up.

Quentin stood. For a moment Ilse thought they would leave, and she braced for it the way you brace for a wave you cannot stop. Instead they said, "If I opened the lining and read them, and told you what they said — whatever they said — would that change anything? If they named you? If they ordered the Meridian to leave allies behind?"

"Yes," Ilse said. "Because then I'd know whether I was running from my country or merely from bad luck. Those require different maps."

"And if they didn't name you? If they're just routing orders, fleet movements, money?"

"Then I'd know my fear was bigger than my enemy, and I could sleep." She smiled, and it cost her. "You see? Either way I win. Only you lose."

"That's not — " Quentin pressed both hands flat against their thighs, a gesture Ilse remembered from examinations long ago, stress finding the body's oldest exits. "I swore an oath."

"You swore it to a state that denounced me for softening three clauses about dockworkers. Weigh your oaths, Quentin. Not equally. By their weight."

* * *

They stayed until the light failed. She made tea on the small spirit stove and they drank it while it was hot, which neither of them mentioned, because some mercies are better passed over in silence. They talked about small things — the chandler below, the price of coal, whether Callowmark's winter would be crueler than Vessany's — and it was almost like the annex again, two people keeping the world at the distance of a tabletop.

But Ilse was a reader of structures, and she could see the thing Quentin was building underneath the talk: a scaffold, load-bearing, not yet tested. They were going to do something. The measuring way their eyes had moved along her body earlier — not desire, appraisal — told her they were fitting her for something. A disguise. Papers. A berth.

At the door, she caught their sleeve.

"Whatever you're planning," she said, "don't plan it around me alone. And Quentin — if it comes down to your codes or me, choose the codes. Swear it. Because if you ruin yourself for me and I never know whether you'd have done it anyway, I will spend the rest of my life paying a debt I never incurred. Choose your oath, and let me choose mine."

Quentin looked at her hand on their sleeve and did not swear anything, which was, in its terrible way, an answer.

"I'll come back tomorrow," they said. "Before the tide after next. There's something I have to arrange."

"Arranging things is what got Aldo Renzi his thirty pieces of silver."

"Then let me be careful about what I arrange." At the threshold they paused, half into the stairwell's dark, half into her lamplight, a person translated imperfectly between two rooms. "Ilse. The clauses you softened. About the dockworkers."

"What of them?"

"They were right," Quentin said. "I read the final protocol. Everyone in the service knew. We just needed someone brave enough to render it honestly."

Then they were gone, footsteps fading down the stairs, and Ilse stood alone with her cooling cup and the word *love* still lying on the floor between where they'd sat, unclaimed.

She did not sleep. Toward morning she heard the harbor bells mark the change of tide, and she thought: *they sail soon, and whatever they're arranging, it involves that ship.* She thought about the manual section she'd written on lining-codes, and about how easy it would be, really, for a courier to cut the stitches — and how impossible, and how the impossibility was the entire point of oaths, and how she had just spent an evening demanding that the person she loved most in the world break theirs, and hating herself, and meaning it.

Below, the chandler's boy began hauling crates, and somewhere out in the grey streets a man was making rounds with a census book, writing names in margins.

Ilse dressed, bound her hair, and went down to set almanacs. Two days. Whatever was coming would come on the tide after next, and she would be ready, or she would not, and either way the type would need setting, and the ink would need mixing, and the woman who did not exist would go on printing the days of the week for a city that did not know her name.
