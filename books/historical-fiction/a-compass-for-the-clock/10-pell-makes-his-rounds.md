## 10. Pell Makes His Rounds

Ambrose Pell rose each morning at the same hour he had risen for twenty-two years — half past five, before the gulls began their arguments, before the harbor bell counted the quarter-day — and dressed in the dark, so that his wife would not wake and see him as he truly was first thing: a man assembling himself, button by button, into an instrument.

The uniform helped. Census takers were not soldiers; they carried no sidearms and wore no braid worth saluting. But the gray coat with its ink-blue collar, the leather ledger case slung across the back like a schoolboy's satchel, the little brass badge stamped with the double seal of the Provisional Registry Authority — these transformed a narrow-shouldered man of fifty-one with a weak left eye into something people instinctively straightened for in doorways. Pell had learned early that fear did not require a sword. It required only a form, and the patient willingness to fill it out.

He ate his bread and dripping standing at the window of the rooms he kept above the chandler's on Rope Walk, looking down at Callowmark Harbor doing what it always did: lying. Ships lied about their cargoes. Masters lied about their crews. The tide itself came in twice a day pretending it had never taken anything out. Pell had no contempt for this — contempt was a luxury — but he noted it, the way a surveyor notes subsidence in a bridge. Everything here was load-bearing and everything was cracked.

The republic was dying. He had known it before most, because knowledge arrived at his desk in triplicate. When a state begins to fail, the paperwork changes character first: requisitions grow frantic, exemptions multiply, and the census — his census, ordered under the Emergency Enumeration Act nine weeks ago, extended twice since — stops being a counting of persons and becomes a sorting. Who remained loyal. Who owed what to whom. Who could be declared.

Pell did not consider himself a cruel man. He considered himself a thorough one, and in the winter of a republic, those were very nearly the same profession.

* * *

The census office on Custom House Row had been a wool broker's counting house once, and still smelled faintly of lanolin and old money. Pell occupied the corner desk nearest the stove, not for the warmth but because from there he could see every person who came through the door, and every person who came through the door could see the wall behind him, where the master rolls hung in their long vertical panels like the sails of some paper ship. Names, districts, occupations, kinships, debts, declared loyalties. Column after column in his own cramped hand, or in the hands of the six assistant takers he'd bullied into legibility.

It was the margins he trusted.

The printed columns held what people chose to say. The margins held what they couldn't help revealing. A pause too long before naming kin. A wife's name given in a different accent than the husband's. A lodger listed at an address where the rent ledger showed no bed let. Pell wrote these things small and sideways, in pencil, in a private shorthand of his own devising: *q* for hesitation, *d* for discrepancy, *x* for a name spoken as if unfamiliar to the mouth that spoke it.

Nine weeks of rounds, four thousand households, and his margins were beginning to talk to each other.

This morning's work was cross-referencing, which was the part of the trade he loved the way other men loved fishing — solitary, patient, and built around a moment when something pulled on the line. He spread three days of district returns across the desk and began matching names against names, addresses against addresses, and the Saltglass boarding house surfaced almost immediately, the way a drowned body surfaces: slowly, then all at once.

The house on Weaver's Cut was registered to one Sefu, widow, occupation: letting of rooms, seven beds. Seven beds meant, by the Act's own arithmetic, between seven and eleven souls at full complement, allowing for shared pallets, which Callowmark's poor always shared. Pell had enumerated the house personally on the twelfth day of the rounds. He remembered it because no one there had made him work for anything, which was itself unusual enough to note.

Widow Sefu had answered every question with the unhurried precision of a woman reading from an inventory she'd composed years ago. Five lodgers. A clockmaker, Bram Vossel, deaf, occupation mending timepieces. A deserter from the Vessene third infantry who'd given his name as Toller and his hands as proof of his trade — a cooper's hands, honest as split oak. Two sisters from the river provinces who worked the net-mending sheds. And a traveler, arrived on a packet ship, name given as Kavanagh, Quentin, occupation: clerk, purpose in city: awaiting repairs to vessel, expected duration: one fortnight.

All perfectly in order. That was what had bothered him. He had written a single pencil mark in the margin — *q* — because Widow Sefu had hesitated, just barely, just once, and it had been over Kavanagh. Not over the name. Over the space before the name, a silence of perhaps half a second, the length of time it takes to decide whether to tell the truth.

People thought liars rushed. Liars didn't. Liars paused, because lying is a craft and craft takes a breath.

And then there was the traveler himself. Pell had enumerated hundreds of clerks. Clerks came to the census office stooped and apologetic, eager to be done, their whole posture an apology for taking up room on the form. This one had stood with his weight even on both feet and answered each question exactly, neither fast nor slow, giving nothing extra and nothing less — a performance of ordinariness so complete it became extraordinary, the way a coin minted in perfect detail reveals itself counterfeit. Real people were irregular. This man had been regular the way a form is regular.

*x*, Pell had written. Then, beneath it, after the man had gone: *hands wrong for a clerk.*

* * *

By ten o'clock the stove had warmed the office and Pell had assembled his discrepancies into a file, which he labeled in the Authority's required fashion — *SAL TGLASS, WVR CUT — IRREGULARITIES* — though privately he thought of files by their smell, and this one smelled of salt and deliberate calm.

Item: the traveler Kavanagh, arrived aboard the packet ship *Meridian*, diplomatic mail contract, registry Vessene, master one Oduya, R. Packet ships under diplomatic contract were exempt from cargo inspection but not, the Act was quite clear, from passenger enumeration — a clause Pell himself had drafted in the margin of an early memorandum, in pencil, which someone above him had inked and issued. It was the only sentence of his life likely to outlive him, and he was quietly proud of it.

Item: the *Meridian*'s crew manifest, filed at the harbormaster's upon arrival, listed a crew of nine. No passengers. Yet Kavanagh had told the census — truthfully or not — that he had come aboard the *Meridian*. A courier, then. Dispatches. Which meant the republic still trusted this vessel with its paper, even as the republic burned its own consulates two streets from where Pell now sat. He had watched the smoke from the consulate yard three days ago and thought, with professional detachment: *there goes my source material.* States that destroyed their records destroyed the meaning of their own forms. A census taken for a government that would not exist by spring was either an act of faith or an act of theft, and Pell intended to make certain it was the latter — that when the new powers came asking, as new powers always did, *who was here, who owed what, who could be declared* — his ledgers would be ready. Useful men survived transitions. He had seen it in the last transition, and the one before.

Item: the deserter Toller. Pell had run the name through the garrison liaison's desertion lists — a courtesy the garrison major extended to anyone with a seal, since deserters embarrassed everyone — and found no Toller of the third infantry. He found instead, on a list dated the fall of the northern depots, a cooper named Tollerman, Ansel, reported missing with kit at Marren Ford. Deserter or dead man; the list did not distinguish, and neither, strictly speaking, did the law. But a man using half a dead soldier's name was a man with something to shear off, and sheared names left edges.

Item — and this was the item that had pulled him out of bed before the gulls, that had him at his desk with the stove barely lit — item: the deaf clockmaker, Bram Vossel.

Vossel appeared in the census rolls of three separate districts over six years. Once in Callowmark proper, once in the fishing Quarter, once — and this was the gem, this was the fish coming to the hand — once in the border town of Hesselmark, four hundred miles east, enumerated by a colleague of Pell's named Ostrow, whose marginalia Pell had purchased for two bottles of plum brandy during a professional correspondence last summer. Ostrow had written beside Vossel's entry: *deaf-mute, communicates by notes; claims no kin; neighbors say he buried a woman in the garden during the plague year and never reported her.*

Never reported her. Not never killed her — Pell made no assumptions beyond his papers — simply never *reported* her. A death unregistered was a person erased from the count, and an erasure was a kind of door, and doors were what Pell's entire profession existed to find. Because a household practiced in the art of the unreported death was a household that knew how to hold a absence open — how to keep a bed warm on paper for someone who did not officially exist.

Seven beds. Five lodgers declared. One traveler with a clerk's name and a courier's hands. And a house whose keeper answered questions like a woman reciting an inventory — inventories being lists of things that exist, compiled so that no one asks about things that don't.

Someone else was at the Saltglass house. Someone between the lines. Perhaps more than one.

Pell sat with this for a long time, turning his pencil, watching the light move across the master rolls. Then he did what he always did when the picture was nearly complete: he checked it against his own vanity, which was the truest instrument he owned. Was he constructing this? Was the pattern merely his hunger for patterns? He examined each item honestly, the way a magistrate examines a confession extracted too easily.

No. The pieces were real. What connected them was inference, yes — but inference was the legitimate child of enumeration, the whole reason the Authority collected kinships and debts and durations. A census was a net, and a net was only rope until you drew it closed.

He drew it closed.

* * *

The naval liaison office was four doors down Custom House Row, in a building far grander than its importance, staffed at present by a single lieutenant junior in grade named Corr, who spent his days stamping harbor clearances and his evenings drinking in a tavern where he complained, at volume, about having been posted to a neutral port during a war he'd trained his whole life to fight.

Pell dressed for the visit with particular care. He polished the brass badge. He chose his newest ledger case. He wore, inside his coat pocket, folded twice, a single sheet of Authority letterhead bearing the emergency seal — blank, but bearing it — because a blank page with the right seal was worth more than a written page without one, a lesson he had learned from watching magistrates work.

Lieutenant Corr received him in a room with a model frigate on the mantel and offered tea, which Pell declined, because declining established that he had come to transact, not to linger.

"Lieutenant. I'll be brief, since your time is evidently committed elsewhere." A glance at the untouched stack of clearances, mild as milk. "The Authority has identified probable irregular persons connected to the Vessene packet ship *Meridian*, currently at your graving berth for hull repair."

Corr's face did the thing young officers' faces did when paperwork grew teeth: a flicker of interest suppressed by discipline. "Irregular how? Stowaways?"

"Undeclared travelers. Possibly persons subject to declaration orders." Pell opened his file — not wide, just enough that the labeled tab was visible — and set one documented fact on the desk like a coin. "A traveler enumerated at a lodging house in this port states arrival aboard the *Meridian*. The *Meridian*'s filed manifest declares no passengers. One of those documents is false. My mandate requires me to establish which."

"And that's a naval matter because—"

"Because the vessel flies a diplomatic contract and cannot be boarded by civil authority without causing an incident with what remains of the Vessene government." Pell let a silence do a paragraph of work. "Or what remains of it this week. Whereas a manifest inspection conducted under harbor regulations — a simple verification of souls aboard against souls declared, purely administrative, no search of cargo or papers — falls comfortably within naval prerogative. Particularly," and here he produced the folded sheet and laid it beside the file, "when requested jointly by the Registry Authority."

Corr looked at the seal for a moment longer than he needed to. Pell watched him understand it, or think he understood it, which amounted to the same cooperation. A young officer in a backwater, offered a taste of real authority — of course he'd take it. They always did. Pell had built twenty-two years of career on the predictability of ambitious men in boring postings.

"When?" Corr asked.

"The *Meridian* completes hull work within the week. Her master will want to catch the morning tide once the yard releases her — packet ships sail on schedules contracted months ago, and masters are punctual about contracts even when governments aren't." Pell retrieved the blank sheet, folded it away, and left the file's tab visible a moment longer. "I'd suggest dawn inspection. Before departure. A vessel already underway is a jurisdictional quarrel; a vessel at the gangway is a signature."

"Dawn." Corr said it the way men say words that have begun to taste of consequence.

"I'll attend with the passenger roll and my enumeration records," Pell said, rising, extending his hand. "You need bring only your rank and two witnesses. Administrative. Routine." He smiled — he had practiced this smile in a mirror in his thirties and retired it except for official use; it reached the mouth and stopped, conveying sympathy without conceding anything. "In my experience, Lieutenant, the routine ones are where everything is found."

* * *

He walked back along the harbor road in the late light, past the ropewalks and the tar sheds, past the consulate yard where the smoke had stopped but the smell of burned paper still hung like weather. At the mouth of Weaver's Cut he stopped, as he had stopped twice before on his rounds, and looked up the narrow street at the Saltglass boarding house.

It presented a modest face to the lane: slate roof, blue door, a window box gone brown with winter, lamplight in the downstairs room where shadows moved — several of them, unhurried, overlapping, the shadows of people sharing a room because sharing was what such houses were for. From somewhere inside came a thin, sweet sound. A clock striking, and then another, slightly off the hour, and then a third catching up, so that the house seemed to be arguing gently with itself about what time it was.

Pell stood in the cold and listened, and felt — he catalogued the feeling accurately, because he catalogued everything — a small, unwelcome ache that resembled envy. They were in there falsifying the world together, whoever they were: the widow with her inventory voice, the deserter with his borrowed name, the clerk with the wrong hands, the clockmaker who buried his dead unreported. They had built a little structure of lies and heated it and lit it, and it glowed at the end of the street like any other home.

His own rooms above the chandler's were dark when he got there. His wife had gone in autumn to her sister's in the interior, taking the good chairs, and he had not yet decided whether this was separation or ending, and had entered it in no ledger at all.

That was the difference, he supposed, standing a moment on his own doorstep with his breath smoking. They lied in company. He kept accounts alone.

Upstairs, he sharpened a fresh pencil, opened the file marked *SALTGLASS*, and wrote the request for naval assistance in full and proper form — date, statute, grounds, signature, seal — each line laid down as carefully as a keel. When it was done he read it over once, added a final line to the schedule of persons to be verified —

*Kavanagh, Quentin, clerk — verify presence aboard, compare handwriting, enumerate under full powers*

—

and blotted it, and set it where he would see it first thing in the morning, and put out his light.

Dawn inspection, before the tide. Three days hence, if the yard released her on schedule; two, if the master hurried.

Somewhere down in the tangle of streets between him and the harbor, in a house full of struck clocks, someone was preparing to be somebody else at a gangway. Pell fell asleep quickly, as he always did, work concluded, conscience stowed — and dreamed, mildly, without alarm, of long columns of names in which one name kept changing its spelling, and woke at half past five with the solution already in mind: he would count the shadows. Whatever the manifests said, whatever the mouths said, he would stand at that gangway and count every soul that came off that ship against every soul that went on, and paper could lie all it liked to a man who had learned to count.
