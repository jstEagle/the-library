## 11. Dead Man's Papers

The stokehold of the *Meridian* was the only place aboard where a person could sweat honestly in winter. Heat came off the boiler in waves you could lean into, and Fenwick worked it like a man arguing with an old friend — shoveling coal in long, practiced arcs, wiping his face with a rag gone gray as ash, singing under his breath in a language Quentin had never placed and had never asked about.

Quentin stood at the foot of the ladder with their cap in their hands, feeling like a beggar at a church door.

They had rehearsed this on the walk aft. They had rehearsed it in the galley that morning, over tea Tamsin had poured without being asked, and they had rehearsed it again on the quay, watching the harbor ice thin and thicken at the edges of the pilings. Every rehearsal failed for the same reason: there was no way to ask this question that did not reveal everything it touched.

"Kavanagh." Fenwick didn't turn from the furnace door. "Either come down or go up. Standing on the ladder is how men break necks."

Quentin came down.

The stokehold was a cathedral of iron. Light came through the open firebox door in pulses, orange and total, so that Fenwick's face appeared and vanished like something surfacing in dark water. He was older than he'd seemed the first week out — the heat had a way of stripping years back to the bone — and his forearms were mapped with old burns, each one healed into a pale seam. A man who had spent his life being marked by his work, Quentin thought, while I have spent mine making sure mine leaves nothing behind.

"I need to ask you something," Quentin said, "and before I do, I want you to know that you can refuse."

Fenwick closed the firebox. The light died. In the sudden gloom he looked at Quentin steadily, the way he did everything — without hurry, without theater.

"That's a strange way to begin," he said. "Most people who say that don't mean it."

"I mean it."

"Then ask."

Quentin opened their mouth and found the rehearsed words had all burned away. What came out instead was smaller and truer.

"There's someone I promised to get out of Callowmark. Before the war made promises impossible. She has no papers. No papers means no berth, no manifest, no name on any list — and by tomorrow morning, every list will be checked twice." Quentin's hands tightened around the cap. "I've been trying to think of another way for three days. There isn't one."

Fenwick set down his shovel. The sound of iron meeting iron plate rang through the stokehold and faded, and in the quiet after it, Quentin heard the boiler breathing, the hull ticking against the tide, the distant clang of the harbor works carrying down through the deck above. The sounds of a ship pretending to be asleep.

"You know what I keep down here besides coal," Fenwick said.

It wasn't a question. Quentin shook their head anyway.

He crossed to the locker bolted against the bulkhead — his territory, the one square of the ship that belonged wholly to him — and lifted out a canvas tool roll. But when he unrolled it on the workbench, it wasn't tools inside. It was oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, papers.

A seaman's identity book. A stoker's ticket. A vaccination certificate, a discharge record, a photograph of a broad-faced man with heavy brows and a smile that reached all the way to his eyes.

"His name was Aldous Wren," Fenwick said. "Everyone called him Doss. He stoked beside me for six years. He sang worse than I do, and he owed me eleven crowns, and he never once let me carry my own sea chest up a gangway." His thumb moved across the photograph, very lightly, the way a man touches a scar. "Two winters ago, off the Verre shoals, the boiler scalded him. Took three days to die, and he spent them telling jokes so the rest of us wouldn't have to look at what we were looking at."

Quentin said nothing. There was nothing to say yet.

"When we made port, the captain asked me what to write in the log. Accident, drowning, fever — there are ways to phrase these things." Fenwick's voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still on the bench, absolutely still. "And I thought: Doss spent six years getting this ship from one side of the world to the other, and if he dies properly, he becomes a line in a ledger and a grave with no stone, because nobody pays for stones for stokers. So I told them lost overboard. And I kept his papers."

"Why?" Quentin asked, though they already knew. They wanted to hear him say it.

"Because a man's name shouldn't die just because he does." Fenwick folded the oilcloth back over the documents, unhurried, precise. "And because out here, papers are the only mercy that keeps. You can't eat them, but they'll feed you better than bread. I always figured someday some poor devil would wash up needing a name more than he needed anything else in the world, and I'd be standing there holding one." He looked up. "I just never figured it would be you asking."

* * *

The guilt arrived the way the tide did — not all at once, but everywhere at once.

Quentin had spent their entire adult life taking things without appearing to take them: glances, information, passages through rooms, exits from conversations. It was the trade. Nobody noticed the taking because the taking was designed to be invisible, and the republic paid well for invisibility, and Quentin had learned to think of it as clean.

But this — this was different. This was spending another man's dead friend.

"Fenwick," they said slowly, "if she's caught using those papers, she won't be fined. She won't be deported. The consulate man told me yesterday what happens to irregulars found aboard a republican vessel now. They're calling them spies. Spies get shot on the quay, and the paperwork is finished before the body is cold." They made themselves hold his eyes. "If I take those papers and it goes wrong, it doesn't just go wrong for me. It goes wrong for her, and it goes wrong for whoever helped, and this ship gets searched stem to stern with every soul aboard held for questioning. Captain Oduya. Tamsin. You. That's what I'm asking you to hand me along with the oilcloth."

"I know what I'm handing you."

"Do you?" Quentin's voice cracked sharper than they intended, and in the iron hush of the stokehold it sounded enormous. "Because I've done the arithmetic, Fenwick. I do it every night. One promise, weighed against a crew. And every night the arithmetic tells me to let her stay hidden in that print shop until the city decides what to do with people like her, and every morning the arithmetic loses to something I can't write down in any ledger."

For a long moment Fenwick just looked at them. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a low, sooty sound, half cough.

"You sound like Doss," he said. "That exact tone. Like a man apologizing in advance for doing the right thing badly." He pushed the oilcloth roll across the bench. "Take it."

Quentin didn't move. "Tell me why. Not why you kept them. Why give them now. To someone you've known a month. Someone who came aboard claiming to be a courier and has spent every day since being nobody at all." The confession cost something; they paid it. "You must have wondered what I am."

"Oh, I've wondered." Fenwick leaned back against the boiler, arms crossed, the heat pressing at his back. "I've watched you count lifeboats and memorize watch rotations and stand in doorways learning the whole ship in a fortnight. I've seen couriers before, Kavanagh. Couriers relax after the first week. You got worse." He shrugged. "But here's what I know for certain. First storm out, you came down into that galley and peeled potatoes for six hours with your hands bleeding blisters, and you never once mentioned it, and you thanked Tamsin like she'd given you a gift instead of a chore. And last Tuesday you sat up with the widow Sefu's boy till four in the morning when he took the fever, though you'd been awake thirty hours already. People show you exactly who they are in kitchens and sickrooms. Everything else is costume." He tapped the oilcloth once. "Doss would've liked you. He liked anyone who showed up when it was muddy."

Quentin picked up the roll. It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it — that a whole human life, a name and a face and six years of stoking and eleven unpaid crowns and three days of jokes in the shadow of death, could be reduced to a few ounces of paper that fit in one hand.

"I can't promise she'll survive," Quentin said. "I can't even promise the plan will hold past first light. All I can promise is that if she makes it to safe water on Aldous Wren's name, I will spend the rest of my life making sure that name is remembered somewhere better than a logbook."

"He'd hate that," Fenwick said. "All the fuss." But his voice had gone rough at the edges, and he turned away to open the firebox again, and the light leapt up between them, and Quentin understood that the conversation was over in the way that mattered most between men who did not waste words.

They climbed the ladder with the dead man's life pressed flat against their chest.

* * *

Above decks, Callowmark lay gray under a sky the color of dishwater. Quentin walked the quay to clear their head and instead filled it further.

The repairs to the *Meridian*'s hull plating were nearly done; the yard gangs knocked off at dusk. Tomorrow, first light, high tide. Which meant tonight was the last night in which Ilse Marchetti was safe simply by being unremarkable, and the first night in which she would become, on paper, a dead man's cousin — and if Ambrose Pell pulled one thread of that fiction, the whole garment came apart in his soft, patient hands.

Quentin stopped at a chandler's window and studied their own reflection in the glass without meaning to. A courier. A ghost. A person sewn shut with secrets, now carrying a second man's identity over their heart like contraband — which, they supposed, was exactly what it was. *Spending another person's mercy.* The phrase wouldn't leave. Because mercy, unlike money, didn't circulate; it was given once, by one particular person, and Fenwick had given his away to a stranger whose real name he still didn't know.

"I'll tell him," Quentin murmured to the glass. "After. When there's an after."

Behind them, boots on the cobbles — unhurried, deliberate. Quentin turned with a courier's blankness already fixed in place.

It was Tamsin, collar up, breath steaming, a wrapped parcel under one arm. She stopped a few feet away and glanced at the chandler's window too, catching both their reflections in it, and something in her expression suggested she'd been following Quentin longer than the length of one street.

"You look like a woman burying money," she said.

"And you look like someone counting the shovels."

Tamsin smiled thinly. Then she stepped closer, lowered her voice beneath the wind coming off the harbor, and said the thing that stopped Quentin's heart mid-beat:

"Pell was at the harbormaster's office an hour ago. Asking for the *Meridian*'s passenger manifest. For tomorrow." Her eyes held Quentin's, bright and unblinking. "So before you spend whatever you're carrying in that coat — you should know the man's already reading our lists. And you should know I read his margins over his shoulder."

She held out the parcel. Inside, Quentin could feel, was paper. Fresh paper, and ink.

"Come below," Tamsin said. "You're going to need a better forgery than the one you've been practicing."
