## 12. The Consulate Burns

The summons came by a boy who did not knock so much as materialize at the Saltglass door, holding out an envelope the color of old teeth. *Consular Services, Republic of Vessany*, said the seal — though the wax had been applied carelessly, thumb-printed, as if the man who pressed it had been doing ten things at once and cared about none of them well.

Quentin read the note twice at the kitchen table while Widow Sefu pretended to scrub a pot that was already clean.

*Deliver remaining dispatches. Consulate, Harbor Road, after six bells. Come alone. Burn this.*

They burned it in the stove, and Sefu watched the curl of paper catch and said, without looking up, "You'll wear a coat with pockets today."

It was not a question. Quentin had learned that about her in the two weeks since taking the corner room under the eaves: she issued instructions disguised as observations, and she never asked why. "I will," Quentin said.

"And you'll come back for supper." This was also not a question. Bram sat at the far end of the table, dismantling a mantel clock with the patience of a surgeon, and he did not look up either, but his hands paused on the escapement for half a beat — long enough that Quentin understood it for what it was. A held breath. A wish.

"I'll come back for supper," Quentin said, and heard, to their own surprise, that they meant it as a promise rather than a report.

* * *

Harbor Road climbed away from the water in a series of crooked terraces, and the consulate stood at the top of the third one, a narrow stone house with iron balconies that had once been elegant and now looked like something caged. Quentin had delivered dispatches there twice already since the *Meridian* put in for repairs, and both times the building had felt like a held note — staff moving quietly, doors closing softly, the whole apparatus of the republic abroad performing composure. It had been eerie in an abstract way, the way a stage set is eerie when you wander onto it between performances.

Today there was smoke.

Not billowing — a thin, deliberate gray thread rising from the courtyard behind the house, the way smoke rises from a chimney that has been fed the wrong things. Quentin smelled it from the bottom of the terrace: hot paper, hot ink, hot glue. The smell of ledgers dying. Their feet slowed on the cobbles without their permission, and a woman carrying a hatbox hurried past them downhill, then another man with a sea chest, and neither of them looked like visitors.

At the door, a clerk Quentin half-recognized — Verlaine? Verhoef? — met them before they could knock. He had his coat on indoors and a satchel slung across his body like a bandolier, and his eyes went past Quentin's shoulder to the street, the roofs, the harbor below, as if expecting the view itself to arrest him.

"You got the summons." Not a greeting. An inventory.

"Six bells," Quentin said. "I'm early."

"Everyone's early now. Nobody's ever late again because nobody's staying." He stepped back and let them in, and Quentin saw immediately that the house had been gutted in the orderly, terrible way that institutions have. The filing room off the hall stood open, shelves bare, drawers pulled out and stacked like coffins. The smell of smoke came from everywhere at once. Somewhere upstairs a shredding knife scraped against a board, rhythmic as oars.

"Where's Minister Corvane?" Quentin asked.

"Gone. Tuesday, on the *Anserine*. He took the codebooks and left us the inventory lists." Verhoef almost laughed; it came out wrong, like a cough. "You're to see Deputy Amsel. Second floor. Mind the stairs, we've been carrying things down them all night."

The staircase was scored and scuffed along its banister where trunks had bumped their way down. On the landing, a framed portrait of the President of the Republic leaned face-first against the wall, its wire already unscrewed from the plaster. Someone had wrapped the frame in newspaper for travel and then never come back for it. Quentin looked at the president's inverted face for a moment — the familiar engraved jaw, the engraved calm — and felt nothing at all, which frightened them more than grief would have.

* * *

Deputy Amsel received them in a room that had been an office and was becoming a bonfire. The shutters were closed against the harbor light, so the only illumination came from a brazier in the hearth, fed low and steady, and by that orange glow Quentin saw the stacks: treaty correspondence, shipping registers, visa ledgers, personnel files, all of it sorted into piles with little paper flags marking categories. A system. Even now, especially now, there was a system.

Amsel himself was a small man made smaller by exhaustion, sleeves rolled, forearms smudged gray to the elbow. He had been feeding papers to the brazier one handful at a time, waiting for each to catch fully before adding more — burning too fast would draw attention; burning too slowly left evidence. He had clearly been doing this for many hours. He had clearly gotten very good at it.

"Courier," he said, without looking up. "Kavanagh. Sit if you can find a chair. We shipped most of them."

Quentin stayed standing. "I have dispatches."

"I know you do. Put them on the desk." A pause; another handful into the fire; the flames turned briefly blue-green where the ink burned. "Then I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to want to argue, and I'd ask you to save us both the time."

Quentin set the oilskin packet on the desk. It weighed less than it had that morning, or their hands had grown stronger, or dread redistributes weight somehow. "Tell me."

Amsel finally looked up. His eyes were bloodshot all the way to the iris, pink as raw meat. "There is no consulate after tomorrow. What's left of us sails on whatever floats. Your dispatches—" he nodded at the packet— "will go into the last diplomatic pouch, which is a euphemism for a sack, which goes aboard a trawler whose captain has been paid in a currency that may be worthless by the time he spends it. That's the honest version. Do you want the official version?"

"I've heard the official version every day of my life."

That earned him something like respect. He wiped his hands on a rag that had stopped being useful days ago. "Then here's the other thing. The honest thing." He lowered his voice, though there was no one left in the building to overhear except the shredder upstairs, and whoever worked a shredding knife wasn't listening. "Callowmark isn't going to stay neutral much longer. The harbor authority is already selling information to whoever pays, and lately there's more than one payer. There's a census taker — Pell, Ambrose Pell — who's been cross-referencing manifests against his registration rolls, and he's requested naval assistance for departure inspections. All departures. Including yours."

The floor did not move, but Quentin felt it try.

"The *Meridian* is a packet ship," they said carefully. "Diplomatic hull. Inspections of diplomatic hulls are—"

"A formality that died with the rest of the forms." Amsel fed another stack to the brazier. Visa records, Quentin saw. Hundreds of names, hundreds of stamped photographs, all of them curling in the heat like leaves. "Pell believes — and he may even be right — that republican ships leaving Callowmark are carrying undeclared passengers. People paying stokers and cooks for berth space. People on borrowed papers." The firelight made his face into a mask with hollows. "The new directive out of what remains of the ministry says irregular passengers are to be treated as saboteurs. Spies. Taken at the gangway, questioned on the quay, and shot if the questioning goes badly. And questioning always goes badly, courier, because there are no good answers anymore. Only answers that take longer to fail."

Quentin kept their face still. They had spent a lifetime keeping their face still; it was the one skill the republic had perfected in them, and it held now the way a dam holds — entirely, until it doesn't. "Is that a warning?"

"It's a weather report." Amsel stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and came around the desk. Close up he smelled of smoke and cold coffee and fear sweat gone stale. "You seem like a careful person. Careful people have been coming to me lately asking careful questions, and I've started reading between their lines, because reading between lines is all I'm good for now." He looked at Quentin the way a man looks at a scale he suspects has been tampered with. "If you were carrying anything irregular on that ship of yours, you'd want to know that the gangway at first light is going to have soldiers on it. And you'd want to know that Pell keeps notes. Margins full of notes. Little annotations about people who lie well."

"Who told you I lie well?"

"No one. That's what worries me. You didn't lie about anything yet, and you're already standing like someone mid-sentence." He smiled without warmth or humor — a spasm of professional courtesy. "Take your receipt. The pouch leaves tonight. And Kavanagh — burn your instructions when you've used them. Everything else in this building is ash for a reason."

* * *

Quentin descended the scored staircase past the face-down president, past Verhoef, who was now stacking the emptied file drawers themselves — wood burns too — past a young woman sitting on a crate in the hall with her hatbox in her lap, crying without any sound at all, the way people cry when they've trained themselves to it. The republic taught that too, Quentin thought. It taught everyone in its orbit the same curriculum: how to hold still, how to cry silently, how to make one handful of paper last exactly as long as it needed to.

Outside, the evening had come on violet and cold, and the smoke thread from the courtyard rose straight up into the windless sky, visible from the harbor, visible from anywhere, a flag no country would claim. Quentin stood at the top of the terrace and let themselves look at it honestly for the first time: the machinery, stripped of ceremony. All those years they had imagined the state as a great engine — valves and pressures, orders flowing through pipes — dignified by its own complexity. But here was the engine opened up: clerks with satchels, portraits unwired from walls, a deputy burning visas one handful at a time so the neighbors wouldn't notice. And beneath it, newly visible, newly admitted, the teeth. *Irregular passengers. Shot as spies.* The machine had always eaten people; the difference was that it had stopped bothering to dress for dinner.

They thought of Ilse's hands around a cup of tea, four years ago, turning it slowly, her voice amused and tired: *You translate everything, Quentin. Even yourself.* If she could see them now, standing over a burning consulate with dead codes sewn against their ribs, translating nothing.

They thought of Fenwick saying *a stoker's cousin*, and of Tamsin measuring the hold with chalk marks hidden under her sleeve, and of Bram's watches appearing on their pillow like small patient arguments that home was a thing you could build by hand.

And they thought of Ambrose Pell, somewhere down in that lamplit city, ruling lines through ruled pages, writing extra words in margins — and requesting soldiers.

Supper, Sefu had said. You'll come back for supper.

Quentin went down the terraces two at a time, coat flying open, the sewn lining dragging at their heart like ballast, and behind them the consulate's smoke rose thin and gray into the dark, the last flag of a republic that had just, in a second-floor room by brazier-light, told them precisely what it would do to anyone caught saving a life at a gangway at dawn.

First light was nine hours away.
