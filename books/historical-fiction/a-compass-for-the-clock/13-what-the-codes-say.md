## 13. What the Codes Say

The Saltglass house went quiet by degrees, the way harbors did. First the stairs stopped complaining under feet. Then the last lamp in the common room was shuttered, and Widow Sefu's keys stopped their midnight jingle against her hip. By eleven there was only the house itself breathing — Bram's clocks ticking out of phase in the hall like a conversation conducted in a language of patience, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, the sea working at the pilings.

Quentin sat at the small table in their rented room with a candle, a saucer of melted tallow, and the coat across their knees like a sleeping animal they were about to hurt.

They had not slept properly since the consulate. Since the young clerk with smoke-stained cuffs had said it almost kindly, the way one delivers any fact too large for kindness: *They'll search her at departure. Irregular passengers are being treated as spies now.* Treated as spies. Shot as spies, was what he meant; Quentin had heard enough euphemisms in the capital to translate fluently.

And so here was the arithmetic that had been waiting for them every night this week, patient as debt: somewhere inside the stitching of this coat were orders. Orders that might say *abandon the allies in Callowmark*. Orders that might contain names. If Ilse Marchetti's name was in those dispatches — if the republic had already decided her fate and written it down in cipher and sewn it against Quentin's own ribs — then Quentin was carrying her death warrant next to their heart, and had been for weeks, and had called that duty.

The candle guttered, righted itself.

Quentin turned the coat inside out on the table. The lining was good waxed linen, chosen for exactly this quality: it took a seam ripper's blade without fraying, held a stitch like an oath. They found the left inner pocket, the false bottom of it, where the packet lay flat and thin as a wafer. Three leaves. On each leaf, rows of figures worked in a fine hand — not ink that any light would betray, but the pale chemical wash that needed developing, which was why Quentin could not simply read them over someone's shoulder or in a stolen moment. Which was why the republic had designed them this way. A courier who couldn't read the dispatches under torture was worth more than a courier who could. Quentin had always admired the elegance of it, in the abstract way one admires a well-built trap.

They had the key. That was the bitter joke of it. The key lived in Quentin's memory the way the codes lived in the seams — both sewn in, neither removable without damage. The phrase was from a childhood prayer, bent and folded until it made numbers, and the numbers walked across the cipher grid in their head, and the grid was drawn nightly on nothing, in the dark, behind their eyes.

All they lacked was the developer. The solution came in a vial the size of a thumb, and the vial was supposed to be in the dispatch case, and the dispatch case was locked in the consulate strongroom, and the consulate was a chimney now.

Quentin had realized this three days ago and had felt, for one shameful moment, relief.

* * *

They tried anyway. Of course they tried. Duty was not a thing you could put down because it became inconvenient, or heavy, or monstrous; duty was precisely the thing you carried when it was all three. Quentin went back to the ruined consulate in the gray morning and picked through the outbuildings. The clerks had burned in order of importance — personnel files first, then correspondence, then the codebooks themselves, fed to the fire in armloads by men whose faces said they understood they were erasing their own names. But bureaucracy sheds. Quentin found, in a flooded storeroom where the rain had come in through a broken skylight, a tray of photographic chemicals gone to crust, bottles unlabeled or labeled in a clerk's hurried hand. They took two bottles that smelled right and paid the widow of the building's caretaker too much for them and walked home through the fish market with their pockets weighing like sins.

That had been yesterday. Tonight was the night the argument in their chest had to end, because the Meridian sailed on the morning tide after next, and Captain Oduya had told Quentin at supper, flatly, watching them over the salt: "There'll be an inspection before we clear. Pell's requested naval assistance. You should know that, courier." The whole table had gone quiet — Fenwick's spoon paused halfway to his mouth, Tamsin suddenly very interested in the grain of the tabletop — and Quentin had understood that the Saltglass family had decided, without consulting them, that whatever Quentin carried was dangerous, and had decided to stand near it anyway.

So: tonight. Know or don't know. Carry blind or carry informed.

Quentin dipped the pen knife under the pocket seam and cut three stitches. The sound was very small. It sounded enormous.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about secrets: they do not stay where you put them. The republic thought it was sealing information inside ciphers and couriers and burning buildings, but a secret is water; it finds the low places. It had gotten into the boarding house. Quentin saw it everywhere now that they knew to look. It was in the way Sefu asked no questions but kept the good room empty "in case," though she never said in case of what. It was in Fenwick's careful cheer, and the way he'd shown the dead stoker's papers without being asked twice, his big hands trembling slightly as he handed over another man's name — *spent another man's mercy*, Quentin thought, that's what I've done, and the guilt of it sat in the chest like a swallowed coin. It was in Tamsin's silence, most of all. The ward had caught Quentin pacing the hold with a lantern, measuring berths, and had said nothing at the time, and that silence had followed Quentin around the ship ever since, polite and watchful, like a cat deciding whether something was prey.

Even the clocks were complicit. Bram wound them all at nine, and they ticked through the house in their loose agreement, no two keeping the same hour, and Quentin had come to love that — a house where time refused to be uniform, where every instrument insisted on its own account of things. But tonight the ticking sounded like a countdown, and Quentin resented the metaphor even as they thought it.

Three stitches. Six. The packet slid free into the candlelight: three leaves, blank-seeming, faintly greasy to the touch, holding their message the way amber holds an insect.

Quentin set them down and did not touch them again for a long moment. Their hands were steady — couriers' hands, trained steady, steady in storms and searches — and that steadiness felt like the most damning thing in the room. Look, the hands said. This is what we are. We can do anything if you tell us it's duty.

*Need to know.* That was the doctrine, and Quentin had believed in it sincerely once, the way one believes in load-bearing walls. The courier carries; the consul decides; the minister commands; somewhere above it all, the republic wills. Each person along the chain holds one link and trusts the chain. It was a beautiful system for distributing responsibility so thinly that no one ever had to feel its whole weight. Quentin had spent years feeling only their link. Now, sitting in a borrowed room above a house full of people the republic might already have condemned, they felt the entire cable at once, and understood that *need to know* had another meaning when you read it honestly: someone had decided Quentin did not need to know what would be done in their name. Had decided that Quentin's ignorance was safer than Quentin's conscience. And Quentin had agreed. Had rehearsed being nobody, had worn invisibility like armor, and armor keeps out more than knives. It keeps out questions. It keeps out the faces of the people your orders fall upon.

If they deciphered the dispatches and found Ilse's name among the abandoned — and they knew, they *knew*, with the certainty of nightmare, that they would find it, or something worse, something procedural, *assets in Callowmark are not to be extracted, no exceptions authorized* — then what? Knowing would change nothing. The orders were the orders. The Meridian sailed; the consulate burned; Callowmark's refugees stayed refugees. Knowledge would not save anyone. Knowledge would only make Quentin into a person who knew, and knowing, obeyed — or knowing, broke, and breaking had consequences that landed on other people, on Fenwick and Tamsin and a captain who had invoked maritime law at a gangway for a woman she'd met five minutes before.

So don't. Leave the seals intact. Sail out with clean hands and a sealed coat, and let ignorance keep its ancient bargain.

But Ignorance had a face now. That was the ruin of it. For six years the far side of the cipher had been abstraction — ministers, ministries, the gray machinery of state. Now the far side of the cipher was a print shop on Tanner's Row where a woman with ink on her fingers translated pamphlets under a dead girl's name, and looked up one afternoon to see Quentin in her doorway and flinched, actually flinched, thinking they'd come to arrest her. Ilse Marchetti, translator, traitor by decree. If her name was in these pages — if some clerk in the dying capital had typed it into a list between a union organizer and a harbor pilot, *no protection afforded, extraction denied* — then the republic had answered, years late and in cipher, the question Quentin had never dared ask anyone: does the promise of a courier bind the state?

It doesn't, whispered the part of Quentin that had been paying attention lately. It never did. The promise bound only the promiser.

* * *

The developer, such as it was, decanted cloudy into the saucer. Quentin worked by the book from memory: damp the leaf, lay it face-down, wait for the figures to rise like drowned things surfacing. They pressed the first page flat against the tallow-warm dish and watched the paper drink.

Nothing. The chemical was wrong, or too old, or the flood had killed it. Quentin waited anyway, counting Bram's clocks in the hall — the tall one, the carriage clock, the ship's chronometer Bram was forever nursing — and the counts would not line up, and somewhere below a floorboard announced Sefu on her rounds, checking locks, checking her strange family the way she checked everything, silently and thoroughly.

Quentin lifted the page. Faint ghosts, maybe. Or maybe only watermarks, the paper's own history rising. They tilted it to the flame.

And stopped.

Because here was the thing they discovered in that moment, alone at a cheap table with a failed reagent and a candle: they did not want to know. Not in the cowardly way — in the truest way. Somewhere in the last month the center of gravity had moved. Once, the fear had been *what if I fail the mission*. Now the fear was *what if the mission succeeds*. What if the dispatches reach whatever remains to receive them, and the orders execute, and the Meridian sails clean on schedule carrying Quentin's obedience like ballast, and behind it Callowmark's allies discover what they had always been to the republic: entries in a ledger, expendable, unwept. Quentin could not unread these pages. Whatever rose out of the chemicals would be in their head forever, and it would sit between them and every person at the Saltglass table like a third language none of them spoke.

Dread, they understood suddenly, was not the opposite of complicity. Dread was complicity's advance payment. Every night they had lain awake fearing the contents of the dispatches, they had already accepted the premise that the contents must be obeyed — had trembled before authority instead of questioning it, had suffered on schedule like a good courier. Fear was how the chain held. Cut the fear, and you still had to cut the chain.

Quentin peeled the leaf off the saucer, blotted it, and set it beside the other two. Then they sat with their hands flat on the table — steady hands, damn them — and did the honest arithmetic at last.

Option one: try again for a working developer tomorrow, decipher the orders, and then — obey? Refuse? Refusal while carrying meant the papers had to die, and if the papers died unread, why read them at all? Option two: stop trying. Let the seals stand until morning, then destroy the whole thing unread — treason, plain, clean, and deniable to no one, least of all themselves. Option three, the courier's option, the coward's option: resew the packet, sail on the tide, deliver the orders to an addressee who might be ash by then, and spend the rest of their life telling themselves they never knew what they carried.

Their hands remembered Ilse's, years ago, across a tea table in the capital, both of them warming palms against cups because the republic heated its offices grudgingly. *When there's peace,* she'd said, laughing at her own sentimentality, *you'll take me to the coast, yes? Show me an ocean that isn't in a book?* And Quentin, armored in junior-officer invisibility, had given the easiest promise in the world: *In peacetime, anything.*

There was no peacetime coming. There was only this: a winter port, a morning tide, two days. The promise had come due in the only currency it was ever going to be paid in, and it was not safety, and it was not peace, and it was certainly not the republic's permission.

Quentin looked at the three leaves lying in the candlelight, blank as snow, and felt the choice settle over them not with horror but with an awful, unfamiliar lightness — grief and relief in the same breath, indistinguishable, like the moment a held rope finally slips from blistered hands.

Not yet. One more night of not-knowing. One more night of dread, the last payment on an account about to be closed.

They gathered the leaves, slid them back into the false pocket, threaded the needle Sefu had left in the room's mending basket — of course she had left a mending basket; the woman repaired everything that crossed her threshold — and began to close the seam, stitch by small stitch, the needle making its tiny sound in the great ticking quiet.

Which was when they noticed the door.

It stood open less than a hand's width. It had been closed when Quentin sat down; Quentin had closed it, Quentin closed doors the way they breathed, automatically. And now, through the gap, lamplight from the hall fell across the floorboards in a thin bright bar, and just beyond the edge of it, unmistakable in the smell of lamp oil and tar, stood someone who had been listening long enough to hear everything that mattered and nothing that could be proven.

The floorboard outside creaked, deliberately, giving warning the way Tamsin gave everything — exactly when she chose.

"Courier," came the ward's voice through the gap, level as a compass needle. "You'd better let me in. I know what you're sewing."
