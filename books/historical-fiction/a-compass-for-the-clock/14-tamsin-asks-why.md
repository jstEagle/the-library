## 14. Tamsin Asks Why

Tamsin had learned to read a ship the way other people learned to read scripture: not by the words it said aloud but by what it left unsaid in the margins. The Meridian spoke to her constantly. It told her things through the pitch of its deck at dawn, through the particular groan a mooring line made when the tide turned wrong, through the way Captain Oduya's boots paused for half a second outside the galley when something troubled him and he hadn't decided yet whether to say so.

So when the passenger — Kavanagh, the quiet one with the careful hands and the coat that never came off — began spending his evenings belowdecks with a length of string and a stub of chalk, Tamsin noticed before anyone else did.

She noticed the way he counted paces along the hold's starboard wall on the third night after Callowmark, his lips moving without sound. She noticed him crouching at the frame of the forward cargo bay with a lantern hooded under his coat, measuring with his arms outstretched like a man embracing something invisible. She noticed, most tellingly of all, that he always glanced up first — always checked the ladder well, the hatch grating, the seam of lamplight from the crew quarters — before he did any of it.

Nobody measured a hold they weren't hiding something in.

* * *

The berth ledger lived in the chartroom, chained to the desk by a short brass link that Captain Oduya pretended was a formality and everyone else understood was law. Passengers, cargo, livestock, mail: everything the Meridian carried had to be written there in Oduya's own hand, because a captain who didn't know his ship to the last crate was a captain who could be ruined by the last crate.

Tamsin kept the ledger's inkwell filled. She kept the pen wiped. She knew every entry the captain had made since Vessany, because she'd read them all twice — once out of habit, once out of the old hunger she'd never quite trained out of herself, the hunger to know where people were going and how many of them were lying about it.

That was why the new entry caught her eye the way a wrong note catches a musician's ear.

*One (1) additional hand, stoker class, signed aboard Callowmark. Wages deferred to home port.*

The handwriting was Oduya's. The hand itself was nearly perfect — the same slight rightward slant, the same cramped capitals. But Tamsin had spent four years copying that hand herself, practicing it against the captain's signature until hers could pass at arm's length, back when she'd still thought forgery might someday save her life. She knew the way Oduya crossed his sevens. She knew he never wrote *stoker class*; he wrote *stoker*, full stop, because he considered class a thing you were born to or weren't and saw no reason to decorate it.

This entry said *stoker class*. And the ink was still faintly glossy at the tail of the final stroke, as though someone had blotted it in haste rather than let it dry with the patience of a man writing at leisure over his evening tea.

Someone had added a soul to the Meridian's manifest. Someone intended that soul to be aboard when the lines went out on the morning tide.

Tamsin stood very still in the chartroom with the lamp turned low, and felt an old feeling rise in her chest like water finding its level. It was not alarm. Alarm would come later, maybe. What rose first was recognition — the specific, humming recognition of one hider seeing the tracks of another.

* * *

She found Kavanagh that night by simply waiting where he would have to pass.

The aft companionway was narrow, lit by a single oil lamp that swayed with the harbor swell and threw shadows that climbed the bulkheads like slow animals. Tamsin sat on the lowest step with her knees drawn up, mending a sock she didn't need mended, and let her needle go quiet when she heard boots on the deck above.

Kavanagh came down the ladder with the careful economy she'd come to expect of him — no wasted motion, no sound beyond what iron and wood demanded. He saw her and stopped, one hand on the rail, and for a moment they regarded each other across three feet of lamplit air.

"Evening," Tamsin said.

"Evening." His voice was level. His eyes were not; they went past her shoulder to the chartroom door, then back, quick as a fish turning.

"The captain keeps odd hours," she offered. "Sometimes he does the manifest last thing, after his tea."

"Does he." Kavanagh's hand hadn't moved off the rail. He wasn't going anywhere, which meant he understood exactly what she was telling him, which meant he was deciding — right now, in this swaying corridor — how much of a threat she was.

"He does," Tamsin said. "Though I don't recall him taking on another stoker. We're a small ship. Fenwick runs the boilers alone and likes it that way. Says two stokers argue about the fire."

Silence. The lamp swung; their shadows leaned together and apart.

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Kavanagh said.

"No," Tamsin agreed pleasantly. "I expect you wouldn't." She bit her thread, tucked the finished sock into her pocket, and stood up. She was fifteen and half a head shorter than the courier, and she made sure to look him directly in the face while she said the next part, because she'd learned that height mattered less than steadiness. "I also expect that whoever wrote that new line in the ledger wanted it to look like the captain's hand. And I expect that whoever measures holds at night with a piece of string is planning to put somebody in one of the spaces that isn't on the plans. And I expect—" she held up one finger before he could speak— "that none of those someones has asked me a single question yet, which is either an oversight or an insult, depending on how you look at it."

Kavanagh's composure held, but barely. She watched something move behind his face like weather behind glass — fear, calculation, and underneath both of them, so faint it might have been nothing, hope.

"You've been watching me," he said.

"Everyone watches everyone on a ship this size. That's not the interesting part." Tamsin folded her arms. "The interesting part is what happens next. So let's skip to it. Are you going to lie to me, or are we done pretending?"

* * *

Here is the thing about Tamsin that nobody aboard the Meridian fully knew, because she had arranged it that way: she had not always been the captain's ward.

There had been a port called Greyharbour once, and a fish market, and a mother who sold eels and coughed blood into a rag she kept folded in her apron pocket, and a stepfather whose temper arrived with the weather off the north sea. There had been a night when Tamsin was eleven years old when she'd looked at the shape of her whole future — the market stall, the rag, the fists, the gray water — and walked to the commercial quay instead of home.

There had been a brigantine called the *Larkspur*, riding high and empty at the end of the pier, loading grain sacks in the dark. Tamsin had known ships all her life from the outside; she knew that between the grain sacks and the hull plating there was a space, if you were small, if you were patient, if you could bear the dark and the rats and the terrible arithmetic of how long a loaf lasts. She'd cut a hole in the tarpaulin with a gutting knife and gone in.

For nine days she had lived in that space. She had learned things in there that she never told anyone, not even afterward, not even Oduya: how silence feels when it is the only thing keeping you alive; how your own heartbeat becomes a noise you must apologize for; how a person can want, more than food, more than freedom, simply to be *seen* — to have one voice say her name and mean that she existed, that she was somewhere, that someone would notice if she stopped being anywhere at all.

On the ninth day the mate had found her, dragged her out blinking into daylight, and put a knife to her throat while the crew argued about what to do with stowaways. Some said put her ashore. Some said worse. And then a woman in a salt-stained coat had pushed through them — young then, still young, though Tamsin hadn't been able to see it — and said, in a voice that ended arguments:

"She's mine now. Put it in the log."

Captain Sefu Oduya, twenty-nine years old and already famous among the crews of three nations for never raising her voice and never repeating herself, had claimed a gutter thief off the docks of Greyharbour because Tamsin, knife at her throat, starving, terrified, had refused to cry. Had looked the mate dead in the eye. Had hidden well enough to survive nine days, which meant she was useful, which meant — in Oduya's cold arithmetic of mercy — she was worth saving.

"You hide beautifully," Oduya had told her that first night, wrapping her in a blanket that smelled of tar and tea. "It's a skill. Most people can't do it at all. But hear me, girl: a person who only knows how to hide is a person other people get to decide about. Learn when to stand in the open. That's the harder lesson. I'll teach it to you slowly, because it took me thirty years."

Tamsin had never told Kavanagh any of this. She had never told anyone. But she had watched the courier for weeks now — watched the way he ate, precise and uncomplaining, making himself small even at a full table; watched the way he answered direct questions with answers shaped like answers but containing nothing; watched him sit at the Saltglass house among Widow Sefu's exiles and deserters and Bram the clockmaker and remain, somehow, *apart* — present at the table, absent from the room — and she had recognized the posture the way you recognize your own handwriting.

He was hiding. He was hiding professionally, permanently, in plain sight, and he was good at it, and it was eating him alive.

And now he was hiding someone else.

* * *

"So," Tamsin said, twenty minutes later, sitting cross-legged on a coil of spare rope in the shadowed corner of the forward hold, where the string marks were still faintly visible in chalk dust and the air smelled of bilge and hemp. Kavanagh had not confessed in words. He had simply led her here, which was confession enough, and now stood with his back against the frames, arms crossed, watching her the way a man watches a fuse he's already lit.

"You're not going to ask me why," he said finally. It was almost an accusation.

"I know why," Tamsin said. "You're getting someone off this ship's sister problem — someone who can't be on the manifest because the manifest gets inspected, and inspected manifests get people shot. You've got papers that belong to someone who isn't using them anymore. You needed a space big enough for a person to breathe in for however many days the crossing takes, close enough to the galley to feed them and far enough aft that Pell won't poke around twice." She shrugged. "The string told me most of it. The rest is just knowing ships."

Kavanagh stared at her. In the dim light filtering through the hatch grating, his face had lost the courier's smoothness entirely; what was underneath was younger than she'd guessed, and tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

"Pell," he said. "You know about Pell?"

"Everyone knows about Pell. He's been sniffing round the gangway twice since we docked, asking Fenwick questions about crew rotations like he's making conversation." She picked at the rope fibers. "He'll be back. Men like Pell are always back. They're like tide."

"And when he comes back, and he counts heads, and there's one more head than the ledger says—"

"There won't be," Tamsin said. "Because the ledger will say what it needs to say, and the ledger is my job." She let that sit. "I keep the inkwell. I copy entries for the port authority whenever the captain's busy, which is often. Nobody questions my hand. I've been writing Oduya's name on documents since I was twelve."

"That's forgery."

"That's *housekeeping*," Tamsin said, with a flash of the temper she usually kept banked, and was surprised at herself for. "Look — you've spent however many weeks deciding whether to trust anybody on this ship, and I respect the caution, I do, it's kept you breathing. But you're doing this alone, and alone is how these things fail. You need someone who can walk into the chartroom in broad daylight and touch that ledger without anyone thinking twice. You need someone who knows which inspector takes bribes and which one takes notes. You need someone who's been the person in the dark space between the grain sacks and knows exactly how much water to bring and how little noise a body can afford to make." Her voice dropped. "You need someone who's done it."

Kavanagh was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, the harbor bell rang the second watch, and the whole ship seemed to lean into the sound.

"Why?" he asked at last. It was the question she'd been waiting for, and he asked it honestly, without flattery — *why would you risk yourself for a stranger's errand* — and she found she respected him for it.

Tamsin pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them.

"When I was eleven," she said, "I hid in a ship for nine days, and the whole time, the thing I wanted most in the world wasn't out. It was for one person to know I was there. Just one. To have my existence confirmed by a single living witness." She looked up at him. "Somebody did that for me. Captain Oduya walked into a circle of men with knives and said *she's mine* and wrote it in a book, and that book — that stupid brass-chained ledger — is the reason I exist. Paper saved my life, Kavanagh. A line of ink. Somebody put me on the record and the record became a shield." She shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, and covered it with briskness. "So when I see a person trying to write somebody *into* existence — onto a page, onto a ship, out of a country that wants them erased — well. I don't ask why. I ask *how fast*."

Kavanagh's composure broke then — not dramatically, nothing so generous as tears, but something in his jaw gave way, and he turned his face briefly toward the hatch grating and the thin gray light, and breathed. When he looked back at her, he was, for the first time since Callowmark, actually *present* in his own eyes.

"It's a woman," he said quietly. "Her name is Ilse Marchetti. She was a translator. The republic named her an enemy, which means the republic named her a target, and the consulate here will hand lists to anyone who asks." He hesitated. "I promised her passage once. Peacetime. It seemed like an easy promise then."

"They always do," Tamsin said. "That's what makes them promises."

"She comes aboard at first light. Day after tomorrow. As the cousin of a stoker who died off Cape Verren last spring." He paused. "Fenwick's papers. He — Fenwick gave them to me. I haven't worked out how to repay that yet."

"You repay it by not getting caught," Tamsin said practically. Then, softer: "And by remembering it. Fenwick will pretend it cost him nothing. It will have cost him something. People like us collect debts like that our whole lives and call them favors so we don't have to feel the weight." She unfolded herself and stood, brushing rope fiber from her skirt, and stuck out her hand — a gesture so ordinary, so shoreside, so utterly unlike anything that happened aboard the Meridian that Kavanagh blinked at it before taking it.

Her grip was stronger than he expected.

"Here's what happens next," she said. "Tonight I re-ink that ledger entry properly — the captain's real hand, his real spacing, and I'll age the ink with a breath of the galley stove so it doesn't shine. Tomorrow I walk the port authority's manifest clerk past the forward hold so he sees crates and nothing else. And day after tomorrow, when your Ilse comes up the gangway in a dead man's coat, she's going to find that the space between the grain lockers has a lantern, a water tin, and a blanket that smells like somebody's been caring about her survival since Tuesday." She released his hand. "Because that's the difference between smuggling and rescue, Kavanagh. Smuggling moves a body. Rescue makes sure the body arrives *as a person*. I've been both cargo and crew. Trust me on which one works."

Kavanagh opened his mouth — to thank her, probably, or to warn her, or to give her one last chance to walk away clean — and Tamsin waved it off.

"Don't," she said. "Save the speeches. Spend the time checking your spaces again. And Kavanagh—" she paused at the foot of the ladder, looking back up at him with the lamp glow gilding the edge of her jaw— "the census man, Pell. He requested the naval office inspect our passenger list. Fenwick heard it from the harbormaster's runner an hour ago. They'll be at the gangway when we sail."

Above them, faint through the planking, the harbor bell rang the half hour, patient and indifferent, counting down toward a dawn that neither of them had chosen but both of them, now, intended to survive.
