## 15. The Night Before the Tide

The plan, when Quentin finally spoke it aloud in the narrow parlor of the Saltglass boarding house, sounded absurdly small — a handful of sentences that could be counted on two hands.

At first light, before the harbor office opened its shutters and before Pell's clerks began their copying, Ilse Marchetti would walk down the quay carrying the papers of Josiah Fenwick, stoker, drowned off the Corvel Bank eleven months ago. She would present herself at the Meridian's gangway as the dead man's cousin, come to see the last of him off, and she would not get off again. Tamsin would log her into the berth book in the handwriting Fenwick had practiced until it looked convincingly like his own. Captain Oduya would sign what needed signing and look at no one too long. And Quentin would stand at the gangway with their orders sewn against their ribs and do the one thing they had been trained never to do: be seen helping.

"That's it?" Ilse said. "That's the whole plan?"

"It's most of the plan," Quentin said. "The rest is weather."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter were something she had to dig for now — and pressed her hand flat against the table as if steadying it. The print shop ink had faded from her fingers but the habit of rubbing them clean remained; Quentin watched her do it twice without noticing she was doing it, and had to look away.

"The rest," said Widow Sefu from the doorway, "is supper."

* * *

Sefu cooked the way Bram repaired clocks: with an economy of motion that concealed how much she understood about what was broken. By dusk the long table in the Saltglass kitchen had been extended with a plank across two crates, and every chair in the house had been found, borrowed, or improvised. There was a hen that Sefu claimed to have bought honestly and which everyone else suspected of having wandered in from somewhere it wasn't missed. There was bread, dark and dense, and pickled things in chipped jars, and a bottle of plum brandy that Fenwick had contributed with the solemnity of a man surrendering a family heirloom.

Everyone pretends this is ordinary, Quentin thought, taking their seat. That is the gift they are giving us.

The pretending took effort, and everyone performed it differently. Fenwick told a story about a cook he'd known aboard a grain ship who seasoned everything with tar because the tar had been closer to hand than pepper, and he told it badly, with three false starts, and it got funnier each time. Tamsin sat with her elbows on the table and asked questions of everyone — where Bram had learned his trade, whether Sefu had really once refused payment from a magistrate, what the moon looked like over Ilse's home country — questions with no edges on them, blunt and safe, keeping the conversation moving the way one keeps a lantern swinging in wind so it won't gutter.

Ilse answered them all. She had a way, Quentin remembered now, of making even a small answer feel like a confidence, as though she were translating herself into whatever company she was in. When Tamsin asked about the print shop, Ilse described the smell of the ink and the rhythm of the press and the foreman who pretended not to know what was printed on the pamphlets he set each night, and she made it sound almost funny, and only Quentin saw the flicker at the end of it, the place where the story stopped being hers alone and became a thing she'd survived.

Bram did not speak. He never did. But he ate with attention and approval, nodding slowly at intervals like a man auditing the meal for craftsmanship, and when Fenwick reached for a third piece of bread Bram slid the plate toward him without looking up, and Fenwick accepted it as naturally as breathing.

Quentin kept catching themselves memorizing the room. They knew why. Tomorrow morning the tide would come in and take the Meridian out, and Ilse with it, and Quentin would stand on the quay and let her go — because the promise had never been *we will leave together*, it had been *you will be safe*, and those were different promises wearing similar clothes. So tonight the room itself was cargo. The chipped jar of plums. Tamsin's laugh, quick and bright as a struck match. The plank across the crates bowing slightly under Sefu's forearms when she leaned forward to say something she didn't want the far end of the table to hear.

"Quentin." Sefu's voice, low. "You're a thousand miles off and sitting right here. Eat."

"I'm eating."

"You're performing eating." Sefu's eyes were kind and utterly unsentimental. "There's a difference. I've fed both kinds."

Quentin ate.

Later, when the hen was bones and the brandy was going around a second time, Fenwick grew quiet in a way Quentin hadn't seen from him before. He turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left.

"Eleven months," he said, to no one in particular. "Josiah Fenwick. Good lad. Snored like a landslide. Went over the rail in fog and we never even slowed proper, because slowing in fog off the Corvel Bank kills more men than the rail does." He looked up, and his eyes found Ilse. "You'll forgive me for lending you a dead man. He'd have liked the use of it. He hated the idea of just — stopping. Being done. This way he gets to sail tomorrow."

"Fenwick—" Ilse began.

"He'd have insisted." Fenwick drank, and his voice roughened. "I'll thank you not to argue with a drowning man's wishes."

Nobody argued. Tamsin topped up his cup. Bram raised his glass a few inches, a silent toast, and held it there until everyone followed.

Under the table, out of sight of all of them, Ilse's hand found Quentin's knee and stayed there — not gripping, just resting, the way you rest your hand on something you intend to keep.

* * *

The dishes were done by lamplight, Sefu refusing help in a tone that ended the discussion, and the household dispersed to its rooms in the unhurried manner of people stretching one evening into as many hours as it could hold. Tamsin announced she was sleeping aboard tonight, "to check the lines," which fooled no one and comforted everyone; she would be on the ship at dawn, and she wanted her pillow to know the ship's creaks before the hardest hour came.

Quentin and Ilse climbed to the small back room under the eaves that the house shared between them these past days — Ilse sleeping in the bed, Quentin on the pallet by the window, an arrangement maintained with scrupulous innocence that fooled absolutely no one in the house and was remarked upon by no one either. That was the Saltglass way. Questions were currency, and nobody spent them here.

The room was cold. Ilse lit the stub of candle and then stood looking at it instead of undressing, her back to Quentin.

"Tell me what happens if he comes aboard," she said. "Pell. Tell me the truth this time."

"If he comes aboard and finds a discrepancy in the manifest, he can hold the ship. He can request soldiers from the naval office. If they find you listed nowhere and traveling under a dead man's name—" Quentin stopped.

"Say it."

"They shoot irregular passengers. As spies. It's become fashionable wording."

Ilse nodded slowly, absorbing it the way she absorbed a difficult text: reading it twice, marking the hard passages, filing them where they could be worked with later. "And you'll be standing at the gangway."

"Yes."

"Then your lie has to be better than his paperwork."

"My lie," Quentin said, "will be very good. I've been rehearsing being nobody my whole life. Tomorrow I get to be somebody specific. It's almost a promotion."

That earned her the ghost of a smile over her shoulder. Then she crossed the cold floorboards and sat beside Quentin on the pallet, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the candle burned, and somewhere below them Bram's clocks ticked in their loose, imperfect chorus, none agreeing with any other.

"When you found me," Ilse said quietly, "at the print shop. For a moment I thought you'd come to arrest me. I want you to know I decided I didn't care. I decided that if it was you, I'd put down the pamphlets and go with you wherever you were taking me, and I wouldn't even ask." She turned her head; the candlelight moved along the line of her jaw. "That frightened me more than the denunciation did. Caring about a person more than about surviving. So if tomorrow goes wrong — if any of it goes wrong — I need you to know that the tea, that afternoon in the capital, with the translation drafts and your terrible opinions about my word choices — that was the last time I remember being entirely unafraid. You gave me that once already. Everything since is interest."

"Ilse—"

"Don't make me a speech." Her voice frayed, just barely. "Make me safe. That's the only vow you ever made me that matters, and you're going to keep it in nine hours, and then I'm going to stand on a ship and watch you get smaller on that quay, and I have decided — I am telling you formally, translator to courier — I have decided that this counts as a happy ending. Do not argue with my translation."

Quentin laughed, and the laugh cracked open into something else, and Ilse held them while it happened, her hand steady at the back of their head, saying nothing, needing nothing said. The candle burned down. The clocks ticked. Outside, the harbor breathed — water on stone, rope on wood, the vast slow inhale of the tide beginning to think about coming in.

Later, in the dark, Ilse slept. Quentin lay listening to her breathe and did not sleep at all.

* * *

It was past two when Quentin gave up on it and went softly down the stairs, meaning only to sit by the banked stove until the sky considered dawn. But there was light under the door of Bram's workshop, and the door stood open a hand's width, and inside, the clockmaker sat at his bench with a single lamp burning, tools laid out before him in their exact order like a sentence waiting to be read.

He looked up. He did not seem surprised.

Quentin came in and stood, unsure of the etiquette of interrupting a deaf man's work, until Bram pointed at the stool opposite. Quentin sat.

For a long time Bram simply worked: a movement held in his jeweler's clamp, a wheel being trued by increments, his breath slow and even, his eyes narrowed to the width of the work. Quentin watched his hands. They were old hands, spotted and scarred, and they moved with such unhurried certainty that watching them was a kind of sedative. Quentin felt their heartbeat come down out of its gallop for the first time in days.

Then Bram set down his tool, wiped his fingers, and reached beneath the bench. What he brought up he placed in the center of the workbench and slid across, and then he folded his hands and waited.

It was a pocket compass. Brass-cased, worn smooth at the hinges, older than anything else Quentin had seen in the house except perhaps Bram himself. Quentin picked it up and opened it — and frowned.

The needle swung. It swung past north, settled briefly, overshot again, and finally came to rest pointing — not north. Not any direction Quentin could name. It aimed steadily out through the workshop wall, through the dark city, toward some point on the compass rose that existed on no chart Quentin had ever sailed by.

"It doesn't point north," Quentin said, before remembering, and then said it again, slower, and gestured.

Bram smiled. He pulled a slate toward himself and wrote in a careful, schoolroom hand:

**IT POINTS WHEREVER YOU DECIDE HOME IS. IT IS NOT A GOOD COMPASS. I MADE IT BAD ON PURPOSE, YEARS AGO, FOR A WOMAN WHO WAS LEAVING AND DID NOT KNOW HER DIRECTION. SHE WROTE BACK THAT IT LED HER CORRECTLY ANYWAY.**

Quentin read it twice. Their throat closed.

"Why give it to me?" they asked aloud, and then, uselessly, "I leave at dawn. I'm staying. Everyone else is sailing away or staying and I'm the one staying, I don't need—"

Bram took the slate back and erased it slowly, thinking. Then he wrote:

**THE ONES WHO STAY NEED IT MOST. LEAVING IS EASY TO NAVIGATE. STAYING REQUIRES INSTRUMENTS.**

He turned the slate around, then tapped the compass case twice — a jeweler's tap, precise, final — and returned to his wheel, lowering his eyes to the work in a gesture that was unmistakably dismissal, and unmistakably benediction, both at once.

Quentin stood in the doorway a moment longer. "Thank you" seemed too small and also too large. In the end they said neither it nor anything else, only closed the door softly behind them, the compass riding in their palm, its wrong needle holding faithfully to its chosen direction all the way up the dark stairs.

Back in the eaved room, Ilse had not moved. Quentin lay down beside her, carefully, and set the compass on the sill where the first gray suggestion of morning was already assembling behind the rooftops.

Below, faint through the floorboards, the house's clocks ticked toward five. Somewhere out on the black water, the Meridian rode her anchor, and Tamsin slept among the lines, and a dead stoker's papers lay squared and ready in Fenwick's sea chest, and the tide was gathering itself beyond the mole like a promise the world intended to keep whether anyone was brave enough or not.

Three hours to first light. Two hours to the gangway. One hour to the lie that would have to hold.

Quentin closed their eyes and did not sleep, and did not need to.
