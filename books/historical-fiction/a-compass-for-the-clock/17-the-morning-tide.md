## 17. The Morning Tide

The tide turned before the sun did.

Quentin felt it before they saw it — a long, muscular shift in the harbor water, the whole gray surface of Callowmark beginning to move as one animal waking. The lamps along the quay still burned in the fog, but their light had gone thin, ceremonial, like candles left burning after a funeral. Somewhere behind the mist the sun was climbing, and it turned the fog from white to a dirty pearl, and under that pearl the *Meridian* waited at her berth with steam up and lines singled, ready to go.

Quentin stood at the foot of the gangway with their hands in their coat pockets, one hand closed around Bram's compass, and watched the water move.

*She is aboard.* The thought arrived again and again, each time with the same small shock, like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth had been. *She is aboard, and she is safe.*

Ilse had come up the gangway forty minutes ago, in the dark, wrapped in a dead man's coat. She had walked past Pell's clerk at the head of the berth without looking left or right, in exactly the shuffling, half-asleep gait of a woman going to see a relative off — which was true, if you squinted, if you were generous, if the dead stoker Fenwick carried in his papers had ever had a cousin, which he now did, forever. Her name on the manifest was Marta Hollan. Her face beneath the hood had been pale as paper, and she had gripped Quentin's hand once, hard, at the top of the gangway, and said nothing, because there was nothing that could be said in front of the clerk, and perhaps nothing that needed saying at all.

Now she was somewhere below decks, in the berth Tamsin had forged into existence three days ago, and the ship was alive around her, and the tide was going out.

Quentin breathed the harbor air — coal smoke, fish, tar, the cold mineral smell of dawn — and tried to feel triumphant.

They mostly felt like a man standing on a dock watching his own hand being sawed off, slowly, and being grateful for it.

* *

"You're up early." Captain Oduya's voice came from behind, low and even as always. She came to stand at Quentin's shoulder, hands clasped behind her back, watching her ship the way she always watched her: not with love exactly, but with the attention that passes for love among people who are responsible for things.

"I slept," Quentin said, which was a lie so obvious it functioned as a joke between them.

"Good. Lying well before breakfast is a sign of character." Oduya did not look at them. "Your friend is settled. Fenwick found her a blanket and told her about his mother for twenty minutes. I believe that's how he says welcome."

"Thank you," Quentin said. "For everything you—"

"Don't." Oduya's voice didn't sharpen; it simply stopped moving, the way a clock stops. "I'll hear gratitude when I'm at sea and can't be arrested for it. Right now there's a customs official asleep in the harbor office who will wake with a warrant and a bad temper, and I'd like to be a mile offshore before either arrives." She paused. "You're not sailing."

It wasn't a question. Quentin realized they had never actually said it aloud to anyone — not to Sefu, not to Tamsin, not to Ilse, whose face at the top of the gangway had asked it and been answered by Quentin holding still. But Oduya read manifests the way other people read faces.

"No," Quentin said. "I'm not sailing."

Oduya nodded slowly, as though confirming an entry in a ledger. "Then here is what happens next, so you can't say no one told you. When Pell comes back — and he will come back — the first thing he does is check whether the irregular passenger has a relative on shore waving goodbye. Kinship is evidence. It's the thing he collects." She turned then, and looked at Quentin directly, and her eyes were very old and very kind in a face built to show neither. "So whatever you're going to do this morning, do it loudly. Be seen. Be a cousin too. Be a weeping aunt. Fill his margins with things that are boring."

"I understand."

"I know you do. That's what frightens me about you." She allowed herself, at last, the ghost of a smile. "There's coffee in the galley. Fenwick made it strong enough to strip paint, which means he's happy. Go drink some. And Kavanagh—" She was already turning away. "The letter you mail. Make it honest. Lies sink slower than stones but they sink all the same."

She walked back up the gangway, calling for lines, and her voice took on the flat carrying tone of a captain with a working deck, and Quentin stood alone at the foot of it with the compass in their pocket and the morning coming up gray over the water.

* *

The Saltglass family arrived while the crew was casting off the aft spring.

They came down the quay in a loose cluster, out of the thinning fog, and Quentin's chest did something painful and structural at the sight of them. Widow Sefu in her good shawl, walking with the unhurried dignity of a woman attending a christening rather than a departure. Bram beside her, coat buttoned to the throat, his repair bag over his shoulder because Bram did not go anywhere without it. Fenwick had a half-holiday negotiated and a flask in his pocket. And Tamsin — Tamsin should have been aboard already, but there she was, running the last stretch of quay with her cap crooked, skidding to a halt at Quentin's side breathless and furious and grinning all at once.

"They wouldn't let me stay up there," she said. "Oduya said I was making the crew nervous by hovering over the hold like a hen. As if I hover."

"You hover," said Fenwick.

"I *supervise*," said Tamsin, and then, quieter, bumping her shoulder against Quentin's: "Is she all right? Down there?"

"She's all right."

"Did she eat?"

"Fenwick fed her."

"Good." Tamsin nodded once, businesslike, and then ruined it entirely by blinking hard and staring ferociously out at the water.

Widow Sefu arranged them along the quay rail with the tactful firmness of a woman who had seated difficult dinner guests for thirty years, and then the work of pretending began. They talked. That was the point of it — Oduya's advice, taken up without anyone having to explain it. They talked about the boarding house roof, which leaked; about Bram's mantel clock, which had begun striking thirteen for reasons only Bram understood and refused to explain; about whether the herring this year were fat or merely abundant. Ordinary words, thrown like planks over deep water. Passersby saw a family seeing off a relation. Pell's margins, wherever Pell kept them, would find nothing here but boredom.

And underneath the ordinary words, everyone knew what they were doing, and nobody said it, and that was its own kind of language — the household's native tongue, learned by Quentin in six weeks of suppers and lamplight: *we see you, we are standing where you are standing, look at us looking at you.*

Quentin watched the quay through the corner of their eye and let the talk wash past, and thought about promises.

Here was the shape of it, which they had somehow not let themselves see until this exact hour, standing on this exact stone: the promise made to Ilse Marchetti over cold tea and translation drafts, seven years ago, in a capital that still believed in itself — *when there's peace, I'll get you out; if there's ever trouble, I'll get you out* — had never contained Quentin in it. Quentin had never promised to leave with her. Had never been asked to. The promise had a single direction, like water finding a downhill: *Ilse gets out.* That was the whole of it. Everything else — the codes sewn against their ribs, the invisibility rehearsed for years, the name chosen precisely so that it would leave no one behind grieving — all of it had been built on the assumption that Quentin was a courier, and couriers deliver, and are not delivered.

But knowing a thing and feeling it are different countries, and the border between them runs through the chest.

Because the *Meridian* was going to cast off, and Ilse was aboard, and Quentin was going to stand on this quay and watch the ship take away the person they — 

They had not said the word yet. Not to her, not seven years ago, not in the print shop two nights ago when she had gripped their sleeve and asked *why are you doing this* and Quentin had answered with logistics. There had been a moment, at the top of the gangway in the dark, her hand crushing theirs, when it had almost come up out of them like a splinter working loose. And then the clerk had coughed, and the moment had folded itself away into some interior pocket, and she had gone below, and now the tide was running.

Maybe that was why Quentin couldn't board. Not duty, not the letter, not even the family on the quay — or not only those. Maybe some stubborn, cowardly, honest part of them understood that if they went below decks now, into the noise and the engine-room dark, they would finally have to say the word out loud, with nowhere to walk afterward, no dispatch to carry, no country to serve as an excuse for silence. A ship is a very small place to hide in.

Or maybe that was vanity. Maybe it really was simpler than that: the republic had spent Quentin's whole life teaching them that they were a channel and not a person, and this — this quay, these people, this deliberate heartbreak — was the first order Quentin had ever given themselves.

* * *

"Heave!" Fenwick's voice, surprisingly loud, joining the bosun's. The aft spring came off the bollard and coiled into the water, and the *Meridian* leaned away from the quay like a horse testing a rein.

Forward, they could hear Captain Oduya's orders, crisp in the cold. Steam curled from the fantail vents. The hull came alive against the fenders with a groaning, intimate sound, wood remembering that it belonged to the sea.

Tamsin grabbed Quentin's arm. "Wave," she hissed. "Somebody on deck is looking — wave like you're seeing off your own mother."

Quentin raised their arm and waved, and the whole line of them waved — Sefu with quiet dignity, Tamsin with both arms like a windmill, Fenwick lifting his flask in salute — and up on the afterdeck a small figure appeared at the rail. Hooded, in the dead man's coat. Ilse.

She looked down at the quay, and found them immediately, as though she had known exactly where to look, and Quentin's whole body turned into one held note.

She did not wave. She put her hand flat against her sternum, just over her heart, and pressed it there — once, deliberately, so they could not mistake it — and then she lifted that same hand, fingers open, toward the quay, toward all of them, and mouthed something the distance ate.

"What did she say?" Tamsin demanded. "Can anyone read lips at this range? Fenwick, you were a sailor—"

"She said 'thank you,'" Fenwick said comfortably. "Or possibly 'turn around.' Hard to say."

"She said *go home*," said Bram.

Everyone looked at him. Bram, who communicated in gestures and pencil notes, who had never in six weeks volunteered more than four written words at a time, stood at the rail with his face tilted up toward the ship, and repeated it with his hands, patient and precise: *go home.* Then he shrugged, as if to say that lips were just another kind of clock and he had spent his life reading dials.

The *Meridian* gathered herself. The tug took her bow, the fog swallowed her stern, and the gap of dirty water between hull and quay widened — a meter, two, five — and with every meter Quentin felt the promise completing itself and the cost arriving in the same motion, inseparable, like a coin with only one face.

Ilse stayed at the rail until the fog took her too.

* *

They stood a long time after the ship was gone. The quay emptied of ceremony; gulls reclaimed the bollards; the harbor resumed its indifferent commerce. But the five of them stayed at the rail, shoulder to shoulder, watching the place where the ship had been, and none of them suggested moving, because moving would mean beginning the day after, and nobody was ready to begin the day after.

Finally Sefu spoke.

"In my country," she said, "when someone leaves on a journey, the ones who stay behind sweep the road behind them. So that nothing follows." She smoothed her shawl. "I have always thought it was also for the ones staying. Something to do with the hands, while the heart catches up."

Nobody answered. But Quentin saw Tamsin look down at her own hands, and saw Fenwick pass his flask to Bram, who drank from it, which he never did, and coughed, which he never did either.

"Breakfast is at eight," Sefu said, turning. "Latecomers get the porridge with the skin on it." And she walked back up the quay, unhurried, leaving a swept road behind her.

Fenwick followed. Then Tamsin, who squeezed Quentin's arm hard enough to leave a mark and said, "Eight o'clock. Don't be late. I mean it. I'll come find you." Then Bram, who paused, and took Quentin's hand, and pressed something into it — the compass, which Quentin realized they had been gripping so hard its brass case had printed itself into their palm. Bram folded Quentin's fingers closed over it again, tapped the back of their hand twice — *keep this, keep going* — and walked away whistling, silently, the way deaf people whistle, all breath and no sound.

Quentin stood alone at the rail.

The fog was burning off. Across the harbor, the consulate's windows caught the first actual sunlight the city had seen in days, and flashed gold — and Quentin noticed, with a small cold start, that the flags above the entrance were gone, hauled down sometime in the night, and that carts were drawn up in the courtyard, loaded high under tarpaulins.

Somewhere behind them, on the quay, a familiar voice was asking a fish-porter cheerful, meticulous questions — *name, trade, kin, debts* — and writing answers in a margin.

Ambrose Pell had arrived early.

Quentin turned, and Pell, some fifty yards up the quay, looked up from his ledger at exactly that moment — the way a man looks up when he already knows what he'll see — and found Quentin standing alone, watching the empty water where a ship used to be, with a compass in one fist and salt wind pulling at their coat.

Pell smiled. It was not an unkind smile. That was the worst of it.

He closed his ledger, tucked it under his arm, and began to walk toward the *Meridian*'s berth — toward the manifest clerk's booth, where the passenger list lay open on the desk, waiting to be checked against the shore.

Quentin's feet were already moving.
