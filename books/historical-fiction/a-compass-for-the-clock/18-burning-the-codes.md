## 18. Burning the Codes

The Meridian went out on the morning tide the way honest things go out — without ceremony, without music, without anyone on the quay quite able to say afterward at what moment she had stopped being touchable.

Quentin stood at the harbor rail where the stone gave way to iron pilings, their coat buttoned to the throat against a wind that came off the water with salt and cold in equal measure. Below them, longshoremen coiled lines they had already cast off, moving with the unhurried economy of men whose work was finished whether or not anyone watched. Gulls screamed over the fish market. Somewhere behind Quentin, Callowmark was waking: shutters banging open, a cart of oranges arguing its way up the hill road, a child's voice counting steps in a language Quentin did not know.

On the water, the packet ship leaned into the ebb tide, her patched hull riding low but steady. Fenwick was down in her belly somewhere, feeding the fires. Tamsin would be at the bow rail, because Tamsin was always at the bow rail when there was something to look at, one hand knotted in the shrouds as if she meant to go wherever the ship went by sheer grip alone. Captain Oduya would be aft, hat brim low, watching the channel markers slide past with the particular stillness of a person doing four calculations for every visible one.

And Ilse Marchetti — Ilse would be below, in a berth entered in the manifest under a dead man's family name, lying on a mattress that smelled of tar and someone else's years, learning how to breathe inside the shape of another woman's papers. Alive. Out. That had been the whole architecture of it, all those nights at the Saltglass house when they had bent their heads together over Fenwick's documents and Sefu's candles and Bram's silence. Get her aboard. Get her out. Keep the promise.

The promise had never said anything about Quentin going with it.

They had known that, Quentin told themselves now. They had known it in some chamber of themselves that they had kept carefully dark, the way you keep a room shuttered not because you've forgotten what's in it but because you know exactly what's in it and are not ready to sit with it in daylight. On the quay an hour ago, when the lines came off the bollards, the Saltglass household had stood in a ragged row — Sefu upright and terrible in her dignity, Bram with his good hand raised in a salute that belonged to no army, Tamsin already halfway to crying and pretending she was laughing at the wind. And Quentin had stood among them, not across from them, and understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water that they had never once, in all the planning, believed themselves to be leaving.

The Meridian grew smaller. The channel took her, and the gray morning took her, and then there was only a suggestion of masts against the horizon, a smudge of smoke, a place where the sea held the memory of a ship.

Quentin stayed until the smudge went too.

* * *

Then they were just a person standing on a pier in a city that was not theirs, wearing a coat with secrets sewn into its lining, and the wind found every seam of them at once.

They walked back along the harbor front without deciding to. Their feet knew these stones now; three weeks of repairs and searching and small suppers had worn a groove in them. Past the fish market, where the first catch of the day lay silvering on crushed ice. Past the customs shed with its peeling green paint. Past the spot — Quentin made themselves look — where Pell had stood at the gangway at dawn with his manifest and his soft voice and his margins full of marginal things, before Oduya had invoked maritime law like a wall raised between him and the deck, before Tamsin had misdirected him with the shameless fluency of someone who had stowed away herself once and knew exactly which lies sounded truest. He had gone. He had promised to come back with soldiers. That promise was still standing somewhere in the world, accruing interest.

But that was later's problem. Quentin had learned, in this city, that later could take care of itself if you fed it honestly enough.

They found the bench facing the water — the one where Bram sometimes sat in the afternoons, listening to the harbor through the wood with his whole body — and sat down, and put their hands flat on their knees, and felt it arrive.

Not grief exactly. Or not only grief. It was more like the sensation, after carrying a heavy thing for a very long time, of setting it down and finding your arms still shaped around its absence. For eleven years Quentin Kavanagh had been a vessel for other people's words. Carried sealed dispatches through three borders and two wars. Memorized codes sewn into their own coat lining, thread by thread, in the capital's last gray week, while the republic they served dissolved around them like sugar in rain. Had rehearsed being nobody so thoroughly that nobody had begun to feel less like a costume and more like skin.

And now the ship that carried the last living proof of all that labor was a smudge, and then nothing, and Quentin was sitting on a bench with a coat full of orders that no longer had an orderer.

*Unread,* they thought. *Still unread.*

That had been the discipline, all these weeks. Thirteen nights ago, alone in the narrow room at the Saltglass house with a candle burned down to a thumb's width, Quentin had laid the coat across their knees and put their fingers to the seams and felt the stiff little packets sleeping in there — the cipher keys, the final instructions, the last will of a state gasping its way toward its end. They had known even then what the orders almost certainly contained. Abandon Callowmark. Abandon allies. Names, possibly. A list of persons no longer worth the republic's protection, drawn up in a capital that no longer existed to enforce or forgive it. Ilse's name might be in there. Might have been typed in a clerk's neat hand while Quentin was drinking tea in a boarding house kitchen, being loved by people who asked them nothing.

Thirteen nights ago they had folded the coat, pinched out the candle, and gone to sleep with their jaw clenched so hard it ached till noon. Not knowing had been unbearable. But knowing — knowing would have made them complicit in advance, would have let the republic reach forward from its own deathbed and put its dead hand on everything Quentin chose afterward. As long as the seals stayed unbroken, the orders were only paper. The moment they were read, they became deeds.

So: unread. Through Pell's inspection. Through the forging of the manifest entry. Through the farewell supper where everyone pretended the roast goose was ordinary and Bram pressed a pocket compass into Quentin's palm and closed their fingers over it like a man planting a seed. Unread through all of it, riding in the lining against Quentin's ribs, a second heartbeat made of state ink.

The ship was gone now. And the orders — the orders were still here, still beating, still theirs to carry or not.

Quentin took out the knife.

It was a small thing, the kind sailors carried, with a horn handle worn smooth by hands that were not Quentin's — Fenwick had pressed it on them the second week, wordlessly, the way he did everything. They turned up their cuff and began, with the deliberate care of a surgeon or an arsonist, on the seam.

* * *

The thread came out slowly. It had been good thread; Quentin had chosen it themselves in the capital, in a shop that smelled of tallow and fear, choosing the strongest spool on the shelf because they had still believed, then, that strength was the same as permanence. Now each stitch resisted and then surrendered, resisted and then surrendered, and the sound it made — a small dry ticking, like a clock being persuaded backward — seemed enormously loud against the wash of the tide.

A dockworker passed and glanced over. Quentin did not stop, did not hunch, did not perform innocence. That was the strangest discovery of these weeks: that concealment performed badly was safer than concealment performed well, that a person openly cutting threads on a public bench looked like a person mending a coat, and only a person hiding something looked like a person hiding something. Pell had taught them that, in his office with its ledgers and its lamp, with his single question too many — *and your loyalties, citizen?* — delivered in the tone of a man asking about the weather. Quentin had lied well that day. They were done lying well.

The seam opened. The first packet slid into their palm: oilcloth, folded to the size of a matchbox, sewn flat so it would lie invisible against the ribs. Quentin held it and did not open it.

They worked along the lining instead, methodically, left to right, the way the code clerk in the capital had instructed — *evenly distributed, mademoiselle, monsieur, so that no single blade finds them all* — and the packets came free one by one, five, six, seven, each one a little heavier than its size suggested, the way guilt is always heavier than its dimensions. By the end the coat hung differently, lighter, and Quentin realized they had been walking around for weeks tilted slightly by the weight of it, like a boat ballasted wrong, and that everyone at the Saltglass house must have seen the tilt and said nothing, because that was how that house loved you: it saw everything and demanded nothing.

Nine packets in all. Nine small parcels of a dead country's intentions.

Quentin stood. Walked to the edge of the pier, where the tide ran fast and green-gray, deep enough here that the water swallowed light rather than returning it. Below, in the clear strip near the pilings, a school of small fish hung in the current, holding station against the flow with minute flickers of their fins, going nowhere at tremendous effort.

They thought about reading them. One last time, seriously, with the packets actually in their hand and the harbor wind pulling at the oilcloth corners, they thought about it. Because there was a version of duty that still lived in them, old and loyal and half-starved, and it spoke now in the voice of the instructors in the capital: *You swore. You carry. What you do with what you carry is not yours to decide.* And there was the counter-voice, newer, warm-voiced, smelling of Sefu's kitchen: *Decide for whom? The republic is a rumor with a flag. The people who need you are made of something else.*

If they read the orders and obeyed them, they would be a traitor to everyone who had fed them. If they read the orders and disobeyed, they would at least be guilty with full knowledge — treason with receipts. Only one path left them innocent, and it was the path that required never looking at all.

Unread. It had always been going to be unread. Perhaps it had been decided thirteen nights ago, by candlelight, when they pinched out the flame. Perhaps it had been decided eleven years ago, over tea and translation drafts, when Ilse Marchetti had laughed at their terrible pronunciation and Quentin had promised her safe passage in peacetime — a promise made lightly, in the currency of a peace everyone assumed was permanent, and which had turned out to be the only contract in Quentin's life that survived its issuing authority.

States fell. Promises, apparently, floated.

Quentin peeled back the oilcloth on the first packet — just far enough to see the paper, cream-colored, covered in the cipher clerk's immaculate rows of grouped figures — and did not read it, could not read it, would need hours and the key sewn into their own memory to read it, and folded it closed again, and dropped it into the tide.

The water took it with obscene gentleness. It rode the surface for a heartbeat, turning, the oilcloth flashing pale, and then the current got under it and drew it out toward the channel, out along the wake the Meridian had left, and it sank, or it didn't, and either way it was gone.

The second packet followed. Then the third. Quentin's hands were steady, which surprised them; they had expected trembling, expected some ceremony of resistance in the body. But it was easy. That was the horror of it and the mercy of it both — that after eleven years, treason weighed almost nothing. Each packet left their fingers with less drama than a coin given to a beggar. Somewhere out there, in offices still nominally staffed, clerks were filing rosters and drafting proclamations and listing enemies, believing that the paper made the power, and here was Quentin Kavanagh demonstrating otherwise on an empty pier: the entire operational authority of a dying republic, dissolving in salt water, unread, because one tired courier declined to be its hands any longer.

Liberation, they thought, watching the seventh packet vanish. It feels exactly like treason. It is treason. There is no honest word that separates them, and I am done looking for one.

The eighth packet caught the light strangely as it went — a glint, a hesitation, as if reluctant — and Quentin laughed, once, a short sound the wind took immediately. No. Paper does not hesitate. Only people do, and the person hesitating was standing on the pier with one packet left and their heart going like a trapped bird.

The ninth. The last. The deepest-sewn, set behind the collar seam where no casual search would find it, the packet they had been most careful with, which meant — they understood now — the packet they had been most afraid of. The final orders. Whatever the republic had saved for its last breath. *Abandon the allies. Let the translators hang. The Meridian sails; the rest is arithmetic.*

Quentin held it over the water a long moment.

Then they thought of Bram — of the clockmaker's slow hands setting the compass into the mantel clock's face two nights ago, pointing not north but *home*, whatever direction that was, and of the note Bram had written beneath it in his careful deaf man's script: *A compass is only honest if you choose where it points.*

They dropped the last packet in.

For a while they simply stood there, arms at their sides, coat hanging light and wrong around them, watching the current run. The fish below held their station. The gulls resumed their arguments. The tide went out taking everything with it, the way tides do, impartially, without once looking back to see what it had been trusted with.

Eleven years, and it took less than a quarter hour to unmake. Quentin searched themselves for ruin and found, instead of ruin, space. Vast echoing space, where the orders used to live — room enough, suddenly, to feel the wind properly, to notice that the sun had broken through somewhere behind the cloud cover and laid a long rough gold path across the harbor, out along the track the Meridian had sailed, as if the morning itself were marking the channel for her.

*She's through the narrows by now,* Quentin thought. *Ilse is through the narrows.* Safe in international water, alive under a dead woman's name, carrying nothing but herself — which was, Quentin reflected, the only cargo the republic had ever been asked to move that it had actually delivered.

Their eyes stung. They let them.

Then, from up the quay, footsteps — brisk ones, official ones — and a voice calling their name, the name they'd registered with Pell, and Quentin turned with salt wind still cold on their face and empty pockets and an empty coat, lighter than they had been in eleven years, to see who had come looking for the courier who no longer existed.
