## 21. A Compass for the Clock

The press taught me its temper the way a horse does: by throwing me twice before letting me ride.

Herr Voss's machine was older than the republic, older than most of the men who had argued it into existence, an iron flatbed with a hand crank that required the operator to lean into it with their whole body, as if pushing against history itself. My first week I fed paper crooked and ruined forty sheets of a pamphlet about grain tariffs. My second week I caught my sleeve in the flywheel and tore the seam Bram had mended only days before, and stood there in the sudden quiet of the shop while Ilse's old colleagues — Marta with her ink-stained knuckles, the brothers Anselm and Piet with their identical way of sighing at mistakes — looked up from their type cases and waited to see if I would cry about it.

I did not. I rolled up the torn sleeve, showed them the scar from Callowmark harbor where I'd gashed my arm on a mooring cleat, and said, "At least this one bled honestly."

Marta laughed first. That was the day I stopped being a stranger.

I had never learned a trade. In the republic's service I had been trained in nothing so useful as making things — only in carrying them, hiding them, being no one near them. Setting type was the opposite of all that. You assembled words letter by letter, mirror-reversed, into blocks, and then you pressed those words onto paper by the hundreds, and every person who took one home held something you had made with your own hands. There was no coat lining deep enough to sew this work into. It went out into the world wearing its own name.

"You're getting faster," Piet allowed on a Tuesday, watching me lock a chase without dropping a single piece of eight-point Garamond. From Piet this was a parade, a banquet, a medal struck in bronze.

"The teacher is patient," I said.

"The teacher," Marta called from the composing stone, "is old enough to know that patience is cheaper than replacing apprentices." But she said it warmly, and slid a fresh galley toward me, and we worked on in the smell of ink and solvent while rain came down over Callowmark in long gray sheets.

The pamphlets we printed were careful ones, mostly. Grain tariffs. Harbor wages. A treatise on drainage that nobody read and nobody censored. Callowmark was neutral, which meant it tolerated speech the way a river tolerates stones — everything moved, but slowly, and mostly along the bottom. Once a month, though, Marta set something hotter: an essay on the rights of exiles, or a testimony smuggled out of a border province, and we printed it late at night with the shutters closed, and distributed it folded inside copies of the drainage treatise. Words going where they were needed. It occurred to me, somewhere around my sixth week, that I had simply turned myself inside out. The same skills — routes, dead drops, plausible covers — now served the opposite purpose: not concealment but delivery.

I did not miss the old work. What I missed, some evenings, was the certainty of it. Duty had been cold, but cold is a kind of clarity. This new life was warm and full of ambiguity — whether a pamphlet would help or harm, whether neutrality was a shelter or a slow poison — and I had to think for myself now, every day, without orders to blame.

It suited me better than I expected.

* * *

The letters came through neutral ports, which meant they came late and irregularly, which meant every arrival of any mail packet reorganized the whole city's heartbeats.

The first one reached me six weeks after the Meridian sailed. It came care of the print shop, addressed to "the compositor known as K., who owes the writer a debt of safe passage, now discharged." Ilse's hand had lost none of its discipline — upright, economical, every letter standing at attention — but she allowed herself flourishes in the margins, little sketches: a gull, a ship's rail, a cup of tea with steam rising off it in two looping lines.

*We made port in Solenne on the fourth day,* she wrote. *The captain let me stand at the bow the whole approach. I have been given a room in a house belonging to a bookseller's widow, and within a fortnight I was working again — you will laugh — translating harbor regulations into three languages. The least glamorous words ever put through my hands. I do them well. I do them almost happily.*

*I have learned something here that I want you to know, since you are the only person who will understand it. For years I believed safety was a place. Now I understand it is a tense. Not a city you reach but a verb you keep conjugating — I am safe, I am being safe, I will have been safe. Every morning I conjugate it. Some mornings it comes easily.*

*Tamsin sends her regards and says to tell you she has talked Captain Oduya into paying her actual wages, which makes her a professional sailor, which makes her insufferable. Fenwick has adopted a cat with engine-grease paws and named it after a deceased admiral.*

*Write to me at the address below. Say ordinary things. Ordinary things are the rarest cargo in the world and I am starving for them.*

So I wrote ordinary things. The rain. The price of lamp oil. How Marta hummed opera badly when she was distributing type. How Bram's clocks kept different hours from one another and how the boarding house had simply agreed to live by whichever chimed last. I told her about learning the press, and how the words came out reversed on the composing stick and right-reading on the page, and that it seemed like a lesson about something larger, though I hadn't yet worked out what.

Her replies took five weeks, sometimes seven. I learned to live in the gap between sending and receiving — to hold a version of Ilse in my mind, slightly out of date, always slightly younger than she actually was, like reading light from a star. Between letters, she existed for me as a habit rather than a person: the extra cup I poured each evening at the Saltglass table, set across from my place whether anyone sat there or not. Widow Sefu never remarked on it. She simply began setting out that cup herself.

*You asked what the reversed words mean,* she wrote in her third letter. *Here is what I think. In the service of a state, you were the hidden hand; your work was to be invisible so that power could appear seamless. In the print shop you are the visible hand. Same skill, inverted loyalty. You have not changed trades, Quentin. You have changed directions. North and south are the same axis. What differs is which way you face.*

I read that letter four times, then folded it into my coat — the same coat, the lining now empty and hanging loose where the codes had been cut away — and wore it everywhere like armor again, except that what it carried now could not hurt anyone.

* * *

Bram came to the print shop on a Thursday in my third month, unannounced, as he did everything.

He arrived in the afternoon rain, enormous and deliberate under a borrowed umbrella that looked child-sized in his grip, and stood dripping in the doorway until Piet fetched him a rag. Then he crossed the shop floor with surprising softness for a man his size, examined the flatbed press from every angle, laid his palm flat against the iron frame the way a physician might listen to a chest — feeling, I realized later, the rhythm of the mechanism through his skin, the way he felt every clock he ever mended — and nodded once, gravely, as though the press had passed examination.

Then he produced, from inside his coat, a wooden box the size of two cupped hands, and gave it to me.

Inside, cushioned in wool, was a clock face. Not a pocket watch this time — a proper clock face, brass-ringed, porcelain-white, with black Roman numerals and delicate hands. And set into the center, where a maker's mark would ordinarily sit, gleamed the compass. Bram's gift from the night before the tide, the needle that pointed not north but home — whatever direction that is — now mounted flush into the dial, its glass dome level with the porcelain, so that the whole thing read simultaneously as instrument and question. Around the rim, where a clockmaker might engrave *Tempus Fugit* or a shop name, Bram had chased a single word into the brass:

*QUENTIN.*

I looked up. He was watching me with the particular patience of the deaf, which is not emptiness but attention — the total, undistracted attention of a man who reads the world entirely through his eyes. I signed to him, clumsily, the small vocabulary he'd been teaching me since the Saltglass nights: *Thank you. Beautiful. Why?*

He took the chalk he always carried and wrote on the slate hanging by the shop door, in his careful draftsman's hand:

*A CLOCK TELLS YOU WHAT TIME IT IS. A COMPASS TELLS YOU WHERE YOU ARE. MOST PEOPLE OWN ONE OF EACH AND CONSULT NEITHER. YOU WILL CONSULT BOTH. I HAVE KNOWN YOU SIX MONTHS. THAT IS LONG ENOUGH TO KNOW WHAT A MAN NEEDS ON HIS MANTEL.*

*But why the clock face too?* I wrote back.

Bram considered this. His fingers moved through the sign for *think* — fingertip to forehead, circling outward, a small slow orbit. Then he took the chalk again.

*BECAUSE THE COMPASS ALONE IS ONLY A DIRECTION. THE CLOCK MAKES IT A PROMISE. WHEREVER HOME IS, IT HAS AN HOUR. IT IS WAITING FOR YOU AT A SPECIFIC TIME. THAT IS WHAT MAKES IT HOME AND NOT MERELY A PLACE.*

I confess I stood in that print shop, ink to my elbows, rain still running off the windows, and had to turn away and pretend great interest in the type cases for a while. Piet and Marta, mercifully, pretended not to notice. Exiles learn early when to look elsewhere.

That evening I carried the clock home through the wet streets wrapped in my coat, and Widow Sefu cleared a space on the parlor mantel between the candlestick and a chipped figure of a harbor saint, and the whole Saltglass household gathered to watch Bram hang it and set it ticking. He regulated it against his own pocket watch, adjusted the pendulum twice, and stepped back.

Tamsin would have called the moment sentimental. Tamsin was away with the Meridian, running neutral routes and collecting wages and, per Ilse's letters, becoming steadily more insufferable, so there was no one to mock us, and we were free to be as sentimental as we liked. Sefu touched the mantel lightly, once, with two fingers, the way she blessed things. Fenwick's cat — left behind in Fenwick's brother's care, and visiting us weekly on principle — climbed into the chair beneath the mantel and went to sleep directly under the new clock, as if appointing itself guardian.

The compass needle, freed of my pocket and mounted in its dial, settled and trembled and pointed. East-southeast. Toward Solenne, if I read the harbor charts correctly. Toward Ilse.

Toward home, whatever direction that is. Bram had built the joke into the instrument itself, and every morning when I wound it, the needle swung and found her anyway.

* * *

Winter loosened its grip on Callowmark by degrees, grudgingly, like a creditor. The harbor ice broke up in February and the first honest shipping resumed, and with it the mail, and with it — on a bright cold morning when the print shop windows threw rectangles of gold across the composing stone — a letter with Solenne postmarks and something heavy enclosed.

Ilse's handwriting had changed. Not degraded — liberated. The letters leaned forward now, urgent and loose, as if she'd stopped marching and started walking somewhere she wanted to go.

*News first, because you will want it before anything else: the widow I work for has a nephew in the book trade, and between the three of us we are opening a shop. Books in four languages, a printing press in the back room — a small one, don't be jealous — and rooms above. Two rooms above. One of them has your name on it already, written on a card and pinned to the door, because I refuse to let the universe claim I didn't ask properly.*

*Second news, which is larger, though the papers will bury it: Vessany is finished. You know this. Everyone knows this. The government in exile quarrels in hotel lobbies and the provinces have stopped answering letters. What I am telling you is that I have stopped grieving it in the present tense. I grieve what it was supposed to be. I grieve the people it spent. But the thing itself — the machinery, the dispatches, the lists of enemies — I find I mourn it the way one mourns a fever breaking.*

*Which brings me to the real subject of this letter.*

*You burned nothing and saved everything, Quentin. You stayed on that quay when every instinct of your training screamed at you to sail. You mailed an honest letter to an office that no longer existed, and — I have this from a cousin in the postal service — someone found it, months later, in a crate of unclaimed documents, and read it, and copied it, and passed it hand to hand among people who needed to hear a confession that cost the confessor something. Your resignation has become, of all improbable things, a document. People quote it. They don't know your name; they call it "the letter from the courier." You are anonymous and famous at once, which I believe was always your condition, only now the anonymity is chosen and the fame is useful.*

*Come east when the passes open. Take the coastal packet to Solenne; it is dull, cheap, and safe, all three qualities you have earned the right to enjoy. Bring Bram if he will come — I have a mantel here too, and clocks that need a father. Bring Sefu's recipe for the fish stew, written in her hand, because I have tried to reproduce it from memory and produced something the harbor gulls refused.*

*There is work for your hands here, and readers for your words, and a door with your name pinned to it.*

*Conjugate the verb with me. I am safe. You are coming. We will have been home.*

I read the letter standing at the shop window, the gold light moving slowly across the page, and when I finished I read it again aloud to Marta and Piet, because they had become the kind of colleagues with whom one reads such letters aloud, and Marta cried without apology and Piet swore in three languages and immediately began planning how the shop would survive without me, which was his way of conceding that I would go.

That evening at the Saltglass table I laid Ilse's letter beside the stew pot and let everyone read it in turn while supper cooled, which Sefu declared a perfectly respectable way to serve a meal. Bram read it twice, studied the postmark, and began signing something rapidly, which Sefu translated between bites: he was asking about sea passages, cabin positions, the tonnage of coastal packets, and whether the fish in Solenne were as good as the fish in Callowmark, because a man should know what he is committing to.

"You're all just — deciding," I said, when I could speak. "Like it's already done."

Sefu refilled my cup — the cup, the one she still set across from my place even now that I sat elsewhere and Ilse sat nowhere — and said, in the tone she used for settling household disputes before they started: "Child, you decided six months ago on a quay at dawn. Everything since has just been the rest of us catching up."

Bram tapped the table for attention. When he had it, he pointed at the ceiling — toward the parlor, toward the mantel — and then made the sign he used for *pack*, and then, after a moment's thought, the sign for *clock*, and then drew in the air a shape we all understood: the box, the wool, the clock face with its stubborn needle. He would bring it. Of course he would bring it. A clock tells you what time it is; a compass tells you where you are; and both of them, apparently, were pointing east.

Later, alone in the parlor with the lamps low, I wound the clock and watched the compass needle steady itself against the porcelain, trembling faintly, faithful, east-southeast. On the table lay Ilse's letter and a half-written reply in which I had managed, so far, only the opening line: *The ordinary things you asked for have happened to me, all of them, and I hardly know how to report so much wealth.*

Through the window, the harbor lights burned in their long scattered row, and somewhere out in the dark the tide was turning, filling, preparing to carry whatever we entrusted to it — packets and pamphlets, letters and the living — toward the places that were waiting at specific hours, keeping our time, holding our names pinned to their doors.
