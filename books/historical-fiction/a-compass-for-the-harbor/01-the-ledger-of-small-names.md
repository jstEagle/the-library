## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The fog came off the harbor at dusk the way it always did in December, sliding under the doors of Ward Three like a patient nobody had admitted. Marguerite Zhou had learned to read it the way other nurses read faces: tonight it was a thick one, a wool-blanket fog, the kind that muffled the coughing from the far cots and made the oil lamps bloom into soft yellow moons.

He liked the fog, if he was honest about it. Fog meant fewer inspections. Fog meant Captain Reyes's clerks stayed at the customs house with their tea instead of marching through the ward with their clipboards, counting men the way a grocer counts tins. Fog meant that for a few hours, the harbor hospital belonged to the people inside it—the dying, the mending, and the ones who sat up with them.

Marguerite was one of the sitting-up kind. Officially he was an orderly, which meant he carried bedpans and boiled linen and hauled coal, and was paid rather less than the coal was worth. Unofficially—and this part appeared on no roster, no report, no document with a seal at the bottom—he kept accounts.

Not the hospital's accounts. Nobody trusted him with those, which he found amusing, since he could have done them standing on his head. No: Marguerite kept a different ledger entirely.

It lived beneath a loose floorboard by the third window of Ward Three, a floorboard whose nail he'd worked loose with a teaspoon over the course of four patient evenings while pretending to scrub the same spot. Under it lay a book bound in oiled canvas, roughly the size of a psalter, with pages ruled in his own careful hand. On its cover, nothing. No title. A book with no title invites no questions; a titled book is practically begging to be confiscated.

Inside were names. Not many names yet—a hundred and six, as of Tuesday—but each one written whole, the way the army never managed to write them.

*Private Thomas Corrigan, 4th Fusiliers, age 23. Last words: "Tell Nell the swing wants oiling on the left chain. She'll know."*

That had been in October. Marguerite still thought about the swing sometimes, at odd moments, between ladling broth and changing dressings. Somewhere out there was a girl with an unoiled swing and a father who had spent his last breath not on God or country or even fear, but on a squeaking hinge. The official record would say *Corrigan, T., died of wounds, 14 October.* It would not say what Corrigan had actually died saying, and Marguerite had decided, in his quiet and stubborn way, that this omission was a small crime, and that small crimes deserved small punishments—punishments like a pencil, a ledger, and a man willing to sit up all night listening.

"Mister Zhou."

The voice came from cot eleven, thin as the fog. Marguerite set down the basin he'd been carrying and crossed the ward, stepping over the raised board of the man in cot nine, who had a habit of sticking his feet out into the aisle like a tollgate.

Cot eleven held Sergeant Bramley, forty-one years old, shot through the lung in a skirmish whose name changed depending on which officer was telling it. He was dying, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it, because there are conversations that go better sideways.

"Sergeant."

"You writing?" Bramley's eyes flicked to Marguerite's breast pocket, where the stub of pencil lived. The soldiers had noticed it weeks ago. They always did. Men who are dying develop an eye for anything that might carry their voices past the walls.

"I'm writing," Marguerite said.

"Anything good?"

"There's a woman two streets over," Marguerite said, settling onto the stool beside the cot, "who waters her geraniums at midnight because she believes plants sleep better damp. There's a boy on Pier Three who charges sailors twopence apiece to tell them lies about sea monsters, and business is booming. And there is a cat at the tavern called Admiral who has been promoted twice this year for services to rat control."

Bramley laughed, which cost him, and Marguerite watched him pay the price without flinching, the way old soldiers pay for everything.

"You're a strange one, Mister Zhou."

"So I'm told. Frequently. By superiors."

"No." Bramley's hand came out from under the blanket and caught Marguerite's sleeve. His grip was still strong; the lung was weak but the hands held on. "I mean—you look at us. Properly, I mean. Most folk look through. You look at."

Marguerite said nothing. There was an art to receiving a compliment without letting it knock you off balance, and he had practiced it for years, mostly because compliments came so rarely that each one arrived unannounced, like a debt collector.

"My wife," Bramley said. "Ellen. If you're keeping—" he stopped, tried again. "When I go. Tell her the last thing I thought of wasn't the fighting. It was her bread. The burnt loaves. Sundays. She burns every third loaf and swears at the oven and I used to think, well, that's home, isn't it. That's the sound of home."

"Bread and swearing," Marguerite repeated, already composing it in his head. "Sundays."

"And tell her the oven's innocent. It was me leaving the door open to warm my boots. Every time. Confess it for me, she'll want to argue and she can't argue with a dead man, so you'll have to take her side."

"That," said Marguerite, "is the most dangerous assignment anyone has given me all year, and I once helped move a colonel's piano."

Bramley smiled and slid back down into the pillows, and within the hour the fever took him up again, and Marguerite moved on down the row with his basin, doing the ordinary work, the visible work, the work that kept him employed and therefore invisible.

* * *

Here is what people believed about Marguerite Zhou, in the order they usually believed it:

First, that he was foreign—not properly foreign, not interesting-foreign, but half-and-half foreign, a Chinese merchant's son with an English mother and a face that made officials squint at his papers a beat too long. Second, that he was quiet. Third, that he was harmless, which was the conclusion everyone reached after the second, and the conclusion he had spent thirty-one years polishing to a shine.

His father had taught him the creed without ever stating it outright, back in the years when the family still had a shipping office and then, more urgently, in the years after they didn't. *When the storm comes,* his father used to say, sorting receipts in a room that had once held cargo, *you cannot save the warehouse. Save what fits in your coat.* Marguerite had taken the lesson further than his father probably intended. What fit in your coat, he had decided, was not money or papers or even, strictly speaking, survival. What fit in your coat was other people's last words. Everything else got taken by storms. But a sentence—*tell Ellen the oven's innocent*—a sentence weighed nothing and survived everything, provided somebody carried it.

So he carried. And he wrote it down, because memory alone was a leaky bucket, and because ink had a way of making things true in a way that remembering didn't.

At half past ten, when the ward had gone quiet except for the breathing—that particular chorus of ragged breathing that fever wards perform, part wind, part water—he knelt by the third window, checked the aisle in both directions, pried up the floorboard, and took out the ledger.

He turned to the first empty page and wrote, in his small, even hand:

*Sergeant William Bramley, 2nd Rifles, age 41. Last words: "Tell my wife the burnt loaves were my fault. The oven is innocent. I left the door open for my boots."*

Then he sat back on his heels and looked at the page for a long moment.

A hundred and seven names now. Each one a man the reports had flattened into a line item. The army recorded deaths the way a bank records withdrawals: date, unit, cause, next of kin if known, effects forwarded where applicable. *Effects:* a pocketknife, a photograph, three shillings and a button. As though a life could be forwarded in a parcel. As though the button were the point.

Marguerite had stopped being angry about it somewhere around the fortieth name, because anger was heavy and he traveled light. What he felt instead was something quieter and harder to dislodge, the way a splinter works deeper than a knife wound. Defiance, if he'd had to name it. Though defiance suggested noise, banners, confrontation, and Marguerite had never been any of those things. His defiance was conducted entirely in lowercase.

An officer could march into Ward Three tomorrow and demand the records, and Marguerite would produce the official register, correct in every particular, and the officer would leave satisfied, having learned precisely nothing true. That was the trick of it. Invisibility wasn't hiding. Hiding was for fugitives. Invisibility was standing in plain sight holding exactly what everyone expected to see, while the real thing rode quietly in your coat.

He returned the ledger to its floorboard grave, pressed the wood down with his palm until the nail seated, and stood, brushing dust from his knees.

Which was when the screaming started from the private rooms at the end of the east corridor.

* * *

The private wing of the harbor hospital was a contradiction in terms—a place where officers came to be ill in comfort, with screens instead of curtains and wine instead of broth. Marguerite was not ordinarily permitted there. Orderlies belonged to the public wards; the private wing had its own staff, its own hierarchy, its own smell of beeswax instead of carbolic.

But rules bend at night, and especially in fog, and especially when a man is screaming hard enough to wake the entire corridor.

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had been brought in nine days ago, fevered, raving, some infection in a leg wound that the surgeons had opened twice and cursed at both times. Marguerite had seen him only at a distance, being fanned by a private nurse who looked perpetually on the verge of resigning. Now the nurse was gone—gone home, gone drunk, gone somewhere—and the colonel was thrashing against his sheets, calling names Marguerite didn't recognize in a voice cracking down the middle.

Marguerite went in, because someone had to, and because the night matron had gone to bed an hour ago with instructions that she was not, under any circumstances, to be disturbed before dawn.

"Colonel." He put his hands on the man's shoulders, firm, steady, the weight of them a language older than words. "You're in the hospital. You're safe. The fever's lying to you."

Ashworth's eyes opened—gray, sunken, burning—and fixed on Marguerite's face with terrible clarity, the clarity that fever sometimes grants in its final hours, like a candle flaring before it gutters out.

"You," the colonel rasped. "The Zhou boy."

Marguerite went very still. He was not, as far as he knew, *the Zhou boy* to anyone in this building. He was the orderly. He was a pair of hands. He was furniture that emptied bedpans.

"Sir?"

"They'll come for it." Ashworth's hand shot out from under the blanket and seized Marguerite's wrist with a strength that should not have existed in a body that wasted. From beneath the pillow—Marguerite hadn't known, hadn't guessed—the colonel drew a letter. Sealed. Heavy cream paper, closed with dark red wax, pressed with a signet ring Marguerite didn't recognize. "They're watching the house. Watching the lawyers. New Year's Eve—the estate passes at midnight, do you understand me, midnight, and everything goes to *them*, everything goes wrong unless—"

"Colonel, you mustn't—"

"It names the Zhus." Ashworth pulled him closer, close enough that Marguerite could feel the heat rolling off the sick man's skin, could see the map of veins in his temples. "Do you hear me? It names *your family*. Your father. Your father's name is in here, boy, and what they've done to it, and where the money was meant to go—it's all in here, and I am not going to live to deliver it."

The fog pressed at the windows. Down the corridor, a lamp guttered. Marguerite became aware, distantly, of his own heartbeat, loud and absurd, like a drum at a funeral.

"I don't understand," he said, which was the truth, and also the safest sentence in any language.

"Nobody ever does. That's how they get away with it." Ashworth pressed the letter flat against Marguerite's chest, over his heart, over the pocket where the pencil stub lived. His eyes were wet, whether with fever or something else, Marguerite couldn't say. "Harbor people. Harbor people move things. Ferries, crates, letters. Nobody watches a harbor man carrying a parcel. Please. Before the century turns. Before they erase—" His grip failed suddenly, all at once, like a rope cut, and his hand slid down Marguerite's apron and fell to the blanket.

For a long moment the only sounds were the colonel's ragged breathing and the fog ticking against the glass.

Marguerite looked down at the letter in his hands. Heavy cream paper. Dark wax. Nothing written on the outside. A book with no title, he thought absurdly. A book that invites no questions.

Except that this one had just invited every question there was, and answered none of them, and named his father—the disgraced, disowned, long-dead merchant whose name Marguerite had spent half his life learning to pronounce without flinching—in connection with an inheritance conspiracy reaching, if the colonel's ravings could be believed, toward the governor's own office.

He should burn it. That was the sensible course. Feed it to the stove, keep his head down, keep his name small, keep carrying only sentences and never parcels. Thirty-one years of careful invisibility whispered to him in his father's voice: *save what fits in your coat.*

But the letter already fit in his coat. That was the trouble.

Down the corridor, a door opened and closed. Footsteps—the night porter, making his rounds, unhurried, bored, oblivious. Marguerite slid the letter inside his shirt, against his chest, where it lay flat and warm and impossibly light.

Weighted nothing, he told himself. Weighed absolutely nothing at all.

He straightened the colonel's blankets, checked the fever with the back of his hand, and walked out into the corridor wearing the same blank, competent face he wore everywhere, the face that had kept him safe for thirty-one years.

It occurred to him, somewhere around the stairs, that the face was going to need to work considerably harder than it ever had before.

And it occurred to him, a moment after that, that he knew exactly one person with a boat—who happened to be a woman the authorities had stripped of her license, drinking at the tavern on Pier Three, and who owed him, as far as he knew, precisely nothing.

New Year's Eve was twelve days away.
