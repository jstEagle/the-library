## 2. Fog at the Third Pier

The requisition order for the fever ward read, in Captain Reyes's tidy hand: *Six crates laudanum substitute, two bales lint, one cask lime for the drains. Collect from the customs pier before noon.* It did not say *in the fog*, but everything in this harbor happened in the fog, so I suppose it went without saying.

I had made the supply run eleven times since taking the ward. Eleven times I had walked the mile from the hospital down Cooper's Hill with a handcart that squeaked on its left wheel, and eleven times I had passed the tavern at the third pier without going in. The Blue Anchor, it was called, though the sign showed an anchor painted a blue so faded it might have been gray, or regret, or simply old paint. A woman named Odile ran it—I knew her name the way I knew everyone's name in the district, from requisitions and gossip and the particular arithmetic of who owed whom—and I had never once spoken to her.

This was not shyness. I want to be clear about that, because people mistook it for shyness all my life, and shyness is a small affliction while mine was a policy. My father had been a merchant of the loud kind, a man whose voice filled rooms and whose name filled ledgers, right up until the year the ledgers turned against him and he learned what I had understood watching him: that a visible man can be found, and a man who can be found can be taken. So I had built myself into the shape of a person nobody took. Nurse Zhou, of the harbor hospital. Mixed, mild, meticulous. The kind of face clerks forgot while they were still looking at it.

It was, most days, a comfortable way to be invisible.

The fog that morning came in thick off the water, the sort that turned the piers into suggestions and made the gulls sound like they were laughing at something private. My handcart's left wheel squeaked its protest down the hill, and I counted crates at the customs shed—six, two, one, all present—and signed the chit with my careful, forgettable signature, and began the long squeak home.

I got as far as the third pier when the argument started.

You could hear it over the foghorn, which takes some doing. A man's voice, official and rising, and then a woman's voice, low and amused in a way that made the official voice rise higher. I would like to say I walked on. I had eleven years of walking on behind me. But the woman said something I didn't catch, and the man said *"revoked,"* very loudly, as though volume were a legal instrument, and I found my handcart stopping all on its own, the squeak dying mid-note.

Around the pilings of the third pier came a ferry boat—not a government ferry, one of the little coal-fired ones that used to run workers across to the mills before the military took the crossings—and tied up at her mooring like she owned the water. At her tiller stood a woman with forearms like a stevedore's and hair the color of wet iron, and she was looking up at the customs officer on the pier with an expression I can only describe as *patient contempt*, the way you might regard weather.

"I'm telling you for the last time, Brandt," the officer said, "your license is revoked. You're not licensed to carry passengers, freight, mail, or yourself, as far as I'm concerned. If I see this vessel move, I impound it."

"Then you'll be moving it yourself," the woman said. "Because if you think I'm going to row you anywhere, Pieter, you've been drinking Odile's gin, and it's gone to your sense of proportion."

"That's *Inspector* Voss to you."

"That's *Pieter* to me. I taught your brother to swim off this pier. You were there. You cried."

He cried? I thought, absurdly, and then caught myself—leaning, actually leaning, toward the conversation like a plant toward light. I made myself straighten. This was none of my business. This was precisely the sort of thing that was none of my business: authority, defiance, the public friction of people who had decided their names mattered more than their safety. My business was six crates, two bales, one cask, and getting them back up the hill before the ward needed me.

But I did not walk on. I have wondered since why I didn't. The best answer I have is that envy is a kind of gravity, and I had been circling it for years.

"You refused a lawful troop transport order," Inspector Voss said, falling back on the phrase like a man falling back on a wall. "That's not nothing, Brandt. That's the whole thing. There are regulations."

"There are," she agreed pleasantly. "And I read them. All of them, that winter, after they took my ticket. Do you know what I found? Regulations about tonnage. Regulations about ballast. Regulations about the proper storage of lamp oil." She stepped down off the gunwale onto the pier, and even through the fog I could see she did not lower her voice because she did not need to; the pier lowered itself around her. "Not one regulation that says what to do when the manifest says forty men and the boat holds thirty safely. Not one line about that, Pieter. So I drew one myself, in the only ink I've got, which is refusing."

"And now you run *laundry*," Voss said, with the particular disgust officials reserve for reduced circumstances.

"Now I run laundry," she agreed. "Clean sheets across to the fort every Thursday. Very subversive. Watch out for my pillowcases."

He blustered, threatened, wrote something in his book—the book! I thought, we all have our books, don't we, his and mine—and stamped off up the pier into the fog. And Ilsa Brandt stood a moment watching him go with something that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same house as one, and then she turned her head and looked directly at me.

I want it noted that I was standing beside a handcart holding a cask of lime, wearing a nurse's gray coat, entirely unremarkable, and yet she looked at me as though I had been announced.

"You need something, hospital man?" she called. "Or do you make a habit of listening to other people's court trials?"

"Just passing," I said. My voice came out steadier than I expected. "With the lime."

"With the lime," she repeated, and now the smile arrived properly. "Well. That's a man who knows what his business is. Mind the left wheel—it's been complaining since autumn and nobody listens to wheels in this town."

She went back to her boat. I stood there feeling, absurdly, *seen*, which was against my entire religion, and pushed on up the pier.

* * *

The trouble—or the gift, I still argue with myself about which—was that the route past the Blue Anchor was the only sane route uphill with a loaded cart, and the fog chose that hour to thicken from suggestion to soup. I came abreast of the tavern's door just as it opened and a tray of cups sailed out into the mist, followed by Odile herself, broad and quick, setting the tray on a barrel with the efficiency of a woman feeding an army.

"There's a whole crowd of dockmen standing in my doorway dripping," she said, to no one in particular, "because apparently the rain indoors isn't good enough for them," and then she saw me, and the cart, and the cask, and said, "You. Gray coat. Get in here out of that before you turn into a drain yourself. The lime will keep."

"I really can't—" I began.

"You can and you will. One cup. It's coffee," she added, in the tone of a woman who had learned to specify, "the real kind, or near enough. You look like a man who counts things, so count this as one cup, owed to nobody."

There was no graceful refusal. I have since learned there is no graceful refusal of Odile; there is only delay. I wheeled the cart to the shelter of the tavern's eaves and went inside, and the warmth hit me like a held breath let out.

The Blue Anchor's front room was low-ceilinged and smelled of woodsmoke, tar, and coffee, and it held perhaps nine dockmen, two customs clerks pretending not to be on duty, a dog of no fixed opinions, and, in the corner by the stove, drying her iron hair like a cat that had decided the fire belonged to her, Ilsa Brandt.

"The nurse," Odile announced, as though introducing a new species. "He's having coffee whether he likes it or not."

"He's having coffee," Ilsa agreed, without opening her eyes. "Everyone does eventually. It's the anchor's true cargo."

Odile set the cup before me—thick, dark, honest—and looked at me a moment with the assessing eye of a woman who had spent twenty years deciding which strangers were problems and which were merely tired. Whatever she concluded, she kept it, and moved off to scold the dockmen about their boots.

I drank the coffee. It was, as promised, near enough. Around me the room conducted its morning business: rates and rumors, the price of coal, somebody's cousin lost overboard in November and somebody else's cousin promoted to sergeant, which the room treated as roughly equal misfortunes. Nobody lowered their voices. Nobody glanced at the door when it opened. These were people who said what they thought where anyone could hear it, and I sat among them feeling the way I imagine a man feels swimming under ice—fascinated, warmed even, and aware of the thickness between himself and air.

"You're the one from the fever ward," Ilsa said, eyes still closed. "Up the hill. They say you sit with the dying ones when nobody else will."

"They say a lot of things."

"Do you?"

I considered lying, for form's sake, and found I couldn't be bothered, or couldn't manage it, or whatever it is when a policy fails quietly. "Yes," I said. "Someone should."

"Mm." She opened one eye, which was gray as her hair and missed nothing. "My mother did that work. In Kiel, during the last war. She said the ones who sit with the dying end up knowing more about people than any priest alive." The eye closed again. "She also said it makes you quiet. Quiet as a ledger. Are you quiet as a ledger, nurse?"

"Quieter," I said, before I could stop myself, and Odile laughed from across the room as though she'd heard it—which, knowing Odile, she had—and Ilsa smiled her not-quite smile, and I drank my coffee and understood that I had just told these women something true, and that neither of them seemed inclined to use it against me, and that this was such a foreign sensation I hardly knew where to put it.

"Reyes was in here yesterday," one of the dockmen said to Odile, apropos of nothing. "The captain. Asking after boats."

"Asking after boats," Odile repeated, in a flat tone that managed to contain an entire archive of opinion. "In this harbor. Where there are boats. Shocking."

"Saying as how there's a parcel needs finding, come New Year's. Something to do with probate." The dockman shrugged. "Reward mentioned. Big one."

The word *probate* went through the room like a draft, and I watched faces close around it—watched Odile's jaw set, watched Ilsa's both eyes open now, watchful, and I filed it the way I filed everything: carefully, invisibly, against a day I might need it. At the time it meant nothing to me. Remember that. Remember that I heard the words *parcel* and *probate* and *New Year's Eve* and thought only, *how strange, someone's money troubles,* and finished my coffee, and thanked Odile, and refused her second cup, and went out into the fog pushing my cask of lime.

* * *

Halfway up Cooper's Hill the left wheel gave up its complaint entirely and cracked, and the cart lurched, and the cask slid, and I saved it by catching it against my chest with both arms, which is how I came to be standing in the fog hugging sixty pounds of drain lime like a child, breathing hard, thinking about boats.

Specifically thinking about what Ilsa Brandt had said: *not one regulation about what to do when the manifest says forty men and the boat holds thirty.* She had drawn her own line. She had paid for it—her license, her living, her Thursdays of clean sheets—and she stood on her revoked boat in the revoked fog and made jokes about pillowcases to a man with a stamp-book. And Odile, who had heard a reward mentioned for God knew what and had answered with *shocking*, as though menace were merely bad weather that had come in without wiping its boots.

They refused to disappear, those two. That was the whole of it, and it was the most extraordinary thing I had seen in years. Not brave in the storybook way—brave the way stone is stone, just structurally unwilling to pretend otherwise. And I, who had made an art of being overlooked, stood hugging my lime and felt the ugly honest thing rise in me, the thing I am admitting now only because this account has stopped being able to afford lies:

I envied them.

Not their courage. Their *ease*. The ease of people whose names fit them, who could say *Pieter* to an inspector and *get in here* to a stranger and mean exactly that and nothing hidden underneath. I had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being forgotten, and it had kept me safe the way a cellar keeps things safe, which is to say in the dark and at the cost of ever being used for anything.

I reloaded the cart, limped it uphill on three wheels and stubbornness, delivered the lime, signed the chit, hung up my gray coat, and went down to the ward for the evening shift.

Private Corrigan was worse. I sat with him through the small hours, his fever climbing, his hands plucking at the blanket, and near dawn he surfaced long enough to grip my sleeve and ask—again, the same question every night—whether I'd gotten his letter written to his girl, his Nell, whether I promised, whether I swore it. I promised. I swore it. He sank back, murmuring her name like a man counting his way down to sleep, and I sat in the lamplight with the ward breathing around me and reached beneath the loose floorboard by my chair, and took out my ledger, and turned to page forty.

I wrote: *Pvt. T. Corrigan, 2nd Fusiliers. Said: "Tell my Nell her father kept every one of her drawings. Every single one. Tell her I counted."*

Counted. Like crates. Like cups of coffee owed to nobody. I put the ledger away, and damped the lamp, and thought, with Corrigan's breath rattling gently beside me, about how many kinds of counting there were in the world, and which of them lasted.

It was two nights later that Colonel Ashworth was carried into my ward, delirious, burning, clutching at his coat like a man protecting his chest from the cold—and it was two nights later still, in the last gray hour of a December morning, that he pressed a sealed letter into my hands and whispered a word that undid eleven careful years in a single breath.

He whispered, "*Zhou.*"

But that letter belongs to the next chapter, and the fog, that morning, was lifting off the third pier as though it already knew.
