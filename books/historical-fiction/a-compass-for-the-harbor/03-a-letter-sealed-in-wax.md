## 3. A Letter Sealed in Wax

The fever ward at night was a country of its own.

Marguerite had learned to read it the way sailors read weather — by the sound of breathing. There was the easy tide of men who would wake tomorrow and complain about the porridge; there was the shallow, careful breathing of men rationing their strength like coins they suspected were counterfeit; and there was a third kind, rarer and worse, that came in long silences followed by small, startled gasps, as though each breath had to be remembered rather than taken.

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had crossed from the second kind into the third sometime after eleven.

Marguerite made his rounds by lamplight turned low, because the ward surgeon insisted that dying men deserved darkness as much as anyone else, though Marguerite privately suspected the surgeon simply didn't want to see their faces while he failed them. He paused at the colonel's bed near the window, where the fog pressed against the glass like an old dog asking to be let in.

The colonel had been brought in nine days earlier with a wound in his side that should have killed him in four hours and instead had settled in for a longer argument. He was sixty-one years old, silver at the temples, with the sort of jaw that suggested a man accustomed to being disagreed with and winning anyway. He had been, by all accounts, a decent officer — which is to say, he had filled out his reports honestly and had once, famously, refused to falsify a casualty count even when it made him look careless. The men liked him. The men who could still speak, that is.

"Water," the colonel said, without opening his eyes.

Marguerite raised him gently and held the tin cup to his lips. The drinking took a long time and most of what he managed ran down into his beard.

"You're the quiet one," the colonel said when it was done.

"I'm the one holding the cup, sir."

"That's not what I mean." His eyes opened then, and Marguerite saw that the fever had burned something away in them — not the mind itself, but the furniture of the mind: rank, dignity, the habit of command. What remained looked younger than sixty-one. It looked, Marguerite thought, like a boy who had been sent somewhere terrible and never entirely come home. "You're the one they don't see. I've watched you. Three nights now. You move through this ward like smoke."

"I'll take that as a compliment, sir."

"It wasn't one." The colonel's hand came up from under the blanket — trembling, yes, but with a grip still left in it — and closed around Marguerite's wrist with surprising authority. "Smoke goes where it pleases and leaves no trace. That's a way to survive. It's not a way to live."

"Most nights, sir, surviving is living enough."

"Most nights." The colonel smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and knowing, the smile of a man reading a map of a country Marguerite hadn't admitted to belonging to. "Wait until you meet the night that isn't most nights."

* * *

The fever climbed through the small hours.

Dr. Voss came once, looked at the colonel's pulse, looked at Marguerite, and shook his head in the particular way doctors have that means *I have done everything medicine permits and I will now go do something easier*. Marguerite sponged the old man's forehead, changed the dressing on the wound — angry red, weeping, the flesh giving up its fight with the patience of a garrison surrendering — and wrote nothing in the ledger, because the colonel was not dead yet, and Marguerite's rule was absolute on that point. Names went into the ledger only when the last word had actually been spoken. He did not presume. presumption, he felt, was the official report's disease, and he would not catch it.

Toward three o'clock the colonel began to talk.

Delirium talk, mostly — orders to men twenty years dead, corrections to a map no one else could see, once, oddly, a long complaint about the quality of the marmalade at some mess hall in a place called Kandahar, delivered with such specific grievance that Marguerite had to press his lips together to keep from smiling. That was the thing about dying men. They shed their ranks like coats and underneath they were all just people with opinions about marmalade.

Then, near four, the colonel surfaced all at once — clear-eyed, sudden, like a man coming up from deep water — and said:

"Nurse."

"Sergeant-nurse," Marguerite corrected mildly. "But close enough."

"The Zhus."

Marguerite's hands went still over the basin.

Of all the words in all the languages the fog could carry, those were not ones he expected. *Zhou* — his father's name, worn down by clerks and customs officers into *Chew*, *Zoo*, *Joe*, until his mother had finally surrendered and put *Zhou* in parentheses on every document, like a footnote apologizing for itself. Nobody in this harbor knew. Nobody needed to. He had spent thirty-one years making sure of it. A nurse named Marguerite Sandoval — his mother's name, borrowed whole, a coat that fit well enough if you never took it off in public.

"Sir?" he said, keeping his voice level. "Did you say—"

"The Zhus." The colonel's eyes fixed on him with terrible focus, the fever burning low and bright now, a lamp running out of oil. "Merchants. Canton. My father traded with the grandfather — tea, silk, honest weight, both sides. Good people. Better than my family ever—" He coughed, wet and deep. "There's an estate. Harrowgate. It isn't going to the Zhus. Do you understand me? It's supposed to. It was promised. Before the century turns, before the papers are signed — they mean to bury it. The governor's office. They mean to bury it with everything else."

"Sir, you're feverish. You should rest."

"I am resting," the colonel said, with such indignation that Marguerite nearly laughed. "This *is* rest. You should hear what I'm like when I'm working." He struggled up onto one elbow, and Marguerite moved instinctively to help him, and in that moment — in the tangle of blanket and arm and lamplight — the colonel's other hand found the breast of Marguerite's uniform and gripped it, and pulled, and said, very clearly, in the voice of a man giving an order he intended to have obeyed:

"There's a letter. Inside the case. Under the false bottom, left side. Get it."

* * *

The colonel's effects sat in a wooden campaign case beneath his bed, the kind officers were permitted and privates were not, containing — according to the inventory Marguerite had helped compile — two shirts, a Bible, a shaving kit, a medal in a velvet box, and a bundle of correspondence awaiting return to family.

Marguerite knelt beside it in the dark and felt, distinctly, the sensation of standing at the edge of a high place.

He knew what was in the case. He'd inventoried it himself. There was no false bottom listed. There was no false bottom, he'd have sworn, at all — and yet when he lifted the tray of folded shirts his fingers found the seam along the left wall, a join so fine he had missed it twice already, and the panel came away with a soft click that sounded, in the sleeping ward, like a door unlocking somewhere far away.

Inside lay a letter.

It was sealed in wax — real wax, dark red, pressed with a signet ring he didn't recognize: a bird of some kind, wings spread, over crossed anchors. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, and someone had written across the front in a firm, failing hand:

*To be opened only by the Probate Court of the Harbor District, or by the hand I trust.*

No name. No address. No instruction about whose hand that might be.

"Colonel," Marguerite whispered, turning back to the bed. "Who do I give this to?"

But the colonel had sunk again, eyes rolling back beneath fluttering lids, mouth working through fragments — *the governor*, *before New Year's*, *they'll burn it, they'll burn it all* — and when Marguerite leaned close, the old man's hand rose one last time, found the letter in Marguerite's grip, and pressed Marguerite's own fingers closed around it, deliberately, the way you'd close a child's hand around something precious and tell him *don't drop it*.

"Yours," the colonel breathed. "Until the court. Yours."

And then his eyes rolled shut and his breathing went back to the third kind, the remembered kind, and Marguerite stood alone in the dark between beds holding a piece of sealed paper that weighed almost nothing at all.

That was the strangest part, he thought later. How little it weighed. Less than a bandage roll. Less than a spoon. And yet his knees had gone soft, and the ward seemed to tilt around him, and for one unsteady moment Marguerite Zhou — invisible man, keeper of other people's last words, a person constructed entirely out of things nobody bothered to notice — understood that he was now carrying the single most dangerous object in the harbor district.

Everyone wanted something. He'd known that forever; it was why invisibility worked. But everyone wanting something meant that whoever held the thing wanted became either a servant or a corpse, and Marguerite had built his whole careful life on being neither.

And now here it was, in his hand. Something everyone would want. The estate, the Zhus, the governor's office — words stacking up like crates about to topple.

*The Zhus.* His family. His father's people, disowned and scattered, written out of the merchant ledgers when the trading houses collapsed — written out so thoroughly that Marguerite himself had never been sure how much of the story was memory and how much was his mother's soft-spoken grief. And now a dying colonel was handing him a wax seal and telling him the erasure wasn't finished. That there was more to take.

The fog pressed at the window. Down the ward, a man moaned in his sleep and was quiet again.

Marguerite did what he always did with things too large to hold in the open. He tucked the letter inside his coat, against his chest, flat over his heart, where it sat like a second heartbeat. Then he checked the colonel's pulse, adjusted the blanket, trimmed the lamp, and made his rounds — steady hands, lowered eyes, the whole performance of a man who was nobody.

But walking back down the ward, he caught his reflection in the dark glass of the window, and for the first time in years he didn't quite recognize it. The reflection looked, he thought, like a man standing at the edge of a high place in a strong wind — the wind not yet blowing, the edge not yet crumbled, but the moment balanced on a knife's width either way.

*Before the century turns,* the colonel had said.

New Year's Eve was nineteen days away.

At dawn, Marguerite knew, the paperwork would begin. Officers would arrive with clipboards. Captain Reyes of the garrison provost — punctual, gray-eyed, tireless — would come for the colonel's effects, as regulations required, including *any correspondence*, as regulations also required. Every regulation Marguerite had spent his life obeying would now require him to hand over the letter or lie.

He stood a moment longer at the foot of the colonel's bed, watching the old man breathe his remembered breaths, and thought: *I have never lied in my life. Not once. It was the whole architecture.*

Outside, the first gray light found the fog and began, hopelessly, to argue with it.

Somewhere across the harbor, a bell rang five o'clock, and Marguerite put his hand flat against his chest, over the letter, and waited for morning to come for him.
