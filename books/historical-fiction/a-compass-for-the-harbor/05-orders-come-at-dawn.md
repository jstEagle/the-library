## 5. Orders Come at Dawn

The letter had spent the night against my chest, inside my shirt, where a man might carry a second heartbeat.

I had not slept so much as listened to myself not sleeping — the ward breathing around me in its ragged chorus, the fog pressing its grey face against the high windows, and beneath all of it the small dry sound of my own conscience turning over like a man in a strange bed. Colonel Ashworth had died at ten minutes past four in the morning. I know because I was holding his wrist when it stopped, and because I wrote the time in my ledger before I could think better of it, and because the ledger does not lie even when I am about to learn that I can.

His last words had come an hour earlier, delivered with the peculiar clarity the dying reserve for their most inconvenient statements: *"Tell them the Zhus were owed. Tell them it's all in the letter."*

I had assumed, in my vanity, that he meant some other family named Zhou. The harbor is full of Zhus; we are like sparrows, everywhere and unnoticed. But there is only one family he could have meant, and it was mine — my father's people, disowned twice over: once by the merchant guilds who found my grandfather's shipping ledgers inconvenient during the embargo years, and once by my father himself, who married an Englishwoman and signed his name out of every register that mattered. The Zhou name survived in our kitchen and nowhere else. It was a name kept the way one keeps seeds in a drawer — not planted, but not thrown away either.

Now a colonel had pressed a sealed envelope against my sternum with the last strength in his hands and made me a courier to a history I had been carefully not belonging to for thirty-one years.

* * *

Dawn came the way orders do: without asking whether you were ready.

Sister Aloysius woke me where I'd dozed in the linen closet, which was less a bed than an apology for one. "Gentleman downstairs," she said, in the tone she reserved for gentlemen, which was the same tone she used for weather and other unavoidable miseries. "Army. Says he's expected."

Nobody had expected him. That was precisely what made him dangerous.

I went down with my collar straightened and my face arranged into the mild, competent blankness that had served me through six years of war wards and three winters of this fever harbor — the face of a man whose papers are always in order because his life contains nothing requiring order. The face of a man no one stops.

Captain Reyes stood in the entrance hall as though the building had been built around him and he had merely permitted it. He was perhaps forty-five, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who have carried things heavier than luggage, with a moustache that had clearly outlived several of his opinions. His uniform was immaculate but his boots were not — river mud, dried white at the seams, the mud of the lower road. He had come fast, or often, or both.

"Marguerite Zhou," he read from a paper. Not asked. Read. "Nurse, third class. Attending physician on Colonel Ashworth?"

"Attending nurse," I said. "Dr. Pemberton attends. I attend more frequently."

Something moved behind his eyes — not amusement exactly, but a relative of it. "You attend more frequently. Yes." He folded the paper away. "I'm here for the colonel's effects. All of them."

"Of course. His kit came up from the transport yesterday. There wasn't much. Men travelling light travel lighter by the end."

"I'll see it."

I led him back through the ward, past the rows of men who watched us go with the flat attention of soldiers assessing officers — counting exits, counting weapons, counting whether this visit meant good news or bad. Captain Reyes walked as though the ward were a corridor built for his convenience, and yet I noticed he stepped around Private Dunmore's crutch without looking down, and that he paused, just half a breath, at the cot where Ashworth lay already stripped and sheeted. Half a breath. Then he moved on.

The effects were laid out in the side room where we kept such things: a kit bag, a dressing case, a Bible with a cracked spine, a pocket watch that had stopped — I checked later — at 4:11, seven minutes after its owner. And correspondence, or the lack of it, sitting in a shallow tray like an accusation waiting to be filled.

Reyes went through everything with the thoroughness of a man reading scripture backwards. He turned the Bible upside down and fanned it. He weighed the watch in his palm. He opened the dressing case and counted the bottles against some list he kept in his head. Then he came to the tray.

"Correspondence," he said.

"Nothing personal arrived," I said. "There are two supply requisitions he countersigned, if those count. They're filed with the ward clerk."

"Letters written. Letters received. Anything in his pockets when he came in."

"Pockets?" I said, and here is the thing about lying: I had imagined, in the small hours, that it would be difficult. That my careful invisible life would resist it the way clean hands resist dirt. Instead I heard my own voice produce the word *pockets?* with exactly the right weight of honest puzzlement, and understood with a cold sliding feeling that I had been rehearsing dishonesty all my life without knowing it. Every time I had smoothed my face into nothing. Every time I had made myself unremarkable. I had been practicing.

"He would have had pockets," Reyes said patiently. "Coat. Trousers. Men carry things."

"The coat was cut off him at the door — he came in bleeding from the transport accident, you'll have the report — anything in it went into the property bag." I fetched the bag, a canvas sack tied with a numbered tag, and poured it onto the table with the confidence of a man with nothing to hide and the heart rate of a man with everything to hide: a broken pipe stem, three buttons, a folding knife, a coin, a stub of pencil worn round at the end.

Reyes looked at the little heap for a long time. Somewhere above us a soldier began coughing in the wet racking rhythm that means the winter will keep him or take him.

"No letters," Reyes said.

"Not among his effects."

He looked up at me then, fully, and I learned something important about Captain Reyes: he did not glare. Glaring is for men who need to frighten you. He simply looked, the way an auditor looks, the way fog looks, patient and total, and let you do the frightening yourself. "Colonel Ashworth was a correspondent," he said. "Thirty years of service. He wrote to everyone. He wrote to me twice a month, and I once heard him describe a man he disliked as 'not worth a letter.' Everyone was worth a letter to Ashworth. Are you telling me he crossed the water, took a splintered mast to the ribs, and arrived at your hospital carrying nothing worth writing on?"

What I said next I said slowly, and I have never been sure afterward whether it was courage or cowardice dressed in courage's coat.

"He may have written," I said. "Men write here constantly. Letters get posted from the ward every day — the chaplain collects them Thursdays. If he posted something, it's gone, and I couldn't tell you what was in it. I don't read the mail, Captain. I barely have time to read the thermometer."

The lie was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was smooth and plausible and it used the truth the way a smuggler uses a legitimate crate — the chaplain really does collect the post on Thursdays; I really don't read the mail. The best lies are load-bearing walls built out of true bricks.

Reyes considered the Thursday collection. I watched him consider it, watched him weigh how long the post takes, where it goes, whether it could be intercepted, and I saw the exact moment he concluded that chasing a dead man's letter through the imperial mails was more trouble than his orders required. Orders. That word again.

"Very well." He repacked the property bag himself, neatly, squaring the corners. At the door he paused. "You've been at the hospital long, Mr. Zhou?"

"Six years."

"And before that?"

"Before that I was somewhere else." I smiled the smile of a man with an uninteresting biography. "It's not a story that improves in the telling."

"No," Reyes agreed. "Most stories don't." He settled his cap. "For the record, Mr. Zhou — the colonel was well regarded. There are people who will want his affairs closed cleanly. Tidily. You understand tidy."

"Tidy," I said, "is the whole of nursing."

He left. The door clicked. I stood in the side room among the smell of carbolic and dead man's possessions and discovered that my hands were shaking so badly I had to grip the table edge, and that I was, absurdly, smiling.

I had lied. To an officer. To *that* officer. And the world had continued rotating; the fog had stayed fog; the coughing upstairs had continued its terrible arithmetic. The sky had not fallen. Which meant the sky could be gambled with.

* * *

I climbed to the ward and sat a while with the ledger under my hands, feeling the floorboard's familiar looseness beneath my boot heel, thinking about checkpoints.

Because that was the thought that had arrived sometime during Reyes's audit, cold and complete, the way bad thoughts arrive — not gradually but all at once, like ice forming.

Three checkpoints between this harbor and the probate court in the city. The customs house at the bridge. The garrison gate at Millbrook. The fort at the river mouth. I had passed through them a hundred times on supply runs, showing my hospital chit, watching soldiers wave through coal and flour and coffin wood while they opened other men's bags with bored violence. I had always thought of them as the government's habit, its way of touching everything it owned.

But standing in that side room, I understood. They weren't habits. They were arguments. They were the physical form of the sentence *nothing leaves this province without permission.* Someone had arranged the roads of an entire coastline so that a certain kind of paper could never move without being seen — and now I knew why, knew it in the shape of the wax seal hidden against my chest. The checkpoints hadn't been built yesterday. They had been built for years, patiently, the way a trap is built before the animal is even born. Built for exactly this moment. Built for a letter like this one, should one ever exist.

And built, too, for a man like me. Because a checkpoint does not stop parcels. Parcels can be sewn into coats, baked into bread, dropped from bridges into boats. What a checkpoint stops is a *person* — a person with a face, a name, papers, a history that can be checked against lists. The system was designed on the assumption that everyone is visible, and I had built my whole life on the counter-assumption that nobody looks at nurses. Two philosophies, about to be tested against each other on a bridge in December.

Here is what else I understood, sitting there with my shaking hands: staying invisible had never actually protected anyone. It had only protected me. All those names in my ledger — Corrigan, and the boy from the artillery company, and old Sergeant Mbeke who sang to himself when the morphine loosened him — they had died visible to no one but me, their last words filed under a loose floorboard while official reports recorded them as *died of wounds, effects forwarded.* My invisibility had made me a good archivist of other people's erasures and a poor defender against them. Ashworth had seen that in me, I think — seen the ledger-keeper and known a fellow clerk of the overlooked when he met one. Why else press a dying testament into the hands of a third-class nurse instead of a chaplain, an officer, a friend?

Because a chaplain could be questioned. An officer could be bought. A friend could be traced. But a man no one sees — a man could carry.

Except I had just, in front of God and Captain Reyes, stopped being entirely a man no one sees. Reyes would remember me. Not much — a mild nurse with orderly papers — but a thread, and threads pull.

I lifted the floorboard. The ledger sat in its nest of dust and old bandage wrappers, and beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the letter with its broken seal — because yes, I confess it, I had opened it in the small hours, alone in this same room, steaming it soft over the lamp the way my father once showed me merchants' clerks do, and I had read what the dead colonel had actually set down. But that reading belongs to another chapter of this account; suffice to say the names in it rang bells I had been ringing myself for years, in my own smaller church, page after page.

I put the ledger away. The letter I kept against my chest, where it had begun to feel less like contraband and more like an organ — something I would now die without.

Below, through the window, I could see the harbor: grey water, grey fog, and threaded through both the dark line of the piers, where the ferries came and went and, I happened to know, where a woman named Brandt kept a boat that officially existed nowhere at all. A woman who had refused to carry troops, lost her license for it, and reportedly laughed while signing the revocation papers. A woman who had looked authority in the face and declined to disappear.

I had watched her from a distance the way one watches birds one cannot identify — with interest, with envy, and with no plan whatsoever.

The plan, apparently, was arriving on its own. Because the letter had to reach the probate court before midnight on New Year's Eve, when the estate transferred and the ink dried forever, and between here and there lay three checkpoints, one captain with river mud on his boots, and a growing certainty in my chest that the careful life was over — that it had ended, in fact, at ten minutes past four that morning, when a colonel's pulse stopped and mine, in every sense that mattered, started.

I buttoned my coat over the letter, took my supply requisitions off their nail — the oldest trick, the truest crate — and went out into the fog, toward the third pier, to ask a disgraced ferry pilot for the impossible.
