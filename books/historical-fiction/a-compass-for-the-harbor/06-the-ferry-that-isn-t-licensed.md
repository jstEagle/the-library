## 6. The Ferry That Isn't Licensed

Ilsa Brandt had three rules about her boat, and she recited them to anyone who set foot on the *Magpie* whether they asked or not. Rule one: no guns aboard, not even for show. Rule two: no questions about cargo that didn't smell like anything in particular. Rule three: if you were going to be sick, do it over the side and downwind.

She had invented the rules two years ago, when they'd stripped her license and left her nothing but the boat and her own stubbornness. They'd been meant as a joke—a way of laughing at a world that had taken her wings and handed back a ferry route nobody wanted. But jokes had a way of turning into scripture, and by now she defended all three rules with the fervor of a woman defending her mother's grave.

The trouble was that rules only worked on people who cared about rules. And the man standing at the end of the fourth pier on a Tuesday morning so gray it seemed the sky had given up halfway through rising, well—he looked like a man who kept rules the way other men kept saints' days: faithfully, quietly, and without any expectation of being rewarded for it.

"I know you," Ilsa said, coiling rope because her hands hated idleness. "You're the nurse from the fever ward. Zhou. You came in last spring with the coal barge casualties and splinted a man's leg on my deck while everyone else stood around wringing their hands."

"His name was Aldous Wren," Marguerite said. "Private. He wrote to his sister every Sunday until the day he couldn't."

Ilsa's hands stopped on the coil. That was not what people usually said about her deck. People said *thank you* or *that was quick thinking* or *what a shame about your license, Miss Brandt*, which was the polite way of asking how it felt to fall. Nobody ever said a name.

"Well," she said, recovering. "He didn't die on my deck, at least. Small mercies. What brings the hospital's best hands to the fourth pier? We don't have casualties here, only hangovers, and those belong to Odile."

Marguerite Zhou did something then that surprised her. He looked over both shoulders—actually looked, like a man in a bad play about spies—and said, "Could we speak aboard?"

"No guns, no questions about cargo, and sickness goes downwind," Ilsa recited. "Those are the rules."

"That seems reasonable."

"They're not negotiable."

"I wasn't negotiating." A small smile, quickly suppressed, as though he'd caught himself spending money he didn't have. "I don't carry a gun. The cargo is a question I'll get to. And I'm told I have an iron stomach."

"Everyone says that before the swell past Millbrook." She dropped the gangplank with a bang that scattered gulls. "Come aboard, then. Mind the third plank. It has opinions."

---

The *Magpie* was forty feet of bad decisions held together by good carpentry. Her hull had been patched in four places with wood that didn't match, her cabin roof leaked in exactly one spot—Ilsa had moved her cot twice to keep ahead of it—and her engine was a German marine plant that ran beautifully as long as you talked to it in a respectful tone and never, ever called it by a number. Ilsa had named it Fritz. Fritz had never let her down. More than she could say for the harbor commission.

Marguerite sat in the cabin on the bench across from her stove, and Ilsa noticed things about him the way she noticed things about weather. His coat was too thin for December. His hands were steady but held themselves carefully, like instruments put away properly after use. And he hadn't yet asked her about the license, which put him in a category of perhaps four living people.

"Tea?" she offered. "It's the black kind that could dissolve a nail, but it's hot."

"Yes, please."

She poured two chipped mugs and sat opposite him, waiting. Waiting was a skill. She'd learned it flying mail runs upriver in the old days, when fog closed in and there was nothing to do but hold altitude and trust the river to be where the map promised. Most men filled silence the way ballast fills a hull. Marguerite Zhou did not fill it. He simply sat inside it, holding his mug with both hands, and let it be.

Which meant she had to break it herself, and she resented that immediately.

"You want me to carry something," she said. "Upstream. Before New Year's Eve."

He blinked. "How—"

"Nobody visits the fourth pier in December for conversation. Either you owe Odile money or you need the unlicensed boat. Your collar is clean, so it isn't debt." She sipped her tea. "What's the parcel?"

"A letter."

"To whom?"

"I can't say."

Ilsa set down her mug. "Rule two cuts both ways, Mr. Zhou. I don't ask about cargo—but I also don't carry cargo that gets boats burned. There's a difference between not knowing and not being told. Which is this?"

For the first time since coming aboard, the man looked uncertain. Good, she thought. He should be. Everyone should be, a little, on the water.

"It's a testament," he said at last. "A dead man's will. It names heirs that certain people would prefer had never been born. If it doesn't reach the probate court before midnight on the thirty-first, the estate passes to the men who arranged things otherwise, and the real heirs—people already robbed of nearly everything—lose the last thing owed them."

"What sort of certain people?"

"The sort with checkpoints."

Ilsa laughed, short and sharp. "There are three between here and the probate office, and every one of them knows my face. Do you know why they know my face? Two years ago the garrison ordered me to run troops upriver for a punitive action against the villages at Corley Bend. I refused. I'd seen what punitive actions meant—the last one came back with prisoners, and I heard what happened at the dock. So they took my license, and they fined me everything I had, and they told the commission I was unfit. Now I ferry workers and fish crates because a pilot without a license can't refuse anything official, because officially I don't exist as a pilot at all." She spread her hands. "You've come to the wrong boat, Mr. Zhou. I'm precisely the person who cannot help you."

"I haven't offered you money yet."

"Keep it that way. Money makes it a transaction, and transactions can be bought back. I've watched men take garrison coin and spend a decade trying not to remember what it purchased."

Something shifted in his face—not disappointment, quite. Recognition. As though she'd confirmed something he'd hoped was untrue.

"Then I'll go," he said, standing.

"Sit down." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out with the same authority as the rules, and he sat, and she swore under her breath because now she'd have to think of something better than refusal, and thinking had never been her strongest current. "Finish your tea first. It's a long walk back and the wind's turned."

He sat. He drank his tea. The stove ticked. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanged its slow, patient argument with the tide.

And then, because she had nothing else, Ilsa asked the question she'd sworn off asking strangers. "Why does it matter to you? The letter. You're a nurse. You patch men and move on—that's the whole trade, or the wise ones make it so. Why carry a dead colonel's paper through three checkpoints for heirs you've probably never met?"

Marguerite looked into his mug for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—still quiet, but with weight behind it now, like a door opening onto a full room.

"Because I keep a ledger," he said. "Of the men who die in my ward. Their names, their last words, the things the official reports leave out. Private Corrigan, who died asking whether anyone had fed his dog. Sergeant Mbeki, who spent his final hour teaching me a song in a language I've half forgotten. Lieutenant Farrow, who apologized to me—for what, he never finished saying. Forty pages of them, maybe more. The army writes down dates and regiments and cause of death. I write down that they were people." He paused. "Your name might be in it."

"My—" She frowned. "My name? I'm not dead, Mr. Zhou."

"Not yours. Brandt. A soldier. Corporal Ansel Brandt."

The cabin went very still. Even Fritz, out in the cold beyond the bulkhead, seemed to hold its exhaust.

"Ansel," Ilsa said. The name came out of her like something that had been sitting in her chest for eleven years with nowhere to go. "Ansel Brandt. What did he die of?"

"Wounds taken at Harrowgate. Infection followed. He was in my ward nine days." Marguerite set down his mug and reached into his coat—an act she tracked with sudden, irrational alarm, as if he were drawing a weapon—and brought out a book. Not a fine book. A working ledger, corners soft with handling, string tied around it. He untied the string. "He talked about a sister. A pilot. He said—" The nurse hesitated, reading his own handwriting by the dim light through the porthole. "He said his sister flew the mail boat and could land her on a road if the road held still long enough."

Ilsa discovered she was gripping the edge of the bench hard enough to hurt her palms.

"He said that," she managed.

"He said a great deal, those nine days. Most of it about you. The last thing he said—" Marguerite stopped. "I don't share last words freely. They belong to the dying, and I only lend them out to family."

"He didn't have any family left except me."

"Then I suppose that makes you the heir." He turned the ledger around and slid it across the bench, open to a page near the middle, and turned his head away to give her privacy—the courtesy of it nearly undid her.

The entry was written in a small, exact hand. *Corporal Ansel Brandt, 3rd Rifles.* Date. Regiment. Cause. And beneath, in lines that took up more room than the facts required, the words:

*Said his sister could land a mail boat on a road if the road held still. Asked whether she still flew. Told him yes. He smiled and said, "She always lands true, even when everything else doesn't." Spoke of her till the end. Wanted her to know the river was calm where he was going.*

Ilsa read it three times. Then a fourth, because the third time her eyes had blurred and she wanted to be sure of the last line.

"The river was calm," she repeated. Her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she let it. There was no one aboard but a stranger and a stove and a dead man's handwriting, and none of them would laugh. "They told me he died at some fortification. No details. No one ever gives you details. You write to the records office and they send back a form with a box marked *deceased* and that's supposed to be enough. Eleven years I thought—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles against her mouth, mastered herself. "Eleven years I thought he died alone and unmourned in some ditch, and nobody even bothered to lie to me about it kindly."

"He didn't die unmourned," Marguerite said quietly. "He died in a clean bed with someone holding his hand. I was holding his hand. For whatever that's worth, eleven years late."

"It's worth," Ilsa said, and had to start again. "It's worth more than you know."

She pushed the ledger back toward him. He took it, retied the string with fingers that moved slowly now, as if the book weighed more than paper had any right to weigh.

"All right," she said.

He looked up. "All right?"

"All right, I'll carry your letter. Not for money—you were right not to offer it, and I'd have thrown you off the boat if you had. And not for the heirs, whoever they are; I'm sure they're deserving, but deserving is thin soup." She jabbed a finger at the ledger. "For that. Because Ansel Brandt deserved better than a box marked deceased, and every man in that book deserves better than erasure, and if your letter does what you say it does—if it stops somebody else from getting a box instead of a truth—then Fritz and I will run it upriver if we have to tow it ourselves."

"Miss Brandt—"

"Ilsa."

"Ilsa." He said her name carefully, as though setting down something fragile. "You should understand what you're agreeing to. Captain Reyes has men searching the harbor district already. There are checkpoints at the bridge, the Millbrook gate, and the fort, and each one wants papers neither of us can produce. If they catch us, you lose more than a license. They'll make sure you lose the boat."

"Let them try." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she'd used at the garrison dock two years ago when she'd said *no* to men with the power to end her, and she found she wasn't sorry to hear it again. "Mr. Zhou, I've spent two years learning what losing everything costs, and I'll tell you the secret: it costs less than you fear. The morning after they took my license, I woke up, and I was still me, and Fritz still started, and the river was still where the map promised. Everything else was paperwork." She leaned forward. "But you—you're walking around carrying forty pages of the only truth those men ever got, and you're doing it alone, and you look like a man who thinks that's noble. It isn't. It's just lonely. So here's my rule four: nobody carries anything alone aboard the *Magpie*. Not cargo. Not grief. Nothing."

Marguerite stared at her. And then something happened to his face—it loosened, all at once, like a knot cut rather than untied, and for a moment he looked younger and more frightened and more relieved than any grown man she'd ever seen. He didn't cry. But he looked like a man who had just discovered crying was permitted and had decided, barely, against it.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet. Wait until you've met the Millbrook patrols." Ilsa stood and began pulling charts from the shelf above the bunk, needing her hands busy, needing the familiar rustle of paper. "Now. Three checkpoints, you said. Bridge customs house is impossible with my papers—they flagged the *Magpie* there in October over an invoice dispute, and Inspector Hargreaves holds grudges the way other men hold whiskey. Millbrook doubles patrols after dark but the water route past the millrace—"

"The millrace?" He'd gone slightly pale. "Isn't that the stretch where the current—"

"The millrace is perfectly navigable."

"At night."

"Navigable means navigable, Mr. Zhou."

"In fog."

"Fog is a pilot's friend. Fog means nobody sees you." She smoothed the chart flat and weighted its corners with two mugs and a tin of biscuits. "Besides, you said yourself the letter weighs nothing."

Marguerite looked at the chart, at the narrow inked channel threading between the mill shallows and the garrison lights, and then at her, and Ilsa watched him do the arithmetic of trust—watched him measure a stranger against a deadline and find the stranger heavier.

"When?" he asked finally. "When do we run it?"

Ilsa grinned, and it felt like hoisting sail after two years at anchor. "New Year's Eve, naturally. If we're going to be fools, we may as well be fools while the whole harbor's drunk and the fort's busy with the governor's fireworks." She tapped the chart at the river mouth, where the fort squatted in its little printed square like a fact no one could argue with. "We'll want provisions, a cover story, and a place to lie low the night before, somewhere Reyes's men don't look. Do you know anywhere?"

"I—" He hesitated in a new way, a way that suggested a name he wasn't sure he was allowed to say yet. "There's a tavern. The Anchor's Rest. The widow who keeps it—Odile—she's known me for years. I think she'd shelter us."

"Odile's." Ilsa snorted. "Half the garrison drinks at Odile's. That's not a hiding place, that's a lamp."

"That's exactly why it works," Marguerite said, and there—faintly, cautiously, like sun through fog—was that small smile again, the one that spent money it didn't have. "Nobody searches the tavern where they drink."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, this careful, quiet man who kept the dead alive in a string-tied book, and decided that whatever else happened on the thirty-first, she did not regret her answer.

"Fine," she said. "Odile's it is. But you're telling her, not me. I owe that woman eleven shillings and an apology about her cousin, and I'd rather face the millrace twice."

* * *

They parted at the head of the fourth pier, where the fog had begun to lift and the harbor showed itself in patches: masts, rooftops, the gray line of the bridge with its customs house crouched beneath it like a toll-collecting troll.

"One more thing," Ilsa said, as he turned to go. "That entry. Ansel's. The part about the river being calm where he was going. Did he really say that? Or did you write it for me, to close an old wound?"

Marguerite considered the question with the seriousness he seemed to bring to everything, as if honesty were a ration to be portioned precisely.

"He said the river was calm," he answered. "Where he was going, he didn't say. I chose the rest of the sentence the way you choose words for anyone you're losing—to leave them somewhere gentle." He met her eyes. "Is that a lie, Ilsa?"

She thought about it the whole walk home, past the shuttered fish stalls and the gulls arguing over nothing, and by the time she reached her door she had her answer, and it was no.

Three days later, when she knocked at the back door of the Anchor's Rest and found Marguerite Zhou already inside with Odile's arms crossed and a question forming, the plan was ready—or as ready as any plan is, that rests its whole weight on a fog, a race of white water, and a book of names.

What none of them knew—what the fog itself did not yet know—was that Captain Reyes had posted a reward that very afternoon, and that one of Odile's regulars had read it twice.
