## 7. Three Checkpoints, Counted

The hospital kitchen at four in the morning was the only room in the building where a man could think without being asked to think for someone else. Marguerite had learned this years ago, when he was still new enough to apologize for taking his tea at the scrubbed table where no one else sat. The stove kept a low coal-fire going all night; the kettle had a crack that made it whistle in two keys; and the single window looked out over the gray water toward the bridge, which meant that from this chair, with this cup, a man could watch the whole machinery of the harbor wake up and pretend he was merely observing it.

He spread three sheets of paper on the table and weighted their corners with the salt cellar, the sugar tin, and his own cold hand.

On the first sheet he drew the river.

It was not a beautiful river map. His father had been the draftsman of the family—those merchant Zhou hands could render a cargo manifest like an altarpiece—but Marguerite had inherited only the patience and none of the gift. Still, the line of it came easily enough: the harbor spreading wide at the bottom of the page, narrowing past the piers, threading under the great iron span of the bridge, then bending east through the mill country before it escaped into the hills where, according to everyone who claimed to know, the water ran clean and nobody asked for papers.

Between the harbor and that imagined cleanliness stood three obstacles, and he marked each one with a small, careful X, because his whole life had taught him that the difference between surviving and not surviving was usually a matter of counting correctly.

*One,* he wrote. *The customs house at the bridge.*

Everyone called it the Bridge, with the definite article, as if there were no other bridge in the world worth the name, and in a sense there wasn't. The customs house squatted at its landward foot like a toad that had won an argument with an architect. Every vessel moving upriver passed beneath its windows; every crate, every barrel, every parcel larger than a hymnal was subject to inspection, and the inspectors were paid partly in wages and partly in the pleasure of exercising small powers over large men. Papers were required. Manifests were required. A reason was required, and the reason had to be boring. Boring was the customs house's native language, and Marguerite spoke it fluently—a nurse, a supply run, nothing to declare but lint and laudanum, sir, and a good morning to you too.

But a sealed letter naming the Zhou family did not qualify as boring. A sealed letter naming the Zhou family was precisely the kind of thing the customs house existed to find.

He drew a second X further upriver. *Two. The garrison gate at Millbrook.*

Millbrook was less a town than an argument between a mill and a barracks, and the barracks had won. The garrison gate crossed the river road at its narrowest point, and since the troubles two winters past—the troubles which had filled forty pages of his ledger and eleven pages more besides—the gate had grown teeth. Sentries. A barrier. A sergeant with a ledger of his own, though Marguerite doubted it recorded last words. It recorded faces. He had stood in that queue twice, delivering wounded men upcountry, and both times the sergeant had looked at him the way men look at a coin they suspect is clipped: not quite refusing it, but turning it over.

A Chinese mother's eyes under an English father's jaw. A uniform that belonged to no regiment they recognized. Papers in order, always, but papers only went so far when what a man really wanted was an explanation, and there was no document in the Empire titled *Explanation for Being What You Are.*

*Three,* he wrote. *The fort at the river mouth.*

And here his pen hesitated, because the third checkpoint was not like the others. The customs house wanted contraband. The garrison wanted deserters. But the fort—Harrow Fort, named for the same family whose inheritance was currently being stolen with wax seals and probate clerks—the fort wanted nothing so ordinary. It was the governor's winter residence, and on New Year's Eve its gates would be sealed for the ceremony, and the ceremony would include, if the colonel's whispers had been true, the very men who meant for the estate to pass into the wrong hands before the century turned and took the paperwork's memory with it.

The letter had to reach the probate court by midnight on the thirty-first. Not the thirty-second. There was no thirty-second. The century was a door closing, and documents left on the wrong side of it had a way of never having existed at all. Marguerite knew exactly how things stopped existing. He kept a record of it under a floorboard.

He set down the pen and drank his tea, which had gone the temperature of the river, and looked at his map with the particular honesty that four in the morning makes cheap.

Three checkpoints. Each requiring papers he could not forge—not because he lacked the skill, but because forging was the one crime that would undo everything else. A forged paper discovered at any gate would turn the letter from evidence into smuggler's goods, and Marguerite himself from courier into criminal, and the names in the ledger into the fantasies of a man with a criminal's imagination. No. The papers had to be real, or the route had to be empty of papers altogether, and he could see no version of the second option that ended anywhere but at the bottom of the river or the top of a gallows.

Which left the first option, and the first option required him to be something he had spent thirty-one years training himself never to be: visible. Credible. *Accounted for.* A nurse with legitimate business, vouched for, remembered—which was the opposite of invisible—and remembered by men who might, within the week, be asked whether they'd seen him.

He took out the letter then, because he had promised himself he would stop doing this and had broken the promise every hour since the colonel died, and weighed it in his palm.

It weighed nothing. That was the obscene part. Six pages, folded twice, sealed in wax that had already been broken once and re-sealed with a candle stub and a steady hand—he was not proud of the forgery of that seal, but pride was a luxury item and he had never once been able to afford it. Six pages of a dead man's testament, redirecting the Harrowgate estate away from the governor's cousin and toward families who had never seen a lawyer, and it weighed less than the salt cellar. Less than his tea. He could have slipped it into a boot and forgotten it was there.

Instead it sat in his chest like a second heart, beating out of time with the first.

That was the thing about carrying, he thought. The physical weight was never the weight. He had carried dying men down ward stairs—men heavier than the doorframe was wide—and felt nothing afterward but his knees complaining. But carry a name, a promise, six pages of other people's justice, and the body invented mass for it. Shoulders stooped. Sleep fled. The kettle whistled its two broken notes and he flinched.

*Measure the journey in what can be carried.* His father's saying, from the merchant years, before the disowning, before the slow fade of the Zhou trading house into other men's pockets. Papa Zhou had said it about cargo, about debts, about grief. You cannot carry the ship. Carry the compass. You cannot carry the house. Carry the key. You cannot carry the dead. Carry—

"Talking to yourself again, Mr. Zhou?"

Marguerite's hand closed over the papers a full second before his head came up. Sister Agnes stood in the doorway with a shawl over her nightdress and a candle, sixty-eight years old and capable of materializing anywhere in the hospital without sound, a talent he suspected she practiced.

"The heating," he said. "I was checking the heating."

"At that table." She looked at the salt cellar, the sugar tin, the hand now flattened over hidden pages. "The heating is very fond of you."

"It's mutual."

She came across the kitchen, poured herself the dregs of his teapot without asking, which was her way of asking, and settled into the opposite chair. Her eyes went to the window, to the bridge, faintly orange under its lamps in the pre-dawn fog.

"My sister's boy tried to run the bridge once," she said conversationally. "Smuggling tobacco. Nineteen years old. The customs men found it in the false bottom of a cheese wheel, which I have always thought showed a certain ambition." She sipped. "They were kind to him because his father was a churchwarden. They will not be kind to you."

"I haven't the faintest idea what you mean."

"Mm." She looked at him over the rim of the cup with the mild, terrible patience of a woman who had heard every deathbed confession in the district and found most of them repetitive. "Colonel Ashworth died holding your wrist, the captain has been asking after his correspondence with the persistence of a debt collector, and you have taken to sitting alone in kitchens drawing maps at four in the morning. I am a nurse, Mr. Zhou. Diagnosis is the trade."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a patient coughed twice and was quiet.

"I can't tell you," Marguerite said finally. It cost him something to say it, and he watched her see that it cost him.

"Good," said Sister Agnes. "Don't. Men who confide in old women get them arrested." She rose, tucked the shawl closer, and paused at the door with her candle throwing her shadow enormous up the wall. "But I'll tell you what I told my sister's boy, free of charge, since you're determined to learn it the hard way. Three gates is two too many. Whatever you're carrying—" her eyes flicked, deliberately, to the table, "—find the man who doesn't use gates. There's always one. The river remembers when the roads forget."

Then she was gone, and Marguerite sat alone with his map and the wreckage of his invisibility, because if Sister Agnes knew, then knowing had become a contagious condition, and contagions spread.

* * *

He spent the day being unremarkable on purpose, which was harder work than it sounded. He changed dressings. He wrote the morning's names into the official register in his best bureaucratic hand, leaving off, as always, the words the men had actually said—*tell my girl the dog knows the trick, tell her anyway*—and entering instead *died peacefully, attended.* At noon Captain Reyes's clerk came by with questions shaped like courtesy, and Marguerite answered them with boredom shaped like truth, and hated how good he had gotten at it, and understood with a cold clarity that the goodness was now load-bearing. Everything he built from here would stand on the lie he'd told at dawn. One crack and the whole wall came down on Nell—no. Not yet. On everyone. On everyone.

In the afternoon fog he walked the piers, ostensibly to check a supply consignment, actually to count.

The customs house first, from the far side of the road. He counted sentries (two), inspectors on shift (four, one of whom owed the hospital for treating his hemorrhoids, a debt of gratitude with a strictly limited exchange rate), and the interval of the patrol along the bridge footpath (eleven minutes, sloppy on the hour when the shift changed). He committed it all to the ledger of his memory, the one no floorboard held.

Then the ferry offices, shuttered, and the notice board beside them, where a government placard announced in three languages the revocation of pilot licenses for unauthorized refusal of military conveyance, and beneath the placard, nailed up by some anonymous hand with a hammer and a sense of humor, a smaller card: *BRANDT FERRY — STILL FLOATING — ASK AT THE TAVERN.*

Marguerite stood looking at that little card longer than a man checking a supply consignment ought to.

*Find the man who doesn't use gates.* Or the woman, said the card, who used a boat, and had already proven—by losing her license rather than carry soldiers to a slaughter she could see coming—that she was constitutionally incapable of the careful invisibility that had been Marguerite's entire religion. She refused authority in public. She had opinions in daylight. She was, by every measure he owned, the most dangerous person on the waterfront, and she kept her boat at the third pier, and Odile's tavern sat between the pier and the customs road like a thumb placed deliberately on the scale.

He measured the journey one last time, standing in the fog with the harbor breathing around him. Upriver to the probate court: nineteen miles by road, three gates, papers he couldn't forge, four days if nothing went wrong, and New Year's Eve five days off, which meant the margin for catastrophe was a single day, and catastrophe was famously greedy.

Or by water, past the first gate entirely, under the bridge in the dark, through the customs house's blind spot where the river ran fast and the inspectors' lanterns gave up—

He couldn't do it alone. That was the arithmetic underneath all the other arithmetic, and he had known it since four in the morning and had spent twelve hours trying to unknow it. He could not carry this alone. The letter weighed nothing and everything, and everything was not a thing one back could hold.

Marguerite folded his map into his coat, touched the shape of the letter through the fabric the way other men touched saints' medals, and turned toward the light spilling amber out of the tavern windows, where a widow poured and a pilot with a revoked license was almost certainly saying something unforgivable about the governor.

He had never in his life asked anyone for help with something that mattered.

He walked toward the tavern door, rehearsing, and got no further than her name.
