## 8. Odile's Back Room

Odile Marchand had a theory about men who came in from the fog: you could tell everything you needed to know about them by how they took their first drink. The soldiers drank like they were owed it. The dockmen drank like it was a negotiation. The ones running from something drank slow, with one eye on the door, and those were the ones she watched.

Marguerite Zhou did not drink at all. He sat at the end of her bar nearest the window, where the fog outside could be monitored without seeming to be monitored, and he asked for tea in the apologetic tone of a man requesting something scandalous. Then he counted out his coins exactly, down to the half-penny, and arranged them in a little row on the counter as though presenting evidence.

"Tea," she said. "In a tavern. At nine o'clock of a December night."

"If it's no trouble."

"Everything is trouble. That's what a tavern is for." She filled the kettle and set it on the stove, and while her back was to him she watched him in the mirror behind the bottles — the same cracked old mirror that had watched twenty years of smugglers, deserters, union men, and one archbishop who'd gotten lost on his way to a christening. Marguerite sat very straight, hands folded, like a man at an interview he hadn't applied for. His coat was buttoned wrong. One button too high, pulling at the collar. A man who dressed in the dark or dressed in a hurry, and either way, a man not sleeping.

She'd known him by sight for two years — the hospital nurse who came in once a month, always alone, always ordered the stew and never the beer, always left before the singing started. She'd made up stories about him the way she made up stories about everyone, because a widow who kept a tavern kept a whole parish of imaginary neighbors in her head. Her working theory had been *lonely*. Revised tonight, seeing the wrong button and the fog-glances: *hunted*.

The kettle sang. She poured, slid the cup across, and said, "Back room's got a bed in it. Straw mattress, but I aired it Tuesday."

He looked up so fast the tea nearly went over. "I didn't ask—"

"No. You sat there looking like a man who'd forgotten how to ask." She wiped the counter in front of him, though it was already clean. "Ilsa sent word an hour ago. Said you'd come in wet around the edges and dry around the eyes, and that I should feed you and ask nothing."

"That is — remarkably close to what happened."

"Ilsa Brandt is many things, most of them illegal, but she is not vague." Odile leaned her hip against the bar. Upstairs, her lodger Mrs. Pellowe was dragging furniture around in the manner of a woman who cleaned loudly on principle, and the fire needed another log, and there were seven glasses to wash, and none of it would get done faster by worrying. "Stew first. Questions after. And by questions I mean mine, which will be few, and yours, which I suspect are enormous."

He wrapped both hands around the cup like it was January inside his bones, which, she reflected, it possibly was.

"You're being very kind," he said, with the wariness of a man who had learned that kindness usually arrived carrying an invoice.

"My husband used to say kindness is just stubbornness with its collar turned up," Odile said. "Eat your stew."

* * *

The back room had been built, in some earlier century, as a cellar storeroom for casks, and when the brewery stopped delivering by waterway, Odile's husband Luc had walled off the far corner and put a bed behind it. He'd been proud of that bed. He'd lain on it himself once, to test it, and declared it fit for kings and other criminals, and Odile had laughed until she'd had to sit down on the steps.

That was before the ledger. Before the paper that arrived one gray morning saying Private Luc Marchand had died of fever at the garrison, date uncertain, effects unclaimed, next of kin — and here the clerk's pen had simply stopped, as if the sentence itself had caught the fever. No pension. No body. No record, ultimately, that Luc Marchand had ever drawn breath, because somewhere between the fort and the colonial office, a name had been mislaid, and a mislaid name was a drowned name, and a drowned name left a widow holding a tavern deed and a silence.

She had spent the first year writing letters. She had spent the second year receiving replies written by clerks who had clearly never read her originals. By the third year she understood that the ledger of the empire had a particular kind of arithmetic: a man with no name was worth nothing, and grief with no paperwork weighed less than fog.

By the fourth year she was hiding people.

It started small — a boy who'd jumped ship, a woman whose husband beat her, a printer whose press printed the wrong pamphlet in front of the wrong constable. Word traveled the way damp does, through cracks, and found her. She never advertised. She never asked what they'd done. She fed them, aired the straw, woke them before dawn, and sent them out through the coal chute that Luc had bricked over on the outside and left cleverly loose on the inside, because Luc had always believed in exits the way other men believed in saints.

Twenty years. She'd stopped counting the guests somewhere around forty.

Now she stood in the doorway of the back room with a lamp, watching Marguerite Zhou inspect the bed frame the way he probably inspected everything — thoroughly, quietly, and with visible apology.

"The wall's newer than the rest of the house," he observed.

"Luc's work."

"He was a carpenter?"

"He was a private," Odile said. "Which is to say, he could build anything and be paid for none of it." She set the lamp on the crate that served as a table. "There's a bolt on the inside of this door. It wouldn't stop a determined man, but it would stop a hurried one, and hurried men don't come through my kitchen unless I let them. If anyone knocks who isn't me or Nell Corrigan, you go down the chute and follow the tunnel to the boathouse. Ilsa knows the boathouse."

"Nell Corrigan," he repeated, carefully.

"Private Corrigan's girl. You'll have heard of him, I expect — Ilsa says he was under your care." Something crossed the nurse's face then, quick as a fish under ice, and Odile catalogued it and moved on. She'd been reading faces across a bar for thirty years; this one had *ledger* written all over it, whatever a ledger meant to him. "She sleeps upstairs when her aunt's drunk enough to forget she's supposed to be minding her. Which is most nights, God forgive me for saying it."

"Mrs. Marchand—"

"Odile."

"Odile." He said it like a word in a foreign language he wanted to pronounce correctly. "Why are you doing this? For me, I mean. We've spoken perhaps six times in two years. You know nothing about me except what Ilsa told you, and Ilsa has known me three days."

Odile pulled the stool from under the crate and sat, because this answer deserved sitting down, and because her knees had opinions about long explanations.

"I'll tell you about a Tuesday," she said. "Nineteen years ago, give or take. A man knocked on my kitchen door at two in the morning, soaked through, with a broken wrist and a price on his head I could have paid off my mortgage with. Never knew what he'd done — striking, maybe, or worse. He stayed four days. On the third day he fixed my oven door, which had been hanging crooked since before Luc shipped out, and he taught me a song his mother sang in Portuguese, and when he left he tried to pay me and I wouldn't take it, and he cried into his hat on my doorstep like a boy." She looked at the lamp flame instead of at Marguerite, because some things were easier said to fire. "Nobody had ever thanked me like that. Not for the hiding — for the *believing*. He walked in expecting to be turned in, because everyone he'd met for a year had turned him in or wanted to, and I just — didn't. Do you understand? It cost me nothing. That was the revelation of my life. Twenty years of being told that safety means closing doors, and it turns out a closed door is just a wall you've agreed to."

Marguerite had gone very still. It occurred to her that stillness might be his native language.

"And Luc?" he asked softly.

"Ah." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile real. "Luc comes into it backwards. I started this for strangers. Somewhere in year five I understood I was really doing it for him — for the version of Luc the papers erased. Every man behind that wall is one the empire misplaced, and every one of them makes Luc Marchand slightly more real. The clerks wrote him out." She tapped the floorboards with her heel, right over the hidden bed. "I write him back in. Different ink. Same ledger, near enough."

"The same work," Marguerite murmured, almost to himself.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. Or — something I'm only now understanding." He looked at her then, directly, and up close his eyes were the exhausted brown of old tea, and much younger than the rest of him. "You've been doing this twenty years. Alone?"

"Ha!" The laugh escaped before she could polish it. "No. Nobody does this alone, whatever they tell themselves. There's Ilsa on the water. There's Father Anselm, who loses documents professionally. There's the customs man at Millbrook who owes me for his gout. And there's every soul who ever slept behind that wall and passed the word along to someone else who needed a wall." She leaned forward. "That's what I want to say to you, nurse, and I'll only say it once, because I'm a busy woman. Whatever you're carrying — and it's written all over you the way the wrong button is written all over your coat — you've been carrying it like it's yours alone. Like carrying is your job and asking for help would be cheating."

His hand had drifted, without his noticing, to his breast pocket. She pretended not to see.

"A man taught me otherwise once," she went on. "My Luc. He used to say — and he said it about everything, about hauling casks, about the war, about me refusing to sell the tavern when times were thin — he said: *nothing heavy stays heavy when two people hold it, and nothing held by two ever hits the ground.* Terrible poetry. Excellent engineering." 

"Your husband sounds—" Marguerite stopped. Started again. "You speak of him in the present tense."

"Do I?" Odile considered this. "I suppose I do. Dead's a fact about a man. Present tense is a choice about him." She rose, knees cracking like green wood, and collected the empty bowl. "Belonging's the same way. Everyone thinks it's inherited, like a title or a nose. Comes to you from blood, or country, or a name on a register. Rubbish, the lot of it. Belonging is practiced. Daily. Like scales, or kneading dough. Some mornings you do it badly. You do it anyway."

"And if you've had no one to practice with?"

"Then you start late and you're clumsy and everyone laughs at you kindly." She paused in the doorway, lamp in hand, and gave him the look she usually reserved for drunkards attempting stairs. "You're starting late. Accept it. Now sleep. I wake guests at five, and I make terrible coffee, and neither fact is negotiable."

* * *

He did not sleep. She knew because she checked twice — once at eleven, ostensibly to bring blankets, and saw through the cracked door a man sitting upright on a straw mattress, holding a sealed envelope against the lamplight like a jeweler assessing a stone; and once at two, when she heard, very faintly, the sound of a page turning, and then another, and then a voice barely above breath, reading names.

She stood in the dark of the cellar stair with her hand flat against the cold stone and listened to a stranger give the dead roll call, and thought: *there it is. Another kind of ledger.*

She thought: *the empire keeps its books and I keep mine, and the question has never been who writes, but who reads aloud.*

She thought, with a sudden fierce tenderness that embarrassed her even alone in the dark: *this one will get himself killed unless somebody teaches him to be held.*

At five she brought coffee, as promised, terrible as promised. He was dressed, coat rebuttoned correctly this time — someone had noticed, then — and he took the cup with both hands and said, "Thank you, Odile," and she heard it land differently than it would have yesterday. Not a customer's thank-you. A family word.

"There's bread on the crate," she said gruffly. "And a thing I should tell you before you go out my door, since going out doors seems to be dangerous business for you lately." She set down her own cup. "Reyes's men were at the customs house yesterday asking after a parcel moving upriver. Not your name — they don't have your name yet, or I'd not say it in this room at all. They asked after *nurses*. Plural." She watched that settle into him. "So. Two roads. Ilsa takes you tonight, quiet, and you're a rumor by morning. Or—"

"Or I finish what the colonel started."

"Or that." She studied him a moment — the straight spine, the wrong-then-right buttons, the envelope-shaped shadow riding against his chest. Twenty years of reading men at doors, and this one, she thought, was standing at one. "Then hear the last thing I'll teach you, since you're determined to practice belonging in the hardest classroom there is. When they corner you — and they will, corners are all the empire builds — don't reach for your papers. Papers are their language. Reach for the names. Names are ours."

He frowned. "The names?"

"You'll know the moment," Odile said, and picked up the cups, and paused at the door with the ghost of Luc's grin on her mouth. "And if the moment doesn't come by New Year's Eve, come back here and I'll tell you the story of the archbishop and the coal chute. It's better with gestures."

She was halfway up the stairs, laughing at herself, when the knock came at the front door — three raps, unhurried, official — and through the frosted pane of the tavern glass, in the gray not-yet-dawn, she saw the brass glint of a garrison cap.

Behind her, below, the cellar door clicked softly shut.
