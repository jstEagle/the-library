## 9. The Girl from Page Forty

There is a particular arithmetic to grief that no one teaches you in nursing school. I had learned it instead in the fever ward, one column at a time: how a man's last words weigh less than a button but more than his kit; how a name written in pencil can outlast a monument in marble; and how a promise made to a dying man becomes, without your consent, a debt with your name on it.

Private Declan Corrigan owed me nothing. I owed him a letter.

He had dictated it over three nights in October, when the fever came and went like a tide with opinions, and I had copied it out fair in my best hand because he wanted it "to look official, Nurse, so she'll know I meant it." His daughter, Nell. Nine years old at his last counting, which had been two winters prior. Lodged — the word *lodged* carried all its terrible freight — with an aunt in Salt Lane who had, by Corrigan's own account, "a heart like a rent book."

Page forty of my ledger holds four entries above his and eleven below, and his is the longest, because he would not stop talking, and I would not ask him to. *Tell Nell the harbor isn't a place, it's a direction,* he had said. *You face into it and walk. That's all a compass ever was.*

I had copied that too.

* * *

Salt Lane announced itself before I reached it. The fog that lay over the harbor district like a wet wool blanket thinned somewhere around the tanneries, as though even the weather had standards, and what emerged was a street built entirely of things other people had discarded: shutters, barrels, opinions. A woman beating a rug on a second-floor rail gave me the look that a man in a nurse's gray coat gets in poor neighborhoods — half hope he's come to help, half certainty he's come to take something away.

Number nineteen was a narrow house leaning against its neighbor for support, the way old drunks do. The aunt answered the door herself, a broad woman with flour on her forearms and an expression suggesting I was either late or early, and either way at fault.

"Zhou," I said. "From the harbor hospital. I've come about Nell Corrigan. Her father—"

"I know whose father." She did not invite me in. She stepped out instead, pulling the door to behind her with the practiced discretion of someone hiding something that was not, strictly speaking, hideable. From inside came a sound I knew from the ward: a cough with weight behind it, small lungs working hard. "If you've brought paperwork, you can leave it. If you've come to say he's dead official-like, the girl already knows. Children always know. They count the letters that don't come."

"She knows?"

"She knows." The woman's voice softened by perhaps a degree, which I later understood was, for her, an act of extravagant tenderness. "She sits at that window every evening like a lighthouse in reverse. Sending him out instead of calling him in." She wiped her hands on her apron, leaving the flour exactly where it had been. "You can see her a minute. But there's soldiers been asking after her this week, so make it quick."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with December.

"Asking after her? What soldiers?"

"The kind that ask after girls whose fathers die owing the Crown money." She shrugged, and the shrug said everything her voice would not: *I am a woman with flour on my arms and no weapons except this door.* "Debts don't die with the debtor, Mr. Zhou. They get reassigned. Like everything else."

* * *

Nell Corrigan was sitting at the window of a room the size of a confessional, and she turned when I came up the stairs, and I confess I stopped on the landing like a man who has walked into a door he didn't see.

She had Corrigan's eyes exactly — the same gray-green of harbor water in March — set in a small sharp face that had clearly already learned to measure visitors and sort them into piles. I watched her do it. Useful, probably; dangerous, maybe; mine, certainly not, and yet some traitorous part of my chest filed her under *keep* before my better judgment could intervene.

"You're not a priest," she said. It was not a guess. It was a ruling.

"No."

"You're not army. Army men stand different." She tilted her head. "You stand like you're trying to take up less of the room."

It was such an accurate description of my entire life that I nearly sat down on the stairs. "I'm a nurse," I managed. "From the harbor hospital. Your father and I were—" *Friends* would have been a lie; we had known each other nine days. "He asked me to bring you something."

I took out the letter. Fair copy, sealed with a plain drop of wax, weighing precisely nothing. I had carried it eleven days in my breast pocket, and in that time it had somehow acquired the mass of a paving stone.

Nell looked at it and did not reach for it. That was the part I had not prepared for. In the ward, Corrigan had been certain she'd want it; I had built a whole scene in my head on his certainty — the girl seizing the letter, the girl weeping, the tidy transaction of grief. Instead she folded her arms, nine years old and armored to the teeth, and said:

"Is it true what he wrote? About the compass thing?"

"The harbor being a direction?" I said. "Yes. He was very firm on the point. He made me repeat it back to him twice, like a catechism."

Something in her shoulders let go, a fraction, the way rigging eases when the wind shifts. She took the letter then — carefully, by both edges, the way you handle something that might be hot — and broke the seal with her thumbnail.

I looked out the window while she read. There wasn't much else to do; watching a child read her dead father's handwriting felt like a privacy I hadn't earned. Below, Salt Lane went about its commerce of salvaged things. Somewhere a hand-cart wheel squeaked its one note. And somewhere beyond the lane, presumably, soldiers were asking after orphaned girls, which was the thought I kept trying to put down and kept picking back up, the way you worry a loose tooth.

"He says I should learn letters," Nell said at last. Her voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices go when they're holding a great deal very still. "He says writing things down is how you fight back when people lie. He says—" She stopped. Read again. "He says *the nurse with the ledger will teach you, if you ask him proper.*"

"He said that," I repeated.

"It's in here." She held the page up as evidence, as though I might dispute the existence of my own handwriting. "*Ask him proper,* underline, underline. He underlined it twice."

Corrigan, I remembered, had been an underliner even while dying. He had made me go back and darken his strokes when the pencil faded. At the time I had found it endearing. At present I found it entrapment.

"Nell," I said, as gently as I knew how, which was apparently gentler than I knew, given how quickly she narrowed her eyes, "your aunt tells me soldiers have been asking after you."

"They came Tuesday." No drama in it. Inventory. "A sergeant and another one. Aunt Marta told them I was at my other aunt's. I haven't got an other aunt." A pause, and then, with the terrible practicality of children: "I think I'm worth money to somebody. Or trouble. Aunt Marta says those are mostly the same thing."

Downstairs, the street door opened and closed, boots on the lower stair, and Marta's voice rose in a register I had not heard from her — bright, deferential, terrified under the brightness.

"—yes, yes, she's within, Sergeant, only the girl's poorly, if it could wait till—"

It could not wait. It never waits; that is practically the motto of the workhouse board. I had perhaps forty seconds, and in forty seconds I did something I had never done in my careful, invisible, floorboard-hidden life.

I lied to the King's representatives.

* * *

The sergeant who filled Marta's kitchen doorway had a face like a closed ledger — red tape where the margin should be — and behind him a younger soldier doing his best impression of a wall. On the table between us sat Marta's teapot, deployed as diplomacy, steaming with the desperation of a signal fire.

"The girl," the sergeant said, not sitting. "Corrigan's brat. Board of guardians requisitioned her Tuesday last. There's forms."

"There are," I agreed pleasantly, and heard myself continue in a tone I did not recognize, crisp as fresh linen: "Though I'm afraid you've had a wasted walk, Sergeant. The child was removed from this household eight days ago under hospital authority. Orphan of a fever-dead soldier, infectious contact in the household — you'll find it all in the sanitary record. We don't leave typhus exposure in private lodging. It's the law."

It was none of that. There was no sanitary record. There was no law quite shaped like the one I had just invented. Nell had coughed once, days ago, in a room I was standing in, and my traitor mouth had spun it into a quarantine.

The sergeant looked at me. He had the particular squint of a man doing sums he suspects will embarrass him.

"And you'd be?"

"Marguerite Zhou. Ward nurse, harbor hospital." I produced my credentials — actual, honest, paid-for-in-credibility credentials — and laid them on the table between the teapot and his forms. "You're welcome to verify at the sanitarium office. They'll want the date and the ward number. I can write them out."

I wrote them out. I wrote them out in my best fair hand, the hand Corrigan had trusted with his last words, and every letter of it was fiction wearing a uniform of truth. The sergeant read, squinted harder, and visibly decided that typhus was a problem belonging firmly to some other sergeant.

"Fever ward's full of paperwork as it is," he muttered, gathering his forms. "If this comes back wrong, nurse, it's your name on it."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

That, at least, was honest. He left. The younger soldier left. Marta sat down abruptly on a stool as though her strings had been cut, and pressed the teapot to her sternum like an infant.

Above us, on the stairs, Nell Corrigan was sitting on the seventh step, where she had clearly stationed herself to hear everything, holding her father's letter against her chest with both arms.

"You stood different," she said.

"What?"

"When you talked to him. You didn't try to take up less of the room." She considered me from beneath the fall of her hair, and there was Corrigan looking out of her face, frank and unhurried, a man counting the letters that don't come. "What happens now? Because they'll come back, won't they. With better forms."

Out of the mouths of babes, as the saying goes, and in this case the babe had done the tactical analysis more honestly than I had. They would come back. My invented law had a lifespan of roughly one inquiry to the wrong office. A lie is a floorboard: it holds until someone steps in the right place.

Marta looked from the girl to me, and her expression performed the remarkable feat of being both plea and verdict. "I can't hide her," she said quietly. "I've flour on my arms and a rent book for a heart, Mr. Zhou, and I've done what I could, but they pay informants on this street in bread. You saw how quick I lied about the other aunt. Quick as breathing. That's what living here teaches." She set down the teapot. "But you — you walked in here with hospital paper and a straight back. Where I come from, that's a kind of wealth."

It was, I thought, the first time anyone had called what I possessed wealth. I had spent thirty years treating invisibility as poverty — as the coin of a man who owns nothing and therefore loses nothing. Standing in Marta's kitchen with the smell of cheap tea and the sound of a small girl's measured breathing on the stairs, I understood for the first time that it had also been hoarding. A whole life of carrying names, and not one of them mine, and not one of them shared.

"I know a pilot," I heard myself say. "And a tavern keeper. And a route upriver that needs three checkpoints counted." Which was, admittedly, putting several carts considerably ahead of a single mule, since I had not yet asked Ilsa Brandt for anything and Odile did not know me beyond the price of a supper. But the words were out, and once words are out they become facts with momentum.

Nell came down the last three stairs. She had put on a coat two sizes too large, and she had tucked her father's letter into its inner pocket, and she stood in front of me with her chin up in a way that I recognized with a jolt — it was my own posture, borrowed, the standing-still-so-no-one-sees-you posture — and I hated seeing it on her more than I can say.

"Aunt Marta says you keep a book of last words," she said. "She heard it from a cousin who washed linens at the hospital."

Had she now. The harbor district: a place where even secrets did piecework. "I keep a book of names," I said. "And what people said. It seemed wrong that the reports leave them out."

"My da's in it."

"Page forty."

"Will you read him to me?" And then, before I could construct any answer at all: "All of them. Every name. Da says you'd teach me if I asked proper." She drew a breath and delivered the killing blow with nine-year-old precision: "I'm asking proper. Please. Underlined twice."

* * *

We walked to the tavern as the fog thickened toward evening, the girl and I, our footsteps keeping accidental time on the cobbles. She had insisted on bringing one bundle — one, she said, because more than one made you a refugee, and refugees got caught, whereas a girl carrying a single bundle was just a girl going somewhere — and I did not ask where she had learned that, because I suspected the answer was: everywhere, always, from birth.

Odile was out front turning the sign when we came up, and she took in the sight of me — the nurse who paid for suppers and asked nothing — arriving at her door with a small girl and a face full of whatever mine was full of, and she did not ask a single question. She simply held the door wide.

"Back room's warm," she said. "There's stew. Sit the girl by the stove before you tell me the rest, whatever the rest is."

And Nell — who had sized up every adult in her orbit with those harbor-water eyes, sorting the world into safe and sorry — walked past me into the tavern light without a backward glance, as though she had simply been waiting at Salt Lane all this time for someone to come collect her, and had expected it to work out, the way children expect things, which is a faith adults spend years failing to deserve.

Odile watched her go, then looked at me with an eyebrow raised a precise quarter inch.

"I've acquired something," I said.

"So I see." She turned the sign to CLOSED with a snap. "Does it have a name?"

"Nell," I said. "Nell Corrigan. Her father's on page forty of my ledger, and the army wants her for a workhouse, and I seem to have promised — or been volunteered; the details blur — to teach her every name in the book."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she laughed, low, and shook her head at me with something that looked remarkably like approval.

"Congratulations," she said dryly. "You're a family man now. Hope you weren't planning on remaining invisible."

Through the doorway I could see Nell already installed by the stove, coat off, single bundle at her feet, talking to the fire as though briefing it. And in my breast pocket, against my heart, the colonel's sealed letter shifted with my breath — heavier than paving stone now, heavier every day — and I understood that the two cargoes had just become one journey.

Somewhere across the dark harbor, Captain Reyes was closing a net, three checkpoints stood between us and New Year's Eve, and I had just made myself responsible for a nine-year-old underliner.

Ilsa, I thought, is going to refuse me. And then she is going to say yes.
