## 10. First Checkpoint: The Customs House

The coal dust was the worst of it.

Ilsa Brandt had run the millrace in spring flood, had landed a ferry on a sandbar in a hailstorm with eleven passengers screaming the Lord's Prayer at her in three languages, and had once navigated the entire harbor by smell alone when the fog ate her compass. None of it had prepared her for the particular misery of shoveling coal into the hold of her own boat so that she could be *dirty enough* to pass inspection.

"You missed a spot," Marguerite said. He sat on the crate they'd built the whole plan around, holding a lantern, looking—annoyingly—almost clean.

"I did not miss a spot. You are sitting on the spot." She flung another shovel of coal across the deck and let the wind carry a fine black mist over him. He flinched. She felt briefly, shamefully delighted. "There. Now you look like an honest woman's husband."

"I look like a chimney that lost an argument."

"Better. Honest people lose arguments. Officials win them." She stowed the shovel and wiped her hands on her trousers, which were already beyond dignity or rescue. "Say it back to me."

He recited without hesitation, in the flat voice of a man who had memorized scripture: "We are carrying coal to the Millbrook kilns under contract with Harrowgate Brickworks. The manifest is in the wheelhouse. My name is Nurse Marguerite Zhou, attached to Harbor Hospital, seconded as medical escort because the kiln workers requested one after the fever scare in November."

"And who am I?"

"My cousin's widow, helping with the boat." A pause. "I'm sorry about that part."

"Don't be. I've been dead to the Harbor Commission for two years; being a widow is a promotion." She said it lightly, and watched him decide not to ask whether it was lightly meant. That was one thing she'd learned about him in three days of planning this madness: he noticed everything and reached for nothing. It was a kind of manners, she supposed. Or a kind of fear. With Marguerite Zhou it was hard to tell the difference, and she suspected he'd spent his whole life making sure nobody could.

The letter rode against his chest, sewn by Odile into a false seam of his coat lining, wrapped in oilcloth because Odile trusted neither water nor soldiers nor God on a river at night. Above them, the great iron span of the bridge cut the sky into a black wedge, and beneath the arch, at the water level, the customs house lamps burned yellow through the fog—three lamps, which meant three inspectors on duty. Full staff. Of course it was full staff. The last days before New Year's were when every clerk in the colonial service worked twice as hard at looking busy.

"They'll wave us through," Ilsa said, mostly to convince herself. "Coal barges are boring. Boring is the best disguise there is. Nobody invents trouble for a coal barge."

"And if they do?"

"Then you talk, and I steer, and we do not stop moving until the bridge is behind us." She took the wheel. "Ready?"

Marguerite looked down at the crate he'd been sitting on, then up at the fog rolling off the water like something alive and patient. "No," he said honestly.

"Good. Ready men lie worse."

* * *

The current under the bridge was treacherous, all eddy and suck where the pilings broke the river's back, and Ilsa worked the wheel with her wrists and forearms the way her father had taught her, small corrections, never big ones, letting the water argue with itself while the boat slid through the middle of the argument. The customs house grew out of the fog on their port side—a squat stone jaw with a chain across half the channel and a floating platform where the inspectors waited, one of them already lifting a lantern.

"Coal?" the inspector called. He was young, with the unfortunate mustache of a man still growing into his authority.

"Coal," Ilsa agreed. "Millbrook kilns. Manifest's posted."

He came aboard with the easy entitlement of someone who had never been told no by anything except his mother, and Ilsa let him read the manifest while she held the boat against the current, engine idling, the whole vessel straining gently forward like a dog on a leash. Beside her, Marguerite had arranged himself into exactly the posture of a man who found customs inspections fascinating and wished to return to his newspaper.

The inspector ran his finger down the manifest. Nodded. Looked at the coal dust on everything, including, thanks to Ilsa's earlier efforts, Marguerite's collar. And then his eyes came up—and stopped.

"You're Brandt," he said. Not a question. His voice had changed entirely, gone from bored to bright, the brightness of a man who has just found money on the street. "Ilsa Brandt. The *Meridian*." He pointed at the name on the hull as if she might have forgotten it. "Your license was revoked. Two years ago. Refusal to transport, section fourteen, they made us all read the file in training—" He was grinning now. "You're not licensed to operate anything on this river. You're not even licensed to *row*."

The world narrowed to the width of a gunwale. Ilsa felt the old fury come up her throat, hot and useless, the same fury that had put her in front of that commission board two years ago saying things no pilot should say to men who controlled her livelihood. She opened her mouth to say them again—

"Nurse," Marguerite said quietly.

It was barely a word. It was a hand on a shoulder in the dark. She closed her mouth.

"That would be me," Marguerite said, standing, and Ilsa watched him do the thing she could not do: become small. Not weak—small. He folded his height down, tilted his head, let his voice go soft and apologetic, and became the kind of man officials talked *past* rather than *to*. "There's been some confusion, I'm afraid. This is a hospital charter."

"This is a coal barge."

"With a hospital charter," Marguerite repeated, and produced from his coat—the same coat, Ilsa noted, that hid a document worth more than the boat—a folded paper bearing the harbor hospital's seal. Where he'd gotten a hospital charter for a coal shipment to brick kilns she would never know; she suspected he had simply asked someone with the tired confidence of a man who was owed small favors, and the universe, out of habit, had paid. "The Millbrook workers requested medical escort after the November fever. The kilns fall outside the district surgeon's route, so the hospital contracts passage. Mrs. Brandt holds the boat; I hold the credentials. The arrangement predates her—" a delicate pause— "administrative difficulties."

The young inspector looked from the paper to Marguerite's face, and Ilsa saw the exact moment his ambition collided with his caution. A revoked pilot operating on the governor's river was a story that could make a career. A hospital nurse waving official paper was a story that could end one, if the seal turned out real and the nurse turned out to have friends.

"The manifest's in order," the inspector said slowly.

"It is," said Marguerite.

"The cargo's coal."

"It is."

"And the pilot is—" He struggled. "Suspended."

"*Revoked*," Ilsa corrected, because apparently her mouth had voted to override her survival instinct. "Revoked means they took the license. Suspended means they might give it back. I'd hate for your report to be inaccurate."

*Ilsa,* said Marguerite's eyebrows, in a language they appeared to share.

But something odd happened then. The inspector looked at her—really looked, the way people rarely bothered—and whatever he saw, the grin drained out of him. Maybe it was the two years of her face. Maybe it was the word *revoked*, spoken by the woman it had happened to.

"They read us your file in training," he said again, quieter. "The troop refusal. They said you were a warning." He glanced at the fog, at the bridge, at the yellow lamps behind him. "My brother's regiment went upstream in '98. On a ferry like this one."

Nobody said anything. The engine ticked. Somewhere above them, on the bridge, a cart rattled across the iron, and the whole span hummed like a struck glass.

"The manifest's in order," the inspector said finally, and stepped back onto the platform, and waved the chain aside. "Move along. And you—" this to Ilsa, low, not quite meeting her eyes— "don't come back through here. Not this way. Inspector Okafor works nights starting Thursday, and he reads files for pleasure."

Ilsa took the wheel. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. She brought the boat around the chain and into the open channel, and only when the customs house lamps had dissolved into the fog behind them did she realize she had not taken a full breath since the boy had said her name.

* * *

They ran in silence for a mile. It was a good silence, mostly—the silence of two people who had just survived something and wanted to confirm, privately, that they still had hands and lungs—but Ilsa broke it anyway, because silences that lasted too long started collecting furniture.

"You spent it," she said. "That paper. Once he writes it up—and he will write it up, boys like that write up their breakfasts—your name is attached to this boat, this route, this day. You can't come back through that checkpoint as yourself ever again."

"I know."

"Do you?" She eased the wheel a fraction against the current. "Because I've lived it, Marguerite. The day they took my license, I thought: fine, they take the paper, they can't take the water. But the paper *is* the water, on a river like this. Every pier has a man with a book, and my name is in all their books, and now yours will be in his." She shook her head. "You did it for a piece of paper sewn into your coat. You didn't even hesitate. I want to understand that."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Fog beaded on his shoulders. When he spoke, he didn't answer directly, which she was learning was also a kind of answer.

"There was a soldier in October," he said. "Corrigan. He asked me to make sure his daughter got his letters. That's all—letters. Paper. I carried them for four months before I found her, because carrying them was dangerous and I kept telling myself next week, next week, invisibility first, duty later." His thumb moved against his chest, unconscious, touching the place where the letter lay. "She nearly went to the workhouse while I was being careful. So no. I didn't hesitate. I've done hesitating. It's more expensive than it looks."

Ilsa thought about arguing with that. She thought about telling him that credibility, once spent, bought nothing back; that men like Reyes did not reward honesty discovered late. All true. All beside the point, somehow, in a way she couldn't yet name.

Instead she said, "Corrigan. Big fellow? Red hair, whistled constantly, drove everyone mad?"

Marguerite turned his head sharply. "You knew him."

"Everyone knew Corrigan. He fixed my winch once, refused payment, drank my tea, told my whole life story back to me wrong." She laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been rusting shut for two years. "Small river," she said. "Small stupid river."

"Page forty," Marguerite said softly.

"What?"

"Nothing. Hold your course. There's a snag below the old rope walk—I hit it once and swore off hitting it forever, which is the only vow I've ever kept."

* * *

They passed Millbrook landing as the light failed, and Ilsa nosed the boat into the reeds a half-mile short of the kilns, where the coal would be somebody's problem by morning and no questions asked—Odile had arranged it, because Odile arranged everything within five miles of her tavern the way other women arranged table settings. The kiln road ran up from the water toward the garrison gate, and somewhere past the garrison gate, the fort, and somewhere past the fort, New Year's Eve, and somewhere inside those walls, the rest of Marguerite's impossible errand.

He stood at the rail beside her, watching the kiln fires glow orange through the trees like embers in a grate the size of the world.

"Okafor," Ilsa said. "Reads files for pleasure. Doubled patrols at Millbrook, if the rumors are worth anything. And the fort will be sealed for the ceremony." She counted them off on her fingers, grimly festive. "Three checkpoints. We've spent one bridge, one nurse's good name, and most of my nerve, and we're not halfway."

"We're not halfway," Marguerite agreed. But he didn't sound afraid, and that frightened her more than the checkpoints did. He sounded like a man doing arithmetic he'd already finished. "Ilsa. If this goes wrong—if they take me—you get Nell. Odile knows the address. Don't let her go to the workhouse waiting for me. Promise me the small thing, even if the big thing fails."

"The big thing isn't going to fail."

"Promise me anyway."

She looked at him—at the chimney-dusted collar, the careful hands, the ledger-shaped bulge of a life's secret obedience under his arm—and realized, with the clarity of cold water, why she'd taken no money for this. Not because of Corrigan's name in his book, though that had opened the door. Because of this: a man who promised things to the dead and to the living alike and seemed constitutionally incapable of promising less than everything.

"Fine," she said. "The small thing. Now help me tie off, and then you're going to tell me how a nurse intends to walk into a sealed fort on New Year's Eve, and I'm going to tell you why it's the worst idea I've heard since my own tribunal, and then we're going to do it anyway, because apparently that's who we are now."

Marguerite smiled—the first unguarded smile she'd seen on him, quick and startled, like sun through fog.

"That's who we are now," he agreed, and reached for the rope.

And downstream, far behind them, faint on the evening air, came the sound Ilsa had been listening for all her life and dreaded tonight: a whistle from the landing, twice, sharp—the signal that meant someone had asked about their boat, and the asking hadn't stopped.
