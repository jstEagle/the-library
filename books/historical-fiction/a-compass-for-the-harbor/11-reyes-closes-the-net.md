## 11. Reyes Closes the Net

Captain Emilio Reyes had been awake for nineteen hours when he realized he was no longer sure which side of the desk he was sitting on.

It was a small office above the garrison quartermaster's stores, borrowed for the occasion because the harbor commandant wanted this business kept quiet and the commandant's own offices were too grand, too visible, too full of windows that faced the sea like open mouths. Reyes's temporary quarters had one window, and it faced a wall of stacked crates labeled FLOUR — ARMY ISSUE, which struck him as either an oversight or a joke, depending on what was actually in them.

On the desk before him sat three things: a tin cup of coffee gone cold enough to float a spoon upright; a list of names in his own careful handwriting; and Colonel Ashworth's service record, which was supposed to be closed by now and wasn't, because dead men had a way of leaving doors open behind them.

Reyes rubbed his eyes. Forty-one years old, sixteen of those in uniform, and he could no longer remember whether he'd joined the service to be a good man who did his duty or whether he'd simply told himself that often enough that the two things had fused somewhere along the line, like a coin worn smooth — you couldn't say anymore which face had been heads.

"Captain?" The corporal in the doorway was young, pink about the ears, and frightened of him in the way young men were frightened of anything with more stripes than sense. "The governor's man is here again."

"Send him in, Ruiz. And Corporal — if anyone asks where I am, I am inspecting the flour."

Ruiz glanced at the crates through the open door. "Is there something wrong with the flour, sir?"

"There would be," said Reyes, "if anyone asked where I was."

* * *

The governor's man's name was Halloran, and he wore a gray coat the color of bad weather and the expression of a man who had never once in his life been cold, hungry, or contradicted. He took the only chair without being offered it, which was a kind of signature, the way some men signed their names and others simply sat down.

"Captain Reyes." Halloran laced his fingers over his waistcoat. His signet ring caught the lamplight — a family crest, heavy gold, the sort of thing you inherited instead of earned. "Progress?"

"The district's being canvassed. House to house, tavern to tavern, pier to pier. Ashworth was delirious in his final days — fever does that — and we know he received visitors from outside the hospital. Someone may have taken correspondence from his effects."

"You mean stolen it."

"I mean," Reyes said evenly, "that papers have gone missing, and I intend to find them."

"Papers." Halloran smiled the way a door closes. "Captain, let us not be delicate with each other. It tires me, and I imagine it tires you. There is a letter. Its contents concern certain financial arrangements that will be concluded at midnight on the thirty-first, arrangements in which men considerably more powerful than either of us have considerable interest. If that letter reaches the probate court before the year turns, a great many people will be embarrassed. Embarrassed men are dangerous men, Captain. They start looking for someone to blame, and they rarely look higher than the nearest uniform."

Reyes looked at the man's hands. Soft, manicured, resting on his stomach like a cat that had never caught anything it hadn't been served.

"And the nearest uniform," Reyes said, "would be mine."

"The nearest uniform," Halloran agreed pleasantly, "is always yours. That's rather the point of uniforms." He produced an envelope from inside his coat and laid it on the desk between them. "A reward notice. Two hundred dollars for information leading to recovery of the colonel's effects. We'll post it in every public house from the bridge to the river mouth by tomorrow noon."

"Two hundred dollars is four months' pay for a ferryman."

"Yes," said Halloran, rising. "That is the idea."

He paused at the door, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping its hook, and underneath was something colder and older and very tired of waiting.

"Captain. The colonel was an old man with an old man's fancies. Whatever he wrote in his last hours, it has no legal weight, no witnesses, no standing. What it does have is the power to make trouble for honorable people. Find it. Burn it. Or better, find whoever is carrying it and bring them to me before New Year's Eve, and next year your coat will have more braid on it and your children will have shoes without holes. Do you understand me?"

Reyes understood him perfectly. That was the trouble.

"Perfectly, sir," he said.

Halloran's smile came back up like a shop shutter. "Splendid. Happy almost New Year, Captain."

When the door closed, Reyes sat for a long while looking at the reward notice. Fine paper. Good ink. Two hundred dollars, printed clear as scripture, for the delivery of one sealed letter into hands that had no right to it.

He thought: *I know exactly how this ends. I've known since the day they sent me to collect the colonel's effects and told me which drawer the letter would NOT be in.*

He drank the cold coffee anyway. Waste was a sin, and so, apparently, was everything else.

* * *

The harbor district at dusk in late December was a watercolor somebody had left out in the rain — smudged lamps, bleeding shadows, the fog coming off the river in slow gray rolls like the breath of something enormous asleep under the pilings. Reyes walked it on foot, without escort, because you learned nothing useful from a carriage and less from soldiers, whose approach announced itself half a mile out and gave every guilty conscience time to become innocent.

Besides, he told himself, he was inspecting. Flour, morale, the general state of the district. All perfectly legitimate.

He stopped first at the customs house on the bridge, where Inspector Meech — a narrow man with a mustache like two exclamation points — briefed him on the search with the enthusiasm of a man who had finally been given permission to be difficult in an official capacity.

"No irregular traffic on the river, Captain, none that we haven't flagged. I've pulled the manifests for every licensed vessel going upstream since the twenty-sixth. Coal, timber, grain, coal, ice, more coal." Meech sniffed. "Half these manifests are fiction, of course. The other half are lies. But they're orderly lies, Captain, and I know their authors, and when one of them changes its handwriting, I'll know."

"See that you do." Reyes hesitated. "Meech. The ferry pilots. Who's still running cargo unlicensed?"

Meech's mustache performed a small shrug of its own. "Unlicensed? You mean Brandt."

"Ilsa Brandt."

"Stripped of her ticket two years back for refusing troop transport. Refused it to the major's *face*, if you'll credit it. There are people who should have been born with less spine." Meech leaned in, lowering his voice with relish. "She still runs the river at night. No papers, no lights, no questions. If your missing letter is on the water, Captain, it's on her boat — or it will be. She's the only fool good enough to try it."

Reyes wrote the name down, though he didn't need to. Everyone at the harbor knew Ilsa Brandt the way everyone knew weather.

"Anything else?" Meech asked.

"One question. Have any of your regulars come into money lately? Sudden money. Coin for conversation."

"Ah." Meech's face went knowing and slightly sad at once, the way faces did around here when the reward notices went up. "You mean who's selling. Give it a day, Captain. Two hundred dollars makes liars of better men than these, and worse friends than most."

* * *

He walked down through Millbrook as the light failed, past the garrison gate where the sentries stamped and blew on their fingers, and turned down toward the piers because his feet had decided before his head did, the way feet sometimes did when they were walking toward a thing a man would rather walk away from.

The Widow Odile's tavern stood at the crook of the third pier with its lamp lit and its door open a hand's width, spilling warmth and fiddle music onto the cobbles like something freely given, which in Reyes's experience made it the rarest establishment in the district.

He had known Odile's husband. That was the thing he did not put in reports. Laurent, they'd called him — a deckhand with a laugh like a broken bell and opinions he distributed free of charge to anyone within earshot, including, on one memorable afternoon, a visiting admiral. Reyes had been a lieutenant then. He had stood in the rank behind the officer who had read the order removing Laurent from the rolls — not arrested, not charged, simply *removed*, name struck, record amended, as if the sea itself had never heard of him. Reyes remembered thinking, at twenty-five, that there must be a reason. There were always reasons. Men in gray coats carried them folded in their pockets like prescriptions.

Twenty years later he could not remember what the reason had been. He suspected, some nights, that there had never been one, or that the reason and the excuse had gotten separated somewhere in the filing, and only one of them had survived.

He did not go into the tavern. He stood across the street in the fog like a fool in a melodrama, watching the light, listening to the fiddle, and feeling the particular shame of a man doing surveillance on the widow of a man whose erasure he had once helped stamp the paperwork for.

"You'll catch your death standing there, Captain."

Odile herself, in the doorway, arms crossed, not fooled for a second and not pretending to be. She had a voice like dark beer — warm, bitter, better than it looked.

"Madame Odile." He touched his cap. "I'm conducting inquiries."

"Inquiries." She tasted the word and set it down. "In my street. In my fog. With your hat in your hands like a boy caught courting. Come in where it's warm if you're going to lurk, Captain; lurking gives you pneumonia, and then who'll oppress us?"

"I can't come in."

"I know you can't." But she held the door wider all the same, an offer she knew he wouldn't take and made anyway, because that was the kind of woman she was — the kind who left doors open for men who didn't deserve it. "There's a notice going up tomorrow, I hear. Two hundred dollars for a letter."

Reyes said nothing.

"My regulars will read it," Odile went on, conversationally, terribly. "Most will laugh. One or two will do sums. And one of them — I've known which one since summer, Captain, I keep a ledger of my own, though mine doesn't hide under floorboards, mine hangs over the till where God can see it — one of them owes money to men who don't laugh." She looked at him straight. "So whatever it is you're hunting, you'd best find it before my debtor does. Because the ones who'll take your silver won't bring it to *you*. They'll bring it to the gray coat. And the person carrying that letter — whoever they are, whatever they're carrying it *for* — they'll vanish the way my Laurent vanished. Struck through. Amended. As if the harbor never heard of them."

She went back inside. The fiddle struck up something quick and bright. The door stayed open a hand's width, an inch of golden light on the wet cobbles, until Reyes turned away and it might as well have been a mile.

* * *

Back in the office above the flour, he pinned the district map to the wall and worked the problem the way he'd been taught to work problems thirty years ago at the academy, before the academy taught him other, quieter lessons.

Three checkpoints governed everything that moved upriver. The customs house at the bridge — papers, manifests, Meech's orderly lies. The garrison gate at Millbrook — patrols, doubled now per orders he himself had drafted. The fort at the river mouth — sealed for the governor's ceremony, impassable by land or water after the thirtieth.

Whoever carried the letter had to pass all three before midnight on the thirty-first, or the estate transferred, the century turned, and the whole affair became one more thing that had officially never happened.

By road: three checkpoints. By river: Meech's manifests and one pilot stripped of her license who ran black water at night. By air: nothing, unless you counted rumor, which moved faster than anything with papers.

He sat down and began, methodically, to write the list of names. Tavern regulars. Dockhands. Hospital orderlies. Anyone who had entered the fever ward in Ashworth's last week. He wrote each name in his careful schoolroom hand, and beside each name he wrote what he knew of them, and the column of what-he-knew grew longer than the column of names, because that was the trouble with a harbor district — everybody knew everybody, had buried somebody's somebody, owed somebody a favor or a grudge.

And then his pen stopped, halfway down the third page, on a name he had written without thinking, because he wrote it the way you write your own address.

Private T. Reyes. 4th Company. Died of wounds, March 'ninety-eight. Record: see attached.

There was no attachment. There had never been an attachment. Tomas — his brother, his little brother, who had enlisted at seventeen because Emilio's coat hung in the hall like a promise — had died at a place the dispatches called an engagement and the survivors had called, privately, a blunder. And the official report, the one with Reyes's own signature somewhere in its chain of custody, had recorded the loss of Private T. Reyes in eleven words, none of which mentioned that he had been twenty years old, that he had laughed like their mother, or that the last man to see him had tried, months later, to tell Emilio what his brother's last words had been — and had been ordered, formally, in writing, to confine his account to matters of operational record.

Operational record. Eleven words. *Died of wounds. Buried near the field. Effects forwarded.*

Effects forwarded. A kit bag. A photograph of the family, water-stained. Nothing written, because Tomas had never written anything down; he'd said once that paper was for people who didn't trust their memories, and he trusted his, and Emilio had laughed and said one of them had to.

Somewhere in the ward that night, his brother had said words. Last words. And somewhere in the machinery between that bed and this desk, the words had been ground up into operational record, and the man who had tried to carry them to Emilio had been silenced with a form letter, and Emilio had accepted this, because accepting things was what the uniform was *for*.

Now he was drafting the orders that would grind up somebody else's last words the same way. Two hundred dollars. Fine paper. Good ink.

Reyes put down the pen and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dark behind them bloomed red.

*It has no legal weight,* Halloran had said. *No witnesses, no standing.* Neither had eleven words about Tomas. The whole world ran on documents with no soul in them, and the souls got carried anyway, secretly, by nurses and widows and pilots without licenses, in ledgers hidden under floorboards, and the machinery never caught them because the machinery couldn't imagine that anyone would bother.

Someone was bothering. Right now, tonight, in his district, someone was carrying a dead man's truth against the whole weight of the governor's office — and Emilio Reyes was writing lists to stop them.

Or was he? He looked at his own orders, drafted in his own hand. *Canvass the district. Post the reward. Report all findings directly to the governor's representative.* Directly. Not through channels. Through Halloran. Which meant that whatever Reyes found went into Halloran's pocket, and whatever went into Halloran's pocket came out ash.

Unless.

He sat back. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, a corporal was counting crates of flour, and beyond the wall the fog rolled in off the river, patient as arithmetic, wrapping the harbor in the gray blanket under which Ilsa Brandt ran her black-water nights.

A tired man following orders could follow them slowly. Could post the notices a day late and canvass in the wrong fog. Could write his reports carefully, accurately, and route them — through channels, all proper channels, the slow ones. A tired man following orders had, if he was honest, one advantage over an honest rebel: nobody watched the watchman who seemed bored.

And if it came to the gate on New Year's Eve — if it came to a checkpoint and a carrier and a choice — well. He would decide then. He was a soldier. Soldiers decided then.

Reyes picked up the pen, crossed out one name on his third page, and rewrote the entry beside it in smaller letters:

*Ilsa Brandt. Pilot, unlicensed. Knows every reach of the river. Watch, do not detain. Detainment alerts the district; silence keeps the net invisible.*

Then, beneath, in the smallest hand of all, almost as if he hoped his own eyes would skip it:

*Note: whoever carries this letter carries a dead man's words against the record. Find out what the record is afraid of before you close the net. You owe the record nothing. You owe the words —*

He stopped. Stared at the unfinished sentence for a long time. Then he blotted the page, folded his lists, and blew out the lamp.

Outside, the bells of the harbor chapel rang ten, and under their sound, faint from the direction of the third pier, a fiddle was still playing — bright and stubborn and alive, in a district that was about to learn the price of two hundred dollars.

By morning the notices would be everywhere. By evening, someone would talk.

Reyes pulled his coat tight against the fog and started the long walk back through the sleeping streets, a tired man following orders at exactly the speed orders deserved — and in his breast pocket, folded twice, a blank sheet of good paper on which, though he could not yet have said why, he had begun composing a different kind of report entirely.
