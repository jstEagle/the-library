## 12. Nell Learns the Ledger

Nell Corrigan had been in the hospital cellar exactly four times before, and each time she had counted the steps down—fourteen, with the thirteenth one cracked and tilted like a bad tooth—and each time she had promised herself she would not touch anything. Cellars, her father used to say, are where houses keep their secrets, and you don't go poking at other people's secrets unless you're prepared to carry them home.

She had touched something anyway.

It had started innocently, which was how all trouble started, according to Odile, who said it the way other people said good morning. Marguerite had gone up to the fever ward an hour ago with a tray of broth and a face like folded paper, and he'd told Nell—kindly, in that careful voice of his, the one that made everything sound like a secret being kept rather than a kindness being given—that she was to stay in the cellar until the soldiers finished their searching upstairs. Searching. That was the word he'd used. *Searching*, as if they'd lost a button.

So Nell had done what sensible people did when the world above turned loud: she had explored downward instead.

The cellar smelled of carbolic and coal and old turnips, which was not a combination she would recommend to anyone. There were crates of bandages, jars of plums gone cloudy in their syrup, a broken wheelchair missing one wheel entirely and leaning against the wall like a drunk uncle. And there, under the window that wasn't really a window—it was a slot, barely bigger than a bread box, letting in a gray ribbon of January light—there was a workbench. And on the workbench, half beneath a rag that had been thrown over it carelessly, as if someone had meant to hide it better but been interrupted—

A book.

Now. A person in Nell's position—a person hiding in a cellar while soldiers stomped around overhead looking for her friend—had certain obligations. She knew this. Odile had told her often enough: when trouble comes, sit still, keep your hands in your lap, and think about plum jam until it passes. But Nell was eleven years old, and eleven-year-olds are built with a flaw in them somewhere near the curiosity, and the book was right *there*.

She pulled it out.

It was smaller than she expected. Bound in dark leather gone soft at the corners, swollen slightly with use, the way books get when they are opened often and held gently. No title on the spine. No title anywhere. She opened it to the first page and found only a column of names in small, even handwriting, and beside each name a date, and beneath each date a line or two of writing.

*Private Thomas Weir, 14th October. "Tell my mother the garden got planted after all."*

Nell sat down on the crate quite suddenly, the way a person does when their legs have made a decision without consulting them.

She knew what this was. Of course she knew. She had heard Marguerite and Odile talking in low voices, back at the tavern, before everything went wrong—talking about *the ledger* the way other people talked about a sick relative, carefully and with their backs to the room. She hadn't understood then. She understood now, holding it, feeling the weight of it, heavier than a book this size had any right to be.

She turned the pages slowly. October became November. Names, dates, last words. Some were long—little speeches, whole sentences, a man apparently determined to get his money's worth out of his final breath. Some were almost nothing. *Sergeant Amos Doyle, 2nd November. He hummed. All night, he hummed, and near the end I could make out the tune—his daughter's lullaby, he said once.*

There were drawings, too, occasionally. Small ones, quick, in pencil in the margins. A hand. A harbor buoy. A dog, drawn twice, once badly and once well, with a note: *Corporal Finch's dog, Biscuit. Drawn from description. The second attempt is more accurate, per Cpl. Finch's strict instructions.*

Nell laughed. It came out of her in the silent cellar like a sneeze in church, and she clapped her hand over her mouth, but the laugh wanted out, so she let a little more of it. Biscuit. Somebody's dying thought had been a dog named Biscuit, and somebody else had cared enough to draw him twice.

Then she turned to page forty.

She didn't know it was page forty. Not at first. She was turning pages and reading names, and then she read a name, and the cellar tilted.

*Private Daniel Corrigan, 9th November.*

Her father's handwriting—the handwriting on the envelopes that came, the handwriting in the front of her mother's old recipe book, the handwriting she practiced copying on scraps of paper because she missed the shape of it—was nowhere in this book. But his *voice* was. It was right there, in ink, in a stranger's careful letters:

*"He talked about a girl called Nell. Said she could skip stones flat as plates across the harbor, better than any boy on the pier, and that he'd taught her himself though she'd deny the lessons and claim natural talent. He said—"*

The entry stopped there. No. It didn't stop. Nell's eyes had stopped. She dragged them onward.

*"He said, 'She'll be taller than her mother by spring, you'll see, and stubborn past measuring. Tell her the stone-skipping crown is hers till I come back for it.' Then he asked me to write it down exactly, because, he said, 'I'm a rotten correspondent and she won't believe it unless it's in ink.'"*

Nell read it again. And again. The fourth time, she read it aloud, because her father had been right about one thing: some things weren't real until they were in the air.

"He said—" Her voice cracked like the thirteenth step. She swallowed and started over, steadier. "*She'll be taller than her mother by spring, and stubborn past measuring.*" A wet, hiccupping laugh escaped her. "I'm *not* taller than Mama yet. So there."

"You will be by March," said a quiet voice from the foot of the stairs. "He'd have won that bet."

Nell spun around so fast the ledger nearly went flying. Marguerite stood at the bottom of the stairs with the broth tray still in his hands, and he looked—she would remember this later, when she understood more about fear—he looked less frightened than sorry, the way a man looks when he has come home early and caught a child shaking a wrapped present.

"How long have you been standing there?" she demanded.

"Long enough to know Corporal Finch has strong opinions about drawing dogs." He set the tray down on the workbench and came closer, slowly, the way you'd approach a bird. His eyes went to the open book in her lap, and something moved through his face—not anger. Something older and wearier than anger. "Page forty," he said softly.

"Did you know? When you met me at the tavern. Did you already know who I was?"

"Yes."

"And you didn't say?"

"I was afraid," Marguerite said, simply, as if this explained everything, and perhaps it did, "that if I said it too soon, it would sound like a trick. Or a debt. And it isn't either of those things. It's just true. Your father talked about you for twenty minutes, Nell, and I wrote down every word, because those words belonged to somebody, and you were the somebody."

Nell looked down at the page. The ink had smudged slightly where her thumb had pressed it, and she felt a flash of guilt and covered the smudge with her whole palm, as if she could hold the words in by force.

"The soldiers upstairs," she said. "They're looking for this. Aren't they?"

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. Above them, boots crossed the floor in irregular thumps, muffled to a heartbeat by the floorboards. Somewhere a drawer opened and shut.

"They're looking for a letter," he said at last. "But yes. They'd take this too, if they found it. To them it's evidence. A list of witnesses." He said the word *witnesses* the way you'd say *unexploded shells*. "To me it's—" He stopped. Sat down on the broken wheelchair's twin, dragging it over with a scrape. "May I show you something?"

He took the ledger from her lap gently, turned back toward the beginning, and laid it open between them like a map.

"When a soldier dies here," he said, "there's an official report. You've seen how reports look—rank, regiment, date, cause. *Died of wounds received in action. Died of fever.* Dignified, tidy, and empty. It's a record of the fact that a man stopped breathing. It is not a record of the man." He tapped the page. "This is the other kind. This is the part the reports leave out, which happens to be the entire man. What he said. What he hummed. Who he loved and what he worried about and whether his dog's ears pointed up or flopped down."

"Why do you keep it hidden, though? If it's good?"

"Because the world we live in," Marguerite said, and his mouth did something that was almost a smile but had grief living in it, "has decided that these men are finished. Used up. Filed away. If I stand in the ward and write down a dying man's words where an officer can see, I get ordered to stop—or worse, ordered to explain why the colonel might object to Private So-and-so's last thoughts involving his opinion of the war. So I write small. I write at night. I hide the book under a floorboard like it's contraband." He exhaled. "For three years I told myself that was wisdom. Careful invisibility. Don't be seen, don't be caught, keep your head down and your ledgers hidden, and you'll survive."

"That sounds smart," Nell said.

"It is smart," he agreed. "It's also lonely, and lately I've begun to suspect it never protected anybody but me." He looked at her then, directly, and Nell had noticed before that he did this thing where he looked at you like you were a person of consequence, which most adults saved for other adults. "Your father knew I was writing things down. He asked me to. He held my wrist, Nell—grip like a dock rope—and he said, 'Put it in ink, nurse, or it never happened.' So it happened. You're holding proof."

Nell discovered she was crying, which annoyed her, because she had planned to be brave about all of this. But the tears were doing something useful anyway, washing out the tightness in her chest, and Marguerite did not tell her not to cry. He just handed her a clean corner of bandage linen off the shelf, which was such a hospital thing to do that she laughed through it.

"I read it four times," she said finally, wiping her nose. "His entry. Is that mad?"

"No."

"Will I forget it? The words? Everyone says you forget."

"You won't," said Marguerite. "And even if you wobble on a word here and there—that's why there's ink. Memory leaks. Paper doesn't. It's the whole argument for my profession." He paused, and she watched an idea arrive in his face the way weather arrives over the harbor—gradually, and then all at once. "Would you like to learn them?"

"Learn what? The names?"

"All of it. How I keep the entries. What I listen for. Why I write *exactly*, no polishing, no improving—dying men don't need editors, they need scribes." He was leaning forward now, animated in a way she'd never seen him, this careful folded-paper man unfolding a crease at a time. "It isn't cheerful work. I won't pretend it is. But it's *work*, Nell. Real work. The kind that matters whether or not anyone ever finds out—which, in my experience, is the test of whether work matters at all."

Above them, the boots thundered across the ceiling, and a chair scraped, and a soldier shouted something unintelligible, and the whole cellar seemed to flinch. Nell looked up at the trembling lamplight, then down at the ledger lying open between them, page forty facing the ceiling like a shield.

"Yes," she said. "Teach me."

Marguerite blinked. She had the distinct satisfaction of seeing that she had surprised him, which appeared to be difficult.

"There are rules," he said.

"Of course there are rules. Odile has rules about the plum jars."

"Rule one: exactness. If a man says *tell her the garden got planted*, you do not improve it to *tell her the garden flourished*. Truth first, comfort second. Rule two: no judgment. Whatever a man says at the end belongs to him alone; our job is delivery, not approval. Rule three—" and here his voice changed, went softer and more serious than the lamplight, "—rule three is that every name gets written, Nell. Every one. Even the men nobody liked. Even the men who did wrong. Especially those. A ledger that only remembers the pleasant people isn't memory. It's advertising."

Nell nodded solemnly. Then, because she was her father's daughter and therefore constitutionally incapable of solemnity lasting longer than a minute: "What about rule four?"

"There is no rule four."

"There should be a rule four. Every proper system has at least four rules."

Marguerite considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. "Rule four," he said at last, "is tea. Nobody keeps a ledger of the dead on an empty stomach. It's unseemly."

"Tea," Nell repeated, delighted. "That's the best rule."

"It's the oldest one."

He rose, fetched the kettle from its hook over the little cellar brazier, and set it to heat, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had performed ten thousand small kindnesses in this room. While the water muttered, he turned to a fresh page in the back of the ledger—there were blank pages still, Nell saw, a good dozen of them, which meant the work wasn't finished, which meant somehow that mattered—and he smoothed it flat with his palm.

"We'll start with practice," he said. "You'll take dictation from me. I'll be a soldier with last words; you'll be the nurse with the pen. Ready?"

"What are my last words going to be?"

"I'm a grumpy sergeant with a cold and strong feelings about my horse."

Nell picked up the pencil. Her hand, she noticed, had stopped shaking. "Ready."

"*Tell O'Rourke,*" Marguerite intoned, in a gravelly voice that was honestly terrible, "*the horse's name is Duchess, not Princess, and if he calls her Princess at the remount depot I will come back and haunt him specifically.*"

Nell wrote it, laughing, getting it down word for word, even the *specifically*, which she made him repeat twice because exactness was rule one. And Marguerite watched her write, and something in his face—she'd think about this later too, much later, when she understood what she'd witnessed—something in his face eased, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

The kettle began to sing. Overhead, the boots had gone quiet, though neither of them trusted that, and neither of them reached for the lamp to dim it. There in the coal-smelling dark, with the ledger open and the steam rising and rule four honored properly with two chipped cups, Nell Corrigan began her apprenticeship in the keeping of names—though neither of them knew yet that the very first name she'd help protect was already sewn into the lining of the coat hanging by the stairs, and that by morning, the soldiers upstairs would be pulling apart the tavern on Harbor Row, and Odile would be sitting in the back room answering questions she would refuse, at gunpoint, to answer.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was dictation, and tea, and a grumpy sergeant's horse named Duchess, and page forty resting closed against Nell's heart like a stone skipped flat across dark water—still traveling, still touching down, still not yet sunk.
