## 13. Second Checkpoint: The Garrison Gate

We came up on Millbrook from the south road an hour past dusk, and the first thing I saw was not the garrison gate itself but its shadow—too many shadows, in truth, thrown too long across the snow by too many lanterns. Where last week a single sentry had stamped his feet and dreamed of supper, now there were men in pairs, walking the fence line, and beyond them the yellow windows of the barracks burned with the particular brightness of a building that has been woken up and told to stay that way.

Ilsa pulled the ferry in against the reedy bank a half mile downstream and killed the engine, and for a while we simply listened to the water slap the hull.

"That's doubled," she said at last. It was not a question. Ilsa Brandt did not waste questions on things her own eyes had already answered.

"Tripled, if anything." I counted again, because counting was something I could do while my stomach did other things entirely. "Sentries at the gate, two on the water stair, a patrol crossing the yard every four minutes. They're not looking for smugglers. Smugglers get one man and a dog."

"They're looking for us."

"For a parcel," I corrected, out of habit. In my experience the distinction mattered to no one but me, and even to me it mattered less each day.

Nell stirred under the tarpaulin amid the coal sacks, where we'd stowed her between Millbrook and the river mouth so that anyone stopping us would find only a pilot, a nurse, and a hold full of fuel. She had slept the way children sleep in cold—instantly, fiercely, one hand curled around the ledger's leather cover as though it were a cat. We had not told her what we carried. We had only told her it mattered, which for Nell was the better argument anyway.

Ilsa found the lantern with the blue glass—the one signal left over from some older smuggling trade that the river remembered better than the authorities did—and flashed twice toward the mill. Nothing. She flashed again. A long minute passed, and then from the dark shape of the millrace sluice a boy's voice called back the answering pattern, thin over the water: *bad. bad-bad. go home.*

"Well," said Ilsa.

"Well," I agreed.

Neither of us said the rest of it, which was that *going home* had become a word without a referent, like a compass needle spun loose of its pivot. The tavern was compromised—I had seen to it, simply by sleeping there; the thought of Odile in a cell sat in my chest like swallowed stone—and the hospital would be watched by now, and the customs house inspector at the bridge had my name in his book along with a description he had taken great pains to write slowly while looking directly at me. Every door I had ever kept carefully unlocked for other people was closing behind me, one by one, and I found I could not even regret it properly, because regret requires surprise.

We put in below the mill where the willows grow thick, and the boy came down to us—Tomas, the miller's son, eleven years old with the solemnity of a bishop and chilblains on both hands.

"Soldiers came this afternoon," he reported, hunkering down by the bank as though we were all inspecting the reeds together. "A captain. Not the tired one—the other one, the one who smiles. He'd had a tip, he said. Someone at the tavern talked." Tomas looked at me when he said it, not accusingly, but with the terrible neutral accuracy of children. "They turned over everything. Mrs. Odile went with them holding her head high like a queen being arrested for tax fraud. Then they set men at the bridge and the gate and said anyone asking after ferry licenses should be held."

"Ferry licenses," I repeated.

"Your friend has a revoked license," Tomas said to me, kindly, as if breaking news of a death in the family.

"I'm aware."

"And you're a nurse whose credentials were used at the customs house two days ago."

"Everyone in this conspiracy is extremely aware of everything," Ilsa muttered. "That's our whole problem."

* * *

We took tea in the miller's kitchen, which is to say we sat in the miller's kitchen while his wife pretended loudly to be scalding dishes so that any listener would hear domestic clatter and nothing else. The miller himself, a wide slow man named Petch who moved like a barge, stood with his back to the door and told us the situation in a voice pitched for privacy between friends.

"Gate's hopeless. They've got the manifest books out and they're checking every boat, every cart, every man with a limp. Your faces are known. Even the parcel's known—they described it. Sealed letter, wax seal, carried against the chest." He turned and looked at me then, apologetic. "Against the chest, friend. Somebody's told them exactly how you carry it."

The kitchen was very warm and I felt suddenly very far from it, the way one does when the body decides ahead of the mind that danger is present. My hand had gone to my coat before I caught myself.

"They can describe the wax all they like," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Wax can be changed. Paper can be folded differently. The contents don't alter."

"No," said Petch. "But the date does. Court sits at nine tomorrow morning, probate division, and closes the estate file at midnight. After the century turns, the transfer's executed on the old terms. You know this."

I knew it. I had known it since a dying man pressed paper into my hands and made an archivist of me whether I consented or not. Three days remained until New Year's Eve. Two checkpoints behind us, bought with Ilsa's spent license and my spent good name. One checkpoint left, at the harbor fort, where the governor himself would stand on New Year's Eve reviewing troops and cutting ribbons on a century he believed belonged to him.

And directly before us, useless and bright-lit and triple-manned, the garrison gate at Millbrook.

"We could go around," Nell said.

We all looked at her. She had come in from the cold shed where she'd been nominally helping the miller's wife and actually eavesdropping, and she stood there with her chin up in the way I had begun privately to call the Corrigan angle, because her father had worn the same angle in the hospital ward, insisting he was fine, insisting he'd be up and marching soon, insisting on dignity with his last breaths.

"Around," Ilsa said. "Around the garrison gate. On foot. Through what, precisely?"

"The hills." Nell shrugged. "People do it. Smugglers used to. There's a path behind the quarry."

"There's a path behind the quarry that takes three days and ends in a bog," said Petch mildly. "My grandfather drove brandy over it during the last war and swore off hills forever."

"Then we walk fast," said Nell.

And there it was—the whole tragedy and comedy of us laid out in a mill kitchen on a December night: a child proposing we outrun an army through a bog, a disgraced pilot proposing nothing because she was staring into her teacup like a woman reading drowned futures, and me, the careful man, the invisible man, discovering that the sight of a twelve-year-old ready to march into a frozen marsh on my behalf hurt worse than fear ever had.

"No," I said. "Nobody walks the quarry path. Nobody drowns in the bog for a piece of paper."

"It's not a piece of paper," Nell said, with my own voice, nearly. "It's names."

I had taught her that. Heaven help me, I had taught her that myself, in the cellar, by lamplight, one entry at a time, and now the lesson had gotten up and walked around and was arguing with me in a mill kitchen. That is the trouble with teaching children anything true: they keep it.

* * *

Ilsa excused herself to check the mooring lines, which everyone present understood to mean *to be alone*, and after a decent interval I followed her down to the water because I have never in my life managed a decent interval when someone I cared about walked out of a warm room into the dark alone.

She stood on the ice-rimed boards looking upriver, where the millrace thundered faintly under its frost—the mill worked nights now, supplying the fort, and the race ran hard and white even in this cold. I came and stood beside her and said nothing, which is a skill I learned in fever wards: how to be present without demanding performance.

"The gate's impossible," she said finally.

"Yes."

"The bridge is closed to us. The quarry path is a bog with scenery."

"So it appears."

"My grandfather ran this river forty years," she said. "Before the pilots' guild, before the licenses, before any of it. He used to say the river doesn't have rules, it only has facts. The fact is, the garrison gate watches the channel because the channel is the only sensible way past Millbrook." She paused. "The race isn't sensible."

I looked at the millrace. Even by starlight I could see the water moving wrong—not flowing but *commuting*, fast and furious, funneling through the sluice cut where the mill wheel drew its power, a chute perhaps thirty feet wide between stone embankments, running a quarter mile before it spat out below the garrison's view. Fog lay along it in ropes.

"You're describing putting a flat-bottomed ferry through a millrace," I said, "at night, in fog, within earshot of a garrison that is listening for exactly one boat."

"Flat-bottomed boats ride high through white water if you keep her bow on. Grandfather took the *Gannet* through the old race twice, in daylight, showing off." Her jaw set. "He drowned in the second attempt, but the boat made it, and the brandy made it, and the men drinking the brandy made it, and I have decided as a professional matter to remember only the parts that succeeded."

"Ilsa."

"It's not a proposal I make lightly." She turned to face me, and up close her eyes were doing the thing they did—steady as a mooring post with weather underneath. "It's a proposal I make because I've run the numbers and it's the only column with anything written in it. And because—" she stopped, and started again, quieter. "Because you showed me page forty. You didn't have to. You opened your book and showed me Anton's name in your own hand, with his last words, and nobody in this world had written him down anywhere but you. So when I tell you the race is survivable, understand that I am telling you the way people tell the truth when it matters."

The fog moved along the race and the mill wheel groaned somewhere in the dark, turning its endless night shift.

"How survivable?" I asked.

"Six chances in ten."

"Of surviving."

"Of the boat making it," said Ilsa. "The odds improve for passengers who do exactly what I say. Which brings me to the part I hate, which is that Nell doesn't come."

"Nell absolutely does not come," I agreed, with a fervor that surprised us both.

"She stays here with the Petches. If we go in the water, someone has to carry on. That's not sentiment, that's logistics." She said it firmly, and then her voice cracked just slightly on the next sentence, which I pretend not to have noticed to this day. "Someone has to carry on, Marguerite. That's the whole point of your ledger, isn't it? Names outlive carriers."

* * *

We told them together, in the kitchen, laying it out plainly because these people had earned plain speech: the doubled patrols, the sealed roads, the race, the fog, the six-in-ten, the plan for Nell. I expected argument. What I got instead was a silence in which I could hear the mill wheel turning, and then Petch's wife, Marta, saying, "I'll pack dry clothes at the lower landing," in the tone of a woman arranging linens, and Nell saying, "Six in ten is better odds than Da had," which silenced the room completely and settled it.

But it was later, at the landing, loading the last of the coal and rigging the rails with spare planking to raise the freeboard—because if we were going to be suicidal we would be *tidily* suicidal—that the real weight of the thing settled onto me. Ilsa was forward checking the towline. Nell had gone up to bed under Marta's stern eye. And I stood on the frozen boards with the letter against my chest and understood that dread had quietly changed places with something else while I wasn't watching.

All my life I had believed that caution was a virtue, and perhaps it is, but I had practiced it as a hiding place. Orders came; I endured them. Checkpoints rose; I mapped them. Danger chose me the way weather chooses everyone, indifferently, and I wore my invisibility as a mackintosh against it. But standing on that landing, about to steer a stolen route through a millrace on the strength of a dead man's testament and a living woman's dead grandfather, I felt the difference in my own body: this was not weather. This was volition. Tomorrow night, no one would be forcing us down that chute of white water. We would choose it—each of us, separately, in our own kitchens and cockpits of the heart—and the choosing would be the point. Reyes and his captains moved men like cargo because they had never once considered that the cargo might consent. We were about to do the same mad thing, but we would do it *freely*, and that, I suspected, was the entire difference between an empire and a crew.

Ilsa came aft and stood beside me and looked at the black water and said, "Second thoughts?"

"One or two. They cancel out."

"Good. Doubt makes a poor helmsman but excellent ballast." She checked the line one more time, needlessly, the way pilots do. "Tomorrow we run it at slack after midnight, when the mill stops for the shift change. Fog's due in off the bay around eleven—it'll cover us on the approach and blind us equally, which is fair."

"And the garrison?"

"Hear us, maybe. See nothing. By the time anyone's launched a boat we'll be through the race and out the far side, and if we're not through it—" She shrugged. "Then the fish will know the names before the court does, and that's a kind of record-keeping too."

"Gentle humor has always been your strongest suit," I said.

"It's why they revoked my license," said Ilsa Brandt, and went forward to wait for the fog.

Above us the stars were hard and clear and indifferent, and downriver the garrison lamps burned their stubborn yellow, and somewhere between the two lay thirty feet of white water that we intended, freely and with malice aforethought, to survive. Against my chest, under wool and oilskin and scar tissue old and new, the letter weighed nothing at all.

Everything else weighed plenty.

The fog came off the bay at eleven minutes past ten.
