## 14. The Millrace at Night

Ilsa Brandt had run the millrace twice in her life, and both times she had sworn on her mother's grave that she would never do it again. Her mother's grave was in Kiel, three thousand miles away and unlikely to be consulted, which was the kind of oath a person made when they knew they were lying to themselves.

The third time, it turned out, was the charm. Or the coffin. She hadn't decided yet.

"You're sure about this channel," Marguerite said from beside her at the wheel. He said it quietly, the way he said everything, as though the fog itself might be offended by volume.

"I'm sure it's a channel," Ilsa said. "Whether it's *this* one is between me and the river."

The fog came off the water in long gray ropes, thick enough to chew. The ferry's lantern threw a pale circle maybe six feet wide, and beyond that the world simply stopped existing. Somewhere to their left—she thought left—was the garrison gate at Millbrook, doubled patrols, searchlights sweeping the bank like impatient lighthouse keepers who had lost their lighthouse. Somewhere ahead was the millrace: half a mile of compressed river where the old mill dam funneled everything through a gap barely wider than the ferry herself, at a speed the river had no business achieving.

And somewhere under her feet, wrapped in oilcloth inside a crate marked COAL — HANDLE WITH DISCRETION, which Odile had insisted on painting herself because she found it funny, was a letter that would unmake an inheritance before midnight tomorrow.

Ilsa had carried troops once, years ago, under orders she couldn't refuse. She had refused the second time and lost her license for it. This—carrying a parcel of paper upriver through fog for no pay, with a hospital nurse who lied like a man confessing his first sin—this felt more honest than anything the harbor had asked of her in a decade.

"Tell me again why we didn't take the road," Marguerite said.

"Because the road has soldiers on it, and you have a face that apologizes."

"My face does not apologize."

"Your face apologized to me when you boarded. You stepped on my toe and apologized to your own boot." She adjusted the wheel a degree. "It's all right. Honest faces are useful. Nobody suspects a man who looks sorry to be alive."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. In the lantern light he looked younger than his ledger suggested—he'd told her about the ledger during their planning, page after page of names and last words, and she had thought: *of course*. Of course the quiet one kept the accounts nobody else would keep.

"Hand," she said.

"What?"

"Hand. On the rail. When we hit the race, you'll want to grab something, and I'm telling you now what to grab so you don't grab me."

* * *

They heard the millrace before they saw it, which was the correct order for encountering it. A low, continuous roar, like the harbor clearing its throat, growing teeth as it went. The fog dampened it strangely, so it seemed to come from everywhere—the water around them, the air above them, possibly from inside Ilsa's own chest.

"There's a marker buoy," Marguerite said. "You said there was a buoy."

"There was a buoy in 1894."

"And now?"

"And now we find out what the river did with it."

She cut the engine to a whisper and let the current take them, because the race took what it wanted regardless and it was kinder to pretend she'd offered. The ferry swung its nose toward the gap—Ilsa felt it in the wheel, that hungry sideways pull—and then the world simplified into noise and cold.

The first wave came over the bow like a debt collector. It took Marguerite's hat, which she would mourn later because it had been a good hat, the kind of hat that made him look like a respectable citizen instead of a fugitive nurse. The second wave took the coal crate's tarpaulin corner and tried to negotiate terms with the crate itself. The third wave took her balance, her certainty, and nearly the wheel.

"Hold!" she shouted, though hold against what, even she couldn't say. The ferry bucked sideways, wallowed, righted. The gap walls rose black on either side, closer than they had any right to be, close enough that she could hear the water slapping stone like applause from an audience that wanted them dead.

Then the stern lifted, absurdly, the way a dog lifts when it decides to jump a fence it hasn't measured, and for one long suspended moment they were flying—a ferry, a nurse, a widow's coal crate, and forty years of Ilsa Brandt's stubbornness, airborne together over white water—

—and then not flying, and the river had them.

* * *

Later, she would remember it in pieces. The wheel tearing loose from her grip. The hull striking something submerged with a sound like a church bell falling downstairs. Cold climbing her legs in a single greedy motion. And Marguerite—quiet, apologetic Marguerite—going over the side without a sound, the way he did everything, as if he didn't want to trouble anyone even while drowning.

She got the engine back. That was the whole of her skill and her prayer combined: hands on the wheel, throttle open a quarter, nose into the current. The ferry clawed out of the race's throat into the wide dark below the dam, rolling like a drunk leaving church, and Ilsa spun the wheel in a full circle looking for him.

Nothing. Fog and foam and the roar dying behind them.

"MARGUERITE!"

Her voice cracked on the second syllable, which annoyed her. She had spent twenty years being the woman who didn't crack. She cupped her hands and shouted again, and again, turning the ferry in slow circles through water that was busy swallowing evidence.

Then she saw the hand. Just the hand, and below it a sleeve, and below that a man clinging to the portside gunwale with both arms and whatever remained of his dignity, his legs streaming behind him like a flag of surrender.

She hauled him over the transom by his collar, and he came up coughing half the river back into it, and the first thing he did—before breathing properly, before standing—was press one hand flat against his own chest.

"The letter," he rasped. "Is it—"

"If you say 'is it wet' I'm putting you back."

He laughed, or coughed, or some hybrid of the two, and fumbled open his coat. Inside, wrapped against his shirt in oilcloth and tied with Odile's twine, the parcel sat exactly where he'd tucked it, warm from his skin, dry as a sermon. He held it up in the lantern light like a priest displaying a relic.

"Dry," he said, with the wonder of a man who had just been personally introduced to his own mortality and found it carrying paperwork.

"Of course it's dry. You tied it like Odile taught you. That woman ties knots that outlive nations." She shoved a blanket at him. "Sit down before the river changes its mind."

He sat. He shivered. And then, absurdly, soaked to the bone on the deck of an unlicensed ferry at eleven o'clock at night, he started laughing—low, helpless, genuine laughter, the first she'd ever heard out of him.

"What," she said.

"We passed." He wiped his eyes with a blanket-covered hand. "We actually passed. The garrison gate is behind us and nobody saw us, unless the fish are taking notes."

"The fish," Ilsa said, "have better things to record than we do."

But she was grinning, she realized. Grinning like a girl at the helm of her father's boat thirty years ago, before licenses and refusals and the long gray years of doing nothing that mattered. She turned the ferry's nose upriver and let the grin stay. Some things deserved to be worn a while.

* * *

They ran dark after that, lantern shuttered to a slit, engine murmuring, the banks sliding past invisible except where a farmhouse window burned orange through the fog. Marguerite changed into the spare clothes she kept in the cabin locker—her late husband's, too broad in the shoulder, rolled at the cuffs—and looked, she told him, like a scarecrow who had come into money.

"Better than a drowned rat," he said.

"Barely. Here." She poured two fingers of plum brandy from the bottle she kept for emergencies, and handed it over, and considered the fact that she had never once shared it with another living soul. "For the shaking. It's medicinal."

"So is the ledger," he said, and drank, and coughed, and she laughed at him outright.

They sat in the cabin while the river carried them, shoulder to shoulder against the cold stove, and the silence between them stopped being empty and started being the good kind—the kind two people can sit inside without measuring. Ilsa found she didn't want to fill it. That was new. Silence, for her, had always been a room with furniture missing.

"He loved this river," she said finally. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Johan. My soldier. The one whose name you showed me."

Marguerite didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was careful, the way it was when he read the last words back. "Page sixty-one. *'Tell Ilsa the river was never the enemy.'*"

"That's what he wrote?"

"That's what he said. I wrote it after."

She nodded and looked out at the black water sliding past, and found that it didn't hurt the way she'd expected. It hurt the way a knot hurts coming undone—worse first, then better.

"I refused to carry troops because they put boys on my boat like cargo," she said. "Stacked them against the rails. Johan was one of them, the first crossing. He helped me tie down a crate that came loose, and he thanked me for the rope, like I'd done him a kindness instead of a delivery to a slaughterhouse." She turned the brandy bottle in her hands. "Everyone told me to sign the papers afterward. Take the license back. Say the refusal was a misunderstanding. Twenty years of everyone telling me I could disappear gracefully."

"And you wouldn't."

"Disappearing gracefully is still disappearing." She glanced at him. "You know that better than most. You've been practicing longer than I have."

He was quiet a moment. "I used to think invisibility was safety," he said. "Now I think it's just drowning with better posture."

Ilsa barked a laugh that startled even her. "Odile will want that painted on the tavern wall."

"She'd paint it wrong on purpose."

"She would. And we'd let her." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and checked the compass by feel. The fort's lights should be visible within the hour—no, past the fort, beyond it, to the landing where Odile's cousin kept a cart and a horse named after a general he despised. "Come here. Learn this heading. If I go over the side, you'll need to know which way is home."

"I don't know how to steer."

"Nobody knows how to steer. They learn, or the river teaches them, and the river charges more." She pulled him to the wheel by his borrowed sleeve. "Left hand here. Small movements. The ferry forgives small mistakes; it's only the large ones that end careers."

He put his hands where she showed him, and she stood close enough to correct him, and for a while neither spoke. The fog thinned enough to show stars, the first she'd seen in a week, scattered over the water like spilled salt. His steering was terrible. It improved slowly. At some point his shoulder settled against hers and stayed, and neither of them moved it, because moving it would have required explaining, and some things are better left steered by feel.

* * *

They cleared the fort at half past one in the morning, running dark along the far channel, and Ilsa watched the checkpoint lamps burning on the quay—two soldiers, a brazier, a man warming his hands and thinking about his supper—and felt the strange vertigo of having beaten something without fighting it. No shots. No shouts. Just a river, a fog, and two people who had decided the same thing on the same night without discussing it first.

"They're looking for a smuggler," Marguerite said softly, watching the lights recede. "Someone sneaking. Not a nurse and a ferry pilot going about lawful business."

"We're not going about lawful business."

"No. But we look like we might be. That's all invisibility ever was—looking like you belong to the story they've already written." He paused. "I think I'm finished with it, though. The invisibility."

"Oh?"

"When this letter lands. When the court reads the names." He flexed his hands, as if testing whether they still belonged to him. "I'm going to stand somewhere public and be extremely visible. Possibly loudly."

"God help the harbor," Ilsa said, and meant it fondly.

The landing lamp appeared through the thinning fog, a yellow smudge that grew into Odile's cousin waving both arms, the cart horse stamping steam into the cold. Ilsa brought the ferry alongside with a bump that would have embarrassed her any other night. Marguerite climbed onto the planks, parcel pressed to his chest, and turned back to offer her his hand—an apology of a hand, a careful hand.

She ignored it and hugged him instead, brief and hard, the way you hug someone you've pulled out of a river.

"The letter weighs nothing," she said into his ear, "and I felt it the whole way. Like ballast made of names."

"It does weigh," he said. "That's what I keep learning. Everything worth carrying does."

He stepped off onto the dock. The cart creaked. Odile's cousin muttered something about generals and horses and hours. And Marguerite Zhou, dripping river water and wearing another man's coat, walked up the plank road toward New Year's Eve with his shoulders straighter than Ilsa had ever seen them.

Behind him, on the water, the fog was lifting. Ahead of him, at the harbor fort, Captain Reyes was already awake.
