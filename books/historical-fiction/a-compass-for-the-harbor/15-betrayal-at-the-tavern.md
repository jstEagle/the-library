## 15. Betrayal at the Tavern

Odile Marchetti had been pouring drinks for twenty-six years, and in that time she had learned to read a room the way sailors read weather. A tavern was never quiet; it only *changed*. The clink of glasses became the clink of coins changing hands. Laughter thinned to something brittle. And a man who had nursed the same beer for two hours, saying nothing, watching the door—well. That man was not drinking. He was counting.

She noticed Emile Duquesne on the Thursday before New Year's Eve, wedged into the corner booth by the window, where the fog came in off the harbor and settled against the glass like an unpaid guest. Emile had been a regular since before her husband Tomas died—a stevedore with a bad back and a worse singing voice—and he had always had the appetite of a man being paid by the pound. Tonight he had ordered nothing but a single cider, and he had not touched it.

That was her first warning.

Her second came when Captain Reyes walked in at half past six.

* * *

Reyes was not the sort of officer who drank at taverns. Officers of his stripe took their brandy in officers' messes, where the silver was polished and nobody spat when your back was turned. So when the door swung open and the cold rolled in ahead of him, Odile felt her stomach drop through the floorboards and keep going, down past the cellar, down toward whatever dark place held the things she did not let herself think about during business hours.

He hung his cap on the peg by the door like a man who meant to stay a while. The room went on talking—the way rooms do, that brave collective pretense—but the talk got higher and faster, the sound of people whistling past a graveyard.

"Cider," Reyes said, taking the stool at the end of the bar. "Whatever's local."

"Nothing's local," Odile said. "Everything comes upriver on a ferry."

Something flickered across his face at the word *ferry*, quick as a fish under ice. "Then I'll have whatever came upriver most recently."

She poured it without ceremony. Her hands were steady, because she had trained them to be steady in worse rooms than this. She had sat across from quartermasters who could make a widow's pension vanish with a signature. She had poured for colonels who pronounced sentence on whole districts between courses. A tired captain with wet boots was not going to unmake her.

"You run a clean establishment," Reyes said, looking around. "I'll grant you that."

"The health inspector agreed. Two years running."

"That's not what I meant." He turned the glass a quarter turn, then another. Odile had watched men fidget with their drinks for twenty-six years, and she knew this particular habit: it belonged to a man rehearsing. "Mrs. Marchetti. Odile." He corrected himself, almost gently. "A nurse came through here. Marguerite Zhou. Possibly with a girl. Possibly alone. I'm authorized to offer compensation for reliable information regarding his whereabouts."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because yours is the last tavern inside the harbor district that hasn't been searched, and I'd rather search it than pay you. Honestly, I would. It's cheaper."

Odile dried a glass that was already dry. "Search it, then."

"I'd rather not. It's New Year's Eve tomorrow, and my men have families, and I have a conscience, thin as it's gotten." He set a coin on the bar—not a coin at all, she saw, but a promissory chit stamped with the garrison seal, worth more than her cellar was full. "You were married to Tomas Marchetti. Dockworker. Died in the warehouse collapse on Pier Four, winter of 'ninety-one. There was no inquiry. No compensation. No record beyond a line in a shipping ledger noting spoiled cargo." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the worst thing about him, because they were kind. "I know what it is to lose someone and have the account settle wrong. I'm not your enemy, Mrs. Marchetti. I'm just the man holding the broom."

"Then sweep," Odile said, "and get out of my house."

He left the chit anyway. She swept it into the spittoon after he'd gone, and if the regulars noticed her hand shaking as she pulled the next three pints, none of them were fool enough to say so.

It was Emile Duquesne who slipped out ten minutes later, coat collar up, moving fast for a man with a bad knee.

Odile watched him go through the window glass, and she thought—not yet believing it, saving it the way one saves a receipt—*there's my informant.*

* * *

Marguerite came at nine.

He came the way he always came, which was barely at all: through the kitchen door instead of the front, hat low, shoulders folded inward, taking up less space than any grown man she had ever served. Nell came behind him, bundled in a coat three sizes too big—Ilsa's, by the smell of coal smoke on it—and the girl looked around the taproom with the frank hunger of a child cataloguing warmth.

"Back room," Odile said quietly. "Both of you. Now."

Marguerite read her face the way she'd seen him read charts and ledgers, all at once and completely. "What happened?"

"A captain with a conscience drank my cider. Move."

The back room was really a former storeroom, twelve feet by ten, smelling of hops and lamp oil, containing a cot, a stove, and twenty years of hidden history she did not advertise. She got them settled, fed them—stew, bread, the good butter she hid from the regulars—and only when Nell was asleep on the cot, curled like a comma, did Odile sit across from Marguerite at the little table and tell him.

"He gave me a chit. For information. I put it where chits belong."

Marguerite almost smiled. Then he didn't. "There was someone else. Someone who took it."

"There's always someone else." She turned her cup in her hands. It was her dead husband's cup, chipped at the lip; she drank from it every night and refused to replace it, on principle she could no longer fully articulate. "Twenty-six years, I've kept this place. You learn the ones who'd sell you for less than they'd sell themselves. Emile's been coming here since before my Tomas died. I sang at his wedding, God help me."

"You're certain it was him?"

"I'm certain he left within ten minutes of the captain leaving, and I'm certain he hadn't touched his cider, and I'm certain that when I mentioned ferries earlier today in general conversation, he asked me no questions and remembered everything." She met his eyes. "Yes, child. I'm certain."

Marguerite stood. It was a small motion—he was a small-motion man—but it had finality in it, the way a door has finality when it closes. "Then I leave tonight."

"You leave tonight with nowhere to go, in fog thick enough to bottle, with a child asleep on my cot."

"She can sleep till midnight. Then we go."

"Marguerite." Odile heard her own voice drop into the register she used to stop fistfights. "Sit down."

He didn't sit. He stood at the narrow window—the one that faced the alley, because of course his instincts had chosen the room with two exits—and looked out at nothing, and Odile watched something work itself in him like a knot in wet rope.

"When Tomas died," he said, without turning around, "you told me once. The collapse. And no one was ever held to account."

"That's the short version."

"How do you stand it? Not the grief. The—" His hand made a vague gesture at the air, at the world entire. "The arithmetic of it. Being erased and having to keep living beside the people who did the erasing, pouring their drinks."

Odile considered her answer, because he deserved a real one. "Badly, some years," she said. "And then I remind myself that I keep a ledger too. Mine's just written in bread and beds. Every soul I've hidden, every mouth I've fed when the papers said they didn't exist—that's my entry against theirs." She shrugged. "Tomas used to say belonging isn't a thing you're given. It's a thing you practice, daily, like scales. Some days you practice alone. That's all."

"And if practicing it gets you arrested?" He turned now, and his face in the lamplight was young and old at once. "If my presence here—"

"Stop." She said it flatly. "You'll not apologize me into the poorhouse, boy. I knew the cost of this room when I lit the stove in it. Twenty years I've kept it ready. Do you think I kept it for guests I liked?"

That surprised him. She saw it land.

"Who was the first?" he asked softly.

"A deserter. Winter of 'seventy-nine. Boy of nineteen, wet through, couldn't stop shivering long enough to lie to me about why he'd run. I put him in this room and told the regulars my cousin was visiting from upcountry." She smiled at the memory, crooked and fond. "He stayed four days and fixed my roof before he went, which is more than my cousins have ever done. After him there was a printer. Then a woman whose husband had been transported. Then others. You learn to see them, the ones the records have chewed up. They walk like you walk, Marguerite. Like the floor might charge them rent for standing."

Nell stirred on the cot and murmured something in her sleep—a name, maybe, or a protest—and both of them went quiet until she settled again. When Odile looked back, Marguerite had his hand flat against his chest, over the pocket where the letter rode. He did that often, she'd noticed. The way other men touched lockets or worry-stones.

"She asked me today," he said, "what 'probate' means. She's eleven. I told her it means deciding who a dead person's things belong to, and she said, 'Shouldn't it just belong to whoever they loved?' and then went back to asking whether the stove was warm enough." His voice did something complicated on the word *loved*. "Out of the mouths of babes, the whole century indicted."

"Midnight, then," Odile said, rising. "Not before. If Emile talked, they'll want daylight for a raid—officers don't care for cellars in the fog, and Reyes strikes me as a man who does his ransacking by the book, even his illegal ones. You'll have hours yet. Sleep if you can."

"I don't much, these days."

"No," she agreed. "None of us who keep ledgers do."

At the door she paused, and on impulse older than reason, she kissed his forehead the way she had once kissed Tomas's, and said, "You're practiced at belonging here now. Four days. That counts."

He didn't answer. But when she glanced back from the doorway, he was sitting again at the little table, taking out his ledger, writing by lamplight—and for once he wasn't hiding it beneath his arm when she looked.

* * *

They came at half past two in the morning.

Odile was below, doing the books, when the front door went in—not knocked, not announced, simply *in*, hinges complaining, boots flooding the taproom in a tide of lantern light. She counted eight of them, plus Reyes, plus one more shape hanging back in the doorway with his collar up: Emile Duquesne, come to watch his handiwork, unable even to look at her.

She climbed the stairs slowly, wiping her hands, giving herself the full length of the staircase to compose her face.

"Gentlemen," she said. "We're closed."

"Captain Reyes," Reyes said, though she had already known, "we received word of fugitives harbored on these premises. I'm obliged to search."

"Obliged," Odile repeated. "By whom?"

"By my orders."

"Orders." She tasted the word. "My husband had orders once. To stack cargo past the marked line on Pier Four, because the manifest was behind and the tide wouldn't wait. He followed his orders. They found him under four tons of it, and the inquiry lasted one afternoon." She descended the last step. "So forgive me, Captain, if the word doesn't open my doors."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—she thought she saw it again, that awful kindness in him, that tiredness. "Back room and cellar," he said to his men, quietly. "Careful with the stock. Mrs. Marchetti's a taxpayer."

They were not careful with the stock.

They pulled the cot apart. They emptied the flour bin onto the floor. One of them found the loose brick behind the stove where she kept Tomas's watch and the deed to the building, and he tossed the watch to another, who caught it, weighed it, and put it in his pocket, and Odile wrote his face into memory with the precision of a woman engraving plate: red-haired, gap-toothed, third button of his greatcoat missing. There would be an accounting. There was always, eventually, an accounting.

The cellar yielded nothing, because there was nothing in the cellar to yield—Marguerite and the girl had gone at midnight, up the alley, toward the water, toward Ilsa, toward the millrace and whatever madness waited downstream. But the *room*. The room itself was evidence enough: a cot recently slept in, two cups washed and drying, a child's bootprint in the flour dust, small and unmistakable.

"They were here," Reyes said. Not triumphant. Worse—relieved, in the grim way of a man whose worst suspicion has been confirmed so he can stop dreading it. "How long?"

"Long enough to eat," Odile said. "They were hungry. Children usually are."

"Where did they go?"

"You'd know better than I. You seem to have watchers everywhere."

He came and stood before her, and up close he smelled of river water and cheap tobacco and exhaustion. "Mrs. Marchetti. Harboring a wanted man in possession of stolen military correspondence carries consequences I can't soften twice."

"Then don't soften anything." She lifted her chin. "Arrest me or buy a drink. Standing in my own taproom is free."

He looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, the red-haired soldier was still turning Tomas's watch over in his fingers, and something in Odile's chest went hot and bright and dangerous, the way it had gone the day they carried her husband home on a plank.

"Do you know what I remember about the Pier Four inquiry?" Reyes said, unexpectedly. "I was a lieutenant then. I filed the report. I noted the overloaded stacks in the margin, and my colonel struck it out, and I let him." He put on his cap. "Take her in. Custody at the garrison, not the fort—she's to be treated as a material witness, not a criminal. Feed her properly." And then, to Odile, quieter, almost apologetic: "The watch will be returned. I'll see to it personally."

"You'll see to nothing personally," Odile said, holding out her wrists with a dignity she intended to make theatrical, "until you see to the century, Captain, which appears to be ending badly."

The handcuffs were colder than the fog.

As they walked her out through her own front door, past her own shattered taproom, past Emile Duquesne shrinking against the doorframe—she stopped, one heartbeat, and said to him, pleasantly, "Your tab is paid, Emile. Consider yourself free of this house forever"—and then she lifted her foot over the threshold like a bride, backwards, deliberate, a superstition she invented on the spot and would carry with her into every room they put her in thereafter.

*You will not keep this either,* she told the soldiers, the night, the dying year itself. *You take rooms. You take watches. You do not keep people.*

* * *

They did not find the note until morning, and by then it did not matter, because Odile had already written it, sealed it, bribed the milk-boy to deliver it, and composed herself in her cell to await results.

It went to the ferry pier, addressed in her round schoolroom hand: *For the pilot Brandt, urgent.*

Inside, one line:

*They took me. Room's compromised. The boy knows the route and the girl knows her name—see they use both. Twenty years I kept that room warm for strangers. Turns out it was warming mine. — O.M.*

And beneath it, because she was who she was, a postscript:

*P.S. — Tell Marguerite the arithmetic still balances. He was never the debt. He was the payment.*
