## 16. The Price of Carrying

The cellar smelled of lamp oil and turnips, and Marguerite had been staring at the same brick for the better part of an hour.

It was a good brick. Dependable. It asked nothing of him and threatened nothing, which was more than he could say for the letter folded inside his coat, or the girl asleep upstairs in Odile's bed, or the widow herself sitting in a cell at the garrison house because she had let him in through her door like a fool with a warm heart and a bad sense of arithmetic.

He had done the sums a hundred times since dawn. He was good at sums. His father had insisted on it — *a merchant who cannot count is a merchant who deserves to be robbed* — though his father had never specified what a man should do when the thing being counted was not coin but conscience, and the ledger refused to balance no matter how many ways he turned the figures.

Option one: burn the letter. Take Nell by the hand before first light, walk out along the marsh road where the fog sat thick as wool, catch the morning packet south under false names, and be nobody again by Thursday. Nobody was a country Marguerite knew well. He spoke its language fluently. He had lived there thirty-one years and the rent was cheap: you paid in silence and the neighbors left you alone.

Option two was everything else.

"You could at least light a second candle," he told the brick. The brick declined to comment. It was, on reflection, the only sensible conversation partner he'd had all week.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Small feet. Nell was supposed to be sleeping, but Nell treated sleep the way she treated most authority — as a suggestion from someone with no real claim over her. She came down the ladder with the particular care of a child who has already decided that being told to stay in bed was a rule with an expiration date, and that date had passed around midnight.

"It's still dark," she observed, settling onto the crate beside him without invitation. She was wearing her coat over her nightdress, buttoned wrong, one sock missing. She looked like a small, disorganized admiral.

"It's nearly four."

"That's still dark." She peered at the table, where he had laid out his life's ruin in a tidy row: the colonel's letter, broken seal facing down; his own ledger; a stub of pencil; two tickets for the southern packet that he had bought an hour ago from a man who didn't ask questions because Marguerite had paid enough for three and only taken two, and men who profit from ambiguity do not press it. "Are we going somewhere?"

"I'm thinking about it," Marguerite said.

"Thinking about going or thinking about staying?"

"You're eight years old. You shouldn't be able to ask questions like that."

"I'm nine," said Nell. "And Papa says—" She stopped. That happened sometimes; her father surfaced in her sentences like a fish rising, and she would watch the water close over him again before deciding whether to grieve or continue. This time she continued. "Papa said thinking out loud is cheaper than a doctor. So think out loud, then. I'll listen."

Marguerite looked at her. In the guttering light of the single candle, with one sock and all those buttons in the wrong holes, she was the closest thing to an audience his conscience had ever had. He found, to his mild horror, that he obeyed.

"There's a letter," he said. "If I carry it where it's meant to go, good people get what they were promised and bad people lose what they stole. But the bad people have soldiers, and checkpoints, and Captain Reyes, who is not cruel but is *thorough*, which is worse. If I go, I might be caught, and if I'm caught, everyone who helped me pays for it. Odile is already paying."

"Odile would say that's her own bill," Nell said. "She says that about everything. Mr. Fenwick broke a glass last month and she said it was her own bill for buying thin glasses."

"Odile isn't here to say anything. That's rather the point."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she reached out and touched the ledger, not opening it, just resting two fingers on the cover the way other children touched lucky charms. "Is my papa in there?"

"He's in there."

"Then take it with us," she said simply. "Whatever you decide. You can burn the letter if you have to. But not the book."

And there it was — the whole argument, settled by a child in a misbuttoned coat, cutting through his careful weighing of risks like a hot knife through cold butter. He had been measuring escape against duty as though they were separate cargoes, as though he could set one down and carry the other. But the ledger went with him either way. The names went with him. And if he burned the letter — if he chose the marsh road and the false names and the long gray safety of being nobody —

He would be carrying the names and dropping the justice, and he had spent eleven years in fever wards learning exactly what that trade cost.

"Nell," he said, "go back up and put your other sock on. Not because we're leaving. Because your feet are blue and I refuse to explain frostbite to a girl whose father I knew."

She went, but slowly, and paused halfway up the ladder. "You talk better out loud," she informed him, and was gone.

* * *

Alone again, Marguerite did the thing he had been circling since sundown. He picked up the colonel's letter, unfolded it beneath the candle, and read it a fourth time.

Colonel Ashworth's hand had been a soldier's hand — square, unhurried, built for orders that other men would have to follow. The testament was short. Short documents frightened Marguerite least; long ones were where lawyers hid their knives. This one said plainly that the Harrowgate estate, its rents and lands and the considerable sum held in trust against the century's turning, would pass not to Governor Vane's nominated cousin — a man who had, so far as Marguerite could tell, never met the colonel except across a bargaining table — but to a list of named beneficiaries. Families of men who had served and died and been written off the rolls as deserters, as clerical errors, as *unclaimed*.

Marguerite had memorized the list on his second reading. He did not need the paper anymore, which meant the paper was, in the narrow sense, disposable — and this fact kept whispering to him in his father's voice. *Carry less. Burn what you can't afford to be caught with. A courier who delivers nothing is just a man holding evidence.*

But names are stubborn things. Once you have truly seen one, it does not fold back up.

He ran his finger down the list again anyway, the way a man touches a bruise to confirm it still hurts. Corrigan, widowed mother, Deptford Road — Nell's grandmother, though neither of them knew it yet, and the thought landed in his chest with a weight he hadn't budgeted for. Okafor, brother, ferryman's row. Whitlock, sister, three children. And then, seventh from the bottom, written in the same square hand as all the rest:

*Brandt, Tomas — husband of Odile Brandt, tavern-keeper, harbor district.*

Marguerite set the letter down very carefully, as though it had grown hot.

Tomas Brandt. He knew that name. Of course he knew it — every old regular at Odile's knew it, spoken in the past tense, in the way people speak of weather that happened before they moved to town. A bargeman. Twenty years gone. The story, when anyone told it at all, was vague in the specific way official stories are vague: lost with his cargo, records incomplete, no body, no pension, no grave. Odile had been a wife on a Tuesday and a widow by paperwork on a Friday, and the harbor had closed over the whole business like water over a dropped stone.

Marguerite had recorded men in his ledger for eleven years, and he had believed he understood what the ledger was for. It was remembrance. It was defiance in the smallest available denomination — a name written where no order could reach it. But he had thought of it as private work. Grief-work. A candle lit in a room with no windows.

This was different. The colonel had kept a ledger too, and his had teeth in it. His had *consequences* attached. Two kinds of record-keeping, and Marguerite had spent a whole night failing to see that they were the same work: the testimony of the overlooked, waiting for someone willing to stand up in a room and say it aloud.

And Odile's name was on it. Odile, who was at this hour sleeping on a plank cot because she had hidden him, was owed — by law, by wax and signature and the dead man's plain intent — a share of an estate bought with her husband's erasure.

Marguerite laughed once, short and unamused, into the dark.

"Well," he said to the brick, which remained agreeable, "that settles the arithmetic."

Because here was the ugly sum he had been avoiding: burning the letter would not protect Odile. Odile was already arrested; the letter's destruction would buy her nothing. Burning it would protect only *him*, and it would do so by completing what Reyes's masters had started. Tomas Brandt erased twice — once by the river, once by Marguerite's caution. Corrigan's mother denied twice. Every name on that list handed back to oblivion by the one man whose entire creed was that oblivion doesn't get the final entry.

He had told himself for eleven years that invisibility was humility. Walking home through the fog tonight — every night — he had called it survival, prudence, the wisdom of a man with a mixed face and no country who had learned early that standing out gets you hurt. All true. All genuinely true.

But invisibility had protected no one but himself. It hadn't saved his patients' names from the officers' reports. It hadn't saved Private Corrigan's daughter from the threat of the workhouse — that had been saved by lying, by standing in front of a clerk and being *seen*. It hadn't saved Odile. Being invisible was not a shield you held over other people. It was a coat you wore, and it fit only one person, and everyone around you stood in the weather.

His father had fled his disownment with ledgers full of debts owed to him, and had never once collected, and had died calling it dignity. Marguerite had inherited the ledgers and the instinct both. He could almost hear the old man now: *Don't be a hero, boy. Heroes are what other people bury.* And beside it, newer, smaller, in a child's voice: *Not the book.*

He took the two packet tickets, looked at them a moment, and fed them to the candle. They caught and flared and were gone, and Marguerite felt — not fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that, sloshing around in him like bilge water. Something underneath the fear. Something with a floor to it.

Purpose, he supposed. He'd heard men name it in the ward, in the last hour, when everything else had been stripped away and one thing remained upright. It looked less dramatic than advertised. Mostly it looked like a tired nurse folding a letter back into his coat, smoothing it flat against his chest, and beginning, methodically, to plan.

Three checkpoints. The customs house at the bridge was burned to him — Inspector Halloran knew his face now, and credibility, once spent, did not circulate back. The garrison gate at Millbrook had doubled patrols after the tavern raid; Ilsa had said, in the flat voice she used for things she'd already decided, that the millrace was runnable at night in fog, *if* the fog cooperated and *if* the river didn't want them dead, which made it roughly a coin flip conducted by God. And the fort at the river mouth would be sealed tomorrow night — New Year's Eve — gates shut, guns manned, the governor himself presiding over whatever ceremony rich men hold to celebrate having survived their own century.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow the century ended, and with it the legal machinery that would lock the Harrowgate inheritance behind the governor's seal forever. Whatever was going to happen had to happen at the one gate in the harbor that was designed to stop exactly this.

It was, by any honest measure, a terrible plan. It was barely a plan at all. It was a nurse, a revoked pilot, a stolen boat, a forged coal manifest, a nine-year-old in mismatched socks, and a dead colonel's paper, arrayed against the entire colonial apparatus of the harbor.

Marguerite blew out the candle, and in the dark he laid his palm flat over his chest, over the letter, the way he'd once laid it over the chests of dying men to feel how much time was left.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, he heard Nell's bed creak, and the small scrabbling of her getting up again, and then the whisper of pages. She had found the ledger where he'd left it, and she was reading page forty by moonlight, learning her father's name the way other children learned prayers.

That decided the last of it, if any had been left.

In the morning, he would find Ilsa. In the morning, they would count the hours until midnight. And at midnight, at the sealed gate of the harbor fort, the invisible man intended to be impossible to overlook.

He climbed the ladder quietly, so as not to disturb her — and found, when he reached the top, that she was already watching him from the bed, eyes bright, ledger open on her knees.

"It's my papa's birthday tomorrow," she said. "New Year's Eve. He always said so. Born at the stroke of midnight, and Mama used to ring a spoon on a glass."

Marguerite stood very still in the dark doorway of Odile's back room, doing sums again — but different ones now, the kind that balance.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we deliver him something."
