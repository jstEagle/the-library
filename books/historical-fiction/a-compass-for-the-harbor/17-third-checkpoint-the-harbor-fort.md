## 17. Third Checkpoint: The Harbor Fort

The trouble with being invisible, Marguerite had learned over thirty-one careful years, was that no one ever looked for you until the moment they wanted something you had.

He dressed for the opposite of invisibility on the last evening of the century, and it took him longer than he expected, because he had never before chosen clothes as an argument. The hospital whites first—starched, collar buttoned to the throat, the gray wool overcoat that marked him as senior nursing staff. Then the brass armband with the harbor hospital's caduceus, which he had polished with brick dust until it caught light like a small insolent sun. Into his breast pocket, where a man might carry a watch or a love letter, he slid the ledger. Inside the ledger, sewn flat against the back board with surgical thread and a curved needle borrowed from his own kit, lay Colonel Ashworth's letter.

He looked at himself in Odile's cracked mirror—the tavern was closed, its windows papered, Odile herself two days gone into the military cells across the river—and he saw what he intended the world to see: not a courier, not a conspirator, not even particularly a man. A function. A nurse delivering records on the one night of the year when records were demanded rather than merely tolerated.

"You look," Nell said from the doorway, "like someone who is going to be extremely rude to a soldier."

"That's the idea."

"Good." She was eleven years old and had spent the week learning to whittle, which she practiced on the tavern's chair legs with a dedication that would have bankrupted a lesser establishment. She had also, in the same week, learned forty names by heart, in order, with their last words attached, and recited them at supper like grace. "If they're rude first, be ruder. That's what Odile says."

"Odile," Marguerite said, "is currently eating army stew in a cell that I put her in."

Nell set down her knife. She had a way of looking at him—Corrigan's chin, Corrigan's level stare—that made lying feel like a waste of good material. "You didn't put her anywhere. Reyes did. And she'd say the same thing I'm saying: go get her out."

"I intend to."

"Then stop apologizing with your face and go."

He almost smiled. It was remarkable, how quickly a person could become accustomed to being spoken to like this—as though he were worth correcting. Two months ago, no one on earth would have dared. Two months ago, no one on earth had noticed him enough to bother.

* * *

The fog came off the harbor at dusk the way it always did in winter, thick as spilled milk, and tonight it carried fireworks smoke ahead of schedule—the governor's people testing their display from the fort's high battery, sulfur and saltpeter drifting across the water to mix with coal smoke and salt. The whole harbor smelled like the century ending.

Marguerite walked the long curve of the quay with his ledger under his arm and his hands, for once, empty.

That was the part that kept astonishing him. For weeks he had moved through the district like a man carrying a birdcage made of glass—every checkpoint a potential catastrophe, every uniformed back a question mark, every route planned twice and abandoned three times. He had hidden the letter in a crate of carbolic soap. He had hidden it in the false bottom of Ilsa's engine housing. He had hidden it inside a loaf of Odile's bread, which had been a mistake on every possible level, because the bread had been good and a garrison private had nearly eaten four hundred words of testament along with the crust.

And all of it had failed, or nearly failed, or would fail, because hiding was the one language the army spoke fluently. Hide a thing and you told every man with a bayonet that the thing was worth finding.

So tonight he carried it in plain sight, inside the most boring object in the entire colonial administration: a hospital ledger, volume nine, *Deaths, Ward C, October–December*, stamped and initialed and so relentlessly official that eyes slid off it like rain off oilcloth.

*No one stops a nurse delivering records.* He turned the sentence over as he walked, testing it the way you test ice. It held his weight so far. The fort had been sealed since noon for the governor's New Year ceremony—gates shut, drawbridge up, honor guard drilling on the parade ground with the fog swallowing their count—but sealed gates meant paperwork, and paperwork meant clerks, and clerks meant requisitions, and requisitions meant the quartermaster's office, which meant the hospital, which meant him. The chain of supply ran straight through men like Marguerite. It always had. That was the whole architecture of his life: the empire rested on the backs of people it never bothered to learn the names of.

Tonight he intended to walk up that chain and knock.

There was a complication, of course. There were complications the way there were waves. He counted them as he walked, because counting calmed him, and because Ilsa had taught him—shouting over her engine, the week before—that a pilot who feared everything equally crashed just as surely as a pilot who feared nothing.

Complication the first: Captain Reyes knew his face now. Whatever thin film of anonymity he had owned was spent at the customs house, spent again at the garrison gate by proxy, and spent entirely when soldiers tore apart Odile's back room and found a second bed still warm. Reyes would have his name. Whether Reyes had circulated it was another question.

Complication the second: the reward notice. He had seen one pasted to the customs house shutter that morning—*INFORMATION CONCERNING*, and beneath it a description that managed to make him sound both forgettable and dangerous, *a nurse of mixed blood, quiet manner, carries papers*, and beneath that a figure with two zeros more than Odile's tavern earned in a year. Half the district could buy its way out of debt with him.

Complication the third, the one he did not say aloud even in the privacy of his own skull: the letter had to reach the probate court, not the fort. But the probate court was closed until the second of January, and the estate's transfer executed at midnight, and the only man with the authority to interrupt a midnight transfer was standing inside those fort walls wearing the governor's own commission. Which meant the plan, to the extent that the word applied, was: walk in, find Reyes, and convince a hunting man that he had been hunted.

Ilsa had heard this plan in the tavern kitchen two nights ago and laughed until she had to sit down on the flour bin.

"And then what?" she'd said, wiping her eyes. "You'll read him the ledger? He'll shoot you and use your spine as a hat rack."

"He might," Marguerite had agreed. "But he won't do it in front of witnesses, and the governor's ceremony will be nothing *but* witnesses. That's my whole hand, Ilsa. I'm betting a man who's tired of his orders will take a chance to look at them honestly."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I'll have been unmistakable for approximately six minutes," Marguerite said, "which is six minutes longer than the last thirty-one years."

* * *

The road to the fort climbed away from the quay through streets gone strange with celebration. Every window held candles. Children ran door to door with paper lanterns on sticks, and somewhere up the hill a barrel organ played the same eight bars of a waltz over and over, as if even the music couldn't bear to let the year finish. Ordinary people, marking an ordinary turning. He passed a fishmonger nailing holly to his lintel and felt, absurdly, homesick—for a home that consisted of a tavern with papered windows and a cellar where a child read names aloud—and had to stop and breathe.

Grief, he was learning, did not wait for convenient hours. It ambushed him in doorways, at supper, mid-sentence, usually wearing Odile's face or Nell's, and it always arrived with the same accusation: *you did this by coming here. You did this by belonging.*

And underneath the grief, steady as ballast, the other thing. The thing that had gotten him out of bed and into the starched collar.

He thought about his father, who had spent his disowned merchant years insisting that respectability was a kind of armor, and who had died respectable and erased anyway. He thought about the colonel, who had spent his final feverish hours not confessing but *correcting*—pressing the pen of the dead into the hands of whoever happened to be near. He thought about page forty. Private Corrigan, who had used his last breath not on fear or God or family but on instructions: *Tell my girl the tide table's under my bunk, she'll know why,* which had made no sense whatsoever and had led Marguerite, following it literally, to a tin box containing eleven dollars and a photograph of Nell, and had thereby delivered a daughter out of a workhouse and into a tavern.

The dead left compasses everywhere. You only had to agree to follow them.

"Evening, sir." A sentry at the bottom of the fort road, young, rain-soaked, rifle held with the desperate formality of a boy who had been told the evening was important. Behind him the fort's wall rose black against the fireworks haze, and the great gates—oak and iron, big as church doors—stood shut, lamplit, flanked by two more sentries and a sergeant with a clipboard, which was somehow more menacing than the rifles.

"Evening," Marguerite said, and kept walking.

The sentry looked at the armband, the coat, the ledger, the face above all of it. His mouth opened around what was probably *the fort's closed*—

"Ward C requisitions," Marguerite said, before the sentence could land. He did not slow down. This was the first rule of carrying things through doors, and he had learned it from watching officers: authority was ninety percent momentum. "Ceremony medical support. The governor's aide requested the mortality returns before midnight—they're wanted for the transfer filings, and if they're late the delay gets logged to whoever's holding the gate. What's your name, son?"

"*Pemberton*, sir—" and then, catching up with himself, "but nobody's crossed since noon, sir, I'd have to ask the sergeant—"

"Ask the sergeant," Marguerite agreed pleasantly, and walked past him toward the clipboard while Pemberton performed the arithmetic of a New Year's Eve sentry: *annoying officer versus annoying clerk versus missing the fireworks entirely.*

The sergeant was older, grayer, and unimpressed by momentum. He looked at the armband, then at Marguerite's face, and something ticked behind his eyes—a flicker, quick as a match, of *where have I*—

Marguerite opened the ledger.

He had planned this too, in the cellar hours when planning was the only prayer available. He flipped it open at chest height, angled so the sergeant saw columns, dates, a stamp, the dense bureaucratic thicket of a death register, and nothing else. Page sixty-eight. Not page forty. Never page forty; page forty lived under his tongue now, along with every other name, arranged and ready.

"Mortality returns, Ward C, quarter ending December," he said. "Governor's office wants them countersigned tonight. Probate can't process the Harrowgate transfers without the December figures—it's all tied together, apparently, some question about beneficiaries deceased versus beneficiaries surviving. You know how the lawyers get at year's end."

He said *Harrowgate* the way a man says *coal shipment*: flatly, wearily, daring no one to find it interesting. The sergeant's match-flicker died unfed. Harrowgate was a word in every newspaper and half the gossip in the district; a man carrying paperwork about it was a man to be pitied, not searched.

"Seargeant," Pemberton offered helpfully from behind, "he asked my name, sir. Very proper."

The sergeant sighed the sigh of a man whose evening contained a clipboard and ended with a waltz he could hear but not attend. He wrote something. "Quartermaster's side gate, left of the main doors. Sign the book. If anyone in dress uniform asks, you're with the ceremony support detachment and you got lost, and I never saw you, because I'm not writing *that* up too."

"Understood," Marguerite said, and meant several things by it.

* * *

The side gate took him through a stone passage that breathed cold and gunpowder, and out onto the edge of the parade ground, and there the century showed itself off.

Lanterns by the hundred, strung between the cannon. A band tuning in the shadow of the keep. Officers in dress blues moving among civilians in silk, and at the far end, on a platform hung with bunting, the governor himself—smaller than his portraits, laughing at something, a champagne glass catching the light. Midnight was two hours off. The air tasted of anticipation and cordite, and beneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sea.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the light with a ledger under his arm and let himself be seen.

That was the whole strategy now, and it still felt like stepping off a pier. For thirty-one years he had curated his own absence—lowered eyes, mended step, the talent for standing precisely where sightlines broke. He had believed, right up until Odile's cell door clanged, that invisibility was safety. Now he understood the arithmetic differently. Invisibility had protected exactly one man, and had priced everyone else. So he stood in the open, conspicuous as a lighthouse, a brown-faced nurse in a gray coat at the governor's party, and he waited to be noticed, and when heads began to turn—slowly, the way heads turn toward something slightly wrong—he did not look away.

A steward approached with the harried expression of a man triaging anomalies. "Sir? This area's for—"

"Hospital," Marguerite said, showing armband and ledger together, one to each eye. "Mortality returns for the midnight filings. I'm told Captain Reyes has the authorization. Is he receiving?"

Something crossed the steward's face—not recognition, but proximity to it. "Captain Reyes isn't at the ceremony, sir. He's got the east bastion. Records duty." A pause, and then, with the delicate cruelty of servants everywhere: "Someone has to mind the paperwork while everyone else enjoys themselves."

"Then he and I will get along famously," Marguerite said.

The east bastion was a walk along the seaward wall, fog on one side and festivity on the other, and with every step the sounds of the party thinned until all that remained was the sea working at the rocks below and his own heartbeat, which had opinions. The letter sat against his chest, sewn into the ledger, weighing nothing and everything, the way it had weighed nothing and everything through fog and millrace and a burned safehouse and a tavern raid. He had carried it so long it had become less an object than an organ. Tonight, one way or another, he would set it down.

The bastion door stood ajar, lamplight spilling out. Within: a desk, a stove, a cot, a man sitting with his back to the door, cap off, reading by lamplight with the particular stillness of someone reading a thing for the hundredth time.

Marguerite knew the shoulders. He knocked once on the open door, and Captain Reyes turned, and Marguerite watched the recognition arrive in stages—surprise, calculation, and then the hand moving, without hurry, without drama, toward the drawer of the desk.

"Mr. Zhou," Reyes said. "I was rather hoping the harbor would deliver you."

"So was I," Marguerite said, and stepped inside, and pulled the door shut behind him.
