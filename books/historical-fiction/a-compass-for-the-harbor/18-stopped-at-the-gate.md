## 18. Stopped at the Gate

Captain Emilio Reyes had spent thirty-one years learning that the army was a machine for turning questions into paperwork. A man asked *why* often enough as a boy; by twenty he had learned to file the question instead of asking it, and by forty he could do it without wincing. The filing system was efficient. The filing system kept him fed, promoted, and — most nights — asleep.

Most nights. Not this one.

He stood in the shadow of the harbor fort's seawall with his pistol already loose in its holster, watching the fog come off the water in slow gray ribbons, and admitted to himself, for perhaps the fourth time in his career and the first time out loud even in the privacy of his own skull, that he did not know why anymore.

New Year's Eve, 1899. Behind him, inside the fort walls, the governor's people were stringing lanterns for the century-turning ceremony — bunting and brass and a band tuning strings that would go sharp in the cold. Ahead of him, down the coast road, a figure walked toward the gate carrying nothing but a leather ledger held open against the wind, reading as he walked, like a man saying his prayers with his eyes open.

Reyes had been given a description two days ago. *Hospital nurse, mixed blood, answers to Marguerite Zhou, carries papers.* He had been given an order too, though the order had arrived in the particular soft language men used when they wanted a thing done without their fingerprints on it. *Interview the individual. Recover any correspondence. The matter is sensitive to the Governor's Office.*

*The matter is sensitive.* Twenty years ago Reyes would have laughed at the phrase. Ten years ago he'd have simply obeyed. Tonight the words sat in his stomach like bad rations.

Because here was the thing about Captain Emilio Reyes that no report recorded, the thing filed so deep even he had trouble finding the drawer: he knew exactly what kind of correspondence this was. He had signed the requisitions. He had countersigned the transfers. He had watched the Harrowgate estate pass through four sets of hands on its way to the wrong ones, each set of hands cleaner than the last, until by the time it reached the governor's cousins it looked less like theft than weather — something that had simply happened to the land, the way rain happens, requiring no one's consent and leaving no one to blame.

He had told himself it was arithmetic. Estates were large. Families were complicated. Dead men's wishes were, practically speaking, negotiable, since dead men were famously poor litigants.

And then three days ago Odile Marchand had been dragged out of her own tavern with her hands tied at the wrists, and Reyes had stood in the doorway of that wrecked back room and seen, chalked above her stove where the soldiers hadn't thought to scrape it, a list of supper prices in her round, stubborn hand — *stew 3c, bread 1c, coffee free if you're wet* — and something in him had refused to file it. It kept surfacing. It surfaced while he ate. It surfaced while he slept, which was the worst part, because in the dream Odile charged him for the coffee anyway and he paid gladly and woke with wet eyes and no explanation he was willing to write down.

The figure on the road stopped ten paces away.

Up close, the nurse was younger than the reports implied and more tired. Fog beaded in his dark hair. He held the ledger against his chest now, both arms wrapped around it the way a man holds a child crossing ice, and he looked at Reyes with an expression Reyes had never once received from anyone in uniform or out of it: not fear, not defiance. Recognition. As if Reyes were the one who had been described to *him*, all the way down to the parts that didn't show.

"Captain Reyes," Marguerite said. His voice was hoarse, as though he had been talking all day, or all year. "You're alone."

"I'm alone." It was true. He had sent his sergeant to the north wall with instructions that made sense only as an act of quiet sabotage: *walk slowly.*

"You came without witnesses."

"The matter," Reyes said, and heard how the borrowed phrase curdled in his own mouth, "is sensitive."

Something almost like a smile crossed the nurse's face. "Yes. I've heard."

"The letter." Reyes held out his hand, palm up, and hated the gesture — it was the gesture of a customs man, a toll-taker, small. "Whatever Ashworth gave you. Whatever you're carrying to probate. Give it to me, and you walk home tonight, and none of what happens after touches you. That's the best offer anyone will make you this century, and I haven't got much century left to make it in."

Marguerite Zhou did not run. Reyes noted that first, professionally, with a strange relief — he did not want to chase the man, did not want whatever happened next to happen fast. Instead the nurse stood very still, and the fog moved around them both, and somewhere behind the wall the band found its note and lost it again.

"If I give it to you," Marguerite said, "it dies. You know that. You've seen things die in files before."

Reyes did not answer, which was an answer.

"I thought so," Marguerite said gently, and there was no triumph in it, which somehow made it unbearable. "Then let me make you an offer instead, Captain. Not the letter. The letter isn't mine to trade — I'm only the courier, and I know what that makes me worth. But before you take it from me, you'll stand there and listen to what else I'm carrying. It weighs nothing. It's the heaviest thing I've ever held. And then you can decide whether your orders still fit in your hands."

"That's not—" Reyes began, *that's not how this works*, and stopped, because of course that was exactly how this worked, this was how everything worked, men standing in the cold trading what they carried. He had just never been on the side holding nothing.

Marguerite opened the ledger.

* * *

Later — much later, when he tried to reconstruct it, Reyes could never recover the sequence, only the sensation, like being read to at a graveside — later he would remember that the nurse did not shout. That was the weapon of it, he finally understood. Any fool could shout testimony into a crowd and be dismissed as a madman. This man read names in the voice other men used for inventory, level and unhurried and terribly precise, as if each name were a coin counted out onto a table between them.

"Private Daniel Corrigan," he read. "Eleventh of December. He said, *Tell my Nell the tide comes back.* He had a daughter. She's nine. She has his handwriting — she copies letters off envelopes because she likes the loops."

The fog took the name and gave it back.

"Gunner Elias Wren. Ninth of December. He said, *Tell Ilschen the river runs both ways.* He loved a ferry pilot who lost her license for refusing to carry troops, and he thought — he wrote this in his last lucid hour, Captain — he thought she was the bravest person in the province, braver than any man he'd served under, and he included you in that number without ever meeting you."

Reyes's jaw tightened. "You don't know who you're talking to."

"I know exactly who I'm talking to." Marguerite turned a page. The paper whispered. "Sergeant Anton Ferreira. Fourth of November. He said, *My wife feeds everyone, tell her to keep feeding them on my account.* He was erased from the garrison rolls eleven years ago, Captain. Struck out with a clerk's pen. His wife has been feeding fugitives out of her own kitchen ever since, and three days ago your soldiers put her in irons in her own tavern."

"Stop," Reyes said.

It came out harder than he intended. His hand had drifted to the holster without consulting him, and now the pistol was out, low against his thigh, and he stared at it with something like betrayal. When had he drawn it? What precisely did he intend it to accomplish — shoot the names out of the air?

"He was my friend," Reyes said quietly. "Ferreira. We drank together in '88. Then he was gone and nobody would tell me where, and I filed the question." He heard himself say it, heard the word *filed* break in half coming out. "That's what I do. That's all I am anymore. I file questions until they stop being questions."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "I know. I used to be the same kind of man. I hid under a floorboard and called it care." He did not lower the ledger. If anything he held it higher, angled so the lantern light from the gate fell across the pages. "One more, Captain. Then you can decide."

"Don't."

"Santiago Reyes," Marguerite read. "Fourteenth of October. Fever ward, third cot from the window, the one that catches the draft. He said—"

"Stop." The word came out cracked down the middle.

"—he said, *Emilio will understand why I never wrote. Tell him it wasn't shame, it was protection, and tell him he was always the better of us two.*"

The pistol was shaking. Reyes watched it shake with detached, clinical interest, the way he had once watched his own hands shake during the fever winter when Santiago had — when his little brother had —

Santiago. Who had enlisted under a shortened name so the family wouldn't be shamed by his debts. Who had written exactly one letter in six years, unsigned, and Reyes had filed it, literally filed it, third drawer, and told himself his brother was dead because dead was simpler than estranged. Who had died in a fog-bound hospital with a draft on his cot and a stranger keeping watch over his last words because no one else in the whole machinery of the empire had thought those words were worth recording.

No report had reached him. He understood that now with terrible clarity. There had never been going to be a report. Men like Santiago didn't generate reports. They generated entries in other men's ledgers, private ones, kept under floorboards by careful invisible men.

"You went through my brother's effects," Reyes said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "You buried him?"

"There was no one to notify. He listed no next of kin." Marguerite paused. "But he talked about you. Every night of the fever he talked about you. The better of you two — that's what he called it, *the better of us two*, like a title he was handing over. I've carried that sentence since October, Captain, waiting for someone to deliver it to. I didn't know until tonight it was you."

The fog rolled through the space between them, and Reyes became aware that he was crying, furiously, silently, the way men cry who have not done it since boyhood and are furious at the body for remembering how. He wiped his face with his sleeve, a rough childish swipe, and the pistol hung at his side now pointing at nothing, at the mud, at the old dying century.

"The governor's ceremony starts at eleven," he said at last, and his voice was wrecked but functional, a bridge with boards missing but still standing. "Probate closes at midnight. After midnight the estate transfer executes automatically and every court in the colony will call it legal forever." He holstered the pistol. His hand would not stop trembling, so he pressed it flat against his belt. "The east sally port is unlocked at quarter to twelve for the staff changing shift. Nobody checks credentials on New Year's Eve — they check punch bowls. You'll never get through the front gate, but you won't need to. I have a sworn affidavit to file at the probate clerk's window before the bell finishes ringing. It's been in my coat for two days. I wrote it the night we arrested Marchand, and I've been walking around with it like a man carrying a lit stove, waiting to talk myself out of it."

Marguerite stared at him. For the first time all night, the careful invisible man looked genuinely unguarded — mouth open, ledger lowering an inch.

"You wrote it before I read you the names."

"I wrote it because of the stew prices," Reyes said, and heard how insane it sounded, and kept going anyway. "Chalked over her stove. Coffee free if you're wet. Twenty years of that, and we tied her wrists with cord." He exhaled, long and ragged, steam dissolving into fog. "You think you made me defect tonight, nurse. You didn't. You just showed me the receipt for something I'd already decided. Names help, though." He nodded at the ledger. "They'll help at the hearing. Courts can't argue with ink."

"And you?" Marguerite asked quietly. "What happens to you, when the affidavit bears your name?"

Reyes considered it. Disgrace, probably. A tribunal, certainly. Perhaps the same erasure Ferreira had suffered, struck from the rolls with a clerk's bored pen — and he found, examining the prospect honestly, that it weighed nothing at all beside the third drawer of his desk, which he intended to go home and empty tonight, and the single unsigned letter inside it, which he intended to read properly at last.

"My brother said I was the better of us two," he said. "I intend to spend about five minutes proving him right before midnight. Quarter to twelve. East sally port. Don't be late, and don't be early — the changing shift walks fast and doesn't look at faces." He hesitated, then, awkwardly, because warmth had never come naturally to his mouth and was arriving now like cargo off a listing ship: "Your Nell. Corrigan's girl. When this is done, there's a pension fund for dependents that requires a sponsoring officer. I'm apparently going to have sudden free time."

The bells began.

Not midnight yet — the quarter-hour, thin and silver across the water — but enough. Enough to move. Marguerite closed the ledger and hugged it once, hard, to his chest, and Reyes saw that the man's eyes were bright too, and neither of them mentioned it, because some treaties are signed in silence.

"East sally port," Marguerite repeated. Then, already turning, already quickening down the fog-bound road toward the walls and the lanterns and the closing century: "Captain — the coffee at Odile's really is free if you're wet."

"I know," Reyes said, following him into the dark. "I'm buying a round anyway."

Behind them the first firework of the evening went up, gold and brief, and the old century began, at last, to let go.
