## 19. Before the Century Turns

### I.

Captain Reyes had held many things in his hands in twenty-two years of service—rifles, dispatches, the folded flag at his father's funeral, his brother's last letter, returned unopened because the army had already read it and found nothing worth keeping.

He had never held anything like this ledger.

Marguerite Zhou stood on the frozen mud outside the harbor fort with his coat open and the book held up between them like a lantern, and he was reading names. Not shouting. Reading them the way a man reads scripture in a language only half his own—carefully, stumbling sometimes, going back to get it right. Private Corrigan. Sergeant Okafor. A boy called Tomas Brandt whose entry said only *asked for his mother, then asked for the fog to lift, then stopped asking.*

Reyes's pistol was still in his hand. It felt suddenly like a thing he'd picked up at a market by mistake.

"Say that name again," Reyes said.

"Tomas Brandt." Marguerite did not lower the ledger. His breath came out in small white flags. "He served under you at Millbrook, Captain. He died on a Tuesday. There is no Tuesday in any report you filed."

The bells of Saint Adelheid's began somewhere behind the fog, counting toward midnight. Ten. Eleven.

"There is a probate clerk," Reyes heard himself say, "on Rue de la Chancellerie who keeps his office open until the year turns. It is the one night a year he cannot refuse a sworn deposition. After midnight, the Harrowgate transfer executes."

Twelve strokes, far off, bronze and patient, the old century letting go of everything it had been holding.

"I know," Marguerite said. "That is why I am standing here instead of running."

Reyes looked at the ledger. At the boy—no, not a boy, a nurse, a man who had spent years writing down what the army paid him to forget. At the fog rolling off the river like something being poured out.

"Give me the letter," Reyes said, and put his pistol away.

* * *

### II.

The governor's ceremony at the fort was, by every account that would later be given, a triumph.

There were lanterns. There were speeches. The governor's aide had composed four hundred words about the century dying "as a soldier dies—honorably, in formation," which was the sort of sentence only a man who had never watched anyone die could write, and the governor delivered all four hundred of them while standing beneath a banner reading PROGRESS AND PERPETUITY, because whoever had ordered the banner had not checked whether the lettering would fit, so that the final word read simply PERPET-U, which several officers agreed was an improvement.

Champagne was poured. The band played. And then, at seven minutes past midnight, a junior clerk arrived from Rue de la Chancellerie with a document that had been sworn, stamped, and entered into record at one minute to twelve, and the triumph developed a crack in it, and then another, the way ice does when the thaw comes quietly underneath.

The governor read the document twice. Then he read the signature, which belonged to Captain Emilio Reyes, who had been, until that moment, the most reliable man in his employ, and who had apparently walked out of his own commander's ceremony in full uniform to deposit a dead colonel's testament with a probate clerk before the clock could save anybody anything.

"Where is Reyes?" the governor asked.

No one knew. Someone suggested the tavern. Someone else suggested that perhaps the captain had gone home ill, and the governor looked at this officer for a long moment with an expression that made the officer wish, sincerely and permanently, that he himself had gone home ill.

By half past midnight the rumor had reached the champagne table: the Harrowgate estate—the one everyone had assumed would dissolve politely into the colonial fund, into accounts, into the comfortable nowhere where inconvenient money goes—had been willed, in a sealed testament dated the previous spring, to a list of families no official record had ever bothered to keep. Fishermen. A ferry family. A tavern-keeper's widow. Names that sounded, one colonel observed into his glass, suspiciously like people.

"People," the governor said coldly, "do not inherit estates."

But the century had turned, and the paper was filed, and the old order discovered—as old orders always do, too late and all at once—that it had been cracking along seams drawn years ago by men who were already dead.

* * *

### III.

Ilsa Brandt heard the bells from the deck of her unlicensed ferry and counted all twelve without missing one, which she considered a professional accomplishment, since her hands were still shaking.

They had run the millrace six hours ago. Her arms remembered it the way a burned hand remembers fire: the black water standing up in ridges, the garrison lamps smearing past through the fog like drowned stars, Marguerite clinging to the hull beside her with one arm hooked through the rail and the other pressed flat against his chest, holding the letter against his heart as though the river might be persuaded to take everything except that.

She'd screamed at him to let go if he went under. She'd meant it. She'd also known, with the certainty of a woman who has loved exactly two stubborn people in her life and lost both, that he wouldn't have.

Now the ferry sat quiet at Odile's pier, iced to the waterline, and Nell Corrigan sat on the roof of the cabin swinging her legs over the edge, and Marguerite stood at the rail listening to the bells finish, and Ilsa thought: *we actually did it.* Then she thought, more cautiously: *something always happens after we actually did it.*

"What time does the court close?" Nell called down.

"It stayed open till midnight," Marguerite said. "One night a year. The clerk hates the governor. It's the only honest thing left in this city and it runs on spite."

Nell considered this. "Spite seems like a strange foundation for justice."

"Spite is the best foundation for justice," Ilsa said. "Gratitude gets tired. Spite keeps regular hours."

Below them, at the tavern door, lamplight. Voices. And then—a sound none of them had let themselves hope for, a sound Ilsa felt in her sternum before her ears caught up—the rattle of the tavern's bolt being thrown back from the inside, which meant someone was unlocking it, which meant someone was home to unlock it.

Odile came out onto her own doorstep carrying a lamp like a woman carrying a torch she intended to hold parliament hostage with, and behind her, absurdly, apologetically, trailed the young constable who had arrested her, hat in hand, looking like a schoolboy returned to return a borrowed book.

"They released me at the gate," Odile announced to the whole harbor, in the voice she used for announcing the price of herring had gone up. "No charge. The arresting officer received new orders he could not explain. He was *visibly suffering*, poor lamb."

"I explained the situation to him," Marguerite said.

"You threatened him with your ledger."

"I read him three names."

"You threatened him with your ledger," Odile repeated fondly, and set the lamp on its post, and looked up at the three figures on the ferry—at the disgraced pilot, the orphan, the invisible man made unmistakable—and said, "Well. The century's turned and my kettle hasn't. Come inside before the new one starts badly."

* * *

### IV.

The tavern smelled of woodsmoke and spilled beer and, tonight, of Odile's celebratory plum cake, which was slightly burnt on one side because Odile had been distracted by the news of her own exoneration halfway through baking it, and which she served anyway with the defiance of a general presenting a costly victory.

"The burnt side builds character," she said, dealing slices.

"So does prison," said Ilsa. "You'd know soon."

"Careful, or you'll get the corner piece."

They ate. Outside, the fort's lanterns were going out one by one, and the rumor was moving through the harbor district the way fog moves—under doors, along gutters, finding every low place. By the time the plates were cleared, three fishermen had come in just to stand near Odile and touch the doorframe of her tavern as though checking it was real, and one of them, old Pieter, who never bought anything, bought a beer, paid for it, and left it untouched on the bar like an offering.

Nell had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was working on something. Finally she put down her fork.

"Mister Zhou. Did my da's name get read? At the fort?"

Marguerite set down his cup. "It was read at the checkpoint. And it will be read again, in court, where it counts. Private Declan Corrigan. Page forty." He hesitated, then added, gently, "He's in the testament too. Your father. There's a sum set aside in his name. For his daughter."

Nell looked at her hands. The tavern was very still; even Ilsa had stopped chewing.

"He used to write letters," Nell said at last. "I got one a month and they were mostly about the food. Terrible food. He wrote about it like it was an adventure." She looked up, and her chin was steady but her eyes weren't quite. "Did you know him?"

"I knew his last hour," Marguerite said. "And his last words, which were about you. But I feel I've come to know the rest of him through the letters you keep. You're the better archive."

Nell absorbed this enormous word, decided she liked it, and filed it away visibly for later use against her schoolmistress.

Then she said, "When the money comes—I don't want it to just be mine. Odile kept me fed all autumn. And Ilsa ferried us. Can an inheritance be split? Is that allowed?"

"That," said Odile, wiping her eyes with a bar rag very briskly, as though the rag were the problem, "is the most sensible question anyone has asked in this tavern in twenty years, and I include the questions I have asked myself."

"We'll ask the clerk," Marguerite said. "He owes the century one more favor."

* * *

### V.

At half past one in the morning, Captain Reyes stood alone in the probate clerk's office, which smelled of ink and cold tea, watching the old man lock the testament into the iron cabinet with the tenderness of a man locking up a canary from a cat.

"You understand," the clerk said, turning the key, "that by morning they will come for me. Then for you. In that order, probably, because you're easier to find."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway."

"Sheer spite," Reyes said. "It keeps regular hours."

The clerk snorted, then sobered. "The brother," he said, because clerks read everything, including the old files. "Tomas Brandt. Page forty-something of whatever that nurse carries around. You requested the file yourself, once. Nine years ago. It was denied."

"I remember."

"You could have requested it again after tonight. Now there'll be a reason to grant it."

Reyes looked out the window at the fog, at the harbor he had policed for a decade believing the policing meant something, at the first gray suggestion of a year that had begun, minutes old, with a crack running through it.

"Yes," he said. "I expect there will."

* * *

### VI.

Much later—past three, past caring—Marguerite climbed the stairs to Odile's spare room with a candle stub and found Nell already asleep in the bed, having annexed it hours ago with the ruthless efficiency of a girl who had slept in a workhouse cot for one night and resolved, permanently, never again. A second pallet had appeared beside the bed. No one had discussed it. Odile had simply carried it up, dropped it, and dared anyone with her eyebrows to comment.

Marguerite sat down on the pallet. From inside his coat he took the ledger—the real one, water-spotted now at the corners, smelling faintly of the millrace—and opened it to the last written page.

He had a pencil. He had, for nine years, had only ever a pencil and other people's endings.

Tonight he wrote: *December 31st, 1899. The letter arrived. The names held. Odile is home. Nobody had to disappear.*

He looked at it a long time. Then, beneath, smaller:

*Tomas Brandt — survived. Not the man. The name. Ask Reyes. Ask Ilsa. Ask the court.*

Because the century had turned, but the morning was coming, and mornings after victories had a shape he knew from the fever ward: the fever breaks, and then comes the long accounting, and some things the fever broke could not be grown back. The officials who signed the plot would wake to find their scheme dead but themselves alive, and men like that did not apologize; they maneuvered. Somewhere across the dark city the governor was awake too, Marguerite would have bet the ledger on it, doing sums, deciding which subordinates were expendable and which documents were flammable.

Let him. The names were in the cabinet now, sworn and stamped, undeniable. That was the work. That had always been the work.

Marguerite closed the book, blew out the candle, and lay down on the pallet between a sleeping child and the sound of Odile banking the fire below, and for the first time in his careful life fell asleep in a house where every single person under the roof knew his name.

Downstairs, the fire settled. Out in the harbor, the fog began, very slowly, to lift.
