## 21. A Compass for the Harbor

Winter loosened its grip on the harbor the way a fist opens—slowly, finger by finger, reluctant to admit it had been holding anything at all. By the third week of February the fog had thinned to gauze, and the sun came through it in pale coins that lay on the water without warming anything, but everyone agreed this was progress.

Marguerite noticed the change most at the pier. For months the third pier had belonged to Ilsa alone: her ferry tied up like a reprimand, her license framed nowhere because there was nothing to frame. Now there was a queue of harbor workers each morning—dockhands, rope-makers, two of the customs clerks who had once pretended not to know her—and Ilsa stood at the gangplank checking them off against a list she kept in her head, which was a thing she claimed was more reliable than paper and Marguerite had learned not to argue with.

"Zhou," she said, spotting him before he'd cleared the fish crates. "You're late. The six o'clock crossing left without you."

"I wasn't on it."

"That's what makes it late." She grinned, and the grin did something to her whole face that the winter had forbidden. She looked younger by a decade and harder by none. The harbor office had returned her license in an envelope with no apology enclosed—Ilsa kept the envelope nailed above her wheel as a kind of trophy, empty, the way one displays a snake's shed skin. "Well? Are you getting on or are you just going to stand there admiring me?"

"Admiring," Marguerite said, stepping aboard. "Briefly."

The ferry took the workers across to the yards, and afterward Ilsa let him ride along while she made her second run, carrying no one but him and a crate of Odile's ale bound for a tavern upriver that had finally worked up the courage to stock something other than government-approved cheerlessness. The water ran brown-green and patient. Gulls argued overhead about nothing.

"They gave me the workers' route," Ilsa said, steering with two fingers, the way she did everything—loosely, as if daring the world to object. "Officially it's because of 'distinguished service to the community.' Unofficially, Reyes signed the paper himself and didn't look at me while he did it."

"He's still at the fort."

"For now. They're saying he'll be transferred by spring. Promoted sideways somewhere far away, where his brother's name isn't painted on any walls." She glanced at him sidelong. "You heard about the wall."

"I've heard rumors."

"Rumors." She snorted. "You'll see it tonight. Everyone will see it tonight. Odile's been threatening to unveil it for two weeks and postponing every time she finds another name to add. At this rate we'll be old people waiting for a wall that never finishes."

Marguerite looked out at the water. "There are always more names."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment, hands steady on the wheel. "Yes," she said. "But that's not the same as saying there should be."

* * *

Odile's tavern reopened on a Friday, though Odile would have told you it had never properly closed—"a tavern is a door, dear, and doors don't stop being doors just because soldiers nail them"—but the reopening had candles in the windows and a fiddler in the corner and more people crammed inside than the fire code would have permitted if anyone had thought to write one.

The wall was at the back, behind the bar where the mirror used to hang. The mirror was gone; Odile had sold it weeks ago to buy paint, which she mentioned to everyone who came near it, several times, until even strangers knew the mirror's biography by heart.

And there were the names. Dozens of them, in white paint on the dark wood, in a hand that was unmistakably Odile's—large, unhurried, slightly theatrical. Corrigan. Ashworth. Brandt's soldier, whose first name Marguerite had only ever known from the ledger and which he had spoken aloud exactly once, into Ilsa's ear, before she went to fetch the brush herself. And there, halfway down, in letters taller than the rest: *Tomas Odile*, her husband, erased twenty years ago from a ledger much less honest than Marguerite's.

"You put Corrigan at the top," Marguerite said, standing beside her with a cup of the good cider she saved for people she liked.

"Nell asked me to."

"She asked you?"

"She stood behind me while I painted and said, 'He goes first, he's my papa,' and I said, 'Yes, miss,' because some requests you don't negotiate." Odile sipped her own cider and surveyed her work with the satisfaction of a general. "Besides, alphabetical order is a tyranny. Every list should begin wherever love says it begins."

People drifted up to the wall all evening. That was the thing Marguerite hadn't anticipated—the way they touched it. A dockhand ran his thumb under a name as if reading braille. An old woman pressed her palm flat against the wood and left it there a long moment, the way one touches a doorframe of a house one grew up in. Nobody explained the wall. Nobody needed to. It was simply understood that these names had been carried somewhere private for too long and had now been set down where everyone could help hold them.

Nell found him near midnight, when the fiddler had gone soft and slow and half the room had given itself over to talking instead of dancing. She was eleven now by her own insistence—"nearly twelve, practically"—and she wore her hair in a braid Odile had taught her to do herself, which meant it was magnificent and slightly crooked.

"There you are," she said, as if he'd been hiding, which perhaps he had; crowds still sat uneasily on him, like a coat cut for a broader man. "Come on. Your stool's getting cold."

"My stool."

"The one beside mine. Odile moved it behind the bar so we can do the ledger after close. She says if we're going to keep accounts, we might as well keep them where the light is good and the cider is free."

So that was how it began—not with ceremony, but with a stool. Each night after the last customer stumbled out into the fog, Nell would climb onto hers, Marguerite onto his, and between them on the bartop they would spread the ledger, its pages soft as cloth now from handling. Nell had appointed herself keeper of the ink, a responsibility she discharged with tremendous gravity and occasional spills.

"Read me today's," she said that first night, uncapping the ink like a surgeon.

"There's only one entry. Old Pieter, the lighthouse keeper's brother. He passed this morning, quiet, in his sleep. He served in the coastal artillery thirty years ago and nobody wrote down anything about him anywhere official except his pay records."

"What did he say? At the end?"

Marguerite smiled. "His niece says his last words were a complaint about the soup."

Nell considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "That counts."

"It counts," Marguerite agreed, and dictated while she wrote:

*Pieter Van Dorn. Coastal artillery, retired. Forty years keeping the light. Last words, per his niece Marta: "The soup is better than your aunt's, don't tell her." He was joking. He loved her soup.*

"That last part," Nell said, looking up. "You added that yourself."

"He'd want it known. Otherwise the soup complaint sounds cruel, and he wasn't a cruel man."

Nell nodded and added it. Then, after a moment: "Someday I'm going to keep a ledger too. My own. When I'm grown."

"You'll want your own book," Marguerite said. "Not mine. Mine has my handwriting, which is a punishment."

"Your handwriting is fine."

"My handwriting is a crime scene."

She laughed—she laughed easily these days, which was the thing he was proudest of and took no credit for, having learned long ago that the credit belonged to time and to Odile's cooking and to the simple fact of being wanted somewhere. But she laughed, and the fiddler caught it across the room and swung the tune brighter to match, and Marguerite sat on his stool behind the bar of a tavern full of people who knew his name and thought: *so this is what it's like, ordinary evenings.* He had spent forty years believing invisibility was safety. It turned out visibility was. It turned out the safest place in the world was a stool in a lit room among people who would notice if you weren't in it.

* * *

Near closing, Ilsa came in with river water still on her boots and Odile pressed a plate on her whether she wanted it or not—Odile's method of feeding people resembled a siege—and Ilsa dropped onto the end of the bench beside Marguerite with the sigh of a woman whose day had been long in the specific way that river days are long.

"Customs man stopped me again today," she said around a mouthful of bread. "Young fellow. Wanted to inspect my cargo."

"And?"

"And my cargo was eleven dockworkers and a bag of onions. He inspected them. All eleven. Made them open their coats." She shook her head. "They're nervous, Zhou. Since New Year's Eve they've been nervous everywhere. Every clerk in the probate office jumps when a door opens. You can feel it from the river."

"Good," said Odile, passing with a stack of bowls. "Let them be nervous. Nervous men sign honest papers."

Marguerite turned his cup slowly on the bar. The court's decision had come three weeks ago—the estate redirected, the named families notified—but the officials themselves had slipped out the side door of justice with their pensions intact, and he still felt the injustice of it like a stone in his boot. Nell had talked him down from it once already, in her flat eleven-year-old way: *"The courts got the money right and the people wrong. The wall gets the people right. Between the two of them it nearly balances."* Nearly. He was learning to live on nearly.

Ilsa nudged him. "Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere. Here." And he found, testing the words, that they were true. "I was just thinking that I used to measure everything by what could be carried. One bag. One ledger. One letter, once, that weighed nothing and nearly cost everything."

"And now?"

He looked down the bar—at Nell asleep sitting up on her stool, chin on her chest, the ink bottle still guarded in her fist; at Odile scolding the fiddler affectionately about playing something slower; at the wall with its white names catching candlelight; at Ilsa, mud-booted and fed, who had trusted him first, with more to lose than he had.

"Now I measure things differently," he said. "Now I measure by who'd notice if I didn't come home."

Ilsa studied him for a moment, then raised her cider an inch. "To being noticeable, then."

"To being noticeable."

Outside, the fog was coming back in off the water—it always came back; that was the harbor's nature, to hide and reveal in turns—but inside the windows were gold, and on the wall the names stood in their white paint like a fleet at anchor, and on the bartop the ledger lay open to a page that no longer hid beneath any floorboard.

It was Nell, waking just enough to mumble it, who gave the evening its last word. She blinked at the ledger, then at Marguerite, and said sleepily, "Tomorrow I get to write the date. It's my turn. You always do the dates."

"It's your turn," he agreed.

"Because next year," she said, already sliding back toward sleep, "I'll know the way myself."

He looked at her a moment—this small keeper of names, this apprentice of memory, pointing at tomorrow the way a compass points north—and understood that she was right, and that she had been right all along, and that the compass had never been a needle at all.
