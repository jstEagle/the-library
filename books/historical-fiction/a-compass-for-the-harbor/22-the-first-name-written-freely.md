## 22. The First Name Written Freely

The colonel had died on the ninth of December, in the third bed from the stove, and for one full year the world had gone on turning without anyone important noticing, which Marguerite considered the finest memorial a man could ask for.

He thought of this on the morning of the anniversary, standing at the window of the room above the tavern with his breath fogging the glass, watching the harbor do its slow winter arithmetic. Fog on the water. Gulls arguing over something neither would share. The *Kestrel*—Ilsa's ferry now, properly licensed, her name painted on the hull in letters Odile had insisted be gold—sliding out toward the workers' pier with its morning load of shipwrights and dockhands and one confused customs official who took the crossing every day and still got seasick.

A year since a dying man had pressed a letter into his hands and said, *the Zhus*, and turned Marguerite's careful invisible life inside out like a coat pocket.

"You're doing the window face," Nell said from behind him.

"I don't have a window face."

"Everyone has a window face. Yours is the one where you look like you're adding up numbers that won't behave." She was fourteen now, or fifteen—the workhouse records had been destroyed in the same fire that had destroyed so many other inconvenient records, and Nell had decided, with characteristic authority, that she was fifteen because it got her the bigger portion of breakfast. "Odile says come down. She's made the anniversary pudding."

"There isn't an anniversary pudding."

"There is now. It's a new tradition. Traditions have to start somewhere, that's what makes them traditions." She grabbed his sleeve and hauled, and Marguerite went, because he had learned over the past year that resisting Nell Corrigan was like resisting tide: possible in theory, embarrassing in practice.

The tavern below was warm and smelled of suet, cloves, and the particular smoke of Odile's stove, which she refused to have cleaned because, she claimed, it gave the food character. The walls had changed most of all. Where once there had been shipping notices and a faded portrait of some governor nobody could now look at without laughing, there was paint—names, hundreds of them, in Odile's broad hand and Ilsa's careful one and Nell's looping schoolroom script and even Reyes's stiff military capitals, filling the plaster from the wainscot to the beams. Private Corrigan was there. Tomas Brandt was there. Sergeant Wren, who had wanted someone to tell his mother the fog wasn't his fault. Captain Ashworth himself, near the ceiling where Odile said he could keep an eye on the room.

Marguerite's ledger, copied out in full, made public, made permanent. Made undeniable.

"Sit," Odile commanded, setting down a bowl of pudding that could have anchored a small boat. "Eat. You're thinner than yesterday, which shouldn't be mathematically possible."

"I'm exactly the same as yesterday."

"Then eat it twice."

He laughed, and sat, and ate, and let the morning happen around him the way it did every morning now—Ilsa coming in off the first run with wet cuffs and opinions, Reyes appearing at the door in his civilian coat looking like a man perpetually surprised to find himself welcome, and being welcomed anyway, because Odile had decreed it and because somewhere in the past year everyone had quietly agreed to stop keeping score of what each of them had done or failed to do during the bad winter. That was the thing about the harbor, Marguerite had come to understand. It kept no ledger of its own. The water came in, the water went out, and whatever you'd done last Tuesday was already too far under the fog to find.

But he kept his ledger. Of course he did. Some habits were not burdens; they were spines.

* * *

The old ledger lived now in the probate court's archive, surrendered formally as testimony, catalogued by a clerk who handled it as though it might bite, which Marguerite supposed it had. He had expected to feel bereaved without it, the way Ilsa had described losing her license—as though a hand had been cut off at the wrist. But the strange truth was that he didn't. The names were safer there than they had ever been beneath his floorboard, backed by law and ink and Reyes's sworn signature, and a record-keeper who couldn't let go of a record had missed the entire point of keeping one.

Still. A year was a year. And on the ninth of December, in the quiet hour between the lunch crowd and the evening one, Marguerite climbed to his room and opened the package that had arrived from the stationer's three days earlier and that he had not yet found the courage—or perhaps found the excuse—to unwrap.

It was a ledger. Blank. New. Leather the color of strong tea, corners bound in brass, pages ruled in faint blue lines that waited like an audience before the curtain rises.

He set it on the desk by the window. He sat. He did not open it.

For eleven years he had kept ledgers, and every one of them had begun the same way: with other people. Names gathered in fever wards and field tents and the cellar of a hospital where the lamps burned low and the orders came down to leave the paperwork blank. His own name had never appeared in any of them—not at the top of a page, not at the bottom, not in the margins. He had told himself this was discipline. Discretion. The creed of the invisible man: measure everything in what can be carried, carry nothing that can be traced to you, trace yourself to nothing at all.

He had believed, for a very long time, that invisibility was safety. Then he had spent one winter learning that it was only safety's cheaper cousin—that it protected no one but himself, and barely that. Odile's back room, emptied by soldiers' boots. Odile herself, two weeks in a cell. Nell, nearly taken while the man charged with her father's memory stood by pretending to be furniture. Invisibility had been a kind of lying, and Marguerite had been a liar so long he'd mistaken it for modesty.

He opened the ledger.

The first page lay white under the gray harbor light. Somewhere below, Nell was teaching Odile a song with an obscene verse that Odile pretended not to know and sang loudest. The gulls kept up their argument. The *Kestrel* sounded her horn at the pier, one long note like a held breath finally let go.

Marguerite dipped his pen.

His hand, which had written eleven hundred names without trembling, trembled now, and he let it, because a trembling hand was an honest hand and he had promised himself, this year, to stop being economical with the truth.

He wrote, slowly, at the top of the first page:

**Marguerite Zhou-Ashworth**

Not legally—not yet, the petitions were filed and the wait was long and the colonial office moved at the speed of continental drift—but truly, which was better. The colonel had no children. The colonel had, in his last delirious hours, looked at a nurse he'd mistaken for someone else and called him *son*, and then, more lucidly, had pressed a letter into his hands and chosen him on purpose, in ink, before witnesses dead and living. The court had redirected the estate to the families the testament named. It had not named Marguerite. But Reyes, signing his affidavit, had added a line no clerk dared strike: *and to the keeper of the record, whatever the record owes him.* Odile had translated this over pudding: "He left you the names, love. The rest is just plumbing."

Marguerite looked at his own name sitting there at the top of the page, black and wet and unmistakable, and felt something turn over in his chest—not grief, though grief was always in attendance these days, quieter than it used to be, like an old soldier who'd taken a chair by the fire—and not triumph either, or not only. It was closer to the feeling of setting down a load carried so long the shoulders had forgotten their shape without it.

No longer omitted. No longer merely carried.

Kept.

Below the name, in smaller script, he wrote: *Being a true record of the people of this harbor, begun the ninth of December, 1900, by its keeper, who signs himself plainly and expects to be contradicted by no one.*

Then, because the page should not belong to him alone, he went downstairs.

* * *

They were all in the back room, which no longer hid anything and so had become the warmest room in the building. Ilsa had her boots on the table, which Odile permitted on the grounds that arguing about it exhausted more energy than the boots cost. Reyes sat in the corner with tea he hadn't touched, reading a letter from his sister in the capital and wearing the expression of a man being forgiven more thoroughly than he'd asked to be. Nell had the ledger wall chalk in her hand and was adding a name at the bottom—a fisherman lost off the point that morning, young, whose girl worked the net shed. Odile stood over her, supervising the lettering with the severity of a cathedral architect.

"I've started the new book," Marguerite said from the doorway.

Four faces turned.

"And?" said Ilsa.

"And the first page needs witnesses." He laid the ledger open on the table, between Ilsa's boots and the teacup, and watched them read the top line. There was a silence of the particular harbor variety—not empty but full, like the moment a wave gathers before it decides about the shore.

"Well," said Odile at last, gruffly, dabbing at her eyes with a dishcloth that had seen worse. "About time somebody wrote it down where the authorities can't lose it."

"The authorities," said Reyes, folding his sister's letter, "have proven remarkably resistant to losing things lately. It's the rest of us who used to misplace people." He took the pen Marguerite offered and signed beneath the dedication, stiff capitals, *Elias Reyes, witness*, and if his hand pressed harder than the words required, nobody remarked upon it, because the wall behind him held a name he read every evening with his tea and had never once mentioned aloud, and that was its own kind of signature.

Ilsa signed with a flourish that used half the margin. Odile signed and then dated it wrong and then crossed out the date and initialed the correction. Nell signed last, tongue between her teeth, and beneath her name added, uninvited, *apprentice.*

"Apprentice of what, exactly?" Marguerite asked.

"Of you. Obviously. You can't keep doing this alone, you'll get a cramp." She pointed the chalk at the fresh ledger. "Besides. Somebody has to know the names after you. That's the whole point of a ledger—it outlives its keeper. You taught me that. Page forty."

Private Corrigan. Her father. *Tell my Nell the harbor counts,* the entry read, because it was all he'd had strength to say and because, in the end, it had turned out to be enough.

Marguerite found, to his considerable embarrassment, that he could not immediately speak. Odile handed him tea. Ilsa studied the ceiling with great interest. Reyes became absorbed in refolding an already-folded letter. The harbor, mercifully, made noise on his behalf.

"Yes," he managed at last. "All right. Apprentice. But we start with penmanship."

"He's strict," Nell informed the room, delighted.

* * *

Evening came down over the water like a lid settling on a pot, and the fog rolled in through the harbor mouth the way it always did, indifferent and ancient, wrapping the masts and the fort and the customs house where a clerk who owed Marguerite a favor had once looked the other way for ninety seconds and changed the course of an inheritance. The lamps went up along the piers one by one, small stubborn circles of gold.

Marguerite walked home along the seawall with the new ledger under his arm, light as bread, heavy as everything. At the third bench—the one facing the river mouth, where the fort's New Year guns had once announced a century and gotten instead a scandal—he stopped and sat awhile, because the colonel had liked this view, or Marguerite had decided he had, which amounted to the same thing now that deciding was all anyone had left to offer the dead.

"You'd laugh at the pudding," he told the water. "You'd pretend to laugh, and then you'd have seconds. I know your type."

The water said nothing, in the way it had of saying nothing eloquently.

Somewhere behind him the tavern door opened, spilling light and Odile's voice calling that supper was ready and would not wait, which was a lie, because it always waited, it had waited eleven years. Nell's silhouette appeared in the doorway, arms crossed, tapping her foot with theatrical impatience. Up the hill, the *Kestrel* sounded her horn for the late run, one long note, a held breath let go.

Marguerite stood. Settled the ledger firmly under his arm. And went in to supper—through the door, into the light, among the names—leaving the fog behind him to keep its own accounts, which it had always done poorly, and which was no longer his department.

The book on the table waited, first page full, second page blank.

Nell had already sharpened the pens.
