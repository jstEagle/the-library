## 1. The Weight of Glass

There is a particular weight to a storm lantern when it's full of oil and lit — about as much as a sleeping cat, or a newborn child, or the last argument you ever had with someone you loved. I know this because I have carried all four. The cat ran off. The child was never mine to begin with. The argument I keep on a shelf in my head where I can reach it on long nights. The lantern I carry up ninety-three steps, twice a night, every night, weather permitting.

Weather has not been permitting for three days.

The first great storm of autumn came off the water like something owed, and it arrived at my door at roughly two o'clock this afternoon demanding payment. By dusk the wind had taken to throwing spray against the tower's seaward face hard enough that the glass sang — a low, tuning-fork hum that old keepers say means the panes are holding and younger keepers say nothing about because they're too busy praying they hold. I am fifty-one years old, which puts me somewhere between the two camps, closer to the shelf where the arguments live than the shelf where the prayers go.

My name is Silas Blackwood, and for seven years I have kept the light at Harrow Point, and if you believe that sentence contains everything there is to know about me, then I have done my work properly.

* * *

A lighthouse keeper's inventory is a short one, and I have made a study of keeping it shorter. This is not poverty — the Trinity House pays me a wage that would embarrass a curate but sustain a hermit, which is what I have become by most definitions. It is philosophy. Everything you own owns a little piece of you back, and I found, after Maren died, that I could no longer afford the interest.

So: measure your life in portable things.

One sea chest, scarred, that came with me from — well, from before. One oilskin coat, patched at both elbows so many times it's less a garment now than an archaeology of patches. Six books, of which three are logbooks from my predecessor, dead these twenty years, whose handwriting slants leftward as though even his pen were trying to escape. A tin of tea. A tin of nails. A photograph I do not look at more than once a month, on principle, though some months the principle bends.

And the lamp. The great lens itself, all eight feet of it, weighing upward of two tons and turning on a bath of mercury so smooth the whole apparatus will rotate at the pressure of a fingertip. You cannot carry it anywhere, obviously. It is the least portable thing on this coast. Which is precisely why I trust it. It cannot leave me, and I cannot leave it, and there is a kind of marriage in that arrangement too — the second one of my life, and better kept than the first, God forgive me for thinking it.

The wind rose through the evening the way debt does: gradually enough to ignore, then suddenly impossible. At nine o'clock I went out onto the gallery to check the lightning conductor, and the gale took my breath away like a pickpocket — one moment it was in my chest, the next it was gone entirely, and I had to stand there gripping the rail while my lungs renegotiated their terms. Below me the sea was not water anymore. It was a moving landscape, hills and valleys traveling at speed, black on blacker, and every few seconds a wave would strike the base of the rock and throw up a detonation of white that lit the whole headland for half a heartbeat, like God taking photographs.

I have watched this perhaps four thousand nights. It has never once become scenery.

* * *

Here is what people imagine about lighthouse keeping, if they imagine it at all: solitude, yes, but a picturesque solitude, a man leaning philosophically on a rail. What they don't picture is the arithmetic. Trim the wicks at four-hour intervals. Wind the clockwork that turns the lens — forty-five pounds of crank pressure, every hour on the hour, or the rotation slows and the flash character changes, and ships that expect Harrow Point's double-flash every ten seconds will see something else and start consulting charts for what else they might be near. Pump the mercury bath clean of dust, because dust in the mercury makes the lens grind and stick. Keep the oil store dry, the spare panes racked, the fog signal ready. Log everything. Weather, wind, visibility, consumption of oil, consumption of self.

I write *all well* in the remark column every single day. Seven years of *all well.* It is the longest lie I've told anyone, including myself, and I tell it to a government ledger, which seems fitting somehow. Governments collect lies the way the sea collects wrecks — without effort, and eventually in bulk.

Maren used to laugh at that column. She'd read over my shoulder while I wrote it — she was forever reading over shoulders, hers was a life conducted at other people's elbows — and she'd say, *All well, is it? Your boots are leaking and you haven't smiled since Tuesday,* and I'd say the remark column concerns the light, not the keeper, and she'd say the light doesn't need remarks, it needs oil, and we'd arrive, as we always did, at the place where our jokes ended and just being together began, which was the best country I ever lived in.

She died in March, seven years ago, in the little room below the lamp room, with the winter still going on outside like it had nowhere better to be. Consumption — the fashionable word for it, as though her lungs had merely developed expensive tastes. For eleven months she got thinner and funnier in equal measure, until at the end she weighed about as much as the full lantern and laughed about that too, said now he'll finally be able to lift me, and I did, I lifted her, down those stairs and out into the churchyard cart, and I have been measuring things ever since.

Everything portable. That was her rule before it was mine. She grew up moving — a family always one step ahead of something she never fully explained to me, a brother she spoke of only twice and then stopped speaking of altogether, as though the name had been confiscated. *Never own what you can't carry running,* she'd say, folding her clothes into the same small trunk every spring whether we moved or not. I thought it was a quirk. It was probably an inheritance. People come from somewhere; I've learned to stop pretending otherwise, at least at night, at least alone, at least in the space between winding the clockwork and trimming the wick, which is where all honest thoughts live.

* * *

Midnight. Time for the first trim.

I climbed the ninety-three steps with the storm pressing at the tower like a crowd at a door. Up here the noise changes — the wind stops being sound and becomes architecture, flowing around the glass in shaped rivers, and inside the lamp room you stand in the eye of a stone whirlwind with a flame the size of your two cupped hands keeping the whole arrangement honest.

The burner was burning true. I trimmed the upper wick, turned down the lower, checked the oil feed — a drop every four seconds, steady as a pulse. The lens turned above me on its mercury bed, silent, effortless, gathering my little flame into a beam that would cross nineteen miles of open water and knock politely on the eyes of whoever was fool enough to be out in this.

Somewhere out there tonight there were ships. There are always ships. Men who signed on in ports with names I'd never pronounce, hauling coal or timber or letters, trusting that a stranger on a rock they'd never see would keep a fire burning so they wouldn't die. I used to find that thought comforting. Now I find it accurate, which is different. Comfort implies you're included in the arrangement. I am the arrangement.

I wound the clockwork — forty-five pounds, the counterweight descending its shaft into the tower's guts — and stood a moment watching the beam sweep. Flash. Darkness. Flash. Ten seconds, faithfully stupid, faithfully immortal. If you want to know what faith looks like, don't go to church. Come stand under a Fresnel lens at midnight in a gale and watch a piece of French glass do the same thing it did yesterday and will do tomorrow, asking nothing, promising everything.

Then I heard it — beneath the wind, beneath the clockwork's soft descent — the other sound. The one that comes once a season if the season is cruel.

Wood on rock. Far below, muffled by water and distance, but unmistakable to a man who has spent seven years learning what this coast says in its sleep. Something had struck the outer shoal, or the base of the point itself, or one of the teeth of rock we call the Widow's Comb because of what it collects.

I went down the stairs faster than a man my age should, and out onto the gallery, and swept the darkness below with the storm's own strobing light. And there — there, riding the white chaos at the tower's root, a shape. Not a ship. Too small. A boat, maybe, or what a boat becomes after the sea has finished editing it. And caught on the rocks beside it, snagged like a leaf in a gutter, something pale that moved.

Or didn't move. In this light, at this distance, with this wind, a man can see whatever the night hands him.

Now, I should say plainly: I have pulled men from this water before. Four, over the years. Fishermen mostly, and one customs officer who never thanked me, presumably because thanking a lighthouse keeper would have required admitting he'd needed saving. Each time I did it without deciding to. There is no decision in it. There is a rope, there is a ledge, there is a body, and there are the ninety-three steps behind you, and afterward there is tea and blankets and a story the man tells you or doesn't, and then he leaves, and you add nothing to your inventory, because people are not portable, whatever else they may be.

But tonight the storm was the worst I'd seen in seven years, and the shape below might be driftwood, and I am fifty-one years old, and the sensible thing — the *keeperly* thing, the thing that goes in the remark column as *all well* — was to wait for daylight and report wreckage to the harbor master and let the sea keep what it had taken.

I got the rope anyway. Of course I got the rope.

Because here is the other thing about measuring your life in portable things, the thing nobody tells you until it's too late to arrange your life differently: sooner or later you look at the inventory — the chest, the coat, the six books, the tin of tea — and you understand that you have catalogued your way down to nothing, and that the only items you ever owned worth the carrying were the ones that couldn't be packed. A woman laughing over your shoulder. A promise made in a small room with winter going on outside. A brother-in-law whose name was confiscated, whose face I have spent seven years carefully forgetting, per instructions, per grief, per love — the three forces being, in my experience, largely indistinguishable at close range.

The shape below moved again. Definitely moved. And the wind dropped for just long enough — storms do this, they lean close to listen, same as wives — for me to hear, thin and torn but human, a voice crying out from the base of the rocks.

I tied the rope to the gallery rail with the knot Maren taught me, the one she swore her father used for mooring boats in weather worse than this, and I started down.

Ninety-three steps up. Down is another matter entirely.
