## 2. A Question Too Many

The storm went out with the tide, the way storms do—reluctantly, like a guest who has broken something and hopes you won't notice until he's gone. By dawn the wind had dropped to a mutter, and the sea lay against the rocks looking bruised and apologetic. I climbed down from the tower at six o'clock with the lamp trimmed and sleeping above me, and I stood on the gallery for my customary minute of pretending I was a man who enjoyed views.

Seven years of that pretense. You'd think I'd have gotten better at it.

I want to be clear about something before this story goes any further: I did not dislike company. That would have been too simple, and simple things were the one luxury I couldn't afford. What I disliked was being counted. There is a difference. A man can share his bread with a shipwrecked sailor, lend a rope to a fisherman, sit an hour in the village tavern listening to Hobbs tell the same story about the same eel—and enjoy all of it. But the moment someone takes out a ledger, or a form, or even just a particular kind of attention, and begins to arrange me into columns—that is when I remembered why I had come to the edge of the map in the first place.

So when I saw the figure coming up the cliff path that Tuesday morning—a woman, gray coat, carrying a leather satchel and walking with the deliberate stride of someone who intends to arrive—I felt the old familiar contraction in my chest, the one that had nothing to do with my heart and everything to do with my name.

She reached the door as I was still deciding whether to pretend I hadn't seen her. Cowardice, I find, works best when you commit to it fully.

"Mr. Blackwood?" she said, though she said it like a question about a word she wasn't sure she'd pronounced correctly.

"That's the name on the door," I said. This was true. I had painted it there myself, four years ago, after the surveyor complained he couldn't find me. SILAS BLACKWOOD, LIGHTKEEPER, in letters a foot high. It seemed safer to hide in plain sight than to hide in a way that suggested hiding.

"And is it your name?"

That was the first question, and it was already one too many.

* * *

Her card said MISS ADA QUILL, CENSUS ENUMERATOR, DISTRICT SEVEN, and beneath that, in smaller print, BY APPOINTMENT OF THE REGISTRAR-GENERAL, which struck me as a great many words to say *I'm here to count you*. She stood in my kitchen without being invited in, which is a skill some people are born with and others learn from experience of doors closing. Her eyes went around the room the way mine went around a horizon: methodically, missing nothing, assigning everything its weight.

I have spent seven years learning what a room says about its occupant. Mine said: one man. One chair pulled out from the table, one cup beside the teapot, one set of oilskins drying by the stove, one of everything and never two. A room that had been arranged, carefully and over years, into a single sentence.

Miss Quill read that sentence in about four seconds. I watched her read it.

"You keep the light alone," she said. Not a question. Enumerators, I would learn, ask very few actual questions. They make statements and wait for you to contradict them.

"Since my wife died. Seven years this autumn."

"I'm sorry." She said it correctly—the right words, the right pause—but there was something underneath it, a second layer, like the sound under a bell. Then she opened the satchel and produced the schedule, a printed sheet with ruled lines, and set it on my table as though laying out instruments for surgery.

"The census requires," she said, "the name, condition, age, occupation, and birthplace of every person who slept under this roof on Sunday night."

"One person slept under this roof on Sunday night."

"Name?"

"You know my name. It's on your card."

"It's on your door, too," she said mildly, writing anyway. "Occupation?"

"Lightkeeper."

"Married or widowed?"

"Widowed."

She wrote. Her pen made a small dry sound, like a bird walking on paper. I found myself watching that pen with an attention I did not care for. In my experience, pens are where trouble starts. Words go onto paper and become facts, and facts become property of the Crown, and the Crown keeps them in buildings full of other facts about other men, and somewhere in those buildings a clerk who has never seen the sea decides what your life means based on how it fits in a column.

"Birthplace?" she asked.

"Inland."

"Inland," she repeated. "That's not a county."

"It's a direction."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might build if it ever got planning permission. She wrote *inland*, or wrote something, because her hand moved, and I noticed she didn't ask me again. That should have relieved me. Instead it bothered me more than a third question would have. A woman who gives up that easily is a woman who intends to come back.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about measuring life in portable things. When Maren died, I inherited a houseful of her, and I could not stay in that house, so I took a post nobody wanted—at the far end of nowhere, on a headland the Admiralty had only grudgingly agreed existed—and I brought with me exactly what fit in two sea chests. Her Bible. Her sewing box, unopened since. The good brass compass her father had left her, which pointed faithfully at everything except the thing I needed to find. And the locket.

The locket stayed shut. I've told myself that's because I couldn't bear to open it. The truth is nearer the opposite: I couldn't bear what opening it would mean, which was deciding, finally and forever, whether I was keeping her memory or keeping her promise. Those are different weights, and only one of them can be carried indefinitely.

For seven years the arrangement worked. Storms came; I lit the lamp. Ships passed; most of them continued past. Men washed ashore occasionally, and I pulled them out and fed them and sent them along the coast road, and no one ever asked me anything harder than *how many candles do you burn per season*. I was, by every measure the government possessed, a single man doing a single job at the end of a single road.

I had built that invisibility plank by plank, the way you build anything worth having. And on a gray morning between storms, a woman with a leather satchel sat down at my table and began, politely, professionally, to count the planks.

* * *

She was efficient, I'll grant her that. Age: forty-one, or near enough—she wrote *41* without asking, which meant either that I look forty-one or that enumerators guess and let God sort out the error. Condition of dwelling: sound. Number of rooms: she counted them herself rather than ask, stepping into my bedroom doorway long enough to glance around, and I confess I stood in the kitchen feeling as though a stranger had put a hand inside my chest and counted ribs.

It was when she came back and dipped her pen again that she asked it.

"And kin," she said. "Any family besides? Children elsewhere, brothers, parents living?"

"No."

"Any kin *expected*?"

The question landed strangely, the way a step lands wrong in a familiar staircase. Expected. As though family were weather. As though somewhere in the Registrar-General's arithmetic there was a column for men who were waiting.

"Why would kin be expected?" I asked.

"They sometimes are," she said. "Sailors' families move between ports. Widowers take in sisters. Sons come home from the merchant service." She looked up from the page then, directly, and her eyes were the flat gray of the sea an hour before it decides to be dishonest. "You'd be surprised how often a household changes between the asking and the counting."

"No kin expected," I said.

And here is the thing—the thing I have turned over perhaps nine hundred times since. I told the truth. On that morning, sitting in my own kitchen with the storm-wash still dripping off the eaves, there was no one expected. Tomas was a name in a dead woman's mouth, a promise folded away with the locket, a possibility so remote I had stopped paying interest on it years ago. If Miss Quill had asked me that question a week earlier or a month later, my answer would have been the same and equally true.

But I hesitated first.

Half a breath. Perhaps less. The time it takes a man's eyes to flick toward a drawer—or in my case, I learned later, toward the corner of the room where Maren's sea chest sat, which was the worst possible place for my eyes to go, because it was the one object in the house that had no business mattering to a census.

She saw it. I knew she saw it the way you know a wave has changed direction before it breaks: not by evidence but by the sudden quality of the silence. Her pen stopped moving. She did not look up. She let the silence sit there between us like a third person at the table, and then she wrote something—one line, unhurried—and closed the schedule.

"Well, Mr. Blackwood," she said, gathering her things. "That's all I require today."

"*Today*," I repeated.

"The district runs from the headland to the ferry road. I'll be making the rounds through the village over the next fortnight." She stood, buttoned her coat, settled the satchel. At the door she paused, and I braced for a parting question, and she didn't ask one, which was somehow worse. Instead she said: "You have a fine view from up there. I imagine you see everything that comes along this coast."

"Most things," I said. "The fog takes the rest."

"Mm," said Miss Quill. "Fog always does seem to take precisely what we'd rather it kept."

And she went down the path, gray coat fading into gray morning, leaving me standing in my own doorway feeling—for the first time in seven years—*perceived*.

* * *

I gave the fire a piece of driftwood it hadn't earned and sat with my tea going cold, turning the morning over like a stone looking for what lived underneath.

A census. An ordinary, lawful, once-a-decade nuisance that every farmer and fishwife in England endured without losing sleep. There was nothing in my house to find. There was no one in my house at all. I had answered every question honestly except none, and yet I had lied twice—inland, and the hesitation, which is a lie the body tells before the mouth gets involved—and she had caught both, and written neither down, and left.

That last part worried me most, once I got to it. An enumerator who writes down your evasions makes a report. An enumerator who writes down nothing keeps them. I couldn't decide which category Miss Quill belonged to, and a man in my position cannot afford mysteries he hasn't purchased himself.

Because—and I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the day's theme—there was a reason my eyes went to that chest. There was a reason the question *any kin expected* landed like a cold hand between my shoulders.

Three nights earlier, in the tail of the storm, I had stood my watch and seen something on the water. A boat's lantern, low and small, running dark along the shoreward side of the point where no honest boat runs dark, and then gone. I had told myself it was wreckage-light, a fisherman sheltering, one of Hobbs's cousins poaching in the wrong cove. I had told myself several things, in fact, the way a man tells himself things while climbing down a tower at two in the morning with his heart going like a pump in a flooded bilge.

And I had gone to the sea chest that night. First time in three years. I had taken out the locket, held it shut in my palm—warm metal, her weight, her brother's name engraved on the back where no one could see it—and put it back, and locked the chest, and stood a while in the dark listening to the wind.

No kin expected, I had told her.

But the sea doesn't consult the census. And somewhere down that coast, in whatever cove the fog had taken, a boat had come ashore in the night with its lantern burning low, the way boats do when the man steering them is afraid of being seen—and the only person on this headland with a reason to be afraid for him was me.

I washed the cup. One cup. I dried it and hung it on its single hook, and outside the gulls were working the tide line, screaming their heads off about something the sea had given up.

I decided I would walk the beach that afternoon. Just to look. Just to know.

It is a strange arithmetic, invisibility. You spend years building it, plank by plank, and it weighs nothing and costs everything, and it holds against weather and officials and time. All it takes to crack it is one gray-eyed woman with a pen and a question too many—and the sea, obligingly, sending an answer before you're ready to count it.
