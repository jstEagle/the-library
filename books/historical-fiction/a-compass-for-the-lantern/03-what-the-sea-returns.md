## 3. What the Sea Returns

The coast road between the headland and the village of Porth Annow is two miles of nothing, and I have walked it eleven times in nine days, which I believe makes it the most thoroughly censused stretch of coastline in the Duchy.

I have developed a professional relationship with nothing. Nothing is what most people give you, when you come to their doors with your ledger and your government-issue politeness. They give you nothing with such generosity—nothing to declare, no one lodges here, we are four, we were five, my mother is dead, my husband is at sea, he was always at sea—and you write *nothing* in your neat hand and move along, and the country gets counted in nothings. I sometimes think if you laid all the nothings end to end they would reach from Land's End to John o' Groats and back twice, with enough left over to pave Whitehall.

But every so often a door gives you something else. Not a lie exactly. A hesitation. A silence shaped like an answer, the way a footprint in wet sand is shaped like a foot without being one.

Silas Blackwood gave me a silence shaped like an answer on Tuesday, and I have been carrying it around since, turning it over like a coin I suspect of being clipped.

*Any kin expected?*

I will grant that it is not a question the Census Office requires. It is a question Ada Quill requires, because I have learned—in three years of knocking on doors from Falmouth to this wind-scoured extremity of the map—that the question a person expects is never so revealing as the question they don't. Ask a man how many live in his house and he counts bodies. Ask him who is *coming*, and you watch his face do its arithmetic in public.

Blackwood's face did sums I could not read. Something flickered behind his eyes like a lamp turned down to a whisper of wick—not extinguished, mind, only economized. And then he said "No," in a voice as flat as a slate, and I wrote *no* in my book, and the word sat there on the line looking wrong, the way a borrowed coat sits wrong on shoulders not its own.

I am not in the business of reading souls. The Census Office would be horrified at the suggestion; we count, we do not interpret; we are clerks of existence, not confessors. But there is interpretation and there is noticing, and noticing is a habit I contracted young and have never managed to cure. My mother called it snoopiness when I was eight and called it a gift when I was twenty-five and needed flattering into usefulness. My superiors call it thoroughness when my returns run clean and obstruction when I ask questions above my station.

They are all three correct, which is the trouble with family.

So: Silas Blackwood, lighthouse keeper, widower these seven years, inhabitant of one, occupier of a tower at the edge of the known world, keeper of a silence shaped like an answer. I had copied his household entry out fair that night in the lodging-house parlour, by candlelight, with the landlady's cat sitting on my spare shawl in the manner of cats annexing territory. One name. One occupation. No kin expected.

And I found myself wondering—not interpreting, merely wondering—what kind of man lives alone for seven years at the top of two hundred stone steps and still flinches, just slightly, just visibly, at the word *expected*.

* * *

Friday morning came up grey and mild after two days of gales, the sea lying flat and gleaming like a spilled puddle of pewter, apologetic now for all its shouting. The storms had passed through the night before last with the full orchestra—thunder, spray, the works—and any collector of the sea's leavings knows that the morning after a storm is market day. The water spends all night throwing things away and the shore opens shop at dawn.

I told Mrs. Chegwin at the lodging-house that I meant to walk the coast path for the air and my health, both of which claims contained enough truth to pass inspection, and set out with my boots laced tight and my notebook in the pocket where other women might carry a locket.

The path runs below the headland first, under the tower's shadow—the light itself dark now in the daylight, though I find I look up at it anyway, the way one looks at a shut church. Then it drops toward a cove the charts call Gull's Wreck, which is either grimly descriptive or grimly hopeful depending on your temperament, and then climbs again through tamarisk and thrift toward the village chimneys.

It was at Gull's Wreck that the sea had opened its stall.

There was the usual inventory: kelp in ropes long enough to moor a barge, driftwood pale as bone, a dead gull floating in the shallows with an air of having died of disappointment rather than drowning. A ship's door, torn off its hinges, lay half-buried in the shingle like something trying to hide. And further along, snagged in the rocks at the cove's mouth, the sea had arranged its centrepiece: a section of deck cabin, perhaps six feet of planking with a window frame still attached, glass miraculously whole, rocking gently in the surge as though waiting for someone to come collect it.

Wreckage is common on this coast. This coast *makes* wreckage; it is practically an industry. What stopped me, boots already soaked to the ankles because I had waded out like a fool, was not the thing itself but the thing's provenance.

There was a maker's mark burned into the lintel above the window frame. A stamp, deliberate, official—a crown over crossed anchors, and beneath it letters in no alphabet I could read at first, being neither Latin nor English but some tight northern script, Danish or Norwegian or something adjacent. Below the stamp, painted numerals, half worn: *18—* something. A date. A registration mark.

Foreign. Officially foreign. Which means the ship it belonged to was foreign-built, foreign-registered, very possibly foreign-flagged—and yet it had come apart on an English headland in an English storm, within sight of an English lighthouse, and I would wager my quarter's salary that no foreign vessel is listed as wrecked or harbored anywhere in the harbor master's books.

I know the harbor master's books. That is not sentiment; that is procedure. Part of my commission covers shipping returns—I verify the harbor master's tallies against the customs schedules each quarter, a duty everyone concerned regards as a formality so ancient and meaningless it has practically fossilized. In three years I have never once found a discrepancy. Penhale writes his figures in a lovely round hand and I copy them into mine and between us we produce a document of perfect, useless accuracy.

Now I stood shin-deep in cold water holding six feet of evidence that his accuracy was fiction.

Because here is the thing about a foreign hull breaking up on your coast: somebody notices. Foreign ships mean foreign sailors, foreign cargo, foreign insurance claims, foreign questions—all the things a port is supposed to log and report and tax. A foreign vessel sheltering in an English harbor during weather is one thing; that happens, and Penhale would write it down, because writing it down is safe. But this cabin had been *torn off* a living ship. This ship had put to sea—or been put to sea—in conditions that took her cabin apart, and she had either been running from the storm or running from something worse than the storm.

Or she had been here before, more than once, and simply never entered in any book at all.

I got the lintel section dragged above the tide line, which took twenty minutes and cost me a stocking, and I copied the mark into my notebook with the care I usually reserve for names and dates. Crown, anchors, script, numerals. Then I sat on a rock and let the arithmetic do itself.

Ships that appear in no ledger. Wreckage bearing marks that belong to no reported vessel. A coast where, the fishermen tell me cheerfully, "things wash up regular." A lighthouse keeper who keeps his light burning with a competence that several villagers described to me in tones usually reserved for saints and good dogs—and who goes white around the mouth when asked whether anyone is expected.

I want to be clear about my state of mind, because I have been accused—by myself, mostly, in the small hours—of romanticizing suspicion. I did not stand on that beach and imagine smugglers and fugitives and dramatic lantern signals. What I felt was smaller and colder than excitement. It was the specific discomfort of a clerk whose columns do not add. Somewhere in my tidy ledgers there is a line missing, and the missing line has a maker's mark on it, and the man at the top of the tower knows something about the space where it should be—or knows nothing about it at all, which would be its own kind of interesting, wouldn't it, because then why flinch?

Curiosity, I find, is not a gentle thing once it hardens. When it is soft it is called interest and costs nothing. Hardened, it becomes a duty, and duties have teeth. My commission instructs me to report irregularities in coastal traffic. It does not instruct me to go digging through driftwood for them, but commissions are written by men who sit in offices where the sea is a rumour, and the sea, this morning, had handed me its accounts directly.

I looked at the cabin piece a long moment before I left it. The window glass caught the grey light and held it, and for a foolish instant the empty frame looked like a mirror, and I saw myself in it: a small woman in a soaked hem, holding a notebook, standing precisely at the place where the shore stops keeping secrets.

Then the surge came up and took the reflection away, and I climbed the path toward Porth Annow with my boots squelching and my mind made up in the way that minds make themselves up before the owner has signed off on the decision.

I would call at the harbor master's office. Casually. A census officer verifying quarterly returns, nothing more. I would ask, in my government-issue politeness, whether any foreign vessels had sheltered during the recent gales.

And I would watch Penhale's face do its arithmetic.

If his figures were clean, I would apologize for the intrusion and go back to counting nothings, and I would try—I would genuinely try—to let the matter close like a well-kept book.

But if his hand paused over the page. If his eyes went somewhere else for half a second before answering. If he gave me a silence shaped like an answer—

Well. Then this coast had a second ledger, hidden somewhere below the waterline of the official one, and I intended to find out who kept it. Because a census taker who cannot tell the difference between a coast that is empty and a coast that is merely quiet is worse than useless—and I had begun to suspect, walking those two miles of nothing for the eleventh time, that this coast was very quiet indeed.

Quiet as a man saying *no* too flatly. Quiet as a blank line where a ship should be logged. Quiet, even, as a lighthouse burning steadily over waters that keep returning things the books say were never there.

The village smoke rose ahead of me, thin and ordinary against the grey. I shook the sea from my hem, squared my notebook in my pocket, and went to ask my question too many.
