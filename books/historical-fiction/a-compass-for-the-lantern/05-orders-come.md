## 5. Orders Come

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays are the day I clean the lens, and there is nothing like polishing glass to remind you how badly things can distort when you look through them.

I heard the boat before I saw it—oars, not sail, and the particular arrhythmic slap of someone rowing who doesn't love the water. Government men never love the water. They tolerate it the way they tolerate weather: as an obstacle between themselves and paperwork.

I was halfway up the tower with my chamois when I saw him beaching a skiff below, so I finished the pane I was on first. This is a policy of mine. I have watched enough things go half-done in my life to know that the world does not thank you for abandoning a clean window to greet a stranger.

By the time I came down the hundred and four steps—the number is not a superstition, merely arithmetic—he was standing on the causeway holding his hat as though it had betrayed him. A small man with the complexion of a man who works indoors for other men who work indoors. He had a leather satchel and the expression of someone delivering news he did not personally endorse, which in my experience is the only kind of government expression worth trusting.

"Keeper Blackwood?" he said, consulting a paper as if my name might have changed since it was written.

"That's what's on the door."

"There's no door."

"There isn't," I agreed. "But if there were."

He blinked at me the way men blink at rocks that speak, then produced the envelope. It was thick, cream-colored, sealed with wax that had traveled badly, and heavier than any letter has a right to be. Heavy paper is how the government says *we mean this*.

"You're to read it at once," he said. "And sign the enclosed acknowledgment and return it by the next packet boat."

"I'll read it when you've gone," I said. "The acknowledgment I'll sign now, if you like, sight unseen. Saves us both the pretense that I had a choice."

That got me a second blink. He held out the form, I signed it against my own forearm, and he took back the receipt with visible relief, as though handing off something hot. Then he looked up at the tower—a long look, almost apologetic—and said, "It's a fine light. My father sailed past it forty years."

"He's welcome to sail past it thirty-nine more," I said, which made no sense yet, and would make less sense within the week.

Then he rowed away badly, and I sat down on the causeway stones with the envelope in my hands and the tide coming in around my boots, and I did not open it for a long while. There is a trick to bad news, learned from practice: you hold it unopened as long as possible, because inside the envelope nothing has happened yet. The paper doesn't know what it says. For as long as it stays shut, the future remains negotiable.

But I am fifty-four years old and I buried my wife seven years ago, and I have learned that the future is never actually negotiable. It only lets you believe so while it loads the gun.

I opened it.

* * *

There were three documents inside, because bureaucracy believes in trinity. The first was the order itself, printed in the dense official typeface whose only purpose is to make you work for your own misfortune. I will spare you the full ceremony of it. The substance was this:

*Whereas* the Board of Trade has determined, upon survey, that the headland light may be superseded by an automated apparatus of improved design; *and whereas* the said apparatus shall require no resident keeper, requiring only quarterly attendance by a traveling mechanic; *and whereas* it has been further determined that irregular habitation of the coastline presents certain difficulties of administration—*it is therefore ordered* that the station be decommissioned at the close of the storm season, that the tower be secured, and that all persons residing along the coastal district present themselves for registration upon the general watch list, with names, kin, means of livelihood, and expected movements, under penalty of law.

I read it twice. The first time for the blow. The second time for the details, because the details are always where they hide the second blow.

They were going to put out my light. Seven years of trimming wicks at two in the morning, of carrying oil up stairs in gales that flattened grown men, of polishing glass until I could see my own ruined face in the drum—they were going to replace all of it with a machine that needed visiting four times a year. A clockwork keeper. A mechanism that would never grieve, never tire, never stand at the rail at midnight looking west toward a country where a woman named Maren used to be alive.

You would think that would be the part that cut. It wasn't. Machines keep honest light. I could almost respect the arrangement.

It was the second document that mattered. The watch list registration. Every soul on the coast, catalogued: name, family, occupation, comings and goings. They were building a ledger of the shore. And a ledger, I know from hard experience, is a weapon that looks like a book.

Here is what you must understand about me, and about why a printed order made my hands go cold on a warm afternoon: I have spent seven years becoming no one. When Maren died, I did not merely bury her—I buried the man she married, or rather the name he went by, the life he'd led before the coast swallowed him whole. I came out here with her trunk and her brother's promise folded up inside me like a letter never sent, and I let the sea erase me. Silas Blackwood, keeper. No history. No kin. No debts. A man measured entirely in portable things: lamp oil, glass cloth, bread, salt, the small weight of a wedding ring.

Invisibility is a craft, same as keeping a light. You tend it nightly. You leave no windows open in it.

And now the government proposed to walk the whole coastline asking every question I had spent seven years refusing to answer, and to write the answers down where anyone with authority could read them. *Any kin? Any kin expected?* I thought of the census woman, Quill, and her too-quiet question, and the way she'd looked at me after I lied—not angry, not accusing, just filing it away somewhere behind her eyes, the way careful people file things.

The third document was instructions for the decommissioning. Inventory of stores. Surrender of keys. Sealing of the oil store, the lamp room, the lower galleries. A schedule. Season's end. Eleven weeks.

Eleven weeks, and then a machine would do my job, soldiers' clerks would do the census woman's, and I would be a man with no post, no house, no function, standing on a shingle beach being asked by strangers to state his full name and business.

* * *

I did what I always do with unbearable things. I cleaned.

The lens got polished twice. The brass got rubbed until it complained. I inventoried the oil store with a pencil, ticking barrels, and found myself counting everything twice, because measuring life in portable things is a habit that dies hard even when the life is being taken out of your hands. Forty-one barrels of oil. Sixty panes of glass in reserve. Three months of flour, two of salt pork, one of tea. One tower, one light, one keeper, one ghost.

Toward evening I climbed to the gallery and stood where the wind comes off the Atlantic like a debt collector, and I looked at the sea and tried to feel what I supposed I ought to feel. Rage, maybe. Grief. Something dramatic and useful.

What I felt instead was a kind of grim recognition. Of course they were coming. Of course the world eventually sends its clerks to find the man hiding from it. You cannot outrun arithmetic forever. I had owned nothing, owed nothing, been seen by no one—and it turned out that being nobody is itself a thing worth taking away. The ground shifting under a man who owns nothing: it turns out the ground was never his either.

The storm season was coming early this year. The gulls had been saying so for a fortnight, flying heavy and low, and my knee had joined their chorus. An automated light, installed by mechanics in fair weather, tested in none of it, standing guard over the worst coast in the kingdom while the real storms came through.

Someone was going to die out there under a machine's honest, stupid light. That is not prophecy. That is just knowing this shoreline the way you know a face.

* * *

I lit the lamp at dusk because the lamp gets lit at dusk, orders or no orders. There is a stubbornness in routine that no government has ever successfully legislated away. I trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turns the lens, and settled into the chair by the window with the order spread across my knees, reading it a third time as though repetition might reveal a loophole large enough to live in.

It didn't. But it did reveal a date, tucked into the schedule annex like a thorn in a rose stem: the registration surveyors would begin at the harbor end of the coast and work outward, reaching the headland in approximately three weeks. Three weeks before some clerk knocked—there being still no door—and asked me, in writing, with penalties attached, for my true name and the names of all persons connected to me.

All persons connected to me. I laughed at that, alone in the lamp room, and the laugh came out wrong, more bark than humor.

Because here is the thing I have told no one, not even the page, not even the dark: for seven years the emptiness of this station has been a fortress. No kin. No kin expected. And every night I have kept that sentence polished like the lens, and believed it, because believing it was the only way to sleep.

But promises do not expire just because we pray they will. Somewhere east of here, across whatever borders men draw and undraw, there lives—or lived, God help me, there is no way to know—a man named Tomas, my Maren's brother, who carries in his pocket the same claim on me that I carry on his behalf. *If he ever comes to you,* she said, her voice already thin as lamp smoke, *you hide him. No questions. No borders. Swear it.* And I swore it, because she was dying and because it cost nothing to swear, or seemed to then, in peacetime, with her hand still warm.

No questions. No borders. I had built my whole invisible life on the assumption that the promise would stay theoretical—that the sea between Tomas and this coast was wide enough, and the Crown's reach long enough, and luck kind enough. Now the Crown was coming to my doorstep with a list, asking after exactly the sort of man a promise like mine would shelter, and the sea had gone very quiet, and luck—well. Luck is just the name sailors give to weather they haven't met yet.

Three weeks. I looked at the schedule again. Surveyors from the harbor end. Which meant they would pass through the fishing village first, the wreckers' shanties, the whole ragged congregation of people who owed me nothing and everything, getting asked their own questions, giving their own answers—and among those answers, sooner or later, some careless tongue mentioning the keeper on the headland. Mentioning that he pulled drowning men from the water. Mentioning, perhaps, that he asked no questions while doing it.

A man known for asking no questions. On a coast about to be registered. Under an order that wanted every irregular soul accounted for.

I snuffed the lantern over the chart table and stood a moment in the rotating dark, watching the beam sweep the water—light, shadow, light, shadow, the oldest rhythm I know. Eleven weeks until the machine. Three weeks until the questions. And somewhere between those numbers, arriving on no schedule at all, everything else.

I went down the hundred and four steps and slept badly, and woke before dawn to the sound the gulls make when a boat is crossing the bar in fog—and I stood at the window telling myself it was only fishermen, only weather, only the ordinary machinery of an ordinary coast.

It was, as far as I knew then, the truth.

It stayed the truth for nine more days.
