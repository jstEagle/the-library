## 6. The Wreckers' Ledger

The village of Porth Anwin had, as far as I could determine, two collective occupations: fishing, and the prevention of my doing anything useful.

I had come down from the headland on the morning coach with my satchel of forms, my spare nibs, and my firm intention to verify the coastal households against the census rolls before the season's paperwork swallowed me whole. The rain had other ideas. The rain, in Porth Anwin, always had other ideas; I suspect it was on the parish payroll.

"Names," I said to the fourth fisherman of the morning, a man built like a knot in timber whose hands suggested he had personally wrestled every cod in the Celtic Sea into submission. "I simply need names. Who lives in which house. How many souls under each roof."

He looked at me the way one looks at weather that has arrived indoors.

"Hendry," he said at last.

"Hendry what?"

"Just Hendry."

"Just Hendry doesn't appear on any roll I hold, Mr. Just Hendry."

"Might be I'm not on it, then." He shrugged with the magnificent finality of a man closing a shop. "Might be there's no such person."

I wrote *Hendry — refuses all particulars, possibly fictional* in the margin, mostly because I knew the clerks in London would hate it, and small cruelties keep a civil servant warm.

This was the texture of my week. I had walked the coast road from the lighthouse down into the village three days prior, and I will confess — since no one is auditing this account but myself — that I had not stopped thinking about the keeper. Silas Blackwood. A name like a locked drawer. He had answered eleven of my twelve questions with the flat precision of a man reading from a script he'd memorized years ago, and then the twelfth — *any kin expected?* — had landed on his face like a gull hitting a window. He said no. People say no all day long in my trade. But there are ways of saying no that amount to a confession delivered at volume, and Blackwood's had been one of them.

So when the district office instructed me to sweep the coastal parishes for irregularities, I told myself my interest was professional. I am an excellent liar, particularly to myself; it is the one talent the Crown never thought to test me for.

* * *

Porth Anwin clung to its harbor the way limpets cling to a hull: tenaciously, joylessly, and with the full expectation of being scraped off eventually. Forty houses, a chapel, a tavern called the Drowned Rat — the locals' humor was of the salt-cured variety — and a smell of fish and tar that had soaked so deep into the stone I believe the buildings themselves were technically seafood by that point.

The tavern was where the truth of a place lives, so on my second evening I took a table near the fire, ordered a stew of aggressively local composition, and did what census takers do best: I listened while appearing to be no one in particular.

They talked of nets and prices and a chapel elder's scandalous new hat. And then a woman named Tressa — widowed, I gathered, with the particular competence of women who have been widowed by the sea and have made their peace with suing it for nothing — said the thing that rearranged my whole ledger.

"He had the boy out before the Capstan even turned," she said. "Third wave over the rocks and there he is, rope round his middle, cool as you please."

"Who?" I asked, before wisdom could clamp a hand over my mouth.

Four heads turned. Tressa studied me with the frank appraisal of a woman deciding whether I was worth the trouble of disliking.

"The keeper," she said. "Up top. Blackwood."

"Silas Blackwood pulled a drowning boy from the surf?"

"Last Tuesday. Young Pascoe. Went after a drifted line and the sea went after *him*." She stirred her cider. "Blackwood came down the cliff path in weather that would've kept the devil indoors, swam out twice — twice, mind, the second time for the line too, because he knows the boy'd sooner drown than lose it — and handed them both up the rocks like parcels."

"That seems," I said carefully, "beyond the duties of a lighthouse keeper."

Tressa laughed, short and hard as a wave slapping a wall. "Beyond his duties. Aye. Everything that man does is beyond his duties. That's rather the point of him."

"And does he—" I cast about for the shape of the question. "Is he paid for these rescues?"

The table went quiet in a way that told me I had asked something both obvious and obscene.

"Paid," repeated the knot-in-timber fisherman, who turned out to be named Garren and possibly real. "You don't pay a man for handing back what the sea borrowed. You thank him. Badly. With fish, usually." He frowned. "He won't take the fish neither, most times."

"He takes them," Tressa corrected. "Then he leaves them on old Widow Chegwin's step, because her son's lost to the herring grounds and her pride won't let her ask." She said it without softness, as a fact among facts, but her hand tightened briefly around her cup. "Seven years he's been up there. Seven years of pulling our people out of the water. My own Tam, the winter of '54 — swept off the Gull's Teeth. I was told to prepare for a body." Her voice did something complicated and then refused to elaborate. "Instead I got a husband back, dripping, wrapped in the keeper's own coat, with Blackwood walking behind him carrying my Tam's boots because the man had lost those and Blackwood judged a man ought to walk home in his own boots."

She looked at me then, and her eyes were shrewd beneath the weathering.

"You're the counting woman. The one been up to the tower."

"I'm conducting returns for the Crown, yes."

"Mm. Well. Count carefully." She drained her cider and stood. "But count fair. There's a difference, and I'd wager your forms haven't got a column for it."

* * *

I want to explain what her remark did to me, though explanation may ruin it.

My profession is suspicion with a salary. I am paid to notice that Hendry may not exist, that the harbor master's ledgers show four wrecks this year while the customs office in Falmouth shows one, that wreckage washes up on this coast bearing maker's marks from ports that no legitimate trader would visit in this weather. I had come to Porth Anwin already half-convinced that the silent keeper on the headland was the still point around which some quiet illegality turned. Wreckers need a light, after all. They need it wrong. A man who keeps a lamp true through seven years of storms is either a saint or a very patient liar, and I had met enough of the latter to budget accordingly.

And now this. A man who swam into breakers for strangers' sons and asked for nothing. Who carried boots. Who redistributed fish according to some private arithmetic of need. It is very difficult to build a case against a man when the evidence keeps testifying to his character.

I did what any rigorous professional would do. I got thoroughly drunk on two-and-a-half cups of cider — my tolerance is a matter of public embarrassment — and lay awake in the inn's best room listening to the sea work at the harbor wall like a creditor, turning the problem over and over.

Here is what troubled me, and I record it plainly: admiration is more dangerous than suspicion. Suspicion keeps its distance. Admiration climbs the cliff path. I had gone up that headland once and come down with a crack in my certainty shaped exactly like Silas Blackwood's face when I asked about kin. If I went up again, I might come down with worse.

* * *

The next two days I conducted my survey with method if not enthusiasm, and the village, in return, conducted its own quiet referendum on how much to tell me.

What they told me — in fragments, in asides, in the particular silence that follows a question — assembled itself into something I can only call a ledger, though no page of it was ever written.

A deckhand off the *Providence*, drowned within sight of shore in '53; Blackwood brought him up alive against all sense. Two brothers whose smack foundered on the Teeth; Blackwood stood on the rocks guiding them in by lantern through a fog you could carve, and when they ran aground anyway he was in the water before their mast finished falling. A French sailor — here several speakers grew vague in the manner of people approaching a subject sideways — washed ashore two winters back, half-dead, speaking no English; the village nursed him, and when he was whole again he was simply no longer there, and no one remembered asking where he'd gone, and I was invited to infer that no one ever would.

"And the keeper?" I asked the chapel minister, a gentle, evasive man named Curnow who polished his spectacles when lying. "Was he part of that kindness?"

"The keeper," said Mr. Curnow, polishing, "is a man who believes the light belongs to whoever is swimming toward it. I have never found it profitable to inquire further into his theology."

It was Garren, on my last morning, who put the shape of it into words. I found him mending net on the quay — or pretending to; I suspect his nets were fully mended weeks ago and the mending was a stage set for avoiding his wife — and I sat on a bollard beside him and asked, outright, why a man would spend seven years rescuing strangers for nothing.

Garren considered this for the length of a tide.

"You've seen the tower," he said. "One man, one lamp, all that rock and weather. You think about what that costs a body?" He pulled a strand of net tight. "Some men pay their debts in coin. Some pay in solitude. He pays both, regular as rent, and asks the world to leave him be while he does it. We honor that." A pause. "Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" I repeated.

"Well." He squinted at the horizon with great innocence. "We're only human. Sometimes we bring him fish anyway. Man can't swim away from every kindness."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and Garren looked pleased, the way a fisherman looks when he has landed something heavier than expected.

"Counting woman," he said, as I rose to go, "you'll be going back up the headland, I expect."

"It's on my route."

"It is not on anyone's route. That's the beauty of it." He tied off his knot. "When you go — and you will go — ask him about Maren."

"Who is Maren?"

"That," said Garren, "is the question. See how he answers it. Then you'll know whether you're hunting a smuggler or burying a grief, and you'll conduct yourself accordingly." He returned to his net. "Or you'll do neither, because it's none of your business, but I'm an optimist about government."

* * *

I spent the coach ride back along the coast road doing sums, because sums are where cowards hide.

On one side of the ledger: a harbor master reporting fewer wrecks than the sea delivers; foreign wreckage on a beach that officially receives none; a keeper who lied to a Crown officer about kin expected; a village whose collective memory developed convenient gaps whenever my satchel came into view.

On the other side: a boy named Pascoe alive. A man named Tam alive, walking home in his own boots. A widow's doorstep provisioned by stealth. Seven years of a flame kept burning true — for I had checked the shipping notices; masters spoke well of the headland light, spoke of it the way sailors speak of things that have decided they will live.

Both columns could be true at once. That was the trouble. I had built my career on the comfortable axiom that people sorted: the honest and the false, the reported and the hidden. Porth Anwin proposed instead that a man could keep a lawful light with unlawful hands, could lie about one thing while being truthful about everything that mattered, could be — the word arrived uninvited and stayed — *good*, in defiance of every form I carried.

And beneath the sums, quieter, the thing I did not write down: I recognized him. Not the circumstances — I have never hauled anyone from a sea in my life; I get seasick in bathtubs — but the strategy. A life measured out so nothing could be taken from you. A self edited down to function. I had chosen invisibility too; I had simply chosen it with better stationery.

The coach rounded the last bluff and the tower came into view against a bruised afternoon sky, thin and white and stubborn, exactly the sort of structure that believes it exists to be useful and has never once considered being loved.

I had three days of reports due. I had a blank line in my notes where a certain household's expected kin should be recorded. I had instructions, standing orders, a career constructed on the principle that what I observe, I report.

I also had a fish, left anonymously on the inn's step that morning, which everyone in the village swore they knew nothing about, and which I strongly suspected had traveled by way of a keeper who couldn't prevent kindness and wouldn't accept thanks.

I looked at the tower a long moment. Then I took out my notebook, and instead of writing my report, I wrote a single line in the margin, small enough that only I would know it was there:

*Maren.*

Three days later the post rider climbed the headland road with a government envelope in his bag, and whatever was in it would make my little marginal note look like the smallest of the secrets gathering around that tower like weather.

But that, as they say, is another chapter of the ledger — and this one wasn't closed yet.
