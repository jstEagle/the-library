## 8. Enemy of the Crown

There is a particular arithmetic to being wanted by a government. I have had seven years to learn it, and I can report that the sums never balance. You are worth, at various times, three hundred pounds, then one hundred and fifty, then — after some minister's embarrassment faded from the papers — a mere fifty, which stung more than the larger figures, I confess. A man's pride is a stupid animal. It cannot be told that a discount on your head is not a judgment on your character.

My name, before it became a warrant number, was Tomas Ferro. My sister married a man who measured his life in portable things, and I loved him for it before I understood it, and I understood it too late, which is the usual order of understanding.

But I am ahead of myself. Fugitives are always ahead of themselves; it is how we stay alive.

* * *

I was a cartographer. That is the part people find funny, when they find anything about me funny at all, which is rare. Not a revolutionary, not a pamphleteer, not a man of powder and grievance — a mapmaker. I drew coastlines for a living. I could render a harbor so faithfully that a sailor who had never seen it could bring a ship in safe on my lines alone. It was considered a gift. Men bought me dinners. One of them, a Mr. Halloran with rings on four fingers, bought me several, and asked careful questions between courses, and paid for my ink and paper for a year, and I was flattered enough not to wonder why.

A coastline, you see, is only information. And information, in the year of our Lord 1852, was becoming the most valuable cargo in Europe.

What I drew was ordinary. Harbors, channels, depths, the positions of batteries — all of it public knowledge to anyone with a boat and patience. But Mr. Halloran did not sell to sailors. He sold to men who thought in fleets. And when the trouble came — and trouble always comes; it is the tide of politics, it asks no one's permission — the maps were found in places maps should never have gone, in hands that should never have held them, and the trail led back through Halloran, who had by then vanished into some empire's pocket, and stopped, as trails do, at the last name written in the ledger.

Mine.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I have carried free of charge across three borders: I did not do it on purpose, but I did not do it innocently either. I suspected Halloran. There was an evening — I remember the rain, the particular grey rain of that city, the way I remember everything now, because memory is the only country that will have me — there was an evening when he asked me about a naval anchorage and I felt something cold walk down my spine, and I ignored it, because he had bought me dinner and flattery is cheaper than dinner but twice as effective. I finished the chart. I told myself a coastline belongs to everyone.

It does not belong to everyone. That is a lie drafted men tell themselves while other men drown.

Fourteen people died in what followed. I know the number because I made myself learn it, the way Silas makes himself trim wicks — a discipline against softness. Fourteen souls, two of them boys under sixteen, drowned or burned depending on which account you believe, and every account differs, and none of them matter to the dead. The Crown decided I was the traitor. The other side decided I was their man and abandoned me the moment I stopped being useful. I have been claimed and disowned by both sides of a war I never believed in, and the only person in the world who ever understood how that feels died believing I would come to her brother someday.

No. Wrong order again. She died asking me to go to him.

* * *

Maren wrote to me for years. Letters that read like weather reports, mostly — she knew better than to put names in ink that might be read. *The light held through March. S. talks to the lamp like a parishioner. Come south when you can; the fish is good and nobody asks questions.* Nobody asks questions. It was her great theory of the coast, that the sea washes curiosity out of people, leaves them clean and incurious. She was half right. The other half is what killed her, slowly, of damp and stubbornness, tending a light beside a man who would not leave it.

The last letter came eleven weeks after she died. I did not know she had died; the letter announcing it went to a safe house that was burned by then, and so I learned of my sister's death the way I learned of my own condemnation — secondhand, late, in handwriting I didn't recognize. It was from Silas. Three sentences. I have them by heart, which is not difficult, as they are short, and grief compresses men:

*Maren passed on the ninth. The light burns. If you ever reach this coast, come.*

That was all. No address beyond the lighthouse, no signature but his initial, no comfort offered or asked. Three sentences, and I sat on the floor of a stranger's kitchen in Antwerp and wept like something broken open, because those three sentences were the entire estate of my sister's husband, and they were worth more than any will.

*If you ever reach this coast, come.*

You must understand what that invitation cost him. I am, on paper, an enemy of the Crown. Sheltering me is not kindness; it is treason, priced at hanging. Silas Blackwood had just buried his wife, and he spent his first free breath inviting a hunted man to his door. I have turned that over for seven years like a stone in the pocket, feeling for its flaw, looking for the self-interest, the guilt, the debt he imagined. There is none. Or if there is, it is only this: he loved her, and she loved me, and love does not recognize borders, which is precisely why governments hate it.

And yet I did not go. Seven years, and I did not go.

Shame is a strange anchor. You would think it would drive a man toward redemption, but shame is a coward; it hides. Every year I told myself next year, when I am less watched, when the warrant cools, when I deserve it. Deserve it — listen to me. As if grief were a wage to be earned. Meanwhile the warrant cooled and heated and cooled again like iron in someone else's forge, and I lived under six names in five countries, and I grew skilled at being no one, and let me tell you what no one told me: being no one is a death conducted in installments.

* * *

The prison was called Fort Meridian, though the men inside called it the Ledger, because everything about it was counted — your steps, your rations, your sentence in days chalked on a wall and rubbed out each morning by a guard who enjoyed rubbing it out. I was there nineteen months before the escape, and I will not describe it in detail, partly because it does not deserve good prose and partly because I have found that if I say the whole of it aloud, even on paper, I begin to plan my days around avoiding it again, and a fugitive cannot afford new fears. I will say this much: they put me in a cell with a window facing the sea, which the commandant thought was mercy, or cruelty — with such men it is impossible to know which — and it was the worst thing they could have done, because I am a cartographer, and every day for nineteen months I looked at that water and mapped my way out of it.

The escape itself was not daring. Daring is for novels. It was a bribed cook, a laundry barrel, forty minutes in the hold of a fish-smoker among crates that smelled so violently of brine that the dogs refused me, and then the open deck of a boat whose captain never once looked at my face, which is the finest quality a captain can possess. From there: the long shamble north along coasts I had once drawn for money, working passage, sleeping in hay, learning that hunger has a flavor (copper) and fear has a sound (your own breath, at night, in a room that should be empty).

And everywhere I went, I checked the papers. Not for news — fugitives have no use for news — for names. Warrants list names alphabetically, and I had worked my way down through the T's of three nations' worth of gazettes like a man saying rosaries. Somewhere in the spring of this year, in a port town whose name I will not record even now, I found a small item: coastal decommissioning orders. Lighthouses automated. Keepers displaced. And among the stations listed, one name of a headland I knew from Maren's letters, and beneath it, in the dry language of bureaucracy, a note about registration of coastal residents against a general watch list.

They were going to automate his light. They were going to count every soul on that shore.

And I understood, with the cold clarity that comes perhaps twice in a life, that my window was closing. While Silas Blackwood was invisible — one keeper, one tower, no neighbors closer than a village that owed him nothing on paper — I could reach him. But when the lists came, when every head on that coast required a name and a justification, then a strange man arriving at a lighthouse would not be a tragedy; it would be an entry in a ledger. The Crown was about to do to the whole coastline what it had done to me: make it visible, and thereby destroy it.

So I went. Finally. Shamefully late, but I went.

* * *

The crossing nearly killed me, and I almost let it, which I am not proud of either. There is a moment in rough water when your body starts negotiating on its own behalf, and your mind — tired, hunted, nineteen months of the Ledger still folded up inside you like a letter never sent — your mind considers the terms. The water was black and thorough and it asked nothing of me except everything, and part of me wanted to pay. It was Maren who stopped me. Not a ghost, nothing so kind. Her locket, which I carried against my chest, waterlogged, the chain cutting my throat as the sea threw me around, and I thought: she gave me this face to carry, and I will not deliver it drowned. Sentiment, pure sentiment, saving a man where courage failed. Let the moralists make of that what they will.

I washed up, more or less literally, into a boathouse on his headland, and I lay there on the cold boards listening to my own heart argue with the surf, and I made myself a promise with the last of my coherence: I will not ask him for forgiveness. Forgiveness is a tax on the forgiver. I will ask him only for shelter, which is what was promised, and I will carry my guilt out of his house the way I carried it across Europe — alone, quietly, in the pocket where the locket sits.

Then the door opened, and there he was.

Seven years older. Greyer. Thinner, the way wood goes thin when it has been left out in weather. He stood in the doorway holding a lantern, and the light fell across me, and I watched him understand — first that I was alive, then who I was, then what I meant, all in the space of a single breath, and I watched the fear arrive and the tenderness arrive and neither one win. He said my name. Just the name, the real one, the one no living person had spoken aloud since Maren died, and I heard it break something in me that had been sealed shut since the Ledger, some valve, and I am not ashamed to say I wept, though I am ashamed of everything else, so perhaps the accounting balances after all.

He did not ask what happened. He did not ask if I had done it. He lifted me up the way you lift something you intend to keep, and he fed me, and I thought: this is what she meant. *Come.* Not *come and explain yourself.* Just *come.*

* * *

Now it is morning, or what passes for morning under this weather — a grey suggestion of light through the boathouse seams — and I sit wrapped in his spare coat writing this in my head because a mapmaker thinks in documents even when he has no paper, and because I need to rehearse what I will say to him when I am strong enough to say anything useful. Because here is the truth I have not yet told him, the thing that arrived with me in the water besides the locket:

They know I came this way.

The night before I sailed, in the last port, a man bought me a drink and asked careful questions between courses, and I felt the cold walk down my spine again, the same cold, Halloran's cold, and I told myself paranoia, told myself trauma, told myself a coastline belongs to everyone. Then my boat's captain vanished before dawn, taking his fee and leaving no forwarding shadow, and I knew. Someone sold my route. Someone always sells the route; betrayal is the only reliable export of the smuggling trade. Which means somewhere behind me, perhaps hours, perhaps days, is a man with a list and a warrant, and somewhere ahead of me is a coast about to be swept by soldiers with the same list.

Silas has spent seven years building a life out of portable things, small and hidden and safe. I am about to become the heaviest thing he has ever carried.

Above me I hear his boots on the cliff path, coming down through the rain, and I hear a second set of footsteps — lighter, unfamiliar, pausing too often. A stranger, walking the coast road with him. Asking questions, perhaps.

Everyone asks questions eventually. Maren was only half right about the sea.

She was entirely right about him.
