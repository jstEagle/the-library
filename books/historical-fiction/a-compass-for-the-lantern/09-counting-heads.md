## 9. Counting Heads

There is a particular species of letter that arrives folded twice and sealed with wax the color of old teeth, and it never contains good news. I had received three in my life: one informing me of my father's debts, one informing me of my father's death, and one informing me that the debts had survived him. The fourth arrived on a Tuesday in November, and it informed me that I was to return to Blackwood Head.

*Verification of household composition prior to incorporation of coastal registry*, it said, in the round, blameless hand of a clerk who would never smell salt spray in his life. *The keeper's returns are irregular. Confirm occupancy, enumerate all persons resident or harbored, and note any anomaly for the watch list.*

Harbored. Note that word. It does a great deal of work in government prose. A man can be *staying* somewhere, *lodging*, even *sheltering from the weather*, which is practically a constitutional right in this country. But *harbored* — harbored is what you do to criminals and contraband. Harbored implies intent. Someone in an office with a carpet had looked at the phrase *any kin expected* written in my careful hand, and then at Silas Blackwood's answers, and decided the arithmetic did not sit right.

It didn't sit right with me either. That was the trouble. I had spent three weeks trying not to think about it, and had failed in the way one fails at not thinking about an itch, or a debt, or a face seen briefly at a window where no face should be.

Because that was the thing I had not put in my report. On my first visit, while the keeper fetched his papers — taking rather longer about it than a man with only himself to count ought to take — I had looked up through the tower window at the path above the boathouse, and someone had been standing there. Tall, dark-haired, moving with the particular caution of a person who has learned that being seen is expensive. He was gone between one blink and the next, the way figures go when they have practice at going.

A brother, perhaps. A servant. A ghost of the headland, of which the coast manufactures a reliable supply. I had told myself all three and believed none of them.

Now the Crown wanted me to go back and count.

My landlady, Mrs. Trench, watched me pack my satchel with the expression of a woman who considers all government business a symptom of moral failure. "Back to the lighthouse man?" she said. "In this season?"

"The season is precisely why," I said. "The watch list closes at midwinter."

"Watch list." She said it the way she said *methodist* and *foreigner*. "Half the fishing families will be on it. They've been on this coast since before there was a Crown to watch them."

"Then they'll be well documented at last."

"They'll be *taxed* at last, you mean." She sniffed, but her hands kept folding my spare collars, because Mrs. Trench's radicalism has never once extended to wrinkled linen. "You mind yourself on that road. The sea's been wrong all week. Old Pell down at the Anchor says the gulls are flying inland like they owe money."

I did not tell her that I found the gulls more credible than most of my official correspondence. Some sympathies are best left unregistered.

* * *

The coast road in November is a lesson in humility. The wind comes off the water flat and personal, as though it has taken a dislike to you individually, and the gorse bends low and whispers things about your life choices. I walked it with my collar up and my ledger buttoned inside my coat, because paper and seawater have never reconciled, and by the time the tower rose out of the murk I was rehearsing questions in my head the way other people rehearse apologies.

The lighthouse looked different from the landward side than it had from the village — less like a monument and more like a man standing very still, pretending not to be listening. The tower, whitewashed and scarred; the keeper's cottage hunched against it like something grown there over years, organically, the way barnacles grow on hulls. Smoke from the chimney, thin and domestic. Two chairs' worth of smoke, I thought wildly, and then made myself stop thinking in smoke-signals like a schoolgirl reading penny dreadfuls.

Silas Blackwood opened the door before I knocked. I never did learn whether he watched the road or simply heard me; he had the sort of stillness that suggested both.

"Miss Quill." Not a question. He remembered my name, and gave nothing away about how he felt using it. He was taller than I'd allowed myself to remember, and grayer, and his eyes went past my shoulder to the empty road behind me with an expression I could only call *reconnaissance*.

"Mr. Blackwood." I produced the order like a warrant, which it nearly was. "I'm afraid I'm back. There's to be a coastal registry — you may have heard. All residents enumerated before the light changes hands."

Something moved behind his face, quick as the figure at the window had moved. "I heard."

"May I come in?"

He stood aside. It was permission of a kind. The cottage interior was exactly what his exterior promised: clean without comfort, everything squared to walls, everything portable. I had noticed that on my first visit and misread it as poverty. I was beginning to suspect it was philosophy. A man who owns nothing that cannot be carried in five minutes is either very poor or very prepared, and Silas Blackwood did not strike me as poor.

"You'll want the forms again," he said. It wasn't a question either. We were two people conducting an entire conversation in declarative sentences, like rival lawyers.

"I'll want the truth, actually. The forms are just where we file it."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth in the room, and the room contained a fire.

"Name?" I said, pen ready, though I knew it.

"You know it."

"For the record, Mr. Blackwood. Records don't run on memory. Memory dies; ink doesn't."

"What a cheerful faith you have in ink." But he sat, and said it again — *Silas Blackwood, keeper, widower, age forty-four* — and I wrote it, and the pen scratched in the quiet like a small animal in a wall.

"Occupation: lighthouse keeper, pending decommission." I let that word sit on the table between us. "And the household. Persons resident. That's the irregularity, you understand. It's not personal."

"No," he said. "Nothing is personal anymore. That seems to be the whole of the new century arriving early."

"It's still 1859."

"Is it." He looked at the fire. "Some days I'd swear it was 1793. They also enjoyed counting heads, that year."

I wrote *household: one* and hated myself a little for how easily the numeral came. Then I set the pen down, which surprised us both, I think.

"Mr. Blackwood. Before I write anything else. Is there anyone else here?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above us the lamp mechanism ticked through its rotation, patient as a heart.

"Why would you ask that?"

"Because on my first visit, while you were fetching your papers, I saw a man on the path by the boathouse. And because your answers about kin expected were the least truthful thing I've encountered in eleven years of census work, and I once enumerated a smuggler who swore under oath he'd never been east of Exeter." I kept my voice level, professional, the voice of the form rather than the woman. "I am going to ask you plainly, and I want you to understand that how you answer determines what I do next. Is there someone in this house?"

He held my gaze, and I will say this for the man: he did not insult me with theater. What passed across his face was not innocence but arithmetic — a man counting costs, deciding what he could afford to hand me.

"There's no one in this house," he said slowly, "who is here against anyone's law but his own conscience's."

"That is not an answer suitable for a government form."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

I looked at my ledger. *Household: one.* One line, already inked. Below it, space for amendments. My hand hovered there longer than a hand ought to hover over its own handwriting, and I was aware of him watching it hover, and aware — this was the part that frightened me — that I was not hovering out of doubt about the facts. The facts were nearly settled. I was hovering because I was waiting to find out what kind of person I was, and no one had ever warned me that census work included that particular enumeration.

"Whoever he is," I said quietly, "if he is what I think he is, then writing his name on this list isn't verification. It's a sentence. You know what happens to names on watch lists now. They don't get questioned. They get collected."

"Yes," Silas Blackwood said. "I know."

"And if I leave the line as it is — one person, one keeper — and someone else counts this house later, and finds two—"

"Then it falls on me. Not on you. Your form says one. Let their soldiers count whatever they like; the discrepancy is mine to explain."

"That's very noble and completely useless, since I'd know I lied, which makes me the accomplice regardless of what the ink says."

For the first time, he turned fully toward me, and I saw the man Maren Blackwood must have married — not the granite keeper, but something warmer and more reckless underneath, banked these seven years like a stove overnight.

"Then don't lie," he said. "Leave the line blank. Write *verification incomplete, weather preventing full survey.* It's even true. The weather," he added, glancing at the window, where the light had gone the color of wet slate, "is certainly coming."

"That's a stalling tactic."

"Everything on the coast is a stalling tactic, Miss Quill. The tide stalls, the seasons stall, the wrecks stall before they break. Eventually everything arrives. But not everything needs to arrive by midwinter."

Outside, the wind changed its mind and came around hard from the southeast, and the whole cottage leaned into it like a ship. I heard, distantly, beneath the tower's ticking — a sound. A floorboard, perhaps. Or a foot shifting weight after standing too long in one place, in a room above ours, being very careful not to exist.

Silas heard it too. He didn't move, didn't glance at the ceiling, which was itself the confirmation — an innocent man would have looked up. Instead he watched me, and I understood that he knew I'd heard it, and that everything now depended on what a tired woman with a satchel and eleven years of ink-stained integrity decided to do about a floorboard.

I closed the ledger.

"Verification incomplete," I said. "Weather preventing full survey. I'll return after the blow, when conditions permit a proper count." I stood, and my knees informed me that the walk back to the village in a rising gale would be memorable. "Mr. Blackwood. If I come back and find one person in this house, I shall write one. If I come back and find two, I shall write—" I stopped, because finishing that sentence honestly required knowing things about myself I hadn't yet surveyed. "I shall write what conscience permits."

"Conscience doesn't keep records," he said, opening the door onto the roar.

"No. People do. That's the whole problem with it."

* * *

I made perhaps two miles before the storm closed its fist. The rain came not down but sideways, with malice, and the road dissolved into a stream that had opinions about my boots. I sheltered in the lee of the coastguard hut at Gull Point, shivering, watching the sea stand up in gray slabs and throw itself at the rocks, and I took out my ledger there, absurdly, to look at what I had written, as though the words might have rearranged themselves into clarity while I walked.

They hadn't. *Household: one. Verification incomplete.* Two sentences, and between them the width of a man's life.

Eleven years, I have carried this satchel. Eleven years of doors opening onto honest faces and dishonest ones, and my job has always been the same: to reduce people to lines, so that the government can hold them all in one hand. And I had always believed it was neutral work — that ink was ink, that a name recorded was a name preserved, that I was the memory of places that had none. I had even been proud of it, in the modest way one is proud of good penmanship.

Now I understood what I had actually been all along: the tip of something. A finger pointing. And fingers don't choose what they point at — except that mine had hovered today over a blank line, and hovering, I discovered, is choice. The moment of hesitation is where the soul files its own report.

Through the hut's cracked shutter I could see the headland, and the tower standing in the murk with its flame not yet lit — early yet, too early — and I thought about whoever was up there in that sealed white room, breathing carefully, being no one, while the wind tried every seam in the building. I thought: he trusted a stranger with a government satchel because the alternative was letting me leave certain. That is either love or madness, and I have seen enough of both to know they use the same grammar.

The mail rider would pass the Anchor tonight, I knew, and my report — *incomplete, weather delaying* — would reach the district office by Thursday, and some clerk with carpet under his feet would read it and stamp it and file it, unless the storm delayed the mails too, or unless the district office had begun, as rumor said, sending its verifiers back within days rather than weeks, impatient, suspicious, closing the net before midwinter.

I buttoned the ledger away and turned my face toward the village and let the wind push me, and behind me the great lamp finally bloomed to life on the headland, sweeping its slow gold blade across the water — once, twice — the way it had swept for seven years over a man who had taught himself to need no one.

Not, I suspected, anymore.

And not, if the sky had anything to say about it, for very much longer.
