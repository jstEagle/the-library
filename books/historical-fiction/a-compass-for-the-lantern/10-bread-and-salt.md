## 10. Bread and Salt

There is an arithmetic to feeding a man you shouldn't be feeding. I learned it the second night, standing in my kitchen with two bowls instead of one, and understanding that the difference between hospitality and treason was roughly the width of a second spoon.

I'd told myself I would think on it. That was the phrase I'd used, lying awake while Tomas slept in the boathouse among the nets — *I'll think on it* — as though thinking were a currency that could be exchanged later for a clear conscience. Seven years I had kept Maren's promise the way you keep a coat in a closet: present, unworn, never quite fitting the man you'd become. And now here was Tomas Rennick, half-drowned and hollow-cheeked, sitting at my table with Maren's locket open in front of his bowl like a portrait attending dinner.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm deciding something."

"About me."

"About soup," I said. "Whether there's enough for seconds. There isn't. So eat slow."

He ate slow. He was good at doing what he was told, which troubled me more than defiance would have. Prison teaches obedience the way the sea teaches swimming — thoroughly, and only to those it hasn't drowned yet. His hands had a tremor he kept trying to disguise by wrapping them around the bowl, as if the warmth were the point and not the steadying.

"The bread's three days old," I said. "It improves with guilt. Stale bread and a guilty conscience — that's the whole of housekeeping on this rock."

"Maren baked," he said. Not a question. A measurement, the way you'd say *the tide comes in*.

"She baked like a woman settling a score with the flour. Everything came out of her oven either magnificent or on fire, nothing between." I heard my own voice do something it hadn't done in years — soften around a name. I put a stop to it. Sentiment, I've found, is like oil smoke: harmless in small quantities, and lethal when it accumulates where the flame needs clean air. "Eat. Then sleep. Tomorrow we'll talk about how long you intend to make lying to the Crown my full-time occupation."

He looked up at that. Brown eyes, Maren's exactly, and just as incapable of deceit as hers had been — which is to say, entirely capable of it, but apologetic about the practice.

"I can leave," he said quietly. "When I'm able. I didn't come to bury you, Silas. I came because she said—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I had nowhere else that was hers."

And there it was. The debt, presented not as demand but as inheritance. I took his bowl before he could see what his face was doing to mine, and washed it, and did not answer him, and understood as I scrubbed a dish that needed no scrubbing that I had already answered.

Some men decide with declarations. I decide with dishes.

* * *

The third morning I taught him the lamp.

Not because I trusted him. Because a man who lies in your boathouse all day is a man whose absence will be noticed, whose presence will eventually be discovered, and whose hands — idle hands especially — will find trouble the way water finds a seam. Better he should be useful. Usefulness is camouflage. Nobody interrogates a keeper's assistant; they interrogate strangers, and strangers are made of unoccupied hours.

"This is a Fresnel lens," I said, running my sleeve along the brass frame out of habit rather than need. It was already clean. It is always clean. A keeper's lens has less to do with glass than with penance. "French. The finest piece of engineering ever wasted on a coastline nobody important has ever bothered to visit. Prisms — thirty of them in the belt — take one flame and throw it nineteen miles. You don't feed the light, understand. You *aim* it. The light was always there. The craft is in the pointing."

Tomas circled the lens the way a man circles a cathedral. He reached toward the glass and stopped short, his fingers hovering, waiting for permission.

"Touch it," I said. "It forgives fingerprints. It's the rest of the world that doesn't."

He laughed — a small sound, rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and touched the prism where it met the frame. "It's warm."

"That's the lamp below. The heat rises through the floor. In winter it's the only courtesy this tower extends." I showed him the clockwork then: the weight-driven rotation, the great drum of cast iron descending its shaft at a pace so patient you could mistake it for stillness. "Every four hours I wind it. Miss a winding and the light stands still, and a standing light tells a captain the coast is safe when it's anything but. Ships have died of an unwound clock. Remember that. Most disasters aren't failures of strength. They're failures of attention."

He nodded, and I watched him file it away, and thought: *he'll remember. This one remembers things.* Which was precisely what I could not afford. A man with a memory like his, in a household under census, on a coast moving toward registration and watch lists — every fact he learned was another fact that could be tortured out of him or dug out of his pockets if the wrong patrol got ambitious.

But I taught him anyway. That is the thing about family, I have since learned: it doesn't consult the intelligence.

"Oil store is below," I said, taking him down the winding stair. "Rape oil. Don't ask me why they call it that; the French named it and the French name everything twice. Three barrels. The vaporizer, the burner assembly, the spare wicks. You'll learn the trimming. A bad wick makes a bad flame, and a bad flame makes dead sailors, and dead sailors make widows, and widows write letters to the Admiralty, and the Admiralty writes back with men in uniform. Everything on this coast is downstream of a wick."

"You talk like her," Tomas said suddenly, from behind me on the stairs.

I stopped with my hand on the rail. Below us the oil store smelled of tallow and cold stone, and above us nineteen miles of sea waited for its flame, and between the two stood a man being compared to his dead wife by the last person alive who had earned the comparison.

"I talk like a tired man with a lecture prepared," I said, and went down the rest of the steps.

* * *

We fell into a rhythm the way shipwrecked timber falls into a current — not by choice, but because everything floating eventually finds the same water.

He rose when I rose. He hauled coal without being asked, and split it fine enough for the burner, and learned within four days the particular temper of our stove, which runs sulky in damp weather and must be coaxed with patience I do not personally possess. He wound the clockwork on the odd watches and never once let it run down. On the fifth evening I came up to the lantern to find the prisms polished and the log written in a careful hand, the entries correct but for one word — he wrote *visibility excellent* where I write *clear*, because Maren wrote *excellent* too, and somewhere in his memory the two of them had braided together into a single vocabulary.

I corrected the entry. Then I sat with the correction for a long moment, feeling like a man correcting a dead woman's handwriting, and left it.

At meals we talked, which was the dangerous part. Meals are where evidence accumulates. Two bowls in a wash basin. Two chairs drawn up instead of one. A second set of boots drying by the door, patched at the heel — any surveyor, any passing coastguard with eyes would read that doorway like a page. I began eating earlier, with the shutters closed, and burning the candle low, and I caught myself listening for hoofbeats on the cliff road during supper the way other men listen for weather.

"What happens," Tomas asked one night, turning his cup without drinking, "if they come?"

"They won't come tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I'll say you're a wrecker I've taken on for the season. Wreckers are local sin, not national ones. The Coastguard tolerates them the way a farmer tolerates crows — with grievance and familiarity." I tore the loaf and gave him the larger half, an act of arithmetic I performed before I could catch myself doing it. "It won't hold against anyone determined. But determination requires paperwork, and paperwork requires the season, and the season may save us."

"May," he said.

"I'm a lighthouse keeper, not a prophet. My entire profession consists of saying: *there is danger ahead, proceed carefully.* No one's ever paid me for certainty."

That earned the rusty-hinge laugh again. And then it faded, and he looked at the locket — he wore it now, always, under his shirt, and I confess I had not asked and did not want to know how he'd come by it, whether Maren had sent it or he had stolen it, and what either answer would cost — and he said, very low:

"She told me about you. Before any of it. Before the arrests, before the charter went wrong, before they put my name on lists in three capitals. She said, *Silas keeps things. He'll keep you too.*" His thumb moved over the table's grain. "I thought she meant it the way she meant everything — fondly, and slightly wrong."

"She was never wrong," I said. "She was merely early. There's a difference, and living with it is the whole work of loving someone like that."

He looked at me then the way you look at a light you've been steering toward for a long time in bad water. I stood, gathered the bowls, and informed him that the clockwork wanted winding, which it did not, and went to wind it.

Evidence, I reminded myself, over the basin. Every meal. Every corrected log entry. Every hour of his handwriting in my ledger. I was building a case against myself with the diligence of a customs officer, and the terrible thing — the thing I turned over at night while Tomas slept and the light turned overhead like the hand of a patient clock — was that I did not want to stop.

Seven years I had measured my life in portable things. What could be carried, what could be hidden, what could be surrendered to an official with a clipboard without breaking me. I had whittled myself down to a lamp and a grave and a grief small enough to fit in a coat pocket.

And now here was a second chair. A second bowl. Boots by the door and a man upstairs who said *goodnight, Silas* in Maren's cadence, and expected nothing except breakfast, and had no idea that breakfast was the most expensive thing on this headland.

Unportable, all of it. Unhiddenable. Worth everything.

* * *

On the eighth morning Ada Quill came back up the cliff path, and I saw her from the lantern gallery while Tomas slept, and stood there in the wind doing sums I couldn't balance.

She walked with her satchel and her head down against the breeze, a small dark figure on the green slope, and I knew — keepers know weather and I knew this — that she had not come about the census. Census takers do not return to households already counted. She came because she had seen something on this coast that didn't reconcile, because that was her nature, and because the blank space where my answers should have been complete was itself an answer, and she was the sort of woman who could not leave a sum unfinished.

Below me, through six floors of stairwell, I could hear Tomas turning in his sleep, murmuring something in a language the sea had given him.

I went down to wake him, and decided — with the bowl-washing part of me, the practical part, the part that had already decided everything days ago — exactly how much truth a census taker could safely be told.

Then I opened the door, and put the kettle on, and set out two cups instead of one.
