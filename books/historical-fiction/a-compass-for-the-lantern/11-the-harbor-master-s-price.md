## 11. The Harbor Master's Price

I had been in the village three days when I learned that the man who could get me out of England was also the man most likely to sell me.

This is not cynicism. It is arithmetic. I had spent four years in a cell with men who had been sold by brothers, wives, and once — memorably — by a parrot who repeated a name at the wrong window. A smuggler's loyalty is a commodity like any other: it has a price, and the price goes up when soldiers come asking. And soldiers had been coming asking. I'd seen two of them on the harbor road that morning, red as poppies against the grey stone, asking their questions with the leisurely confidence of men who knew the answer would arrive eventually, like weather.

So I did what any sensible fugitive does. I waited for dark, I counted my coins for the hundredth time — eleven shillings and a Dutch half-crown of dubious honesty — and I went looking for Captain Merrick Dune.

The villagers would not say his name aloud, which I took as a good sign. Men whose names are spoken freely tend to have nothing worth buying. They pointed instead with their chins, all of them, in the same direction, toward a low-roofed tavern at the end of the quay that called itself The Anchor's Rest and looked like it had never rested anything but contraband and regret.

* * *

The tavern smelled of tar, tobacco, and fish that had died with grievances. I pushed through the door in my borrowed coat — Silas's, too broad in the shoulders, which made me look either prosperous or deformed — and let my eyes adjust to the pipe smoke.

Eleven men, one dog, and behind the counter a woman with forearms like hawser rope who watched me the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has made an interesting decision.

I did not see Dune immediately, because Dune did not wish to be seen. This I understood later. What I saw first was an empty corner table with a single candle on it, and a voice out of the shadow beside it saying, "You'll be the gentleman looking for passage."

"I might be," I said, to the general area of the voice.

"Then sit down before you let the whole room count your teeth." The shadow resolved into a man of perhaps fifty, lean as a gaff hook, with a face like driftwood — everything softened or stripped away except the grain. He wore his years the way I wore my borrowed coat: loosely, over something harder. "Sit. Drink this. You look like a man who's been drinking river water."

He slid a cup toward me. I will not pretend I hesitated. Poison seemed a tidy way to go compared to some alternatives.

"Captain Dune," I said.

"And you'll be nobody, from nowhere, going nowhere in particular." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man doing sums. "That's how these conversations start. That's not always how they finish."

I sat. The dog across the room lifted its head, considered me, and put it down again. Even the dog was running an assessment.

"I need to cross the Channel," I said. "Quietly. Soon."

"Mm." Dune turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the ring it left. "Everyone needs something quietly done, friend. Quiet costs more than loud. Soon costs more than later. And soon-and-quiet, in this season, with redcoats walking the harbor road twice a day—" he looked up, and his eyes were pale and flat as beach pebbles, "—costs more than you're carrying in that coat pocket."

My hand did not move toward my pocket. I was proud of that. Four years in a cell teaches you to keep your hands still.

"How much?" I asked.

"For anyone else? Eight pounds, and I'd drop them in Boulogne harbor if the tide was wrong." He leaned back. "For you — and we'll come round to how I know what you are — thirty."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out cracked from disuse, but it came. "Thirty pounds. I could buy a ship for thirty pounds."

"You couldn't buy a rowboat. But you've got the figure right, I'll grant you." His pebble eyes didn't blink. "Thirty pounds is what your head is worth, more or less. The Gazette offers twenty for information leading to apprehension. I'm charging a markup. Risk premium, you'd call it, if you were a bank."

The candle flame stood perfectly still between us. Somewhere behind me, dice rattled and stopped.

"You know who I am," I said.

"Course I know who you are." He said it the way another man might say *of course it's raining*. "You think a stranger walks into my port wearing a gentleman's hands and a dead man's boots, and I don't ask the harbor master?" He spread his fingers. "I am the harbor master, friend. In the way that matters."

I looked down at my hands, resting on the table, and hated them for their telltale softness. Seven days at sea in an open boat, and they still hadn't earned their calluses back.

"Tomas Vael," he said softly, and the sound of my own name in a public house went through me like cold water through cloth. "Late of Marshbank Prison. Escaped custody six weeks past. Wanted for sedition, printing what the Crown preferred unprinted, and — here's the part I admire — for refusing to give evidence against the men who paid for it." He tilted his head. "They'll hang you, you know. If they take you. Not the quiet transportation they gave your friends. Hanging. You've become an embarrassment, and embarrassments get examples made of them."

Every hair on my arms stood up. Not from fear, exactly — I had lived so long with fear that it had worn smooth, like a stair tread — but from the particular vertigo of hearing your death described casually, over drinks, by a man pricing your passage against your bounty.

"Then why haven't you sold me already?" I asked. My voice came out level. Another small pride.

Dune took his time answering. He reached into his coat and produced a pipe, packed it, tamped it, and did not light it. All the little ceremonies of a man who wants you to watch him think.

"Because," he said finally, "twenty pounds from the Crown comes with questions attached. Where did you find him? Who sheltered you? Who fed him, who forged what, who looked away?" He shook his head slowly. "Twenty pounds buys a gallows and a lot of trouble. Thirty pounds buys me a clean conscience and a clean hold. There's a difference between selling a man and charging him for the inconvenience of not selling him. The first is betrayal. The second is business. I do business."

"That is a very fine distinction," I said.

"It's the only distinction there is." For a moment something moved behind the driftwood face — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but maybe memory. "I've been sold myself, friend. Rotterdam, 'forty-six. I know the price of it from the inside. So no — I won't sell you." He tapped the unlit pipe against the table. "But I won't carry you for eleven shillings and a bad Dutch coin either, and don't think I didn't see you counting it through your coat. I can smell small change."

* * *

We negotiated for an hour. It was, in its way, the most honest conversation I'd had since Silas pulled me half-drowned from his boathouse floor — honest because neither of us pretended to be anything other than what we were. He was a man who moved goods and people across water for profit. I was a man whose continued breathing depended on finding someone exactly like him.

The problem remained the same after an hour: thirty pounds, and I had less than one.

"Then here's what I can offer," Dune said at last, and the way he said it made me stop reaching for arguments I didn't have. "Sail with me as working crew. Three runs up the coast, moving what I move, keeping your mouth shut and your hands busy. After the third run, you cross. No charge beyond labor."

Hope rose in me like a tide coming in — fast, warm, and, I should have noticed, pulling things loose as it came.

"Why?" I asked. "Why delay the crossing? Why not take me now and be rid of me?"

"Because the packet boats are being watched this month. Every fool with a sail and a debt is being boarded between here and Calais." He shrugged. "A crewman is invisible. A passenger is cargo, and cargo gets inspected. Give it three weeks, let the heat move down the coast to where it usually sits, and you walk off my boat in Boulogne like a man going to buy tobacco." He smiled again, and this time there was something in it that I liked even less than the arithmetic. "Besides. A man of letters, aren't you? Printed pamphlets. Set type. I've got ledgers that would make a bishop weep, and a manifest or two that wants rewriting by an educated hand. You can work off part of your fare honestly."

"And the rest?"

"The rest," Dune said, "we'll see."

There it was. The hook under the bait. I heard it clearly, and I sat with it, and I weighed it against the alternative, which was Silas's boathouse and Silas's neck and the widening circle of soldiers on the harbor road, closing like a hand.

Three weeks. Three weeks of hiding a hunted man in a lighthouse that was already under the government's eye — I knew about the decommission notice, Silas had shown me the paper with the particular stillness of a man reading his own eviction. Three weeks was a long time for a thing like me to stay hidden. Every day multiplied the chances: a fisherman with sharp eyes, a soldier with a list, a census woman — Silas had mentioned her too, mentioned her the way you mention a splinter you can't dig out.

And Dune knew it. That was the whole shape of it. He wasn't offering me labor. He was offering me a leash, dressed as a schedule. Each run I sailed for him bound me tighter; each week that passed made Silas's household more dangerous and my own capture more likely; and somewhere at the end of that narrowing road, Captain Merrick Dune would be standing with his hand out, having discovered precisely what a desperate man will pay when he has no money at all.

Or perhaps he'd simply sell me after all, once the risk premium climbed high enough. Business. The fine distinction.

I should have walked out. I want to be honest about that, whoever ends up reading this — if anyone does, if it survives me. I should have stood up, thanked him for the drink, and gone back to tell Silas that the sea route was closed, that we'd need the cliffs and the coal wagons and whatever mad western plan the wreckers muttered about. I should have known that a man who names your price before you've offered anything is not negotiating. He's investing.

But I was tired. Tired down to the marrow, tired of sleeping in salt hay and waking at every gull's cry, tired of Maren's locket weighing on my chest like a debt rather than a keepsake, tired of watching Silas — good, grave, patient Silas, who owed me nothing and was paying anyway — grow thinner and quieter as my presence hollowed out his life. Three weeks of danger for him versus thirty pounds he didn't have. There wasn't really a choice. There was just the shape of the least harm.

"Done," I said. "Crew. Three runs."

Dune extended his hand across the candle, and I took it, and his grip was dry and strong and entirely without friendship.

"Wise lad," he said. "First run's Thursday night. Be at the north slip at slack water, and come alone — though of course you will, because you haven't got anyone else." He said it pleasantly. That was the worst of it. Everything he said, he said pleasantly.

I stood, dropped my last decent coin on the table for the drink, and turned for the door.

"One more thing, Mr. Vael." I stopped with my hand on the latch. Of course I stopped. That was the point of the sentence. "Your sister — she was Maren, wasn't it? Married a lighthouse keeper up the coast." The pipe finally met the flame, and his face swam behind the smoke, unreadable. "Funny thing. A keeper on that headland pulled six of my drowned boys out of the water over the years, never asked a penny. Man like that, you'd hate to see him registered. Watch-listed. Decommissioned." He exhaled. "Best hope nothing goes wrong on those three runs of ours, eh? For everyone's sake."

I walked out into the dark with the wind coming off the water and my heart going like a trapped bird. Behind me the tavern door shut, cutting off the light.

He had known about Silas all along. He hadn't needed to say it. He had said it so I would know he knew — so that every mile I sailed for him, every secret I kept from him, would carry the weight of that headland and the man on it.

I had come to buy a passage. I had left as collateral.

Thursday was three nights away, and slack water waits for no one. I pulled the borrowed coat tight and started up the cliff path toward the light, rehearsing as I climbed all the ways a man can confess a mistake without admitting he's made one — and knowing, all the way up, that I would have to tell Silas everything, because Dune's pleasant voice had told me plainly:

the clock on all our hides had just started running.
