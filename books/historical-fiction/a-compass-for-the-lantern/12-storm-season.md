## 12. Storm Season

The barometer began its confession on a Tuesday.

I had inherited the instrument along with the post—a brass thing the size of a soup bowl, mounted by the stair door by some keeper dead before the Queen came to the throne—and in seven years I had learned to read it the way other men read faces. It had dropped before. It had dropped badly twice. On that Tuesday morning it fell so far, so fast, that I tapped the glass twice to be sure the needle wasn't stuck, and when it kept falling anyway I went and did three things in order: I filled every oil reservoir to the brim, I ate a proper breakfast, and I told Tomas not to leave the cottage for any reason short of fire.

"Even fire," I added, after consideration, "should probably wait."

He laughed, which was new. Seven weeks under my roof—or rather, seven weeks under my roof, my floor, my boathouse, and once, during an inspection scare, beneath the floor of the boathouse itself, wedged between the keel of a boat that hadn't floated since '51 and forty years of nets gone stiff as church hats—had worn most of the fear off him and left something quieter underneath. He still flinched at footsteps on gravel. But he laughed now, sometimes, at odd hours, over bread or over the lamp mechanism spread across the table like anatomy.

That Tuesday he looked at my face instead of the barometer and stopped laughing. "How bad?"

"I've seen worse," I said, which was true, and then, because lying to Tomas felt different from lying to census men, "once. In '48. The sea came over the top road. Maren stood in the light room all night and swore she could feel the tower thinking."

"Did it?"

"It groaned. Towers think in groans." I shrugged into my oilskins. "Stay away from the windows. If the glass goes, get in the stair well—it's stone all the way down, it's the only part of this rock that's honest."

I went up the hundred and twelve steps to the lantern and started the work that would fill the next thirty-six hours: trimming wicks, warming the vaporizer, testing the clockwork that turned the great lens with its patient, jeweled weight. Outside, the horizon had gone the color of wet slate, and the swell was already coming in long and wrong, sets of waves that arrived too slowly, like guests who know something you don't.

The automatic light—the Commission's pride, the reason my signature was worth nothing anymore—stood half-built on the gallery of the new iron tower down at the point, two miles south. I had watched them erect it through autumn with the particular resentment of a man watching his own funeral arrangements. It was to burn acetylene, they said. It would want no keeper, no oil, no midnight climbs. It would be infallible.

It was also, as of that Tuesday, a skeleton wearing half a lens, waiting on a shipment of valves from Glasgow that the same approaching weather was going to keep exactly where it was. The foreman had told me this on Saturday with a shrug that suggested the information was somehow my fault. *Come spring,* he'd said. *Till then, yours is the only candle on the coast.*

*Till then.* As if I were a stopgap in my own life.

* *

The storm made landfall at dusk, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

By eight the wind was a solid thing against the north glass, not gusting but leaning, a shoulder pressed continuously to the tower. By ten the rain had given up falling and simply traveled sideways, and the sea below had turned from black to a churning grey-white that I could see even from the lamp because it threw its own light upward—phosphorescence, torn and scattered, the water burning where it broke.

I have kept lights through perhaps sixty serious blows in my working life. I will tell you what none of the manuals tell you: the danger isn't the storm. The danger is the hour past midnight, when your hands know the work so well they could do it asleep, and your mind goes wandering off to places it has no business being, and the difference between competence and catastrophe becomes nothing more than whether you noticed the oil level drop a quarter-inch while you were busy remembering your wife's voice.

So I made rules for myself, years ago, and I kept them that night like scripture. Check the clockwork every quarter hour. Walk the gallery between checks, weather permitting—and that night, weather permitted only in fifteen-second installments, clipped to the rail, breath ripped sideways off my face. Keep talking aloud, even to nobody, because a silent keeper is a keeper who's gone somewhere inside himself.

"All right," I told the lens, as it turned its slow circles, throwing its beam out and dragging it back like a man sweeping a floor that would never stay swept. "All right. Just us again."

Maren used to say the lamp and I were married twice over—her to me, me to it—and that she'd made her peace with being second in line so long as she got first claim on whatever was left at dawn. Seven years on, I still caught myself narrating the night to her. *Wind's shifted two points north. Barometer's a liar and always was.* It was not prayer. I am not a praying man; the sea cured me of that early, by answering so rarely. It was just conversation with someone who had earned the right to hear it.

Somewhere below me, in the cottage at the tower's foot, Tomas was doing what I'd told him: staying away from windows, sitting in the stair well with a candle, listening to the tower groan. I thought about him down there and found—to my surprise, and I'll admit it here since I'd never admit it aloud—that the thought steadied me rather than frightened me. For seven years I had kept this light for strangers and paid for the privilege in loneliness. Now there was one person under my roof who knew my name, both of my names, the one I wore and the one I'd buried, and knowing it changed the flavor of everything, even terror. Fear shared is just weather. You can keep weather.

* *

At twenty past one, the packet came into my life.

I heard her before I saw her, which is the way of it—you hear a ship's distress in your teeth before your eyes find anything, some animal register of an engine note gone wrong, running rough, running scared. The *Merrow*, later I learned, a mail and passenger packet out of the northern ports, eleven souls aboard and a hold full of His Majesty's correspondence, blown forty miles off her course and feeling for the land the way a blind man feels for stairs.

Her master did the only sensible thing available to him, which was to run for the one fixed light on this stretch of coast and pray it was where the chart said. And here is the sentence I want carved somewhere modest, perhaps on the back of my dismissal letter: the chart was wrong. The new survey—the one that assumed the automated tower at the point would be operational—had already been printed with the point marked as the primary light. The *Merrow*'s master, peering through a windshield of rain at a coastline trying to kill him, saw two things: a skeletal structure at the point showing no light at all, and, four miles north of where he expected anything to be, my beam, swinging steady as a heartbeat.

He altered for me. God help him, and bless him, he altered for me.

I watched her come in through the glasses when the rain thinned enough to allow it, and I will not pretend my stomach didn't drop, because a ship running for your light in that sea is either about to be saved or about to become your fault, and there is no third category at one in the morning. She was wallowing, over-laden, taking green water over her rails. Her engine was laboring like a heart with bad valves. And she was making straight for the shore with nothing between her and the Black Gutter—that shelf of rock two cables' length out that has eaten better ships than hers—except the channel I knew the way I know the lines of my own hand.

There is a sector of my beam, by the old arrangement of the screens, that falls dark for two seconds each rotation as it passes due east. Every keeper on this coast knows the convention: dark interval toward danger, or toward the safe channel, according to local agreement, and the local agreement here, unwritten these forty years, was that the eclipse marked the deep water between the Black Gutter and the Inner Skerry. The Commission's men had told me in September that the new apparatus would do away with such "folkloric" signaling. That night, folkloric signaling was the entire navigational wealth of the kingdom as far as one blindfolded packet steamer was concerned.

I checked my screens. I checked them twice. Then I did something slightly outside my instructions, which was to stand at the lantern rail in a hurricane waving a storm lantern in the old signal for *follow my light, stand in toward the eclipse*—which no keeper is supposed to use, because it presumes a master crazy enough to trust it, and I could only hope this one was desperate enough to be crazy.

He was. The *Merrow* stood in.

I won't give you the next hour minute by minute; my hands remember it better than my head does and my hands don't talk. Trim the light, watch her answer, watch her come on through the murk with her bow lighting up white each time the sea lifted her. The wind took a panel off the service room roof at some point and threw it fifty yards inland with a sound like God slamming a door, and I noted it, and filed it, and kept my eyes on the water, because a keeper's first duty is the light and his second is everything else in the world, in that order, forever.

She came through the Gutter with ten yards to spare on the northern side. I know because I could see the breakers on the reef to starboard of her, close enough that the spray must have come aboard, and then she was through, into the smoother water under the headland, and her engine slowed, and her bell rang twice—shallow water, all clear, understood—and then, after a moment, three times more, which I chose to interpret as thanks.

Eleven people. Eleven people who at one o'clock had been a hundred yards and a wrong heading from the rocks, and at two were anchored in the lee of my tower, alive, complaining about the weather, as the living are entitled to do.

I went back to trimming wicks. My hands were shaking, so I let them shake and trimmed the wicks anyway; the flame forgives a little tremor. Somewhere in the middle of it I realized my face was wet and that it was only partly spray, and I decided that too was within tolerance.

"You see?" I said to Maren, to the lamp, to the tower groaning around me. "Worthless, the both of us. Perfectly worthless."

* *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater and showed me the damage: the lost roof panel, a boat from the village stove to kindling on the slip, the top road under two feet of kelp and one stranded crate of Glasgow earthenware, smashed, its shards glittering among the weed like a shop window drowned. The *Merrow* rode at anchor in the lee, patched and battered, flying a general signal I couldn't read at that distance but chose, again, to take charitably.

The villagers came out to survey, as villagers do, walking the wrack line with their hands in their pockets. Young Callum Ferrie found me on the gallery steps and looked up with an expression I recognized: the look of a man who has done arithmetic overnight and wants the answer confirmed.

"She came through the Gutter," he said.

"She did."

"On the eclipse."

"On the eclipse."

He nodded slowly, spitting thoughtfully to leeward. "And them down at the point," he said, meaning the Commission men, whose worksite had spent the night converting itself into modern sculpture, "their light burned how long?"

"Not one minute."

"Mm." He said nothing else, but the nod he gave me on leaving contained more official recognition than the Crown had issued in seven years, and I confess it warmed me more than it should have. A man declared worthless likes to be told he's worthless by someone standing dry.

Tomas met me at the cottage door with tea already made—he'd been up all night listening to the tower, he said, and had eventually taken comfort from the rhythm of the clockwork turning above him, a heartbeat in the ceiling. He looked at my face and read the whole night off it, the way I'd once read the barometer.

"The packet?"

"Safe. Anchored in the lee."

"Your light?"

"My light."

He handed me the tea, and we stood a moment in the wreck-smelling wind, and he said, quietly, "Maren would have stood in the light room all night for that."

"She'd have enjoyed it," I said. "That's the obscene part. She'd have loved every minute." And then, because the morning had loosened something in me, I said the thing I'd been carrying since September like a stone in my boot: "They mean to put an acetylene valve where I stand, Tomas. A machine that doesn't know the Gutter. Doesn't know the eclipse. Doesn't know anything except that it burns." I looked out at the *Merrow*, riding easy in the flat water under the cliff, eleven souls alive who owed their morning to a piece of folklore the Commission meant to abolish. "I've been telling myself it doesn't matter. That I'm done anyway, that they can have it. And last night I—" I stopped, embarrassed. "Last night mattered to me. That's the trouble. They've finally found the thing worth taking."

Tomas said nothing for a while. He was learning, slowly, the shape of me—that consolation offered too quickly bounced off, but silence gave me room to finish my own sentences.

Then he opened his mouth to say something, and never got the chance, because we both heard the wheels.

A cart on the coast road, in the aftermath of the worst blow in a generation, when half the lanes were impassable and every sensible soul was indoors repairing something. It came around the headland at a careful walk, mud to its axles, one figure on the bench under a hood of dripping oilskin, and I knew the posture of the driver before I could make out anything else—the upright, unweatherly way of a person entirely unsuited to a horse, holding the reins like a form she'd been ordered to complete.

Ada Quill. Coming back to my headland in the teeth of the season, with her forms and her questions and her blank line, on the very morning after my light had proven itself and my household had grown by one.

Tomas had already seen her. I felt him go still beside me, felt the seven weeks of hard-won calm drain out of him in a single breath.

"Silas," he said. "Who is that?"
