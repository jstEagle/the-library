## 15. Watch List

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which I have always felt was the least dramatic day available to them. Storms come when they please; wrecks come at night; but bureaucracy keeps office hours.

There were six of them, marching up the cliff path in a line so straight you'd have thought the wind had signed some agreement with them. A sergeant out front with a leather case under his arm and an expression suggesting the case contained his opinions of civilians. Behind him, five men in red coats gone pink at the elbows from salt air, carrying tripods, measuring chains, and one instrument like a brass telescope that had been crossed with a church organ and taught to feel superior.

I watched them from the lamp room window for the better part of ten minutes before I went down. Ten minutes is not cowardice. Ten minutes is arithmetic: six men, one path, one door, one man in the tower who could not plausibly be anywhere else. And one man below stairs who could not plausibly be at all.

Tomas was in the kitchen, mending a net he had no intention of using, because mending gave his hands something to do besides shake. Seven weeks he'd been with me now—seven weeks of learning the lamp mechanism, of eating my bread, of laughing once, badly, at a joke about the harbor master that he hadn't understood and pretended to. Seven weeks of me rehearsing, every night, the sentence *you'll sail soon* until it wore smooth as sea glass and meant nothing at all.

"Soldiers," I told him. "Six. Surveying party, by the look. They're here ahead of the decommission."

He stood up so fast the net slid off his knees. "Silas—"

"They don't know you exist." I said it the way I'd say *the tide comes in*. "That's the whole trick of you. You are not a fact yet. We're going to keep it that way."

"And if they search?"

"Then we've thought about that too."

We had. The oil store was the obvious place and therefore useless—the first door any searching man opens. The cellar under the dwelling had a coal chute no wider than a coffin, and Tomas was broader than a coffin. What remained was the lamp room itself. It sounds mad. It is mad. But there's a logic to it, the same logic that hides a coin in a fistful of coins: nobody looks up at the thing they've come to measure. They look at their instruments. They look at their boots. They look at the keeper, standing in the doorway being exactly as dull as a keeper ought to be. The gallery rail casts a shadow along the south side of the lantern where a man can stand flat against the masonry behind the storm panels, and if the glass is dirty—and my glass was dirty, deliberately dirty, because I'd stopped polishing the seaward panes two days prior on the theory that a filthy window forgives everything—it becomes less a hiding place than a rumor.

"The panels," I said. "Behind the third and fourth. You don't breathe unless you must. You don't shift your weight. If I say the word 'meridian'—" I'd chosen it because no soldier alive would use it and no keeper could avoid it, "—you go up the ladder into the ventilator housing. It's tight. Tight is good. Tight means quiet."

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. He has Maren's way of looking at a person when he's deciding whether to argue or obey, which is to say he looks as though the argument is already lost and he's grieving it.

"You've done this before," he said. Not a question.

"I've hidden from census takers," I said. "Soldiers are just census takers with worse manners."

I got him up the tower stair and settled behind the panels while the red coats were still twenty minutes out, boots ringing on the rock like spoons in an empty pot. Then I went down to meet them with my cap in my hand and my face arranged into the particular blankness of a man whose entire inner life consists of oil levels and wick trimmers.

* * *

The sergeant introduced himself as Sergeant Hollis, of Her Majesty's Corps of Royal Engineers, and he had the voice of a man reading aloud from a document he despised slightly less than the audience. His case held orders, maps of the headland with my lighthouse rendered as a small black pentagon, and a schedule. Everything about him was scheduled. I suspected his dreams were scheduled.

"Decommissioning survey," he said. "Tower dimensions, foundation condition, sight lines for the new fixed light on the point yonder." He gestured at a squat concrete block going up half a mile east, ugly as a tombstone and about as warm. "You'll be relieved of duties end of season. The automated apparatus requires no keeper."

"No keeper," I said, as though tasting the phrase.

"It burns without attention. Paraffin vapor, mantle, clockwork turner. Reliable as sunrise." He said it with the mild pride of a man quoting a brochure. "Progress, Mr.—"

"Blackwood," I said, and heard, as always, the small click of the name fitting into its groove. Seven years I'd worn that name. It fit like a boot fits after enough miles: you forget it's there until someone asks you to take it off.

Hollis set his men to work. Two went round the tower with measuring chains, calling numbers to each other in the flat song of men who have measured a hundred towers and loved none of them. One planted the brass instrument on its tripod and squinted through it toward the horizon, muttering calculations. Another began sketching the dwelling. The sixth—the youngest, a boy with cheeks still holding their childhood fat—was assigned, apparently, to follow me.

"Mr. Blackwood will accompany you, Corporal Fenwick," said Hollis, "and answer questions regarding the mechanism. Log books. Consumption records. That sort of thing."

"That sort of thing," I agreed.

Fenwick climbed the tower stair behind me, and I felt each of his footfalls land somewhere between my shoulder blades. Above us, behind two dirty panes and a shadow, a man stood not breathing. I counted his breaths for him anyway, the way you count waves. Every creak of the stair was a sentence in a trial neither of us had been formally charged in.

"The light's a first-order Fresnel?" Fenwick asked, notebook out.

"First-order. 1848." I pushed open the lamp room door and stepped through with the ease of long habit, angling my body so that mine blocked his first sweep of the room. It's remarkable, what a body can do when it's practiced. Mine had seven years of practice being unremarkable. "Come in. Mind the mercury bath."

He minded it. He was a careful boy; I'll grant him that. He walked the circumference of the lantern with his nose in the mechanism, asking questions I answered honestly because honest answers are the cheapest ones and leave no residue. Focal plane height: yes. Rotation: clockwork, weight-driven, I rewound it every four hours. Oil consumption: I showed him the ledger, and he copied figures into his book, and I watched him copy them and thought: *there is a boy writing down everything except the one thing that matters, and the one thing that matters is standing four feet behind him holding his breath.*

Because that's where Tomas was—not behind the panels after all. The survey had caught us earlier than planned, and in the scramble I'd made a judgment call: better the ventilator housing, above, where no chain would reach and no eye would tilt. So Tomas was curled in the iron throat of the ventilation shaft directly over our heads, folded up like a letter never sent, and all through Fenwick's questions I could feel him there—a pressure, a presence, the way you feel weather coming through old bones.

At one point the boy paused, pencil lifted. "Did you hear something?"

"Mercury," I said. "It shifts when the sun warms the bath. Sounds like breathing if you're not used to it." Which was true, and also the most dangerous sentence I'd spoken in years, because I'd handed him a reason not to investigate and wrapped it in truth, and lies wrapped in truth hold better than any knot I ever tied.

"Funny," he said. "Sounded more like cloth."

"Canvas awning," I said. "Stowed in the housing. Flaps when the draught reverses." Also true. There was canvas stowed there. There had been since 1854. I make a point of keeping true things in strategic locations around my life, the way other men keep loaded pistols.

Fenwick wrote something down. I didn't ask what. Asking is how guilty men die.

Below, the chains clinked. Numbers drifted up the stairwell. Somewhere on the point, Hollis was driving a wooden stake into ground that had belonged to no government until Tuesday, and I thought about Maren, who used to say that the Crown's interest in any piece of earth was inversely proportional to how much anyone living on it wanted to be left alone. She'd have liked this morning's irony: they were measuring the tower to replace me with a machine that would burn without love, and meanwhile the only human being in the building besides myself was the one person in England with a price on his head, hanging in the chimney like smoke that had learned to wait.

* * *

They broke for dinner at noon, bread and cheese issued from the sergeant's own stores, eaten sitting on my seawall with their rifles between their knees as if the gulls might file a claim. Hollis came to the door himself, which I had been dreading, because sergeants do not fetch; sergeants send.

"A word, keeper."

I stepped out onto my own doorstep and closed the door behind me with the unhurried motion of a man who has nothing upstairs worth closing a door against. Inside, in the silence, I knew Tomas had come down from the housing the moment the last boot left the tower—he'd have listened for it, as instructed, counting boots the way I'd taught him to count waves. He'd be in the coal cellar now, or behind the flour barrels, or pressed into the space beneath the stair where the shadows pooled thick enough to swim in. My house had grown pockets in seven weeks, secret seams stitched into its ordinary fabric. I hardly recognized the place, and I'd built most of them myself.

"Your log books," Hollis said. "Corporal Fenwick says they're exemplary. Unusual, in my experience. Most keepers keep sloppy books."

"I was raised by a man who believed God read ledgers," I said. This was even true. My father had believed God read ledgers and found them disappointing.

"Mm." Hollis consulted a paper. "Household registration. New requirement—all coastal residents to be entered against the watch list. Name, age, occupation, kin. You're listed here as sole occupant." He looked up. Blue eyes, pale as skimmed milk, entirely without malice and therefore entirely without mercy. "Is that accurate, Mr. Blackwood? Any persons resident besides yourself? Kin expected, perhaps? Family visiting?"

And there it was. The question from the census woman, Ada Quill, come back around wearing a red coat. *Any kin expected.* The same hole drilled in the same wall, and me standing in front of it again with the same lie in my hand.

"Sole occupant," I said. "My wife died seven years ago. No children. No kin. Nobody's coming." Three sentences, four lies if you counted the pause before *nobody*, which I did, because the pause was doing work.

Hollis wrote it down. His pencil scratched like a mouse in a wall. "You understand that misstatement on a watch list registration is an offense."

"I understand that I'm alone on a rock," I said, "and that the Crown is about to take away my employment and give my job to a machine. If I were harboring somebody, Sergeant, I'd be harboring them into unemployment alongside me. Not much of a crime wave."

Something flickered across his face—not suspicion, worse: sympathy, brief and unwelcome. "It's not personal, keeper. There's a man wanted. Political matter. Exile escaped custody on the Continent; may have taken ship; coastal watch is standard procedure." He tucked the paper away. "Name of Rennick. Tomas Rennick. Ring any bells?"

The world went very quiet and very large, the way it does in the moment between lightning and thunder.

"Rennick," I repeated, and let the name sit in my mouth like a stone I'd never held before. "No. Should it?"

"He's from these parts originally, according to the file. Family in trade." Hollis shrugged, a man for whom the entire continent of a stranger's grief was a line item. "Long shot. Most of them are. Thank you for your time."

He turned to go, and I stood on my doorstep with the wind pulling at my coat and my heart performing some slow catastrophic maneuver behind my ribs, and I thought: *Maren. Your brother is Tomas Rennick to them. Your brother, who I fed this morning, whose laughter I've started hearing through the floor again. They put your maiden family name in a government file next to the word WANTED, and I just told a sergeant I'd never heard it.*

"Tough coast," Hollis added, pausing. "For the fishing. Must be lonely winters."

"Lonely's the trade," I said. "Keeps the hands steady."

He nodded, satisfied, and walked back down to his men, and I stayed on the step until he reached them, because turning around too fast was a luxury I couldn't afford. Behind me, through the door, the house was silent—but it wasn't empty silence anymore. It was the silence of a held breath, of a man in a coal cellar listening to his brother-in-law lie to the Crown on his behalf, and doing it well enough that the lie had a future tense.

That night, after the soldiers marched away with their numbers and their stakes and their list with my name at the top of it and *sole occupant* written beside it in a clerk's hand, Tomas came up to the lamp room while I trimmed the wicks. He didn't speak for a while. Neither did I. The light turned overhead, sweeping the dark water, marking our little territory of brightness against all that black.

"My name," he said finally. "Rennick. I haven't said it aloud in three years. In the prison they took it—they called me by number. Out here I've been *the cousin*, *the friend*, whatever story you needed." He turned the word over. "Rennick. It's Maren's name too. Was."

"It's yours," I said. "Take it back. Just not on any lists."

He laughed then, short and startled, and the sound went up through the lantern glass and out over the water, and I thought: *this is what I'm guarding now. Not the light. The laugh.*

But downstairs, folded into the pocket of the coat I'd worn all day, was a copy of the survey schedule the corporal had left on my table—standard practice, he'd said, transparency—and pinned to the second page, in fresh ink, was a notice I hadn't seen before. A requisition. Dated for the following week.

*One (1) additional survey visit required. Subject: interior inspection of dwelling premises, all rooms, per watch list verification protocols.*

All rooms. I read it twice, standing in the turning light, and heard the clockwork tick, and thought about coal cellars and flour barrels and the width of coffins.

They were coming inside.
