## 17. A Debt Among Thieves

There is a particular quality to a smuggler's tavern at midmorning: the emptiness of a room that has only just stopped pretending to be something else. The Salt Cellar in Harrow's Reach smelled of wet rope, tar, and last night's ale given up as a lost cause. Three fishermen played cards without speaking. A dog regarded me with the professional indifference of a customs official.

And at the corner table, alone with a pot of tea he was clearly not drinking for pleasure, sat Captain Emeric Voss.

I had spent two days preparing for this conversation, and I will confess that most of my preparation had been of the sort conducted in front of a mirror. I had rehearsed openings. I had rehearsed retreats. I had rehearsed the expression I imagined a woman carries when she knows more than she says — though I suspect every expression I tried made me look as though I had swallowed a bee.

The truth is simpler and less flattering: I was afraid. Not of Voss himself, particularly. He was a broad, weathered man of perhaps fifty, with hands like knotted hawsers and a face that suggested it had been used as an anvil by several successive generations of blacksmiths. No, what frightened me was the mathematics of what I intended. I had spent eleven years as a census taker learning that information is currency, and that spending it wrongly buys you nothing but enemies. This morning I meant to walk into a smuggler's den and attempt to purchase a man's life with a handful of ledger entries.

I sat down across from him without being invited, which felt correct.

"Miss Quill." He said my name the way a man reads a label on cargo he's deciding whether to accept. So he knew me. Of course he knew me. I had been asking questions in his harbor for a fortnight.

"Captain Voss."

"Tea's cold."

"I'm not here for tea."

"Nobody ever is." He turned his cup anyway, a small ritual, and studied me over its rim. "You've a look about you this morning. Like a woman who's rehearsed something in front of a looking-glass."

I decided in that instant that I disliked him enormously, and that we were going to understand each other perfectly.

* * *

Let me set down what I knew, because knowing it in order matters, and because writing it out — even in memory — steadied me the way counting steadies me still.

Item the first: three wrecks on this coast in eighteen months whose timbers bore maker's marks from ports no honest trader would sail from. Amsterdam. Bremen. One, remarkably, from a shipyard in Danzig that had burned down two years before the ship could have been built. The harbor master's register recorded none of them. Either ships were wrecking themselves in secret and politely sinking their own evidence, or someone was landing cargo that never touched a manifest.

Item the second: the harbor master, Mr. Pell, kept a second set of books. I had seen them. I had not been meant to see them, which was accomplished by leaving them in his office under a copy of the Shipping Gazette while he went to lunch, a maneuver requiring neither skill nor courage, merely a tolerance for other people's assumptions about what a woman with a satchel finds interesting. The second books listed arrivals by moon-phase rather than date. Honest merchants do not schedule their arrivals around the tides of the moon.

Item the third: every arrival in those second books traced back, through fees and favors and one memorable entry reading simply *"V. — settled,"* to the brig *Kestrel*, and the *Kestrel* answered to Captain Emeric Voss.

Item the fourth, and the one that mattered: the Crown was hunting a fugitive. I had read the circular in Pell's office too, folded inside the Gazette like a love letter from a government that had never learned to write them properly. *Tomas Reyes-Vale. Enemy of the Crown. Information leading to capture rewarded.* There was a description. Dark hair going grey early, they said. A scar through the left eyebrow. And there was the name of the man who'd sheltered him, if sheltering was happening, circled in Pell's own hand: *Blackwood, S., keeper, Wren Head.*

They had Silas's name. That was the thing that had kept me awake for two nights running, staring at my ceiling while my forms sat reproachfully unfinished in their case. They had his name, and they had a reward, and somewhere between Harrow's Reach and London there existed men who collected rewards the way I collected signatures.

And item the fifth: Captain Voss wanted something from the Crown. Every man who runs unreported cargo wants amnesty eventually, the way every swimmer wants dry land eventually. It was only a question of what shape his wanting took — and whether it had already taken the shape of a fugitive delivered to the authorities in exchange for his own clean slate.

Pell had let slip — or performed letting slip; with Pell it was impossible to say — that Voss had been summoned to meet with the Revenue men at the end of the month. The same week the lighthouse at Wren Head was scheduled for decommission. The same week the watch list closed.

I did not know yet whether Voss had sold Tomas. But I knew he could. And I had come to make sure that if he sold anyone, he would find the price of selling me considerably higher than the price of keeping me.

* * *

"You've been asking after my ship," Voss said. Not a question. He set down the cup. "In the Anchor and Bell, telling old Tam you were verifying shipping figures for the Census Office. Which is either very clever or very stupid, Miss Quill, depending on how well you can lie."

"And which have you concluded?"

"That you're a terrible liar, which makes you cleverer than most." He leaned back, chair creaking like rigging. "Census doesn't verify shipping. Census counts heads. So either you're working for someone else, or you've gone off your forms entirely. Which is it?"

Here was the moment I had rehearsed seventeen ways and abandoned all of them. I put my hand flat on the table instead, and said the true thing, because I have learned — slowly, expensively, against my own nature — that the true thing is often the only weapon nobody has prepared for.

"I'm working for no one. As of this morning I'm working for myself, which is a demotion in pay and a promotion in purpose. Captain, I know about your second manifests. I know about Pell's moon-books. I know the *Kestrel* has landed goods on this coast at least nine times in two years that exist nowhere in any record the Crown keeps, and I know precisely where the records aren't, because not-existing is my trade, and I am very good at noticing what isn't there."

The card game behind us had gone quiet. Even the dog had lifted its head. Voss's face did not change, which was itself informative; a surprised man looks surprised, and a man who has already priced this conversation does not.

"Go on," he said.

"If I carried what I carry into the Customs House in Falmouth, you'd lose the *Kestrel*. You'd likely lose more than the *Kestrel* — Pell would give you up inside an hour to save himself, and I'd wager my pension there are names in your ledgers above your own that you'd rather not see read aloud in a courtroom. Smuggling's a small crime until it touches great men. Then it becomes treason, and treason is a rope."

"Flattering picture."

"It gets worse. Because the Revenue men coming at month's end aren't coming for brandy, Captain. They're coming for a man. And I believe — I don't know, but I believe — that someone has whispered to them that a certain keeper on a certain headland might be persuaded to give that man up, and that the whisper came from a direction that smells of salt."

For the first time, something moved behind Voss's eyes. Not guilt. Calculation — the visible kind, the kind a man lets you see when he wants you to know he's considering whether to be insulted.

"And if I said the whisper wasn't mine?"

"Then I'd believe you about as far as I could throw your brig. But I'd note that you didn't deny knowing who I mean."

He laughed then, short and hard, like a hatch slamming. "You've got nerve, census woman. Sitting in my taproom accusing me of selling a man I haven't even agreed to carry."

"Have you agreed?"

Silence. Long enough for the fire to pop, for the dog to sigh and resettle. Then Voss did something unexpected: he reached inside his coat and laid something on the table between us. A letter. Folded twice, sealed with plain wax, official-looking.

"Read it."

I unfolded it. It was from the Collector of Customs at Falmouth, and it offered — in the bloodless prose such documents are bred in — a full pardon for all past offenses unspecified, conditional upon information leading to the apprehension of the person named in the enclosed circular. The enclosed circular was not enclosed. It didn't need to be. We both knew whose face lived inside it.

"They sent that a week ago," Voss said. "Delivered quiet, by a Revenue cutter's launch. No witnesses but me." He tapped the paper with one enormous finger. "Now ask yourself, Miss Quill, why I'm showing it to you instead of acting on it."

I asked myself, and the answers arrived in a queue. Because he wanted leverage over me. Because he wanted a witness to his reluctance. Because he was testing whether I'd run to warn the fugitive, thereby confirming where loyalties lay. Or — and this answer arrived last and stayed longest — because a man who lives by silence knows exactly how heavy it gets, and he was tired of carrying this particular weight alone.

"Why are you?" I said.

"Because the pardon's a trap." He said it flatly, without drama. "Amnesty for past offenses, conditional on future cooperation. You know what that means? It means I'm not pardoned. I'm employed. For life. Whatever they want carried next, wherever they want it carried, I'm their boat, or the rope comes back. I've spent thirty years being nobody's boat." His jaw worked. "Besides. The keeper pulled six of my men out of the water off Gull Rock in 'fifty-six. Never asked who they were or what they carried. Just rowed out into weather that should've killed him and hauled them aboard his boat like they were his own brothers. My bosun still won't hear his name spoken without taking off his hat."

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts. I had come armed with suspicion and found a debt.

"So," I said carefully. "You'll carry him."

"I'll consider carrying him. There's a difference, and the difference is that carrying him means defying that letter, and defying that letter means I need the thing it offers to become worthless. Do you follow me? I can't outrun a pardon, Miss Quill. I can only drown it."

* * *

We talked for another hour, and I will not reproduce all of it, partly because some of it must stay unwritten even in my own memory, and partly because a good deal consisted of Voss explaining the sea to me in terms I pretended not to find fascinating.

But the shape of our bargain came to this.

First: passage. The *Kestrel* would take Tomas Vale aboard at dawn on the morning after the lighthouse's decommission, from the cove below Wren Head, on the ebb tide. Not before. Decommission day would fill the headland with officials and soldiers, and paradox was our only cover — no one hunts a fugitive harder than the day after they've sealed the tower he supposedly haunts.

Second: papers. Voss could forge a transit manifest easily enough — he did it weekly — but a passenger needed identity papers, and identity papers needed a clerk's hand. He looked at me when he said it. I looked back. Neither of us said the word *forgery* out loud, because we were both, in our separate fashions, professionals.

Third: silence, mutual and binding. My knowledge of his cargoes would remain unwritten. His knowledge of my — activities — would likewise go unrecorded. If either of us broke it, we drowned together, and we both understood that the Crown forgave informants more readily than accomplices, which made my position, oddly, the stronger. Voss noted this with something approaching respect.

"And the letter?" I asked, nodding at the pardon lying between us.

Voss picked it up, considered it, and then held it to the cold stove, where it caught and curled and was ash inside ten seconds. He watched it burn with the expression of a man closing an account.

"There," he said. "Now it's worthless, and now I'm free to be bribed by better offers. You've cost me a fortune, Miss Quill."

"You're welcome."

"One condition." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the grey in his beard. "You tell the keeper: once my keel leaves that cove, the debt's paid. All of it. Gull Rock, 'fifty-six, everything. I don't want gratitude, I don't want letters, I don't want a man on some foreign shore writing to me about his new life. I saved him because I owed a dead woman's husband, and that account closes at anchor's aweigh. Understood?"

I thought of Maren Blackwood, seven years dead, whose promise had traveled further than her body ever had — through a brother, across a prison, over a winter sea, into a smuggler's arithmetic. Debts among thieves, debts among strangers, debts to the dead. Perhaps all bookkeeping was the same bookkeeping, in the end. Perhaps every ledger in England balanced against somebody's promise.

"Understood," I said.

* * *

I walked back along the coast road in weather that couldn't decide between rain and fog and settled on both. At the top of the cliff path I stopped and looked toward Wren Head, where the tower stood in its shroud of grey, and I thought about the blank line waiting in my census form — the line I had left empty, the line my superiors had begun asking questions about, the line that was, I realized, no longer blank at all. It simply hadn't been filled in yet. There was a difference. I was becoming fluent in the difference.

Below me, half-hidden in the cleft of the cliffs, I could just make out the boathouse roof, and beyond it the dark seam of the cove where a brig would come at dawn on the morning after the flame went out.

One night, I thought. One night between the extinguishing and the tide. One night for forged papers, for a village holding its breath, for a keeper moving along cliffs he knew blind.

And one night for whatever the soldiers at the decommission would do when they counted heads and found one too many.

I opened my satchel, took out my pen, and began composing the transit manifest in my head — names, ports, dates — while the fog came up the cliff path to meet me like something with intentions.
