## 18. The Last Firing

There is an art to surrendering gracefully, and I have never learned it. What I have learned instead is how to stand very still while a man in a good coat tells you that the thing you have kept alive for seven years is now obsolete.

The morning of the last firing came down clean and cold off the sea, the kind of morning that makes a liar of this coast. No wind worth naming. The water lay flat as poured pewter, and gulls wheeled over the headland with the smugness of birds who have never once been asked for papers. If I had been given a choice of skies for the day they put out my light, I would not have chosen this one. I'd have wanted weather. Weather would at least have agreed with me about how it felt.

Instead we got blue.

"They'll want the tour first," Ada had said the evening before, when she appeared at my door with her ledger under her arm and her face arranged into something she probably believed was neutral. "Mr. Halloway likes to inspect everything personally. He'll ask questions. Answer them like a man with nothing under his floorboards."

"Is there something under my floorboards?"

"There's a man under your oil store," she said, "which is worse, because it's on the inventory."

I had no answer for that, so I offered her tea, and she declined it, and we stood there in the doorway like two people who had run out of script and were too proud to admit it. Before leaving she did something I am still turning over: she reached out and straightened my collar. One small motion, brisk as a clerk correcting an entry, and then she was gone down the path with her coat flapping, and I stood in my own doorway feeling like a form someone else had filled in.

* * *

They arrived at nine o'clock by the tide bell—three of them. Mr. Halloway from the Board of Trade, a thin man whose voice sounded permanently disappointed in the acoustics; a surveyor named Pell with a leather case and the eyes of a man pricing furniture in a dead relative's house; and a soldier, because there is always a soldier now, a young one with a red coat too new to have earned its wrinkles, carrying his rifle like a broom he'd been told to hold.

I met them at the foot of the tower with my keys in my hand. All seven of them, on the ring I've worn smooth against my thigh these years. I'd polished them the night before, which is a thing I don't recommend a man do. You find out what polishing is for, and it isn't for handing things over.

"Keeper Blackwood." Halloway consulted a paper as if my name might be misspelled on it. "You understand the nature of today's proceedings?"

"I extinguish the flame," I said. "You seal the tower. The light goes automatic come spring, from the new station down the coast. Nobody lives here anymore."

"Nobody should live here now," Halloway said, with the mildness of a man quoting scripture. "The keeper's residence is to be vacated within the fortnight. Your salary ceases as of today. There will be a gratuity."

"A what?"

"A payment. In recognition of service."

Seven years, one wife, and every storm God saw fit to send, priced out in a gratuity. I felt Tomas's presence below me like a second heartbeat—the oil store sits directly beneath the tower's foot, and I knew he could hear every word through the stone, lying flat among the empty drums with Maren's locket against his chest, breathing the way I'd taught him: slow, silent, patient as ballast.

"Service," I said. "Yes."

* * *

Halloway wanted the tour. Of course he did. Pell opened his leather case and produced a notebook and began writing down everything I owned as though ownership were a disease he was recording for science. The lamp itself he examined with genuine interest, which was the cruelest part—I could see him admiring it. The Fresnel lens with its great honeycomb eye. The clockwork that turned it, wound fresh each night, keeping time so the beam swept the water like a man turning his head in sleep.

"Remarkable condition," Pell admitted. "Most keepers let the brass go."

"My wife liked things bright," I said, and then wished I hadn't, because it was true and true things are heavy, and I was trying to travel light today.

We climbed. Ninety-three steps. I know because Maren counted them once, on a wager, and made me pay up a shilling when I guessed ninety-one. The soldier stayed below—some instinct or order kept him at the door, which suited me fine; a tower is a chimney, and you don't post men inside chimneys.

At the top, Halloway stood in the lantern room with the whole coast laid out beneath him and delivered his speech. I'll spare you most of it. Progress. Efficiency. A light that requires no keeper's judgment, no nightly winding, no human fallibility. He said the phrase "*human error*" twice, and both times looked at me as though I were its exhibit.

"You understand," he said, "that this is no reflection on your conduct. The Board has nothing but respect for the service rendered here. Records show—" he consulted another paper, and here I confess my stomach went tight, because papers and I have history lately— "records show forty-one vessels guided safe past this headland during your tenure. Three rescues effected directly. That's commendable, Blackwood."

"Forty-one," I said.

"At least."

Ada's doing, I'd have bet my last month's salary. The harbor master's ledger said eleven. Somewhere between the village and London, somebody had been adding lines to my account, and I knew exactly whose handwriting did the arithmetic.

"It's a machine's coast now," Halloway concluded, and held out his hand for the keys.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the moment they take your light: it doesn't go dark all at once. I'd imagined it that way—a snuffing, a blackness, drama. But the flame was still burning when we climbed up, steady in its glass, and the procedure, Halloway explained, was mine to perform. They couldn't extinguish a lighthouse lamp without the keeper. Some regulation or other. So the honor of killing it fell to me, the way a hangman's rope falls to the condemned man's own estate.

I opened the oil feed valve and closed it. Slowly. There's a trick to it—if you shut it fast, the flame gutters and throws soot on the lens, and I will not have soot on that lens, not today, not ever, because Maren polished it and I promised her I'd keep it so. So I closed it the way you close a door on a sleeping child, inch by inch, letting the fire grow drowsy rather than frightened.

The light dimmed. Swept. Dimmed further. For nearly a minute the beam went on making its rounds of the empty sea, weaker each pass, faithful to a job that was ending, and I stood there with my hand on the valve watching my whole occupation shrink to an ember the size of a fingernail.

Then it was smoke. Then it was a gray thread rising through the lantern glass. Then nothing but daylight, which had been there all along, indifferent and complete.

"Well done," said Halloway.

I did not trust myself to answer. I busied myself with the routine instead—the cap over the burner, the wick trimmed and wrapped, the dust cover lowered over the lens. Seven years of muscle memory spent on a corpse. It occurred to me, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that I was performing last rites for a piece of glass, and that I had performed them more tenderly than I'd managed for my wife, because with Maren I'd still been drowning when the time came.

Pell photographed nothing—no camera, just his pencil—and wrote *lamp extinguished, 10:14 a.m., keeper cooperative.* Keeper cooperative. Let that be carved somewhere. Let some clerk in London read those two words and feel the full weight of them: *cooperative.* The word you use for livestock and prisoners.

* * *

The sealing was quicker than the killing. Pell walked the tower room by room with his inventory, and I walked behind him answering questions, and all the while I kept one ear tuned downward, toward the stone beneath our feet, where a fugitive from the Crown listened to the empire counting cups.

"The oil store," Pell said, and my heart performed a maneuver I'm not proud of. "Show me."

"This way."

The oil store door opens onto a short stair and a low stone room smelling of paraffin and cold rock. Empty drums along one wall. Shelves. A crate of wicks. And behind the third drum from the left, in a space I'd cleared myself two nights ago and lined with sacking, a man who had escaped a prison hulk and crossed open water to reach me, folded up smaller than any grown man ought to fold.

I opened the door and stood aside with the lamplight of ordinary daylight behind me, and I watched Pell look into that room, and I will tell you honestly: I have faced storms that scared me less. Because a storm only wants your body, and I'd already stopped believing my body was irreplaceable. But that room held the last promise my wife ever asked of me, and if Pell's eyes had gone three feet further left, I would have had to decide, right there, between the law and a dead woman's dying wish, with a soldier at the door and no plan at all.

What saved us was boredom. Boredom and inventory. Pell counted drums—eleven empty, four quarter-full, noted the spillage stains, sniffed at the air, and pronounced the store "adequate." He glanced at the far wall, where a stack of sacking leaned against the stone in a shape that a suspicious man might have questioned and a tired man merely recorded as *sacking, quantity approx.*

He wrote it down and moved on.

Later—much later—I would learn that Tomas had heard every footstep, had lain with his hand over his own mouth to keep his breathing small, had recognized my voice answering questions above him and understood, from the calm of it, that the performance was holding. He told me afterward that the worst moment wasn't fear. It was hearing me give away the count of my own oil drums and thinking, absurdly, *he lies well; Maren would be proud.* Shame transformed, again. That family of ours runs on it.

* * *

At noon we stood outside the tower while Halloway locked it—not with my keys, which he'd taken, but with a new padlock, government issue, brass-faced, the kind of lock that exists to make a point rather than keep a secret. The point being: *this is ours now, and you were only ever borrowing.*

"Your residence," he said, "within the fortnight. The watch-list registration officer will call on you before then. Cooperate fully, Blackwood, and there'll be no difficulty."

"No difficulty," I agreed.

He shook my hand. His grip was dry and administrative. The soldier touched his cap to me—there are moments when the young ones remember their manners before their orders—and they went down the cliff path in single file, three small figures walking back into the country that owned everything they surveyed.

I stood alone on the headland with the padlocked tower behind me and the wide useless sea in front of me, and I took out the ring of keys I'd somehow kept—one key, actually, the smallest, the storeroom key, which Halloway had overlooked in his efficiency—and I held it in my fist until the metal warmed.

Then I went down to the oil store and knocked twice and once, our signal, and opened the door.

Tomas sat up among the drums, gray-faced, cramped, blinking in the light like something exhumed. "It's done?" he said.

"It's done. They think the tower's empty."

"And you?"

I considered the question. My name struck from the payroll. My light sealed under a brass padlock. Forty-one ships' worth of work reduced to *cooperative.* Somewhere south, a registration officer was sharpening his pencil for my household, and Ada Quill had left a blank line on a government form that could hang us both, and a smuggler captain named Corvane was holding a price over Tomas's head like a knife he hadn't yet decided to use.

"I'm unemployed," I said, and found, to my genuine surprise, that I was smiling. "First time since I was fourteen. It suits me worse than I expected."

Tomas laughed—a short, cracked sound, half relief—and then he did the thing that undid me. He stood up stiffly in that cold stone room and extended his hand, formal as a treaty, and said, "Silas. Whatever comes of this—you kept the light. Both lights. She'd have said so better than me, but she'd have said exactly that."

I shook his hand because if I hadn't I'd have had to embrace him, and there wasn't time, and grief postponed is grief preserved.

"Tonight," I said. "Corvane's boat rides at anchor off the point. Ada has the papers. The village knows which doors to leave unwatched. We move at slack water, and by morning this headland can keep its padlock and I won't want it back."

"And if the soldiers come before slack water?"

I looked around the oil store—at the drums, the sacking, the low ceiling pressing down like a held breath. Seven years I'd measured my life in portable things: a locket, a ring of keys, a promise small enough to carry anywhere.

"Then we improvise," I said. "I hear the family trait is surviving."

But even as I said it, I heard it—the sound I'd been dreading all day, faint across the water from the cove side: oars. Many oars, pulling in rhythm. Not fishermen's rhythm. Soldiers pull like they march, all together, all certain.

Tomas heard it too. We looked at each other in the dark, keeper and cargo, and the afternoon sun kept shining its indifferent blue light on a coast that had just run out of hiding places.
