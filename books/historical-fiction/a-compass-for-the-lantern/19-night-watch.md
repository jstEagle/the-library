## 19. Night Watch

### Ada

There is a particular quality to forgery that no honest person understands: it is mostly patience. The ink must be the right age, which means you must steal the right ink weeks in advance, which means you must already have been a traitor in small ways before anyone asked you to be one in large ones. I had been stealing from the Crown for months, apparently. I simply hadn't had the decency to inform myself.

The district office in Keswick kept a store of transit papers in the third drawer of Hobbes's desk, and Hobbes kept his keys in his coat, and his coat kept itself on the same hook from Michaelmas to Lady Day, because Hobbes was a man of habits if not of thought. I had copied his signature four times before I managed one that didn't look drawn by a frightened child. The trick, I discovered, was not to write his name but to be slightly bored while writing it.

Now I sat at my lodgings in the village with a candle shielded behind the flour tin—Mrs. Tregowan had asked no questions when I requested a room facing away from the harbor, which should have told me she already knew everything worth knowing—and I made Tomas Merrick into a man who had never been an enemy of anything except, perhaps, his own government's opinion of him.

*Thomas Quill*, the papers said. My cousin. My late father's sister's son, come from Canada to settle family affairs, in possession of sound character, paid passage on the brig *Cormorant*, bound for Lisbon and points south. I gave him my own surname because it was the only thing in the room I owned outright, and because there is a certain arithmetic in it: if they hang a man for bearing your name, let them at least spell it correctly.

Three sets. One for the ship's manifest, one for the port authority at Falmouth, one for burning in case the first two went wrong. I had learned that much from watching men like Hobbes: always keep a document whose only purpose is its destruction. It is the most honest paper in any bureaucracy.

A floorboard creaked below. Mrs. Tregowan's tread, going to the door. A low murmur. Then her voice through the floorboards, pitched for me rather than the visitor: "Girl. Your cousin's boots have come."

Boots. In what world did this constitute sense? I put down the pen, went down, and found on the kitchen table a pair of salt-stained sea boots, a fisherman's oilskin coat, a sou'wester hat, and Mrs. Tregowan looking at me with the expression of a woman who has buried three husbands and outlived every one of their secrets.

"Dan Pellow says the boy walks like an accountant," she said. "Soldiers watch how a man walks. Fishermen walk like the ground owes them money. Your cousin needs to learn to be owed."

"And why does Dan Pellow care?"

She poured tea I hadn't asked for and pushed it toward me. "Because in the winter of fifty-three, his brother went down off the point, and a lighthouse keeper rowed out into weather that would drown the Devil's own grandmother and hauled him back. Dan has been trying to pay that debt for six years. Tonight the interest comes due." She looked at me over her cup. "You'll want to know something, girl, since you're writing the papers. Nobody in this village will say a word tomorrow. Not under threat, not drunk, not dead. You can build on that or you can build on nothing."

I built on that. What else does a forger do but trust the materials she has?

* * *

### Silas

The cliffs at night are a country I have citizenship in. Seven years of carrying oil up two hundred steps will do that; a man learns each stone the way he learns the flaws in his own hands. But tonight I was learning them for two, and the second man walked like an accountant.

"Left foot first," I said, for the fourth time. "The right one sits on loose shale. It wants your weight about as much as you want the sea's."

Tomas shifted. Somewhere below us the water worked at the rocks with the patience of a debt collector. Above, the lamp turned its steady circle, because I had lit it at dusk as I have lit it every dusk for seven years, decommission orders and fugitives notwithstanding. If the light failed tonight of all nights, some clerk in London would write *irregularity observed* and send men, and men were the one thing this coast had too many of already.

"You hate me," Tomas said. Not accusing. Observing, the way he'd observed everything since I'd pulled him from the boathouse floor—cataloguing me like evidence.

"I don't know you."

"You knew Maren."

"That's different."

"How?"

I stopped walking, which was a mistake, because stopping meant standing still next to him, close enough to see that in certain lights he had her jaw, her way of holding the head slightly tilted as though the world were a sentence she was still parsing. "It just is," I said, with all the eloquence grief allows, and started walking again.

He followed. Left foot first.

We came down through the cut above the cove where the *Cormorant* would lie at half tide, and I made him do the last stretch twice, once slow, once slower. By the third pass he moved like a man who trusted the dark a little, which was either progress or the beginning of overconfidence, and I told him so.

"You sound like her," he said quietly.

"I never met her until I was twenty-four."

"No—you sound like her when she was teaching me something she'd decided I needed to know. That tone. The one that says *I will repeat this as many times as necessary and I resent every one of them.*"

For a moment neither of us moved, and the sea filled the silence the way it fills everything, without being asked. "She taught you the lamp mechanism?" I asked, though I knew the answer. I'd watched him wipe the lens glasses two nights ago with the exact circular motion she used to make, wrist turning at the top of the arc, and I hadn't said a word because some things, once spoken, become too heavy to carry back uphill.

"She said everyone should know how to make a light. Said most people spend their lives looking for one." He paused. "She said you were the best man she ever knew, and that you'd deny it, and that the denying was part of the being."

"We should move," I said. "The tide doesn't argue."

But I carried her jaw-tilt and her teaching tone and her brother down the rest of the cliff path in the dark, left foot first, and if my eyes stung a little I blamed the wind, which has taken blame for worse.

At the top of the cove path we found Dan Pellow waiting with the boots and a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and Dan looked Tomas up and down and said, "Walk for me."

Tomas walked. Left foot first. Dan grunted—the highest praise his people award—and handed over the boots. "Break them in tonight. Tomorrow you're a fisherman who's never been anything else." He glanced at me. "Captain Brannoc sent word. He wants to see the girl's papers before midnight. Says if they're good enough to fool Falmouth they're good enough for him, but he'll want to check the stitching himself."

"He'll check them," I said. "That's what worries me."

"Aye," said Dan, and the word contained the whole village: every wreck pulled off the rocks, every drowning man hauled breathing from the water, every favor banked against a night exactly like this one. "Everything worries us all, keeper. We go anyway."

* * *

### Ada

Brannoc read the papers by a shaded lantern in the back room of the Anchor, and I sat across from him doing my impression of a woman who forged government documents every day before breakfast. Inside, I was doing sums about hanging.

"Thomas Quill," he said. "Cousin."

"Cousin."

"From Canada."

"So the papers claim."

He set them down and looked at me, and I understood why smugglers survive and clerks retire: there was no cruelty in his face at all, only assessment, cold and complete. "I've carried papers from three governments and none," he said. "These are better than mine. Whoever taught you should be arrested."

"He was. That's rather the point of me."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that on another face might have been a smile. "Here's my price, Miss Quill, and it isn't coin. When this is done—whatever happens tomorrow, however it goes—you burn your notes. Every page you've written about this coast. The unreported wrecks, the cargo that passes through the Anchor, the names. All of it."

"That's blackmail."

"That's arithmetic. You hold my life in those pages. I hold your cousin's in my hull. Neither of us enjoys it. Both of us will survive it." He slid the papers back. "They're good. Better than good. Tell Blackwood we sail on the morning tide, not after it, whatever else happens. And tell him—" Brannoc hesitated, and for a moment the smuggler and the clerk in me looked at each other through the same eyes. "Tell him I knew Maren's brother in Pentonville. Tell him Tomas held onto his humanity in a place designed to strip it, and that men like me noticed. That's why the price is only paper."

I went out into the night with the documents against my ribs and found the village awake around me, quiet as a held breath. Nets mended by lamplight that didn't need mending. Boats moved to positions that had nothing to do with fishing. Mrs. Tregowan at her window, watching the cliff path, a candle lit for someone—I never learned whom.

An entire community, invisible for generations, and every single one of them visible tonight, on purpose, for a stranger with an enemy's name.

I had spent my career counting heads. It took me until that moment to understand what heads are for.

* * *

### Silas

Midnight to three is the dead hours, and we spent them in the boathouse, Tomas sleeping in fits against the wall and me pretending I wasn't memorizing him—the locket open in his palm even in sleep, Maren's portrait worn soft by two thumbs now instead of one. I had the twin here against my own chest. Between us we carried her whole, which I suspect is the only way anyone carries anyone.

At half past two Ada came down the path with a wrapped parcel of bread and salt fish, because she is a woman who believes no enterprise should be undertaken hungry, and because feeding people is the one language she speaks more fluently than ink.

"The papers passed," she said. "Brannoc sails on the tide."

"Then it's real."

"It's been real since the census form." She sat down on an overturned dory, and for a while none of us spoke, the three of us arranged around a lantern turned so low it was nearly a rumor. "Silas. After tomorrow. What will you do?"

"Do?"

"They'll know. Even if the village holds—even if I hold—they'll know within a month. A keeper who loses his post the same week a fugitive vanishes isn't subtle, Silas. You've never been subtle. You're the least subtle invisible man in England."

I looked at Tomas, asleep again, or pretending it with the courtesy of a man giving privacy to a conversation he already knew the end of. "Seven years ago I buried my name with my wife," I said. "Figured a man could live without one if he kept his hands busy. Turns out I was wrong twice over. Once about the living, once about the busy." I stood, and checked the tide mark on the slipway stones, old habit, though the tide needed no checking from me tonight. "After tomorrow I expect I'll have to be somebody. Might as well be Silas Blackwood. He was decent company, from what I remember."

Ada laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly put away, but real. "And if they come for you?"

"Then they'll find a man with nothing portable left to lose, and I've been informed that makes me dangerous."

* * *

### Ada

By four o'clock the whole plan ran on rails we'd laid in the dark: Dan Pellow and his brothers on the water, ostensibly hauling nets, actually positioned between the cove and the patrol cutter's mooring. Mrs. Tregowan's nephews walking the coast road with a lame pony and a broken cart, providing the soldiers' lookout a piece of theater to watch instead of a cove to watch. The sexton ringing changes for early practice, which would cover any shouted signal. And me at my window with my forged papers and my burned drafts cooling in the grate, doing the one thing left to a forger: waiting.

Hobbes would notice the missing blanks eventually. Someone would count heads. There is always a count.

But tonight—for one sealed, held-breath night—the count was ours. A fisherman's family larger by none. A census line blank where a cousin should be. A light burning over a headland for the last time, tended by hand, by a man who had measured his whole life in portable things and finally found one thing worth staying for.

The sky in the east went the color of gunmetal, then the color of a healing bruise. Down in the cove, small in the gray, two figures came along the cliff path to the water's edge—one tall and careful, one broader, carrying a sea chest that had never in its life held clothes.

And then the bell at the coastguard station began to ring—not the sexton's changes, not practice, but the alarm pattern, urgent and wrong—and a voice on the road above us shouted *soldiers on the cliff path*, and the morning broke all at once, everywhere, like glass.
