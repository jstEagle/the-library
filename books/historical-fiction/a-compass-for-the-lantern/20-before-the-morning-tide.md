## 20. Before the Morning Tide

There is a particular hour before dawn when the sea stops pretending to be anything but gray. I had seen it seven thousand mornings or more from the lamp gallery, tidy and distant behind glass. From the shingle of Quill's Cove it looked different — closer, colder, the way a thing looks when you are about to hand your whole life into it and ask it kindly not to drop you.

We came down the cliff path in the dark, Tomas ahead of me because he was younger and because I had told him the rocks by feel so many times that my feet no longer trusted my eyes anyway. Ada walked behind with the forged papers buttoned inside her coat, and every few steps she recited their contents under her breath like a woman counting rosary beads she'd made herself and wasn't entirely sure God would accept.

"Name," she murmured.

"Tomas Blackwood," said Tomas.

"Occupation."

"Fisherman."

"Fisherman," Ada repeated, sourly. "You have the hands of a man who has never gutted anything in his life."

"I have the hands of a man who has been in a prison camp for four years. They'll believe what I tell them to believe."

"They'll believe what the papers tell them," Ada said, "and the papers say fisherman, so for the next ten minutes you will smell like herring if I have to rub one on you."

I let them quarrel. It kept the fear moving through them instead of pooling somewhere fatal. That was Maren's trick, though she'd have hated to hear me credit her with it — she'd have said it was plain sense, and that crediting her was sentiment, and that sentiment was a luxury for people whose brothers weren't fugitives.

The *Kestrel's Grace* rode at anchor in the cove, riding badly. Captain Merrick's boat was already in the surf line, and two of his men stood beside it with lanterns hooded to slits, which was either professionalism or nerves, and with Merrick it was usually both wearing the same coat.

Then the fourth light appeared on the road above the cove. It didn't bob like a lantern carried by a fisherman. It marched.

Soldiers.

Four of them, down the switchback at the pace of men who had been told to be thorough rather than quick, and behind them — I counted twice to be sure — a fifth figure with an officer's bearing and an officer's inability to walk on wet shingle without complaint.

"Merrick," I said quietly. The captain turned. "You weigh anchor the moment I nod. Not when it's polite. When I nod."

"Blackwood." He spat toward the water. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," I said. "It will go one way or the other. Weigh anchor when I nod."

* * *

They reached the boat before we reached the water's edge, which is the arithmetic of soldiers: they always arrive where they intend to be, and everyone else intends something, so they always win the race. The officer planted himself between us and the surf like a fence post that had opinions.

"You there. Halt."

I halted. I am good at halting; I have practiced being unremarkable for seven years, and unremarkable men halt promptly and look apologetic about their own legs.

"Name," said the officer. He was young. Young officers are the dangerous ones — old ones have learned how much paperwork trouble makes.

"Silas Blackwood," I said, and gave him the truth entire, because a lie begins better on a foundation of fact. "Keeper of the headland light."

"Not after tomorrow," he said, with the small satisfaction of a man who had memorized his orders and wished to be examined on them. "And these?"

"My son," I said.

The word left my mouth before I had fully considered its weight. Some words you lift carefully. This one I threw, the way you throw a rope across a gap — and then you pray it catches on something, because you cannot take it back.

Tomas went very still beside me. In the hooded lantern light he looked, God help me, like Maren. He had her jaw, her way of holding his chin slightly up as if expecting the world to argue. I had spent seven years not looking at that face too closely because it hurt. Now it was going to save us or hang us, and either way it hurt.

"Your son," the officer repeated. He produced a lantern, unhooded it, and held it up like a man inspecting horseflesh. "Papers."

Ada stepped forward with the smoothness of a woman who had done this forty times in forty villages and been believed thirty-nine of them. "Transit pass, fishing crew manifest, harbor clearance. Captain Merrick is taking on hands at Falmouth roads."

The officer read. Soldiers read slowly — it is part of their training, I think, to make paper feel like a wall. He looked from the page to Tomas. From Tomas to me.

"Your son," he said again, "has a foreign accent."

"He has a mother from the Baltic coast," I said, "and four years at sea, where every port ruins your vowels. You'd know that if you'd served afloat instead of ashore."

That was a gamble. Young officers can forgive a lie sooner than a slight. But something moved behind his face — some memory of a ship, perhaps, or a father who'd served — and his jaw tightened without ordering anything.

"Your son's name."

"Tomas," I said. "Tomas Blackwood."

"And where was your son born?"

Here it was. The whole night balanced on the answer, and I discovered, standing on wet stone with the tide coming in and my heart going like a smith's hammer, that I had already answered it years ago without knowing. Because when you keep a light, you learn that a flame is only ever borrowed — you borrow oil, you borrow weather, you borrow time — and the keeping is the whole of the ownership.

"On this coast," I said. "In the keeper's cottage, in the winter of '31, with the wind coming over the gallery rail like the end of the world. He was born in the light, sir, because the midwife couldn't cross the path in the storm, and his mother—" my voice did something I hadn't authorized, a crack down the middle, and I let it, because cracked things are believed, "—his mother died not three winters since, and I'll thank you not to make me say her name to strangers on a beach at four in the morning."

Silence. The sea filled it, patient as sea is.

The officer looked at Tomas again, longer this time. And Tomas — this is the part I will tell no one as long as I live, and now I am telling it to paper, which is worse — Tomas took one step forward and stood the way Maren used to stand when questioned about market weights, chin up, faintly bored, *dare* written across him in her exact handwriting.

"Sir," Tomas said, in his ruined, foreign-edged voice, "my father is a stubborn man and I would like to catch the tide. Are we finished?"

*Father.* He spent the word like a coin he'd been saving all his life for exactly this purchase.

The officer's lantern wavered — half an inch, no more. A soldier shifted behind him. And then, from the anchorage, came the sound that broke the standoff the way weather breaks things: chain running out through a hawsehole, fast and deliberate, iron singing against iron.

Merrick was weighing anchor.

"Stop that vessel!" the officer barked, spinning — and stopped himself, because you cannot stop a ship with four men and a sergeant's whistle, and everyone on that beach knew it including him. The *Kestrel's Grace*'s canvas fell from her yards in one motion, blooming white against the gray, and she began to pay off from the wind with the unhurried insolence of a cat leaving a room.

"The tide," Ada said mildly, into his confusion. "Captain Merrick keeps his own schedule. He's famous for it. Mostly infamous."

The officer turned back to us. For a long moment the lantern light sat on Tomas's face — on Maren's face — and I understood that whatever happened next, I had already given away the only thing I had left to give. Seven years I had spent buying invisibility with silence, and I had just spent my name, my wife's grave-cold memory, and my claim to a son I never had, all in the space of sixty seconds. It should have felt like ruin.

It felt like breathing after holding my breath since the day she died.

"Go," the officer said finally, not quite looking at any of us. "The harbor master will want those papers countersigned. See that it's done." He paused, and added, quieter, almost to himself: "My mother died in '48. I'd not make a man say it either."

He turned and marched his men back up the switchback, and not one of them looked round, and I have wondered since whether that was discipline or mercy wearing discipline's coat.

* * *

We stood in the surf until the boat came for Tomas. There wasn't time for speeches, which was a mercy, because I had none drafted.

He gripped my forearm the sailor's way, hard, and I gripped his, and for a moment we were two men holding each other up in cold water and neither of us would have admitted it under torture.

"She made you promise," he said. "Seven years ago."

"She made me promise," I agreed.

"Was it worth it? The light, the years, all of it — was it worth what it cost?"

I thought of the lamp turning above the empty headland, of the blank census line in Ada's neat hand, of a boy born in lamplight who never existed learning to call me father in order to save both our lives. I thought of Maren measuring out tea leaves with terrible precision because everything in her world had to be portable, in case they came, in case they ran.

"Ask me at the next tide," I said, "when I've counted what's left."

He laughed — a short, wrecked sound, half sob — and then the sailors were pulling him up over the gunwale, and the boat was turning, and the *Kestrel's Grace* leaned her shoulder to the swell and made for open water with my whole heart stowed somewhere below her waterline.

Ada and I stood on the shingle and watched the gray take her. She did not cry. Neither did I. We were past the country where crying lives.

"Well," she said at last, in the voice of a woman reviewing a ledger and finding it scandalous, "you realize you've acquired a son. On official paper. In front of four soldiers and a Crown officer. That's not a lie anymore, Blackwood. That's a permanent administrative condition."

"I've been called worse things by better forms."

She looked at me sideways, and there was something new in her face — not suspicion, that was gone for good, but something more dangerous: the beginning of a decision she hadn't announced yet. "You understand what happens tomorrow. They seal the tower. You hand over your keys. You'll be a man with no post, no name on any register worth trusting, and a fictitious son at large on the high seas."

"Yes," I said. The tide sucked at our boots, impatient. "It's the freest I've felt in seven years."

Above us, far up the headland, the great lens caught the first thin light of morning and threw it out one last revolving time — my light, still burning, still mine until tomorrow's keys changed hands — and I turned toward the cliff path with Ada beside me and nothing in my pockets but a dead woman's locket and a living man's debt paid in full.

I did not yet know that the officer had paused at the top of the switchback and written something in his notebook.

I would find out by noon.
