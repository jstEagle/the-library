## 21. What Can Be Carried

The ship took an hour to become a smudge and another to become nothing at all. I stood at the cliff's edge the whole while, because someone ought to witness it, and I was the only one left who knew what we'd done.

That's the arithmetic of a thing like that morning. You spend seven years of light, one night of terror, and one lie wearing my dead wife's face, and what you get in return is an empty horizon. By any honest ledger, I'd been robbed.

But I have never once kept an honest ledger.

I turned the locket over in my hand. The twin—the lesser twin, Maren used to say, though she'd said it smiling, the way you say things you don't mean to wound with. Hers had gone into the ground with her, or rather her brother had put it there before they nailed down the lid, because Tomas had always believed that objects could carry messages across the divide, and God help him, he still did. This one he'd pressed into my palm on the boat, wet and salt-stung, before the captain's boy pulled him aboard by the collar like a sack of contraband, which is more or less what he was.

*She'd want you to keep something that isn't grief,* he'd shouted over the engines, or maybe I only heard it that way. Grief was the only inheritance I'd been fluent in for seven years.

I opened the locket. Inside, where Maren's miniature had been, there was a scrap of paper folded to the size of a fingernail. I nearly threw it into the sea out of pure habit—I'd spent so long burning papers that my hands moved toward destruction before my mind could object—but the wind was wrong for burning and my curiosity, long dormant, had woken somewhere around the moment I told three armed soldiers that a wanted man was my son.

The paper read, in Maren's looping hand: *For Silas, who counts everything. Some things cannot be counted.*

She'd written it years ago, before her illness, before any of it. She'd hidden it in the twin locket the way she hid all her best truths—in plain sight, inside something small enough that I'd never bother opening it, because why would I? It wasn't mine. It was hers, and then it was Tomas's, and now it was mine again by the strange relay race that love runs when it refuses to die.

I laughed. First time in a long time. It came out sounding like a rusted hinge, but it came out.

* * *

They came for me at noon, as official things do. Not soldiers this time—worse. A clerk.

He was a young man with a ledger under his arm and sand in his shoes, and he climbed the tower stairs behind me with the resigned air of someone who had been told the view was worth it and suspected otherwise. At the top, he consulted his papers, looked at the lamp I had polished every day for seven years until I could see my own reflection in the brass, and delivered the sentence.

"Silas Blackwood. Keeper of record. Your appointment is terminated effective immediately, pursuant to the decommissioning order. You will surrender your keys."

"You're standing in the reason this light kept burning," I said. "You could just look at it."

He blinked at me. Clerks are not accustomed to being addressed by furniture.

"The automated mechanism," he recited, "will be installed within the month."

"And who trims the wick of your automated mechanism when the salt crusts it? Who clears the glass after a gale throws the Atlantic at it? Who—"

"That will be resolved by the contractor." He held out his hand, palm up, with the confidence of a man who has never had a palm refused.

I gave him the keys. All three. The big iron one that opened the door at the base, the brass one for the lamp room, and the small one that opened nothing anymore—it had belonged to the oil store's private cabinet, and the cabinet had gone to rot years back, but I'd kept the key on the ring because throwing it away would have meant admitting some part of the tower was finished.

I suppose I knew how that felt.

"There's also," the clerk said, shuffling pages, "the matter of your registration. The watch list requires—" He stopped. Frowned. Turned a page as though the answer might be hiding underneath. "Your surname here is recorded as Blackwood."

"It is."

"But the household verification from the spring"—he found the sheet, and I watched Ada Quill's handwriting swim into focus, that careful upright script, the count of one household, one keeper, and beneath the line where a second name should have gone, a blank space that had cost her more than I would ever fully know—"lists no kin. No dependents." His eyes came up. "Yet witnesses report a man at the cove whom you identified as your son."

The wind off the sea came through the lamp room and moved between us like a third party with opinions.

"My son died at birth," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and also neither, depending on which hour of which night you measured from. "Grief makes strange claims at gunpoint. Ask any man who's stood where I stood."

The clerk wrote something. I would have given a year of my life to see what, but I've learned that the words clerks write down matter less than the words they leave out, and Ada had taught me that without meaning to.

"You'll need to vacate the dwelling by week's end."

"I'll need to vacate it tonight," I said. "There's nothing here that's mine."

This was almost true. What was mine fit in one hand: a locket, a folded prayer to no saint I recognized, and a blank line on a government form. Everything else—the chair worn to the shape of me, the mugs with their chipped rims, the seven years of logbooks recording weather nobody else had bothered to notice—all of it belonged to the Crown now, or to the sea, or to no one, which amounts to the same thing.

I went down the stairs ahead of him. I didn't look back at the top of the tower. Looking back is a luxury for men who expect to return.

* * *

Ada was waiting at the bottom, or pretending not to wait—she stood at the gate with her census satchel, examining the headland grass as though it might declare itself on a form. When the clerk had clattered off down the road toward his horse, she spoke without turning.

"They processed your dismissal already?"

"Clerks move quickly when they're erasing a man. Slower when they're feeding him."

"That's not funny."

"No," I agreed. "It's true, though. Truth gets to be unfunny free of charge."

She turned then, and I saw she'd been crying, or had decided against it at the last moment, which leaves a similar mark. "I want you to know that the blank line was not carelessness."

"I never thought it was."

"I sat with that form for two hours. Two hours, Silas. I thought about every consequence down the chain—who signs it, who reads it, who gets sent where because of what I wrote or failed to write. And then I thought about my mother, who came to this country under a name that wasn't hers, and how the census recorded her anyway, neat as anything, four syllables in a stranger's ink, and how that line is the only proof she ever existed." She adjusted her satchel strap with unnecessary force. "So I left it blank. Because some people are better served by absence than accuracy."

"You crossed a line," I said.

"I redrew one." Her chin came up. "There's a difference, and I intend to go on insisting there is."

"What happens to you? For the blank?"

A shrug that didn't reach her eyes. "A reprimand. Possibly transfer to an inland district. They can hardly make an example of me too loudly—the alternative is explaining why the household count needed verifying twice, and why a fugitive walked onto a ship in full view of a survey team, and why the harbor master's books balance like a drunk on deck in a swell." She paused. "Bureaucracy protects itself first. My blank line embarrasses them quietly. Quiet embarrassment is survivable."

"And loud truth?"

"Loud truth gets a lighthouse keeper dismissed," she said, "and a village fined, and a family scattered. Except it didn't, this once. Which is the only reason I sleep."

We stood there in the gate's shadow. Below us the village smoked its breakfast chimneys; above us the dark lens sat in its cage, already going blind, waiting for its mechanical heart. Somewhere far south, past every horizon I'd ever swept with that light, a ship carried my wife's brother toward whatever mercy the world had left in stock.

"He sent this for you," I said, and gave her the folded slip of paper I'd been carrying in my pocket since the cove. On it, written in the boat before boarding, in a hand that shook: *Tell Miss Quill the ledgers balance. Tell her a debt among thieves is still paid in full.*

Ada read it twice. Then she did cry, briefly and furiously, the way people cry who have trained themselves not to, and I had the good manners to study the horizon while she did.

* * *

I didn't leave that night. I lied to the clerk, or rather I amended the truth, which is the family trade now. There was one more firing I wanted—not of the great lamp, that honor had been taken from me, but of the small paraffin burner in the cottage window. Seven years I'd kept it lit on the nights Maren had died, a private light for a private loss, and this was such a night whether the calendar admitted it or not. So I burned it. One small flame in one small pane, facing the sea, doing the only work I have ever understood: saying to the dark, in the only language the dark respects, *not here. Not tonight. Someone is still awake.*

In the morning I packed. It took eleven minutes. I know because I counted, and counting is what I do instead of feeling, and I felt plenty.

What I carried away:

One locket, containing one instruction about the limits of counting.
One blank census line, which weighs nothing and yet I felt it in my pocket heavier than the iron key I no longer owned.
One oilskin coat, not because it was mine but because Ada said the inland districts rain sideways and she'd sooner not bury a second person she'd failed to properly record.

And that was the inventory entire. A man of fifty-three, dismissed, renamed by decree, unemployed for the first time since he was nine years old, walking down the cliff road with his whole estate in one pocket and his whole family on the other side of the ocean.

By any measure, I had lost everything.

By mine, I had finally gotten the sums right.

* * *

The village did not let me pass unnoticed, which was the one thing I'd counted on never happening again, and which turned out to be the one thing I needed most.

Old Mrs. Tredaway pressed a loaf into my arms at her door and refused explanation. The ferryman wouldn't take my coin for crossing to the mainland, then took it anyway when I insisted, then slipped it back into my coat while shaking my hand, a theft in reverse that I chose not to notice out of respect. Two of the fishermen I'd hauled from the water over the years—one of them from water that had already taken his brother—stood at the harbor wall and touched their caps, and neither of them said thank you, because men like us don't, and neither of them said goodbye, because neither of us believed in it.

At the road's turning, where the headland finally lets go of you, Ada fell into step beside me. She had a rail ticket in one hand and her satchel in the other and the expression of a woman who had rehearsed a speech and intended to deliver none of it.

"Inland district," I said.

"Coastal reassignment, actually. They need someone to verify fishing families against the new registry. Apparently I have experience with households that contain irregularities." She looked straight ahead. "It pays badly and it's three miles from your cottage."

"What cottage?"

"The Harrow place. Empty since March. The landlady asks no questions, which I verified personally, on a form, in triplicate, purely out of professional habit." Now she looked at me. "Tomas will write when he can. Letters take months. In the meantime, someone has to teach you how to be seen, and I'm told I have references."

I stopped at the top of the hill. Behind me the tower stood against a clean sky, dark-eyed now, blind, its work handed over to machinery that would never love a single soul it saved. Ahead of me lay three miles of road, a cottage, a village of wreckers and fishermen and outcasts who owed me nothing and kept showing up anyway, and a future with no position, no title, no name worth putting on any list the Crown kept.

Seven years I had measured my life in portable things. Glass, keys, a flame, a lie.

I reached into my pocket and closed my hand around the locket, and inside it, faint as a heartbeat, the paper that said some things cannot be counted.

"Miss Quill," I said, "when the letter comes—you'll want to read it."

"I'll want to *deliver* it," she corrected. "Professionally. With documentation."

We started down the hill together, and behind us the sea went on doing what it does: taking ships, returning wreckage, keeping every account it has ever opened, and forgiving precisely none of them—and ahead of us, just visible past the headland's shoulder, a light burned in the Harrow cottage window that neither of us had lit yet, waiting the way lights do, patient, certain, for whoever was coming home to find it.
