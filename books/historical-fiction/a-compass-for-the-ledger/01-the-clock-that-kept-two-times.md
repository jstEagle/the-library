## 1. The Clock That Kept Two Times

The summons came at the hour when Trieste's lamps still burned against a sea-colored dark, and it came in the person of Maestro Brunner himself, which was the first wrong thing. In six years of apprenticeship Ivo Marchetti had never once been woken by the guild master; apprentices were woken by other apprentices, or by kicks, or by the smell of solder left too long on the iron. But there stood old Brunner in the doorway of the dormer room, holding his hat in both hands like a man come to deliver news of a death, and saying only: "The observatory clock. You will go. Alone."

"Alone, maestro?"

"You have steady hands and no family to notify if you fall from the tower." Brunner said it without humor, the way he said everything, and pressed into Ivo's palm a key heavier than any lock deserved. Then he turned away, hesitated on the stair, and added over his shoulder, words that would sit in Ivo's chest for days afterward: "Whatever you find up there—count first. Fix after. Counting is honest work. Repair can be made to lie."

The streets were empty as spilled water. Ivo climbed through the town with his toolbag knocking at his hip, past the fish market where the night soil men were already working, up the long switchback road to the observatory on the hill, its dome a black shoulder against the last stars. The harbor lay below him spread out like an unrolled chart, masts thick as reeds, and among them—he could not have said why he noticed—one ship riding higher than the rest, fresh-painted, her deck stacked with crates stenciled in letters too far away to read.

The observatory door opened to the great key. Inside, the master clock filled the tower's heart from floor to vaulting: the civic clock, the mother clock, the instrument against which every ship's chronometer, every merchant's ledger entry, every marriage hour and debt due-date in the free port of Trieste was set. Its pendulum swung in its long wooden case with a sound like a patient heart, and Ivo stopped on the threshold because the heart was wrong.

He had heard this clock all his life from a distance, the way everyone in the city had, without knowing he knew it. Now, close, he understood that familiarity was itself a kind of measurement. The beat was too quick. Not broken-quick—the escapement wasn't stumbling, the pallets weren't chattering. It was quick with the terrible smoothness of intention.

He climbed the case on the ladder, opened the glazed door, and counted.

Two minutes fast since the noon signal gun yesterday, if the observatory's records were true—and they always were; the astronomers quarreled about everything except time. He checked the rating nut at the pendulum's foot. Turned. He measured its position against the scribe-marks scratched inside the case by forty years of careful hands. It had been wound upward, deliberately, precisely—a correction made in secret and made well, by someone who understood that a clock need not be smashed to be bent.

Someone had paid to move time itself.

Ivo sat back on the ladder rung and felt the strangeness settle over him like cold water. Ships sailed on tides and tables; tides and tables were reckoned against this clock; and somewhere below him in the dark harbor a ship waited to sail at dawn, and someone wanted dawn to arrive two minutes late by every honest measure in the city—or wanted the city to believe it had arrived, while the true moment slipped away unwitnessed.

There was a smell in the tower that did not belong to oil and brass. Candle tallow, recently extinguished, and under it the fainter smell of a stranger: wool, rain, and something green, like crushed fennel.

"I know you're there," Ivo said, feeling foolish addressing the machinery. "The dust is disturbed on the transom stairs. And you've been eating. There are bread crumbs by the third-weight channel."

Silence, but a listening silence now, no longer an empty one.

"I am apprenticed to the Clockmakers' Guild of Trieste," he went on, more absurdly. "I am unarmed. I have, however, a very good file."

A laugh answered him—short, low, unmistakably female, coming from behind the great counterweight trunk where the driving weights hung in their shaft. She rose out of the shadow between the weights like something the mechanism had produced: a woman perhaps his own age, dark-haired, wind-burned, with sawdust in the collar of a man's coat two sizes too large for her. She held nothing in her hands, which somehow frightened him more than a knife would have.

"You counted," she said. Her Triestine was fluent but accented inland—Dalmatian, maybe, or Istrian. "Before you touched anything. Brunner told you to count."

"How do you know Brunner's instructions?"

"Because Brunner is the one man in your guild who refused to be paid, and therefore the one man they couldn't trust to send." She came forward into the candlelight she'd pinched out hours ago, and Ivo saw her eyes were red-rimmed, sleepless. "They sent you instead. A boy with steady hands and nobody to notify." Something moved across her face—not quite apology. "I'm sorry. I needed them to send someone who would count."

"The clock is two minutes fast," Ivo said. "Deliberately. Wound by hand, expertly. If you're the one who—"

"I'm the one who found it this way four nights ago, when I climbed here to hide." She reached into the coat and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and the sight of it made her hands tremble in a way her voice had not. "Ivo Marchetti. Yes, I know your name. I know your mother's name too."

The tower seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it did in earthquakes, though Trieste had none.

"My mother has been dead five years," he said.

"And before that she was dead on paper for one year before she died," the woman said. "That is what I came to tell you. That is what these letters say." She began unwrapping the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon, and he saw beneath it a sheaf of letters, many letters, bound and sorted, written in a small upright hand with distinctive crossed descenders. "My father was Anton Kovač, clerk to the merchant house of Vralj & Sons. Six years ago the Adriatic gave up a broken boat and a coat of his, and Trieste buried an empty idea of him. He didn't drown. He ran. Because he had copied something, and they wanted him and it at the bottom of the same sea."

"Copied what?"

"A ledger." She said the word the way other people said the names of the dead. "Not their ledger. Their *true* ledger—the record underneath the record. Every debt Vralj & Sons incurred and quietly cancelled. Every dowry diverted. Every inheritance swallowed. Forty-one families, Marchetti, whose property became the victors' history because there was no paper to say otherwise. My father kept the copy for six years. He wrote letters. He watched. And three weeks ago he stopped writing in his own hand and started writing—" her voice caught, steadied— "in mine. He is done running. He is coming in on the ship that sails at dawn, the one whose sailing requires the whole city's clocks to be gently, profitably wrong."

She held the letters out. Instinct told Ivo not to touch them; curiosity, older and stronger, made his hands move anyway. The handwriting was beautiful and strange, and he was bending over the topmost letter when the name stopped him like a hand at his chest.

*Maria Marchetti, widow of the Borgo Teresiano, deceased — principal owing, restored: 340 florins, plus interest accrued to the injury of her minor son.*

Below it, in the same small upright hand, a note in the margin: *The boy repairs clocks. Verify he is honest before contact. HONESTY IS THE ONLY COLLATERAL WE HAVE LEFT.*

Ivo read his mother's name three times. The pendulum swung. Two minutes fast, carrying the whole city toward a dawn that hadn't earned it yet.

"This is a forgery," he said, without conviction.

"Yes," Lena Kovač said. "It might be. Sit down, apprentice, and help me prove whether it isn't. Your guild keeps specimen books—every master's hand, every clerk trained in the house styles. If my father wrote these, we can prove it tonight. If we prove it tonight, I'll show you the rest of what he sent." Her eyes were fierce and exhausted and, he thought, afraid in exactly the way he was afraid. "Because there are men asking questions at your guildhall already, and they are not asking about the clock."

* * *

Outside, far below in the dark harbor, the fresh-painted ship rode at her anchors, and in her hold, wrapped in sacking like something already buried, waited the sanitized history of forty-two families—two minutes fast, and ticking.
