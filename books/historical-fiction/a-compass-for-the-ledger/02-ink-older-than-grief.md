## 2. Ink Older Than Grief

Ivo lit three candles because one felt like secrecy and three felt like work. The workshop below his master's house smelled of oil and brass shavings and the tallow smoke of a thousand nights like this one, and he had always found it comforting. Tonight it felt like a room preparing to hold something too heavy for its floorboards.

Lena Kovač stood at his bench with her hands flat on the wood, as if steadying herself against weather. She had taken off her wet cloak but nothing else — no softness, no settling in. Her letters lay between them in a fan of gray paper gone soft at the folds.

"The guild keeps specimen books," Ivo said. "Every master clockmaker signs the roll each quarter. Signatures, dates, marginal notes. My master calls it vanity. He is wrong. It is habit, and habit is the only honest witness there is."

"You think I forged them." Not a question. She watched him the way she had watched him in the observatory, head tilted, as though he were a mechanism she hadn't decided was trustworthy.

"I think someone did," Ivo said. "I don't yet know that it isn't you."

She slid the top letter toward him without flinching. "Then prove it either way. That's all I ask of anyone tonight."

He brought down the specimen rolls — great leather-bound ledgers, ironically enough, their spines stamped in gold that had dulled to the color of old honey — and opened them across two benches. Forty years of hands looked up at him: the looping *R* of old Master Ferretti, dead before Ivo was born; the cramped, ink-starved script of apprentices promoted too fast; and there, third page of the 1736 roll, a signature he had seen only in guild legend.

*Josip Kovač.*

The letters Lena carried were written in the same hand — but looser, hurried, the descenders trailing off like rope ends. A hand that had aged six years in hiding. Ivo held the candle low and compared stroke by stroke: the peculiar flat-topped *k*, the way Kovač crossed his *t* with a single impatient slash that sometimes caught the next letter, the small hooked flourish after the final letter of his name that no forger would dare invent because no forger would believe a man signed himself with what looked like a sinking anchor.

"It's him," Ivo said quietly.

"He never signs it that way anymore," Lena said. "In the recent letters he drops the hook. As if he's cut himself loose from his own name."

Ivo looked up. "Recent letters come from where?"

"From aboard a ship or near one. He won't say which. He writes that the tide will be right at dawn on the twelfth, and that the historians sail with it, and that everything the merchant house wants forgotten will be forgotten forever unless—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Unless two strangers in Trieste do something reckless."

Ivo turned back to the specimen roll, not because he doubted her but because looking at her face made the recklessness feel suddenly possible. His finger moved down the column of signatures past Kovač's name and stopped.

*Marchetti, Serafina.* Witness mark, 1739. His mother's hand, recorded in the guild's book four years before she died, when she had been hired to sew the canvas dust-covers for the observatory clocks and had signed for the work like any tradesman.

"She's in here," he said. The words came out strange, flattened. "Why would my mother be in your father's papers?"

Lena drew out the last letter, the one she had pressed into his hands at the observatory, and unfolded it with the care of someone handling a wound. "Because your mother wasn't only a seamstress, Ivo. Read what he says about page forty-one."

*Page forty-one of the ledger records the debt of the house of Brenner to Serafina Marchetti, widow, being the sum owed on three shipments consigned in her late husband's name and seized upon his death as 'unclaimed.' The house declared her claim extinguished when I drowned. It was not extinguished. Nothing in that ledger was ever extinguished. It was only waiting for someone who could read numbers.*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, though the letters had begun to swim. Three shipments. He remembered being nine years old and his mother counting coins on this very bench, saying the sea owed them, saying it the way people say things they have given up expecting. He had thought it a figure of speech. It had been an entry. A line of ink in a book, older than his grief, older than her grief, sitting somewhere in a strongbox while he grew up invisible in this city, apprenticed to gears because gears asked nothing of a boy without family money.

"Invisibility was never my nature," he said aloud, not meaning to speak. "It was an arrangement. Someone arranged it."

"Yes," Lena said simply. "That's what the ledger *is*. An arrangement of who gets to be visible."

* * *

By midnight the workshop floor was a map of paper. They had reconstructed the ledger as far as memory and inference allowed: Lena reciting what her father had drilled into her over six years of coded letters — dates, sums, names — Ivo transcribing in the neat vertical hand the guild demanded of its apprentices, each entry flagged with the witness marks that might still exist in civic rolls to corroborate it. Debts. Dowries. Inheritances. Eleven families so far whose legacies had dissolved the day Josip Kovač's hat washed ashore without Josip Kovač inside it.

"The house didn't steal money," Lena said, feeding a fresh candle to replace a guttering one. "They stole the *record* of the money. You can't contest a debt that no longer exists on any page."

"Then we make pages." Ivo sanded the entry he'd just finished — the Brenner shipment, his mother's name written carefully, almost tenderly. "Copies. Dated. Witnessed. Once it enters the civic record through the harbor notary, burning it does nothing. There would be too many hands holding it."

"That is why he trusted you," Lena said, watching him write. "He said Trieste has one clockmaker's apprentice who checks the master clock against the stars instead of against the guild's word."

"Your father watches me?"

"My father watches everyone who is honest. They're rarer and easier to track."

It was nearly two in the morning when the knocking began — not at the workshop door but at the street door above, heavy, patient, the knock of men paid by the hour. Ivo blew across all the candles in one breath and stood in the dark listening.

Voices filtered down through the floorboards. His master's voice, cracked with sleep and then sharpening. And another voice, smooth as oiled case-hardened steel, asking questions in the courteous tone of a man who had already been paid for the answer.

"—a girl, master. Dark hair, speaks German and Italian both. Seen entering your street near the ninth bell. Your apprentice keeps odd hours, we understand. Repairing the observatory clock, is it? Strange hours indeed, when the clock in question runs fast—"

Ivo's hand found Lena's shoulder in the dark and turned her toward the back stair. She moved without argument, gathering the letters into her coat, leaving him clutching the one page still on the bench — the transcription with his mother's name on it, warm from the candle he'd just killed.

At the foot of the back stair, in the black slot of the yard, Lena paused and looked back at him.

"They'll search the observatory next," she whispered. "Everything my father hid there. We go back tonight, Ivo. Before dawn, before the tide — or the last original pages burn on that ship with the historians watching and applauding."

Above them, the street door opened. Boots came into the hall.

Ivo picked up the pendulum weight he had lugged home from the observatory that afternoon — forty pounds of escaped time, still smelling of the clock's oil — and settled it into the sack on his shoulder like a man deciding what kind of night this was going to be.

"Then we'd better outrun the tide," he said.
