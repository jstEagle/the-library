## 3. What the Tide Carries Away

Lena had learned to measure fear in small units: the width of a doorway, the length of a lantern's reach, the number of heartbeats it took a man to decide whether to believe you. Tonight she measured it in streets.

Trieste at three in the morning was a city holding its breath. The lamps along the Riva had burned down to orange cores, and the fog off the harbor had climbed halfway up the old town, softening the stairways into suggestions. She took them two and three at a time, the ledger pages strapped flat against her ribs beneath her coat, Ivo's footsteps close behind her—quick, precise, the footsteps of a boy who had spent his life listening to mechanisms and could not help but move like one.

"The notary keeps hours with the tide," she said, not slowing. "Old Ferretti. My father trusted him before—" Before he drowned, she almost said. Before her father became a name on a stone that held no body. "Before everything."

"Then we pray his habits outlived his courage."

They came down through the fish market, past carts being loaded by men who did not look up, past a dog that raised its head and decided they were not worth the effort of barking. And then the harbor opened before them like a wound—the masts, the rigging, the dark water moving with that slow patient greed the Adriatic showed before dawn tide, and there, at the long quay, the ship.

*The Vittoria*. Three masts, lanterns burning at her rails though no cargo moved. Too many lights for loading. Lena knew what light meant on a ship at an hour when honest work slept. It meant fire kept ready. It meant paper being fed to it page by page.

"They're burning them now," she breathed. "The originals."

"Then your copies are the only ledger left in the world." Ivo stood beside her, chest heaving, and she saw in his face what she had seen growing in him for three days—not the apprentice anymore, but something harder, something forged. "Ferretti's office. Go."

* * *

The notary's window was lit. Ferretti himself answered the door—an old man in a nightshirt with a pistol held low and forgotten, because the moment he saw her face he saw her father's, and the pistol sagged like a dead thing.

"Lena Kovač," he whispered. "God keep us. They told me you'd come or they told me you wouldn't. Six years I've kept ink dry and wax unbroken, waiting."

"We need it now. All of it. Stamps, seals, witnesses if you have them."

"I have myself. On a night like this, myself is worth more than witnesses." He was already moving, pulling ledgers from a locked drawer, laying out the stamped folios of the civic record. "Bring me your copies. Every page. I'll certify against my own attestation of 1742—the debts as they stood before the house 'corrected' them."

Lena unstrapped the bundle. Her hands shook, and she hated them for it. Forty-one nights of copying by candlelight, her father's cramped figures reproduced stroke by stroke, every dowry, every debt, every inheritance the merchant house had swallowed when it wrote one man out of the world and forty families' claims out of the record. She laid the first page down and Ferretti bent over it, quill scratching, and each scratch sounded like a nail driven into a coffin—but a coffin for the house, this time, and not for the truth.

Then the door of the office shuddered.

Not knocked. *Shuddered*, as though the building itself had been struck. Through the window Lena saw them crossing the quay—four men, five, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who owned the ground they walked on. The house's enforcers. The big one in front carried a boat hook; another held a lantern high, and its light caught the wet stone and the fog and made the whole harbor look like the inside of a bell about to be rung.

"Ferretti," Lena said quietly. "Is there another way out?"

"There is the way your father used." The old man did not stop writing. "And there is the way no one uses. Choose fast, girl."

She chose her father's way. Out the back, across the customs yard, onto the quay itself—and then, because the gangway was already filling with the house's men coming ashore, up. Up the mooring lines, up into the rigging of *Vittoria* herself, where the smoke still rose thin and bitter from a brazier lashed to the deck, where the last original pages of her father's ledger were curling to black ghosts in the salt wind.

Ivo came up beside her, and above them both the yards and shrouds went up into the fog until the masts simply ended, dissolved, as if the ship grew into the sky.

* * *

They found the brazier on the quarterdeck, guarded by a single man too confident to expect anyone mad enough to come down from the rigging. Lena came down anyway, boots-first, and took him at the knees. He was heavier than she expected and the deck was slick with tar and ash, and for a moment the whole world was rope and impact and the smell of burning paper—her father's handwriting, six years of his silence, going up in orange curls while she crawled toward the brazier to pull out whatever was left.

A hand closed in her hair.

She twisted, struck upward with her elbow, felt cartilage give. The man grunted and let go and Ivo hit him from the side—a clockmaker's tackle, all timing and no weight, but enough. Enough to buy seconds. Enough for Lena to plunge her hands into the brazier's edge and drag out the iron tray, tipping the half-burned pages away from the coals, pressing the flames out against the deck with her bare palms because there was nothing else and no time and some things mattered more than skin.

Three sheets survived. Charred at the edges. Her father's hand, unmistakable even through the scorch: figures, dates, a signature. Enough to prove the copies were copies of something real.

Then the big enforcer came over the rail, and the night stopped being a fight and started being arithmetic. One of him. Two of them. A quarterdeck thirty feet long with nowhere to go but up or over.

Ivo grabbed her arm. "Up," he said, and they went up, into the shrouds, hand over hand, the ship rolling beneath them, the harbor lights swinging below like dropped coins. Lena heard the man climbing after them, unhurried, letting gravity do his work, letting terror do the rest.

Halfway up the mainmast, Ivo stopped.

"What are you doing—"

"The counterweight," he gasped. "From the observatory. When the pendulum escaped—it fell, it rolled, I thought—I brought it. I couldn't say why. I *had* to bring it."

He had it slung across his back the whole climb, she realized. The great brass-cased pendulum bob of the master clock, forty pounds of it, wrapped in sacking, riding between his shoulder blades like a hump, like a sin, like a compass pointing nowhere. His fingers worked at the knots. The enforcer's face appeared below them, lantern-lit, smiling.

"You're thieves," the man said pleasantly. "Thieves and ghosts. The house will write you out too. We're very good at writing."

"Yes," Ivo said. "You are. You've had practice."

And he cut the line.

The counterweight did not fall so much as *leave*—a dark shape dropping through the fog, gathering the whole night's tension into itself. It missed the climbing man by a hand's breadth, close enough to part the air against his cheek, and struck the deck of *Vittoria* with a sound Lena would hear for the rest of her life: not a crash, a *chime*, a vast cracked bell-note that ran up through the mast into her teeth and her bones and out again into the water.

The deck beam split. The brazier overturned. The enforcer swung loose, screaming, tangled in his own line, and somewhere below—in the observatory on the hill above the harbor, where the master clock sat wounded and dying since the night its pendulum escaped—the great works gave one shudder, one last refusal, and stopped. Lena felt it rather than heard it: a silence opening over Trieste, two minutes wide, the false time collapsing all at once like a held breath released.

For the first time in six years, the city and the truth were running on the same hour.

* * *

They came down the far side of the ship on lines cut from the ensign staff, dropped into a customs skiff, and rowed hard for the stairs below Ferretti's window while shouting broke out aboard *Vittoria* behind them. Lena's palms were burned in the shapes of her father's last pages. She held them closed and did not look at them.

In the notary's office, Ferretti waited with the folios finished, the seals cooling, three charred sheets pressed beside clean copies under official wax. "Done," he said simply. "Entered. It cannot be unwritten now—not cleanly, not without the whole city asking why."

Lena looked at the documents and felt nothing like triumph. She felt the clarity that comes after terror burns everything else away: the truth existed now in exactly two places. In these papers. And in the testimony of two exhausted people who could be written out as easily as her father had been—if anyone let them be.

Footsteps on the stairs. She reached for the fire poker.

But it was only Ivo, gray-faced, cradling his burned hands, standing in the doorway with fog at his back. Behind him, faint through the window, the harbor bell rang four times—and rang true.

"It's done," he said. Then, quieter, looking past her toward the water, where dawn was beginning and a ship's silhouette was making ready to slip the mole: "Lena. There's a berth on *Vittoria*. Someone bought passage this hour, in cash. Under the name Kovač."

She stared at him. At the water. At the ship that carried her father's enemies—and perhaps, at dawn, her father himself.

"He's alive," she whispered. "He's been alive this whole time. And he's leaving."

Ivo met her eyes. "Then someone has to be on that ship when it does."
