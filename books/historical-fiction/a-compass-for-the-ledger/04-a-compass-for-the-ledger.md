## 4. A Compass for the Ledger

The sun came up over the Adriatic the way it always did, indifferent to what it was asked to witness, and Ivo Marchetti stood in the notary's anteroom watching its light crawl across the floor tiles like a slow hand counting out the seconds of a life he no longer recognized as his own.

His hands were wrong. That was the first thing he noticed each time he looked at them—the right one split along two knuckles from the rigging, the left still trembling faintly from gripping the pendulum weight's chain while the harbor burned below him and Lena screamed his name through smoke. A clockmaker's hands were supposed to be precise instruments. These looked like tools that had been used for something other than their purpose.

Perhaps, he thought, they had finally been used correctly.

Signor Barbo, the civic notary, emerged from his inner room with ink still wet to the elbow, carrying the stack of copies like a man transporting a sleeping infant through a thunderstorm. Forty-one pages, copied through the small hours by lamplight in Ivo's workshop—Lena's hand flying across the paper with her father's same slanted urgency, Ivo checking each figure against the fragments they'd rescued, the guild's own apprentice-scribe summoned at gunpoint of pure moral necessity to affix the official seals.

"Entered," Barbo said hoarsely. "Witnessed, sealed, and entered into the civic record of Trieste, this twenty-ninth day of September, in the year of our Lord 1748." He set the ledger down on his desk and looked at it the way men look at things that will outlive them. "Do you understand what you've done, boy?"

Ivo did not answer immediately. Outside the window, the masts of the harbor swayed against the brightening sky, and among them—there, past the mole, already moving—he could see the historians' ship, the *Vigilanza*, her canvas catching the first offshore wind.

"She's sailing," he said.

* * *

They ran. Or rather, Ivo ran, and Barbo followed at the aggrieved trot of a man whose most strenuous daily act was usually the lifting of a seal, and together they came down through the crooked streets to the waterfront as the light turned the water into hammered copper. Dockhands paused in their labor. A fishwife lowered her voice mid-haggle. Somewhere a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

Because everyone in Trieste knew that ship. Everyone knew what she carried: the commissioned historians of the Ridotti merchant house, gentlemen in good coats with clean hands and cleaner consciences, sailing home with the official account of the last six years—the account in which Anton Kovač drowned honorably, in which the shipping house's debts dissolved into honest misfortune, in which forty families' inheritances evaporated like sea mist because no record existed to say otherwise.

No record, Ivo thought, until this morning.

The *Vigilanza* slid past the mole with terrible grace, and Ivo stopped at the harbor's edge, chest burning, and searched the deck.

He found her at the stern rail. Even at that distance he would have known the set of her shoulders anywhere now; he had watched them for three days over candlelight and copying paper, had learned the way she hunched when a figure resisted verification and straightened when the truth clicked into place like a well-made escapement. Lena Kovač stood with the wind pulling at her dark hair, and she was not looking back at the harbor, not looking at him, not looking at the city where her father had been mourned and her family's name erased.

She was facing forward. Toward the open sea. Toward the man somewhere aboard or waiting at journey's end who had chosen death over honesty and anonymity over his own daughter.

She had told him, in the gray hour before dawn, while the ink dried on the last page: *"I put the truth in the archive. But the archive doesn't know my father. I do—or I did. I have to find out which one is real."* He had wanted to argue. He had opened his mouth to argue. And then he had remembered his mother's name in that letter—*Giulia Marchetti, owed eleven ducats and an apology since 1741*—and understood that some debts are not settled by paper alone.

He raised his hand. It felt absurd, a boy waving at a ship, but he did it anyway.

At the stern rail, a smaller shape lifted—one arm, raised in answer. She did not wave, exactly. She held her palm open toward him for a long moment, the way one holds a hand over a flame to learn its distance, and then she let it fall and turned back to the sea.

The *Vigilanza* passed the mole and took the wind full sail, and the morning swallowed her.

Barbo, wheezing beside him, said, "She's mad."

"Yes," Ivo said, and found he was smiling, which surprised them both. "It runs in the family. Her father's, and now—" He stopped. He had almost said *mine*. The word hung there, unclaimed, and he let it hang, because perhaps it was true anyway.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of oil and old wood and, faintly, of smoke that had never quite left the stones since the enforcers' visit. Master Aldo Righi sat behind the long table with the reinstatement papers laid out before him, drafted in his careful secretary hand, and his face wore the particular expression of a man who has rehearsed generosity and resents having to perform it.

"The council met at dawn," Righi said. "Given the circumstances—given your service to the city, such as it was, however irregularly rendered—the guild is prepared to overlook the irregularities. Your apprenticeship resumes today. In two years you'll sit for master examination. The observatory clock will be rebuilt; the commission is yours if you want it."

Ivo looked at the papers. Two years. The word *irregularities*, doing its quiet work, folding three days of terror and one night of violence into a clerical inconvenience. He looked at the window, beyond which the observatory hill rose above the red rooftops, its dome catching the sun. He had climbed that hill a hundred times. He had loved that clock—its great brass heart, its patient hands ruling the harbor's every hour—since he was small enough to hide beneath his mother's worktable.

"It can't be rebuilt," he said quietly.

Righi blinked. "Anything can be rebuilt, boy. Given parts and time."

"Not that clock." Ivo turned his split right hand over, examining the scabs as though they might hold the argument written on them. "Its escapement was cut deliberately. Someone inside this guild was paid to make time lie—to drag the whole harbor two minutes fast so a ship could sail before the tide that mattered. You know it. I know who signed the order; I found the payment entry in the ledger we copied last night, page thirty-four. Your name appears two lines below the signature, Master Righi. As recipient of the audit fee."

The silence that followed had weight and momentum both, like a falling counterweight.

"So," Ivo said, "no. I decline reinstatement. With respect. With thanks, even—for the training, for the tools, for teaching me that a mechanism is only ever as honest as the hand that sets it." He stepped back from the table. "I'll keep the workshop on the canal. I'll take private commissions. And I'll keep the ledger."

"Keep it?" Righi's voice cracked upward. "The copies belong to the civic archive."

"The copies do. All forty-one pages, sealed and safe forever, praise God and Signor Barbo." Ivo smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "But an archive sleeps, Master Righi. Records gather dust; families forget what they're owed; clerks retire and successors misfile things. Someone must remain awake. Someone must be the keeper—not of the paper, but of the promise that the paper exists. When the widows come asking, someone must walk them to the archive and stand beside them while they read. When the Ridotti house sends its lawyers—and it will—I intend to be the man who answers the door with the page numbers memorized."

"You'd make yourself a target for the rest of your life."

"I was invisible for the rest of my life already," Ivo said. "It turned out that was someone else's arrangement, not mine. I'm revising it."

* * *

He worked until dusk.

The guild's smiths had returned the pendulum weight to him that morning—it had saved their lives in the rigging, swung on its chain into the face of the enforcer named Corvi, then torn loose and fallen, a hundred pounds of lead dropping through eighty feet of night air into the observatory tower, through the clock's works, through six years of the guild's quiet corruption, shattering gears and frames and the great brass escapement in a single ringing catastrophe that half the city heard.

It was ruined, the clock. Unrepairable, truly. But its remains were his to arrange, and he arranged them.

He mounted the shattered escape wheel above the guildhall door, its teeth bent outward like a crown knocked askew, and beside it he fixed the clock's old brass dial, cracked across the seven, and beneath both, on a bracket he machined himself with hands that only shook when he let them, he hung the compass.

Not a mariner's compass—a surveyor's, heavy-cased, its needle jeweled and true. He had bought it that afternoon from a chandler on the waterfront with the last of his mother's eleven ducats, finally paid out that very morning from the first settlement claim filed under the restored ledger, and he had engraved the case plate himself:

*FOR THE RECORD. IT DOES NOT POINT HOME. IT TELLS YOU WHERE YOU STAND.*

Passersby slowed to read it. Some of them, he noticed, were women in widow's black, and one of them, an old woman with his mother's same Istrian jaw, looked from the compass to Ivo standing in the doorway with file-oil on his sleeves, and her mouth formed a silent question.

He stepped aside to let her in, and behind her came another, and another, and the keeper's work began.
