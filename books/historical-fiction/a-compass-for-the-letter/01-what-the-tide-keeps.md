## 1. What the Tide Keeps

The tide went out at four, and with it went the smell of the harbor's rot, replaced by the cleaner stink of tar and hemp that lived in the bones of Meridian Trading's Warehouse Three. Tamsin Okafor counted crates.

That was the work. Counting, weighing, marking, signing. He had been doing it for six years, since the company bought out his father's debt from the diving cooperative after the storm season took three boats and most of the older men's nerve. Fourteen years old, a bill of sale folded into his shirt pocket where his mother's pearl had once hung, and a clerk with ink-stained cuffs telling him it wasn't slavery, exactly, it was indenture, and there was a difference, and the difference was that someday he'd be free.

He was twenty now. The someday had not come closer. If anything it receded, the way a ship on the horizon seems to be standing still.

"Okafor." The name came down the aisle of crates like something thrown. It was Mr. Quill, the second clerk — not the head one, never the head one; the head clerks of Meridian moved through the warehouse the way gulls move over water, touching nothing — but Quill, who did the actual counting and therefore knew where everything was, including Tamsin. "The Corbier manifest doesn't match. You're short forty pounds of indigo."

"I'm not short anything." Tamsin didn't look up from the crate he was checking. His chalk hovered over the lid. "The Corbier shipment came in wet at the dock. Water weight evaporated between the weighmaster and us. Forty pounds, give or take. I marked it in the margin."

Quill came up the aisle, small and quick, his ledger held against his chest like a breastplate. He looked at the margin note, looked at Tamsin, and made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter cost less. "You know they'll dock you for it anyway."

"They dock me for everything." He wrote the figure. "The sea takes its share. So does the company."

"You want to watch your mouth."

Tamsin watched his mouth. That was most of what he did. Six years of watching his mouth, keeping his hands busy, keeping his possessions few enough to carry. He had learned early that a man who owned nothing could not be threatened with losing anything, and that this was a kind of freedom, and that it was also a kind of lie, because the company owned him and could threaten him all day.

* * *

At the end of the shift he climbed to the loft above Warehouse Three where the seasonal laborers slept, and took inventory.

This was a private ritual, performed every evening the way other men said prayers. He laid out his life on the blanket: one diver's knife, bone-handled, the blade worn thin from sharpening but still able to hold an edge. One pearl, imperfect, slightly baroque, warm cream rather than white — his mother's last find, pried from an oyster she opened an hour before the dive that killed her. One change of clothes, patched at both knees. A coil of good cord. A flint and steel. Fourteen coppers and a half-sovereign sewn into his coat hem, which was either savings or escape money depending on the day.

No papers except the indenture. No letters — letters tied you to a place, assumed you'd stay to receive the next one. No photographs; nobody had ever taken any. No surname he trusted. Okafor was his father's, and his father was a story told by other people — drowned off the Kettle Shoals before Tamsin could walk, so that even the name felt borrowed from a dead man's estate. At the harbor office they called him Okafor when they bothered with formality and boy when they didn't. Neither fit. A name should be like a boat, he thought: built for you, fitted to your shoulders, seaworthy in weather. His was a derelict he was allowed to salvage.

He wrapped the pearl back in its cloth and put the whole inventory away in under two minutes. That was the point of the ritual. Not remembrance — readiness. Everything he loved could be gathered and gone in two minutes flat. He had timed it.

Below the loft window, Corvane was lighting itself against the dusk. Lamplighters working the docks row, masts nodding in the basin, the great black shapes of ships at their moorings like sleeping animals. Somewhere out past the bar, the tide was turning, and the whole harbor would swing on its anchors, and none of it would have anything to do with him.

He was used to that. He told himself he was used to that.

* * *

The summons came the next morning, before the dew had burned off the crates.

Not Quill this time. Mr. Harrow himself, head clerk of the Meridian Trading Company, Corvane office, standing in the warehouse doorway with the light behind him so that Tamsin saw first the silhouette — tall, narrow, hatless, which was strange — and then the face, gray-eyed and patient as a fisherman watching a line.

"Okafor. You can read?"

It was not a question about literacy. Tamsin's mother had taught him letters on a slate before he could swim, and Harrow knew it; the company kept records of everything, including which of its property could read. But the phrasing was a test of some kind, so he answered carefully.

"Yes, sir."

"And write."

"Yes, sir."

Harrow held out an envelope.

It was heavy paper, cream-colored, sealed with wax — real wax, stamped with a signet Tamsin didn't recognize: a letter H crossed by a keel line. The envelope had weight to it, more than paper accounted for. It sat in Harrow's palm the way a coin sits in a beggar's, offering and withholding at once.

"There is a letter," Harrow said, "which requires delivery. Personally. By hand. To Bramwen Hale, the shipwright, at his yard on Sallow Lane. You know where that is?"

"The old yard by the mudflats. Past the ropewalk."

"Past the ropewalk." Harrow almost smiled. "You will give it to no one but Hale himself. Not his apprentices, not a neighbor, not the postman if you meet the postman on the way. If Hale is asleep, you wake him. If Hale refuses to open the door, you knock until your knuckles bleed or he opens it. Do you understand?"

"What's in it?"

For a moment something moved behind the gray eyes — amusement, maybe, or its dead cousin, contempt. "What's in it is not your concern. What's in it concerns only Mr. Hale. Your concern is delivery. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Harrow did not hand over the letter. He let it sit there in his open palm while the warehouse breathed around them — the creak of cargo settling, gulls arguing on the roof beams, the far-off winch-song of the morning crews. "Why do you ask what's in it? Most men in your position deliver and don't wonder."

Tamsin considered lying, decided it would show, and answered plainly. "Because nobody pays a man to carry something worthless. And nobody seals something harmless in wax."

Now Harrow did smile, briefly, without warmth. "A diver's son. You read the water before you step in." He extended the envelope again, and this time Tamsin took it, and the weight of it traveled up his arm and settled somewhere behind his ribs. "Deliver it today. Come straight back after. There'll be the usual wage, plus a copper for the errand."

"A copper."

"For the errand," Harrow repeated, and turned away, and stopped, half in the doorway, half in the light. "Okafor. Has anyone ever addressed a letter to you? Anywhere, ever?"

The question landed strangely, like a wave from the wrong direction. "No, sir."

"No. I didn't think so." Harrow looked at the envelope in Tamsin's hand, at the wax seal with its H and its keel. "Curious thing, then, that this one should find you carrying it instead of waiting for one of your own. Don't lose it. Don't read it. Don't steam the seal, don't cut the edge — we'll know. Go on, then."

He walked off toward the offices, and Tamsin stood alone in the aisle between the crates with a letter in his hand and a question growing in his chest like weather coming over open water.

* * *

He told himself it was just an errand. He had run plenty. Coal to the weighmaster's office, documents to customs, once a sealed purse to a woman on Anchor Street whose face he still remembered and whose business he had wisely never asked about. Carrying things was what he was for. The company carried cargo; Tamsin was the smallest unit of cargo that could carry other cargo.

But an errand was coal. An errand was papers. An errand did not have a seal with a stranger's crest, or instructions about bleeding on a doorstep, or a head clerk asking questions about whether anyone had ever written to him — as if Harrow already knew the answer, as if the answer were part of the transaction, weighed and entered in some ledger.

He ate his midday bread walking, which broke no rule he knew of, and took the long way along the waterfront because it added minutes and he wanted them. The tide was coming in, the harbor filling like a lung. Out past the breakwater a brig was working in against the ebb, sails hard-set, and he watched her the way he always watched boats, with the diver's habit of reading what lay underneath: the trim, the drag in her line, whether she was happy or fighting herself. She was fighting herself. Bad ballast or bad bottom paint. He looked away.

Everything he owned was in his bag, back at the loft. He had almost brought it. He couldn't say why — only that when he'd picked it up that morning, his hands had done it without consulting him, the way a man's hands close on a rail when the deck tilts. Then he'd set it down again, embarrassed in the empty loft, and left it there, and locked nothing, because there was nothing to steal that mattered and nothing to leave that would be missed.

Sallow Lane began where the cobbles gave up and became packed mud. The ropewalk stretched away to his left, long and low, smelling of pitch. To the right, beyond a leaning fence strung with drying nets, he could see the yard: a gantry, a slipway, a shed with a sagging ridge line, wood shavings drifted against the door like snow that refused to melt. And rising above all of it, skeletal and magnificent, the ribs of a ship under repair — a hull opened up like a patient on a table, planks peeled back, caulking irons racked beside her.

A schooner. He knew her by sight if not by name; everyone in Corvane knew her, the vessel that had been sitting in that yard so long she'd become a landmark. Men said she'd never sail again. Men said a lot of things.

Smoke rose from the shed's chimney, thin and stubborn. Somebody was home.

Tamsin stood at the fence with the letter in his pocket and did not go in. This surprised him. His legs were willing; his legs had been willing all day. But his hand came up and rested on the fence rail, and he stayed there a moment in the smell of shavings and salt, looking at the sagging yard and the great bare ribs of the ship, feeling the letter press its small weight against his chest.

Worthless things don't get sealed, he thought. Harmless things don't get instructions.

He thought about the loft. The blanket. Two minutes flat, everything gathered and gone.

He unlatched the gate.
