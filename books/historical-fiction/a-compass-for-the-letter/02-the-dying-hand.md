## 2. The Dying Hand

The address took him to the wrong end of Corvane, the end the tide forgot.

Tamsin had delivered parcels to every quarter of the city — silk swatches to merchants' wives on Amberlark Row, oilcloth-wrapped documents to customs men who never tipped, crates of dubious weight to warehouses that smelled of opium and lies. He knew Corvane the way a rat knows a ship: by its runways and its rot. But he had never been sent past Saltmere Ditch, where the cobbling gave up entirely and the streets turned to packed mud that swallowed boot heels with a wet kiss.

The letter rode inside his shirt, against his sternum. He was aware of it the whole way — not heavy, exactly. A single sheet of paper in a sealed envelope weighed less than his mother's pearl. But it pressed on him like ballast.

*Deliver it personally. Do not read it. Do not linger.*

He'd done nothing else for six years but carry things, so he carried it. That was simple. What wasn't simple was the question that had kept pace with him all morning, matching him step for step through the fish market and across the ditch: *why does Meridian care whether a dying man gets his mail?*

Companies did not pay couriers to hurry for dying men. Companies paid couriers to hurry for money. Tamsin turned this over as he climbed the last rise, and found nothing underneath it but more questions.

The shipyard announced itself before he saw it: the smell of hot pitch and old oak, sharp as a struck match. Then the yard itself unfolded below him — a long slope of churned earth running down to a slipway, a shed with a sagging roof mended in three different generations of shingles, a fence made of ship parts instead of proper posts, and everywhere, everywhere, timber. Stacked planks silvered by weather. Curved ribs of some half-born hull. A forest that had given up being a forest and was learning to be something else.

And in the center of it all, propped against the slipway like a beached whale with delusions of grandeur, lay the Kestrel.

Tamsin stopped at the fence line and looked at her. He knew ships the way he knew people — by what they showed when they were tired. She was a brigantine, maybe ninety feet on the waterline, and she was showing exhaustion from her keel to her truck. Her paint had gone patchy. Her starboard rail bowed inward where something had hit her hard, once, years ago. Half her planking forward of the mainmast had been torn off and stacked neatly beside her, waiting for replacement that hadn't come.

She was beautiful anyway. That was the trouble with ships. They were beautiful right up until they killed you.

A dog of indeterminate breeding raised its head from a pile of sawdust, considered barking, decided against the effort, and put its head back down.

"Hello?" Tamsin's voice came out smaller than he intended. The yard seemed to swallow sound. "I'm looking for Bramwen Hale."

Silence answered first. Then, from inside the shed, a voice like gravel poured slowly.

"You found him. Come in before the draft finds you first."

* * *

The shed interior was dim and dense with the smell of shavings. Wood curls drifted ankle-deep, and hanging from the rafters were tools Tamsin couldn't name — long wooden planes with handles worn smooth as driftwood, adzes, augers, a saw so enormous it could have felled the tree it now hung above. Workbenches lined the walls, each one cluttered with half-finished things: a model hull pinned with pins, drawings covered in lines like handwriting only ships could read.

At the far bench sat an old man in a chair built too tall for him, so his boots swung an inch above the floor.

That image stuck in Tamsin and stayed there for years afterward: Bramwen Hale, master shipwright, dangling his boots like a boy, because his body had shrunk around his illness faster than his furniture could keep up.

He was thin the way driftwood is thin, bleached and honeycombed. His hands, though — his hands were enormous. Scarred knuckles, nails split and blackened at the edges, fingers thick as rope. Hands that had made everything in the room and most of the yard besides. They rested on the bench beside a handkerchief stained brown at the fold.

"Well." The old man's eyes found Tamsin and held on. They were pale gray, the color of fog over deep water. "Meridian sends me a courier. Haven't seen their colors down here in nine years. Thought they'd forgotten I existed."

"They haven't," Tamsin said. "Sir."

Hale laughed, which turned into coughing, which went on long enough that Tamsin's own chest began to hurt in sympathy. When the old man finally surfaced, he wiped his mouth with the stained handkerchief, folded it carefully so the stain faced inward, and set it down as though Tamsin hadn't seen it.

But Tamsin had seen. Blood. Not a fleck — a blot.

"I've got a letter," Tamsin said. He worked it free of his shirt and held it out. "I'm supposed to put it in your hand. Personally. And then I'm supposed to go back and tell them I did."

"And are you going to do what you're told?"

"I always do what I'm told." Tamsin heard his own voice and didn't like the taste of it. "It's cheaper than the alternative."

Something moved behind the fog-gray eyes. Recognition, maybe — one creature of narrow circumstances recognizing another. Hale took the letter. His huge hands made the envelope look like a playing card.

"That's a company seal," he said, turning it over. "They sealed a letter to me. In forty years of buying my ships, they never once sealed a letter. Sent clerks, sent inspectors, sent men to argue about invoices. Never sealed anything." He looked up. "Boy. Did they tell you what's in it?"

"No, sir."

"Do you want to know?"

"Yes," Tamsin said, before caution could catch up.

Hale smiled. It transformed his face briefly into something younger and much more stubborn. He slid his thumb under the seal and broke it.

Tamsin watched him read. Watched the old man's lips move once, twice. Watched his eyebrows climb, then draw together, then climb again. And then Bramwen Hale did something Tamsin would remember on his deathbed, whatever deathbed that turned out to be:

He threw back his head and laughed.

It was a real laugh, full-throated, delighted — the laugh of a man who has just heard the best joke of his life. It went on and on, and it cost him; halfway through it turned to coughing again and he pounded his own chest with that great fist, laughing and coughing and bleeding all at once, waving his free hand at Tamsin as if to say *don't help, don't you dare help, this is funny.*

"What—" Tamsin took a step forward despite himself. "Sir, should I fetch—"

"Sit down." Hale pointed at a sawhorse near the door. His voice was wrecked and ragged but still vibrating with amusement. "Sit down, boy. You're going to want to be sitting for this."

Tamsin did not sit. He stood where he was with his hands loose at his sides, the way you stand on a deck when the swell comes up strange and you don't know yet which way to brace.

Hale caught his breath in stages. Wiped his mouth again. Folded the handkerchief again. Then he held the letter up between two fingers and squinted at it like a man checking a measurement twice.

"It's a will," he said. "Or near enough. A declaration of inheritance, drawn legal by a solicitor on Cutter Street, witnessed by two names I don't know and paid for — paid for, mind — by the Meridian Trading Company."

"Inheritance," Tamsin repeated. The word meant nothing to him. Words like that belonged to other people's lives.

"My yard. My tools. My books. My debts, such as they aren't." Hale tapped the paper. "Everything I've got, signed over to whoever holds this workshop standing and seaworthy when the Kestrel sails. That's the condition. Written plain. The workshop stands, the heir inherits. The workshop falls—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost him another wince, "—and it goes to the company. All of it. Every plank."

"All right," Tamsin said slowly. "So who's the heir?"

Hale looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, long enough that the shed's silence filled up with the sound of the sea working at the seawall outside, long enough that Tamsin felt his own heartbeat start counting seconds.

"You," Hale said.

The word landed and did nothing at first. It just sat in the air between them, absurd, a coin dropped in the wrong country.

"That's not possible," Tamsin said. "You don't know me. I've never been here before in my life. I'm nobody. I'm an indentured courier with the Meridian Trading Company, third class, and my father's name isn't even written down anywhere official, and—" He stopped, because his voice had started climbing toward something and he didn't want to find out what. "There's been a mistake."

"There's been no mistake." Hale laid the letter flat on the bench and smoothed it with both hands, tenderly, the way you'd calm a horse. "Read it yourself. Second paragraph. There's your name, spelled out letter by letter. Tamsin Okafor. And beneath it, a sentence I asked the solicitor to write exactly so." He recited it without looking down, and Tamsin understood with a lurch that the old man knew it by heart already, had read it a dozen times before this delivery ever arrived: "*'I choose a stranger because strangers owe me nothing and I owe them nothing, and that is the only honest place to begin.'*"

"Why?" It came out cracked. "Why would you — why would anyone—"

"Because I watched you for five minutes through that window before you came in," Hale said, "and because I've been watching this yard die for three years and I know exactly what kind of hands can save it or lose it. Because the company thinks they've bought themselves a fire trap and an insurance payout, and I think—" the cough came again, shorter this time, angrier, "—I think I've bought myself one last fight. Sit down, boy. Please. My legs are older than the rest of me and arguing with the chair."

Tamsin sat on the sawhorse. His legs made the decision for him.

* * *

Outside, light shifted across the shed floor as clouds moved over the harbor, and in that shifting light Tamsin looked at the letter lying on the bench and felt the ground tilt under everything he knew about himself.

Six years. Six years of carrying other people's things, keeping his own life small enough to fit in one bag, learning that anything you loved could be taken by a wave or a ledger or a bad season, so you loved nothing, you carried nothing, you left nothing behind worth coming back for. That was the whole of his philosophy, learned in salt water at eleven years old, and it had kept him alive when bigger philosophies had drowned better men.

And now an old stranger with blood on his handkerchief wanted to hand him a shipyard.

"It doesn't make sense," he said finally. His voice sounded very far away. "Why me? Out of everyone in Corvane. Why a diver's son?"

"Who says diver's son?"

"My file says it. Company file. It's the only thing about me they thought worth writing down."

Hale nodded slowly, and there was something in his face now that hadn't been there before — something tired and sad and certain all at once. "My daughter died eight years ago. My sons died before that, both of them, sea takes what it likes. I've got no one. What I've got is a yard, and a ship that needs saving, and enemies who mean to burn both for profit if I let them." He said it plainly, without drama, reporting weather. "I could leave it all to the church. The church would sell it to Meridian within a month and stand respectfully by while it burned. Or I could leave it to an apprentice, and Meridian would buy him or break him inside a week. They know every man who's worked for me."

"So you picked someone they don't know."

"I picked someone they *carry.*" Hale's gray eyes glittered. "Every indentured soul in their books is invisible to them, boy. They write your wages, not your worth. You're a line item with legs. And a line item with legs walked into my yard today carrying the very letter that makes you my heir, and the company paid for the privilege." The laugh threatened again; he strangled it into a wheeze. "Do you see it yet? They needed the condition met or broken by a witness. Someone neutral. Someone *theirs.* So they sent the heir to deliver his own inheritance. I have never in fifty years of shipbuilding seen anything so perfectly rigged as that."

Tamsin stared at him. Somewhere under the shock, under the disbelief still ringing like a struck bell, something colder was beginning to form. A shape. An arithmetic.

"If the workshop burns," he said slowly, "it goes to them."

"Now you're doing sums."

"And if the ship fails—"

"If the Kestrel goes down, the insurance pays triple, the yard burns conveniently after, and Meridian collects on both ends and writes it off as tragedy." Hale picked up the handkerchief, then set it down again without using it, as if refusing it one more time. "They've been patient. Very patient. Waiting for me to die so the yard would fall to them legally. But I've been inconvenient. I keep breathing." Another smile, thinner now. "So they'll stop waiting."

The word *burn* hung in the shed. Tamsin found he believed it instantly, completely, the way you believe a storm forecast when your bones ache. He had worked for these people six years. He knew what they were.

And underneath the fear, unwelcome as a splinter, something else was happening. He looked at the tools on the walls. At the drawings of hulls, curved and confident. At the half-built model pinned to the bench like a promise. At the sagging shed and the silvered timber and the wounded ship on the slipway, beautiful and dying.

Nobody had ever offered him anything. People gave him orders, wages, packages, trouble. This was different. This was a door, standing open, into a room with his name on it — a name he'd never trusted, suddenly asked to hold the weight of a whole life's work.

It was insane. It was a trap dressed as a gift. It was probably both.

"How long," Tamsin heard himself ask, "until the Kestrel sails?"

"Company schedule says six weeks." Hale reached out and closed his huge hand around the letter, claiming it. "She'll need four months' honest work to be seaworthy, and I've got three apprentices, one good arm between us, and lungs that are leaving ahead of schedule." He looked up, and the fog-gray eyes fixed on Tamsin with terrible steadiness. "So. Courier. You've delivered the letter. Your obligation's finished — walk out that gate and none of this is yours, none of this touches you, and Meridian keeps paying your wages till the day the yard burns."

Tamsin said nothing.

"But if you stay," Hale said, "you stay as heir. And the first thing an heir learns in my yard is that we finish what we start." He pushed the letter an inch across the bench, toward Tamsin, like a man sliding a bet onto a table. "Well, boy? Which are you?"

Through the shed window, Tamsin could see the Kestrel leaning against the sky, patched and patient, waiting for an answer too.

His mother's pearl sat in his pocket, the only thing he'd ever kept. He thought about how it had no weight at all until you closed your hand around it.

"I need to think," he said.

Hale nodded, unsurprised, unbothered, already reaching for his drawings. "Think fast," he said. "The tide won't wait, and neither will the fire."

Tamsin didn't ask how the old man knew about the fire.

Somehow, that was the thing that scared him most.
