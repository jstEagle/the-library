## 3. Apprentices of Sawdust

The yard smelled like the inside of a living thing.

Tamsin had expected rot — the whole place sagged toward the waterline like a man leaning on a fence he'd stopped believing in — but instead there was sawdust and pine pitch, boiled linseed oil, the iron tang of caulking mallets. Bramwen Hale's workshop breathed. Tamsin stood in the gate with his single bag over his shoulder and let it fill his lungs, and hated how much he wanted to stay in that first breath.

"You're the letter boy."

A girl perched on the rail of the slipway, thin as a mast, arms crossed, watching him the way gulls watch a hand near bread. She was maybe seventeen, hair cropped close for work under a hull, forearms scarred white where hot pitch had caught her.

"Tamsin," he said.

"Wren." She didn't offer a hand. "He put you in the will. Word got around before you finished walking up the drive. So. What's your trade?"

"Inventory," he said, because *pearl diver's son* opened doors he'd rather keep shut.

"Inventory." Wren rolled the word around like a bad oyster. "We'll see about that."

She dropped off the rail and walked, and he followed because following was the only skill Meridian had ever taught him well.

She led him past the Kestrel, propped on the ways like a whale beached mid-sentence. Up close she was worse than Hale's proud talk suggested. Her planking bulged at the turn of the bilge in long, lazy curves — hogged, he'd learn to say later, though he knew the look already from watching ships come home wrong. Caulking had sprung along two seams wide enough to fit a coin. The rudder gudgeons were rust weeping orange down the transom like old wounds.

"She sails in nineteen days," Wren said.

Tamsin looked at the ship a long moment. "No, she doesn't."

Wren turned and looked at him properly then, for the first time. "That's what I said. Nobody listened to me either."

*

Hale was asleep in the loft above the workshop, propped on pillows, a leather-bound book open on his chest rising and falling shallowly. Wren didn't wake him. She walked Tamsin through the yard instead, naming people as they came into view.

The deaf caulkers worked in pairs by the hull, one swinging the mallet, one holding the iron, communicating in a language of glances and taps that needed no sound at all. They were brothers, Oren and Pell, gray-haired, enormous through the shoulders, and when Wren introduced Tamsin they studied him without a word, then went back to their seams.

"They came from the naval yard after an accident took their hearing," Wren said. "Company decided deaf men couldn't hear orders worth ignoring. Their loss is our gain — they caulk tighter than anyone on this coast."

At the far bench, a lean man with salt-gray sideburns was drawing lines on a slate with a ruler and no visible pleasure. "That's Sorrel," Wren said. "Don't ask about the *Meridian Star*."

"What happened with—"

"Don't. Ask."

Sorrel glanced up anyway, gave Tamsin one flat measuring look, and returned to his lines.

"And that," Wren finished, nodding at a boy of twelve hauling shavings to the burner, "is Duck. Nobody remembers his real name. He doesn't answer to it anyway."

The whole yard was four apprentices, two half-deaf caulkers, a dying master, and a ship that could not sail. Tamsin set his bag down against the workshop wall, and realized as he did it that he'd done it automatically, the way you set something down in your own house, and he picked it right back up.

Wren noticed. She noticed everything. She said nothing, which was somehow worse.

*

They tested him for three days.

It started simply: tools left just out of reach, instructions given once and never repeated, a maul handle deliberately cracked so it would break on his third swing. When it broke, Oren looked over, expressionless, and Tamsin held up the broken haft, then pointed at the crack line — *here, see, not my swing* — and mimed checking a tool before use. Oren grunted. That evening a sound maul appeared at Tamsin's station.

On the second day they sent him aloft to check the Kestrel's standing rigging, which was a joke, because she had almost none of it, and he came down the ratlines fast and clean, and Pell watched him descend with his head tilted like a bird considering a worm.

On the third day, Sorrel finally spoke to him.

"Read this." He shoved the slate across the bench. Lines drawn on it, a hull section, numbers along the curve.

"Displacement?" Tamsin guessed.

"Draft marks. Tell me what's wrong."

Tamsin studied it. He wasn't a navigator; he was a diver's son who'd grown up reading water the way other children read street signs. But wood he understood, because floating things had been his whole childhood — which hulls sat right, which sat wrong, which carried their weight like a burden and which wore it like clothing.

"The numbers are fine," he said. "The ship isn't. She's carrying her weight too high forward. You've shifted ballast to correct trim, but the frames forward are sprung — see the gap here—" he touched the slate, "—so correcting the loading just hides it. She'll ship water over the bow in any kind of sea."

Silence. Then Sorrel took the slate back slowly.

"Hale said you could read water and wood alike," he said. "I told him he was being romantic." He picked up his chalk again. "Forward frames. We start tomorrow."

That night, working late planing oak for frame timbers, Tamsin caught himself explaining to Duck how to read the grain before making a cut, and Duck listening, actually listening, and something happened in Tamsin's chest that he didn't have a name for and distrusted immediately.

*

*Wren had been at the yard two years when the letter came, and she had learned to count the yard's heartbeats the way you count the ticks of a clock you can't afford to stop.*

*Four of them now, counting the master. Five, counting the new one.*

*She'd voted — silently, internally — against the new one. Indentured company men brought company eyes, and the last thing a dying shipyard needed was a clerk's errand boy memorizing inventory for somebody else's ledger. She'd watched Tamsin the way you watch a tide pool: waiting to see if the thing inside was beautiful or venomous.*

*Then he'd corrected Sorrel's draft numbers in three sentences, and Sorrel had redrawn the forward section without argument, and Sorrel hadn't redrawn anything without argument since the Meridian Star.*

*Now the new one worked like a man trying to earn his supper twice before anyone asked him to pay. He arrived first, left last, ate standing up, and slept — Wren was fairly sure — in the workshop loft rather than walk back to whatever boarding house owned him. He owned nothing. He carried everything he owned in one canvas bag, and she'd seen him move that bag three times in two days to keep it within arm's reach while he worked.*

*Wren knew what that meant. She'd done it herself her first month.*

*What she didn't know yet was whether he'd set it down eventually, or carry it out the gate some night and be gone before the kettle boiled. Both kinds of people came through shipyards. The ones who stayed were rarely the ones you bet on.*

*She climbed the loft ladder that night to fetch wax for the seams and found him asleep at the workbench, cheek on a half-finished pattern, hands still curled loose around the marking knife. The bag was under the bench, tucked against the wall, positioned — she checked — between his body and the door.*

*Old habits. She left the wax and didn't wake him, but on the way down she thought:* maybe both kinds start out looking like that.

*

By the fifth day Tamsin had a working map of the disaster.

He stood at the bow with Sorrel and Wren, chalk in hand, tallying on the Kestrel's stem what had to happen before she sailed: six frame timbers sistered or replaced, two garboard seams recalked, rudder irons reforged, standing rigging spliced and set, ballast re-trimmed, topsides caulked and payed.

"Nineteen days," Sorrel said.

"Six men," Wren said.

"Five and a half," Tamsin said, nodding toward the loft, where Hale's coughing had kept its own rhythm all morning. "And one of us is twelve."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing Tamsin was still getting used to — nobody lied to each other here. At Meridian, every number had been dressed in its best clothes. Here the numbers came to the table naked, and everyone looked at them anyway.

"We do the frames first," Sorrel said. "If the frames hold, everything else is repairable. If they don't, nothing else matters."

"Steam box," Tamsin said. "We can bend green oak if we get the steam right. I've watched riggers do it."

"You've *watched*," Wren said.

"I've also done most things I've only watched." He heard himself say it and was surprised. It was the first boastful thing he'd said in years, maybe ever, and it came out because for once someone might actually need it to be true.

Sorrel looked at him for a long second. "Then you're on the steam box."

*

That evening Hale came down to the yard for the first time since the letter.

He made it as far as the shade under the Kestrel's counter and sat on an overturned keg, wrapped in a coat too big for what remained of him, and watched the five of them work. He didn't instruct. He just watched, and occasionally coughed into a cloth Tamsin tried not to look at.

When the light went gold and low, he called Tamsin over.

"Well?" Hale said. "Tell me the truth. Everyone else does."

"She can't make the sailing date."

"I know that."

"And even if she could—" Tamsin stopped.

"Say it."

"Even if she could, I don't understand why the date exists. A maiden voyage scheduled like that — no shakedown, no trials, cargo booked before the frames were fixed. It's not optimism. Companies aren't optimists." Tamsin looked at the old man. "Someone wants her at sea on that day regardless of whether she's ready."

Hale smiled, and it was the saddest thing Tamsin had seen since his mother's face went under.

"There's my diver's son," Hale said softly. "Nineteen days, boy. Whatever they're planning, it happens on the water, and it happens with my name on the build. So you'd best figure out what Meridian wants to happen — before it does."

Above them, the lamplight came on in the workshop, and Wren's silhouette passed the window, and Tamsin felt the strange double pull of the last week: the warmth below him, the cold question above.

He had come here carrying one bag. Now he was starting to carry other things — a crew, a deadline, a dead man's trust — and every one of them heavier than the bag.

In the morning, he told himself, he would go find the company clerk and start pulling at the money thread. Ships that sink on schedule are always insured first.

But that night he stayed at the bench, planing oak by lamplight with Duck asleep in the shavings pile beside him, and for the first time in nine years, Tamsin Okafor fell asleep without knowing exactly where his bag was.
