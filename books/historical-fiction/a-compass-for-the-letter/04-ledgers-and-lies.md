## 4. Ledgers and Lies

Wren Ashby had three rules for other people's desks: never open the top drawer, never touch anything with wax on it, and never, under any circumstance, read a letter addressed to someone else.

She was breaking all three before the church bells finished striking nine.

It wasn't thievery. She wanted that understood, even if only by the gulls wheeling over the yard. Mr. Hale had asked her to fetch the *Kestrel*'s repair invoices from the office chest so he could argue numbers with Meridian's surveyor, and the invoices weren't where invoices belonged. They were buried under everything else — which meant, in the logic of clerks everywhere, that somebody had hidden them there.

So she dug.

The chest smelled of tar and old paper. Wren sorted with her elbows on the desk edge, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its braid in pale wisps, building neat stacks the way she built everything: planking estimates here, rigging costs there, a folder of caulker wages she'd have to ask about because the sums looked thin enough to insult a deaf man's honesty. And beneath it all, flat as a pressed flower, a ledger bound in green calfskin that did not belong to Bramwen Hale at all.

The embossing on the cover said MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY. The spine said *Vessel Accounts — Kestrel*.

Wren looked over her shoulder. The yard was already loud with morning — mallets, the hiss of the steaming box, old Pieto swearing at a plank in the wordless way of men who couldn't hear themselves do it. Nobody watching. She opened the ledger anyway, because rule-breaking, once started, is a tide and not a wave.

The first pages were dull as ballast. Insurance valuation, hull and cargo. Wren read the figure twice.

Eight hundred corones.

That was wrong. She knew ship values the way she knew grain weights, because Papa had spent eleven years teaching her before the Company took his license and their name with it. A topsail schooner of the *Kestrel*'s tonnage, fifteen years old, repaired but not rebuilt — six hundred at a generous market, four hundred if a buyer knew what he was looking at. Eight hundred was fantasy pricing. Eight hundred was a number you wrote when you wanted a number.

She turned the page.

There it was, in fresh ink still sharp enough to smudge if she dragged her thumb across it — she didn't, quite — the policy amount. Two thousand, four hundred corones.

Triple. Insured for triple the vessel's worth, through a syndicate Wren didn't recognize, brokered by Meridian itself.

Her mouth went dry. People insured ships against loss. That was commerce. But nobody sane paid premiums on triple valuation unless they believed — quietly, privately, with ink still wet — that the loss was coming. You didn't bet like that on luck. You bet like that on certainty.

She turned faster now, hunting dates. Launch date, maiden voyage, delivery contract. Her finger stopped on the schedule line and stayed there, pressing hard enough to whiten the nail.

*Departure: 14th inst., morning tide.*

Fourteen days. The *Kestrel* needed her whole stern reframed, two garboard strakes replaced, and every seam recorked from keel to rail. Hale had told the surveyor, truthfully, that it was eight weeks' work with a full crew and fair weather. They had fourteen days and seven hands.

Unless the schedule wasn't meant to be met.

Wren sat down slowly on the chest's lid, the green ledger open in her lap, and let the arithmetic finish itself the way it always did, cold and complete. If the ship sailed on the fourteenth unready, she'd founder inside a week — everyone in the harbor knew her lines were wrong, even the gulls seemed to know it. If she foundered, the syndicate collected two thousand four hundred on an eight-hundred-ruin. And if the ship burned first — if the yard that held her unrepaired hull burned, insurance being what it was about premises as well as vessels —

No. That was too far. That was Papa talking, eleven years bitter, seeing conspiracy in every clerk's cough.

She looked at the ledger again. It hadn't been lost or misfiled. It had been *placed*, tucked under Hale's own papers where any search of the yard would find it — where it would sit like a lit fuse among Hale's belongings. Evidence, pre-positioned. Or bait.

The bells struck the quarter hour. Wren closed the ledger, held it a moment, then wrapped it in her apron and went to find Tamsin.

* * *

She found him on the slipway, hip-deep in the *Kestrel*'s shadow, passing oakum up to Pieto while the old caulker drove seams with the rhythm of a man beating time for a slow funeral. Tamsin worked like water moved — no wasted motion, weight always balanced, ready to shift. Three weeks in the yard and he still carried himself like a man mid-step between doors.

"Tamsin." She beckoned. When he climbed down, wiping his hands, she pulled him behind the timber shed where the sawdust lay deep enough to swallow footsteps, and unwrapped the ledger on an upturned crate. "Read this."

He read fast. Faster than she expected — she kept forgetting about him, that he'd learned letters somewhere between the pearl beds and the counting house, that the company had taught him just enough to be useful and not enough to be dangerous. His face while reading told her nothing. That was the thing about Tamsin Okafor: he could keep a surface calm on water that was churning underneath, and she'd grown up around sailors, so she knew exactly how rare and how costly that kind of stillness was.

"Triple," he said at last.

"Triple."

"And she sails on the fourteenth." He closed the ledger without slamming it. Even his anger was economical. "She can't sail on the fourteenth."

"No," Wren said. "But she can burn before it."

The words hung in the sawdust air. Tamsin didn't flinch, which somehow made it worse; it meant he'd already been thinking it. She watched him arrive at the rest of the arithmetic, watched his eyes move as though following something below the surface — the letter he'd delivered, the dying man's laughter, the inheritance contingent on the workshop standing.

"The letter," he said quietly. "Meridian sent me here carrying that letter. Me, specifically. Their runner, their indentured boy, hand-picked to deliver Hale's heirship into his hands."

"So?"

"So why bother?" He turned the crate's edge over with one thumb, restless. "If they mean to take the yard, why deliver the thing at all? Why give Hale an heir? An heir means witnesses, claims, trouble."

Wren had been turning that same wheel all morning, and she gave him what she'd caught in its teeth. "Because heirs can be blamed. Think about it. Hale dies, the yard burns, the ship goes down or goes up — and who's the stranger who appeared three weeks before the fire, bearing letters, sleeping in the loft, taking over the dead man's business?" She let it land. "You're not the witness, Tamsin. You're the confession. They wrote you into the story so the magistrates would have someone to arrest."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere beyond the shed, Pieto's mallet kept its patient count, beat after beat, the sound of people building things anyway.

Tamsin looked down at the ledger, then at his own hands — diver's hands, scarred at the knuckle, steady as mooring posts. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something flat and careful, a voice for underwater.

"They chose me because I carry nothing. No family anyone can find, no name worth defending, no one to raise a fuss when I hang. A boy with empty pockets makes a fine scapegoat." A pause. "Or a fine courier. Either way, they used me. The errand was the trap. The letter was bait — for Hale, for me, for whoever bit."

"Bait," Wren agreed softly. Then, because she was her father's daughter and could not leave a sum unbalanced: "Bait works both ways."

He looked up.

"You're holding their ledger," she said. "Their real numbers. Triple valuation, impossible deadline, a syndicate that shouldn't exist. If the yard burns, this page proves what they expected. If the ship sails broken and drowns, this page proves they scheduled it." She tapped the green cover once, gently, the way you'd test ice. "They think they planted evidence in our chest. Instead we've got theirs."

Tamsin was quiet a moment longer. Then something shifted in his face — not relief, nothing so warm. Resolution settling over dread like ballast over cargo.

"Then we don't run," he said. "And we don't just fix her fast enough to sail. We make her seaworthy, truly seaworthy, past arguing — and we get this ledger somewhere safe, somewhere Meridian's fire can't reach it."

"The customs house has a records vault—"

"Tomorrow. Today—" He picked up the ledger, weighed it in his hands as if judging a pearl, and handed it back to her. "Today you hide it better than they hid it. And tonight I want to know who walks the yard after dark. Because if I'm right about the fourteenth, someone's going to come make sure we miss it."

Wren took the ledger against her chest, feeling its small hard weight, so much heavier than paper should be. All morning she'd been grateful for the misfiled invoice that led her here. Now gratitude was draining out of her like water through open seams, and what filled the space behind it was colder and clearer.

They were going to burn the yard with them in it.

Beyond the shed, the mallet stopped. In the new quiet, Tamsin tilted his head toward the yard gate, and Wren followed his gaze — to a man leaning against the fence post in a company coat, watching them, watching the *Kestrel*, making no effort at all to hide that he was watching.

"He's been there since the bells," Tasmın said. "Counting us."

The man touched his hat brim, smiled like a bill presented, and turned away up Rope Walk.

"Fourteen days," Wren said.

"Thirteen now," said Tamsin. "Come on. We've work to finish and a fire to prevent, and only the nights left to learn who's holding the torch."
