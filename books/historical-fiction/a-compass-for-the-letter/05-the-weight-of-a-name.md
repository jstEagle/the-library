## 5. The Weight of a Name

The clerk's name was Pellworthy, and he kept his pens in a row sharper than knives.

Tamsin had watched him for three years without ever once being watched back. That was the trick of being cargo — you learned the men who moved you. Pellworthy inked his figures with a small flourish at the end of each entry, as if signing his name to every crate that passed. He wore his collar high and his opinions higher, and he never looked at the indentured boys except to count them.

So Tamsin did what he had taught himself to do on the docks: he waited.

Three days after Wren put the insurance ledger in his hands — triple value, impossible date, a ship meant to die — Tamsin came to the Meridian counting house at the hour when the clerks went to supper and only the lamps and the ledger-men remained. He carried a delivery receipt that needed signing. It was even true. There was a delivery. It just wasn't the one that mattered.

Pellworthy sat alone behind the long desk, the green lamp throwing his shadow up the wall like something hung. He looked up when Tamsin set the paper down, and for the first time in three years, he actually saw him.

"You," Pellworthy said. "The Okafor boy."

Not *boy*. Not *you there*. The Okafor boy. A surname attached to a face, filed and remembered.

"You knew my name before I gave it," Tamsin said.

"I know all the names in this company." Pellworthy took the receipt, signed it with his little flourish, and slid it back. "That's what a ledger is. Everything in its place."

"Then tell me where I was placed." Tamsin kept his voice level, the way his mother had taught him to keep his breath when the water pressed down. "You handed me a letter six weeks ago and told me to deliver it personal. Not to the harbormaster. Not to the yard foreman. To Bramwen Hale, dying, in person. Why me?"

Something moved behind Pellworthy's eyes — not guilt. Calculation deciding whether guilt was profitable.

"Because you were errand-ranked and expendable," he said. "Isn't that enough?"

"No." Tamsin laid the folded copy of the insurance schedule on the desk between them, smooth as laying out a dead man's chart. "Because expendable boys don't get remembered by name. Because old Hale read that letter and laughed and said it named me heir, and I've been turning over ever since how a shipwright who never met me knew I existed. Somebody told him about me. And I think somebody knew exactly what that letter would do once it was opened."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the harbor wind.

Pellworthy leaned back, and Tamsin watched the man decide — watched the scales tip the way they always tipped in Corvane, toward whatever kept the company safe.

"Sit down, Okafor."

He didn't sit. He'd spent his whole life ready to run; sitting made you heavy.

"Hale came here fourteen months ago," Pellworthy said. "Old, sick, loud about it. Wanted an advance against the Kestrel's refit. The company granted it — on terms. When she sailed, the yard and the debt would come to us if anything... happened." He smiled thinly. "Hale wasn't stupid. He heard the shape of the deal under the words. So he asked for one concession. One name of his choosing, written into the will as heir — contingent on the workshop standing when the Kestrel cleared the bar. He thought a living workshop would make us protect it. Sentiment as collateral. Very touching."

"And he chose me."

"He chose a diver's son from the Point." Pellworthy's pen tapped twice against the blotter. "He'd seen you work. Down at the east slip, two winters back — you went into the water to clear a fouled rudder and came up telling them which plank was sprung before anyone could see it. Read water and wood alike, he said. Said a man who could do both would fight for a yard the way nobody else would. He wanted someone who'd defend the place out of sheer offended competence."

Tamsin stood very still.

"So he didn't pick me because he knew me," he said slowly. "He picked me because I was useful. Another tool. You gave me the letter because you knew what it would do too — string a hook through me and reel."

"The company gave you nothing you didn't take," Pellworthy said. "You took the bait. Men usually do, when the bait is a future."

* * *

He walked out without another word, but his body walked while something else stayed behind in that green-lit room, sinking.

*Chosen or used.* The question went down into him like a dropped line, and the line kept paying out past any depth he'd sounded before, down to where the cold lived.

His mother had been chosen too. Chosen by the water, she used to say, laughing, before she went under the last time. Sela Okafor, who could hold her breath longer than any man on the Point and dive deeper than the pearl beds had any right to ask. Who taught him that breath was a thing you rationed like coin, that fear was a thing you swallowed like salt, that names were things other people gave you and you were under no obligation to keep.

She had gone down on a grey morning when he was nine, working a deep bed off the shoal where the company boats wouldn't bother. He'd been on the skiff, minding the line. He remembered everything in pieces: the rope going taut and then slack in a way ropes shouldn't. The silence after he called her name. The water so clear he could see her below him, twenty feet down, tangled in something — net, weed, he never learned — her hair moving like ink in current, her hands working calmly at whatever held her, because she was Sela Okafor and she did not panic, she rationed.

He went in. Of course he went in. Nine years old, lungs like hers, and he went down and down and the cold closed over his head like a lid and he reached her and grabbed her arm and pulled and she looked at him —

That was the piece that lived under his ribs ever since. She looked at him. And she shook her head. Once. Small. Almost gentle.

*Let go.*

And he had. His hands had opened on their own, obeying her the way they'd always obeyed her, and he'd kicked for the light while she stayed below with her calm working hands, and he'd broken the surface screaming air he couldn't use, and the men on the point pulled him out half-drowned and she never came up at all.

For years he'd told himself she'd shaken her head at the net, at the tangle, at the water. Not at him. Never at him.

But some nights, in the barracks dark, the memory turned over and showed its other face: *she sent you away to save you, or she sent you away because she was tired, and either way you let go, you let go, you carry nothing because carrying things means they can be taken, and she was taken, and you opened your hands.*

He stopped at the harbor rail now, in the wind, and understood — with the clean terrible clarity of deep water — why Hale had chosen him.

Not just because he could read wood. Because he was a boy who had already let go of everything once, and a boy like that would either guard a thing with his life or abandon it without a look, and Bramwen Hale had bet his life's work on guessing which.

And the company had bet the same, the other direction. They hadn't given him the letter as an honor. They'd given it as a test of weight. If he walked away, the yard stood empty, the fire burned cheap and clean. If he stayed, he became the fool who drowned defending collateral — proof that sentiment sinks, a story to laugh over in the counting house.

Either way, they'd measured him. Either way, he was cargo with a price marked.

Tamsin gripped the rail until the barnacles bit his palms and let the grief come up the way his mother taught him breath should — slow, controlled, in its own time. Grief for her, twenty years cold and never once warm. Grief for the boy on the skiff. Grief for the foolish old shipwright who had reached out of his dying to grab a stranger's name because he believed names meant something.

Maybe they did. Maybe that was the whole war, right there — Meridian believing nothing meant anything beyond its ledger line, and one coughing man betting everything that a pearl diver's son would refuse to believe it too.

His hands opened on the rail. Then, deliberately — for the first time since the skiff, since the grey morning, since the shake of her head — he closed them again.

He would stay. Not because the letter hooked him. Not because Hale chose him. But because *he* chose the yard, the misfits, the half-built ship, the whole heavy impossible weight of it — and choice, he finally understood, was the one thing no ledger could carry off.

He turned from the harbor and walked back toward the lights of the yard, and found Wren waiting at the gate, lantern high, face white as sailcloth.

"Tamsin." Her breath smoked in the cold. "You need to come see the keel. There's fresh auger-marks under the waterline — and they weren't there yesterday."
