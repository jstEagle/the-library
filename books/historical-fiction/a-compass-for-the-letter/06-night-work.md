## 6. Night Work

* * *

**WREN**

The lamps hung from the yard's beams like a constellation someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up, and Wren thought that was the only honest way to work — by light you carried yourself, in a dark that didn't pretend to be anything else.

She drove her last seam bolt home and sat back on her heels, palms burning. Below her, in the Kestrel's belly, the deaf caulkers were still going. She could feel them through the keel — a rhythm in the wood, two men working as one pulse. Old Ferro had taught them all that trick: stop listening with your ears, listen with your hands.

The yard had changed since Tamsin Okafor came. Wren couldn't have said exactly how. He was quiet, kept his belongings in one bag like a man perpetually three steps from leaving, and he measured everything twice — planks, wages, people. But he'd looked at the Kestrel's sternpost the way no one else did, reading the grain the way he said his mother used to read water before a dive. And when Bramwen Hale coughed blood into his rag and waved them back to work, it was Tamsin who'd stood there longest, as if memorizing something.

Nine days until the tide that could carry her out. The company's schedule gave them four.

"More oakum," she signed to little Piet below, and Piet held up an empty bucket, and she went to fetch it because that was what the yard was now — everyone fetching for everyone, nobody standing still.

It was near midnight when she noticed the lamp by the slipway's north stanchion had gone out. It hadn't guttered. Lamps in this yard were trimmed every hour; that was Ferro's law. A lamp that went out all at once was a lamp someone had pinched.

Wren stood very still among the shavings.

Then she went to find Tamsin.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

Tamsin had learned to sleep the way a diver sleeps — shallow, one ear open, weight balanced for a fast rise. So when Wren touched his shoulder in the loft where he'd bedded down between the timber racks, he was awake before her hand landed.

He read her signs in the lamplight spilling up through the floorboards. *North stanchion. Lamp out. Wrong.*

He was already reaching for his boots.

They crossed the yard apart from each other, the way he'd seen crabs move along a tidal flat — never straight at a thing, always angled, always ready to reverse. The fog had come off the harbor thick as wool, and it ate sound and shape alike. Tamsin counted what he knew: six lamps burning where seven should be. The apprentices accounted for, five of them visible at their stations. Ferro asleep above the workshop, which meant the old man would wake angry and useful if shouted at.

And then, at the north stanchion, movement.

A man crouched at the base of the Kestrel's cradle, working at the planking where hull met slipway — the joint that took the whole ship's weight when she launched. He had a auger with a shortened bit, and he was drilling not through the wood but *along* it, boring out the hidden grain the way a termite does, so the damage wouldn't show until the day it mattered. Tamsin knew the trick. His father had cursed men who did it to pearl luggers, selling boats with rotten hearts.

He put a hand on Wren's arm: *stay*. Then he moved.

Not toward the man. Toward the man's escape route. There was only one — the gap in the fence behind the tar shed, the gap everyone pretended not to know about because the night watch used it for their own comings and goings. Tamsin leaned against the shed wall in the fog and waited, and let the man finish his work.

It cost him something. Every instinct he owned screamed to grab the man now, shake the truth out of him, name him to the yard. But grabbing got you one man's confession, and confessions died in courtrooms. Following got you the hand above the man's head.

The saboteur packed his tools, rose, glanced around — and walked straight past Tamsin, close enough that Tamsin smelled the tar and the cheap gin beneath it. Then, at the fence gap, the man stopped and looked back at the Kestrel, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that Tamsin recognized from years of watching men leave things behind.

Relief. The man was glad to be done with it.

Tamsin let him go, and slipped after him into the fog.

* * *

**FERRO**

Ferro Hale woke at half past one because forty years of shipbuilding had left him with a carpenter's ear for wrongness — a board out of true, a saw cutting dull, a silence where footsteps should be. He lay in his cot above the workshop and listened to his father's yard breathing, and something in its breath was off.

He got up, lit a stub of candle, and went down to check the accounts chest first. Locked. Good. Then the tool wall. All present. Then he stood in the doorway and looked at the great shadow of the Kestrel on her cradle, and his eye ran down her lines the way his father's eye had taught him, stem to stern, and stopped at the north stanchion.

He limped out. Knelt. Put his palm flat against the planking near the cradle joint, and felt — nothing. That was the trouble. A sound joint has a faint give under the hand, a living flex. This was dead wood over hollow air.

His hands shook, and not from age.

He was still kneeling there when Wren found him, signing fast enough that even he could follow the gist: *Tamsin followed one out. Watch the ship.*

Ferro looked up at the fog-bound dark, at the boy-shaped absence moving away from everything Ferro had built, and understood that the diver's son had done the thing Ferro himself had been too old and too honest to think of.

"Well," he said to the empty yard. "Damn."

He went to wake the caulkers. If men wanted to drill holes in the dark, the yard would spend the rest of this night filling them.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

The man led him down through the dockside streets, and Tamsin followed the way he'd once followed his mother through current — by reading what the water left behind. A wet bootprint drying on stone. The gin smell lingering at a corner. The man walked without looking back, which told Tamsin he'd done this route many times, and that confidence was the loosest plank in his whole construction.

Twice Tamsin nearly lost him in the fish-market crowd of early haulers and gull-cries, and both times the man did something stupid and human — coughed, swore at a cart — and gave himself back.

He ended at the Meridian Trading Company's counting annex on Harrow Street. Not the main office where clerks stamped bills of lading in daylight. The annex, where the company kept the business it didn't stamp. The man knocked a pattern on the side door — two, pause, three — and was let in by someone carrying a shaded lantern.

Tamsin stood across the street in the lee of a bollard, and made himself count to two hundred, because men who left too soon missed the second visitor, and there was always a second visitor.

There was. A broad man in a good coat, sea-boots polished, who let himself in with a key rather than a knock. Tamsin knew the walk before he knew the face. He'd watched it cross the Meridian warehouse floor a hundred times, measuring cargo, measuring men.

Quill. The company's chief surveyor. The man whose signature sat on the inspection report declaring the Kestrel fit to sail.

So it wasn't a hired torch-and-run. It was paper. It was signatures. It was the inspection itself bent like a rib.

The side door opened again an hour later and Quill came out alone, and paused under the streetlamp to strike a match, and in that small flame his face was calm and satisfied and entirely certain that no one in Corvane was watching him.

Tamsin pressed himself deeper into the bollard's shadow and felt something settle in him that he didn't have a name for yet. Not anger — anger was hot and spent itself quick. This was colder and it lasted. It was the feeling of a diver at the bottom of a long dive, lungs burning, eyes fixed on the one glinting shape worth rising for.

They'd chosen him for the letter because they thought a man with one bag would take money over a grave of sawdust. They'd thought weightlessness was his price.

But his mother hadn't drowned clutching pearls. She'd drowned coming up from a dive with her hands full of shells for the children on their street, because that was the kind of fool she'd been, and the kind he was, though he'd spent nine years pretending otherwise.

He turned toward the harbor, toward the lamplit yard, toward the people who were at this moment filling holes in the dark on faith alone, and began to plan what he would say and whom he would say it to and, above all, what he would ask for — because Quill would come to him soon, they always did, and when the offer came, Tamsin intended to be holding something better than a bag.

He intended to be holding evidence.

Behind him, the annex door opened one more time, and the man who stepped out wore the lamplight differently — hunched, hurried, wiping his hands as if they were dirty. Tamsin slid into the fog before his face could show.

But not before his voice did.

*"Well?"* the man called softly after Quill, up the street. *"Will the diver's boy bite?"*

And the answer drifted back through the fog like smoke, patient and sure:

*"Everyone bites, Mr. Vane. You just have to know what they're hungry for."*

Tamsin stood very still in the dark, one hand flat against cold stone, and committed both voices to memory — because now he knew there were two hands above the saboteur's head, and one of them belonged to a man he'd delivered a letter for.

The other belonged to the clerk who'd handed it to him.
