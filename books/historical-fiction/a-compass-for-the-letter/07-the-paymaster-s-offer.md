## 7. The Paymaster's Offer

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy too young to know what he was carrying. A folded card, cream stock, the Meridian crest embossed in gold that caught the first gray light like a fish scale. *Mr. Okafor will attend Mr. Vess at the company offices, eight o'clock sharp.*

No please. No request. Meridian didn't request.

I stood in the doorway of my room above the cooper's shop — a rented cot, a rented window, four weeks now of sleeping where the yard could see me if I leaned out — and read the card three times. Below, Corvane was waking. Gulls fighting over fish guts on the quay. The harbor bar wearing its morning fog like a borrowed coat.

Eight o'clock. I had time to walk to the yard first.

The Kestrel sat on her ways like a patient woman, half-skinned and showing her ribs. Wren was already up there somewhere — I could hear his mallet before I could see him, that steady knock-knock-knock he made even when his hands were bleeding, because Wren said silence let the doubt in. The caulkers had worked through the night. You could smell the oakum and pitch from fifty feet off, sweet and tarred, the smell I was beginning to understand meant *alive*.

I didn't go up. I stood at the gate with my hands in my pockets and looked at what four weeks of other people's labor had built, and I thought: *they're going to ask me to leave this.*

Because Vess wouldn't have summoned me for anything else. Not after the man I'd followed through the dark streets to the counting house door. Not after I'd stood in that alley with sawdust still under my nails and watched a Meridian clerk pay out silver to men who smelled of pitch and matches.

* * *

The offices of the Meridian Trading Company occupied the finest block on Cordage Street, all white stone and tall windows, built to look down on everything the harbor produced and pretend it hadn't come from there. The clerk at the desk knew my name before I gave it. That frightened me more than anything else that morning.

"Mr. Vess will see you."

Vess kept a corner office with a view of the water. He was smaller than I expected — I'd built him large in my head, all these weeks, a giant made of ledgers — and he had the soft hands of a man who'd never held a tool heavier than a pen. He poured two cups of coffee without asking if I wanted one, and set one in front of me like we were friends.

"Tamsin Okafor." He said it slowly, tasting it. "Interesting name. Okafor. Your mother's people?"

"My father's, maybe." I didn't touch the coffee. "I never met him."

"And your mother?"

"Drowned. When I was nine."

Something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy, I decided later. Calculation checking itself against a ledger. "Yes," he said. "So the records say."

He let that sit between us. *So the records say.* As if my mother's death were an entry someone might have falsified, and he was being generous not to pursue it.

"I'll be brief, since you're a working man." He opened a drawer — unlocked, unhurried — and laid a document on the blotter between us. One page. I knew the format instantly. I'd been indentured since I was twelve; I knew the look of those papers the way sailors know weather. My name at the top. Meridian's seal at the bottom. And across the middle, in fresh ink: *Discharged. All obligations void.*

"Your papers," Vess said. "Free and clear. Signed this morning. You walk out of here a man who belongs to no one."

The words did something strange to my chest. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I've got left to sell. For eleven years I had been cargo. Inventory. An entry in a ledger next to pearl-shell and copra and salt beef. Every port I'd ever seen, I'd seen as property. Every bed I'd slept in, I'd slept in on sufferance. And here was a page of paper that would make me weightless.

"You want something for it," I said.

"Naturally." He smiled. "We want you to take a holiday. Go south — I hear Port Sallow is pleasant this time of year. Fishing boats always need hands. Start over. New name, if you like. Men do it all the time in this trade." He slid the paper an inch closer. "The Kestrel sails or doesn't sail, burns or doesn't burn — none of it will concern you. You'll be a free man on open water, which is more than most divers' sons ever get."

"The yard burns anyway," I said. "Even if I'm gone."

"The yard's fate is the yard's fate." He spread his hands. "Fires happen. Old timber, dry season, careless apprentices. Tragic. But ships are insured against tragedy, and so are shipwrights — well. Some shipwrights. Hale's policy lapsed last month, I'm afraid. Sloppy bookkeeping on his part. The workshop itself is worth nothing to anyone but sentiment." His voice went gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "You've known loss, Mr. Okafor. You know how quickly things can be taken. Why tie yourself to something that's already ash?"

I looked at the paper. Just paper. Cream stock, black ink. Eleven years of my life weighed less than that sheet, and they were offering to hand it back.

*Take it,* said a voice I knew very well. The voice that had kept me alive since I was nine years old, since the day the sea took my mother and I learned that everything you love becomes ballast you can't afford. *Take it. Carry nothing. Be nothing. Nothing can burn if you own nothing. Nothing can drown.*

I reached out.

My hand stopped above the page. Not because of some grand feeling. Because of a smell — oakum and pitch, carried in on my own sleeves, on my own skin, soaked into me over four weeks of lamplight and shared bread. Because I could hear, faintly, from the harbor below the window, Wren's mallet. Knock-knock-knock. Still going. Still building something everyone sensible had already given up on.

"When does the fire come?" I asked.

Vess's smile didn't move but everything behind it did. "There's no fire planned, Mr. Okafor. There's an unfortunate accident that no one can prevent."

"When."

"That's not your concern anymore. It won't be, the moment you sign." He turned the paper toward me fully and set a pen beside it. "You have until tomorrow evening. After that, the offer expires, and you return to being what you were — a diver's son carrying pearls for a company that owns his nights as well as his days."

I stood. I left the coffee untouched, the paper untouched, the pen lying in its little pool of lamplight.

"Keep it warm," I said, and walked out.

My legs didn't shake until the street. Then they shook badly, and I had to hold a post outside a chandler's shop and breathe like I'd been taught to breathe underwater: slow, even, spending nothing.

Because here is the truth, and I'll write it plain: I almost took it. God forgive me, I stood in that office with freedom lying flat on the blotter and my hand six inches from it, and the only thing that stopped me was a sound. A mallet. Knock-knock-knock. Four weeks of belonging, weighing against eleven years of surviving, and I couldn't tell you even now which side was heavier. I only know which side I chose, and I only know it because my feet carried me back toward the yard instead of the docks.

* * *

I packed anyway.

That night, alone in my room above the cooper's shop, I took down the bag. The old canvas one, salt-stiffened, patched at the corners with sailcloth I'd scavenged in three different ports. Everything I owned fit inside it in ten minutes. That had always been the point. Ten minutes and I was gone, no trail, no debts, no one left holding anything of mine.

Mother's pearl, wrapped in wool. The diver's knife, my father's, though I'd stopped telling myself that story years ago. Two shirts. A coil of good line. The letter — Hale's letter, the one that named me heir, that I still hadn't shown anyone entire. My whole life, packed by lamplight, light enough to run with.

I sat on the cot and looked at it and tried to feel what I'd felt in Vess's office. Weightlessness. The clean pull of the road.

Instead I kept seeing the yard at night. The lamps strung along the ways like low stars. Old Pell, deaf as a post, laughing at jokes he read off people's lips. The navigator — Marlowe, disgraced, drunk twice a week and brilliant the other five — drawing fairwater lines in spilled flour because he couldn't afford paper. Wren, asleep sitting up against the Kestrel's hull with a mallet still in his hand, guarding her in his dreams.

And Bramwen Hale, coughing blood into a rag, looking at me with those river-gray eyes and saying: *The yard stands, boy, or the name means nothing. Names are earned by what you keep standing, not what you carry away.*

He'd chosen me. A diver's son who could read water and wood alike. Chosen me knowing exactly what I was — a runner, a carrier of portable things — and bet that I could learn to stay.

Or used me. That was the other possibility, the one that came back every night like tide. Used me as bait, as witness, as a name on a document that would make the company's theft harder. I didn't know yet. Maybe I never would.

But I knew this: if I walked, I'd spend the rest of my life lighter than air and emptier than a gutted hull, diving for strangers' pearls in strangers' water, and every night I'd hear that mallet going without me. Knock-knock-knock. Building something. Staying.

I picked up the bag. I put it down again.

Then I heard it — footsteps in the yard below, too many, moving wrong. Not the shuffle of tired men knocking off work. This was purposeful, quick, spreading out along the fence line the way I'd once watched saboteurs spread along the Kestrel's shadow.

I went to the window.

Down in the dark, at the edge of the slipway, a lantern flared — once, twice, three times. Signal fires answering each other along the harbor road.

They weren't waiting for the deadline. They weren't waiting for me at all.

The fire was coming tonight.
