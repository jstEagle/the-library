## 8. Fire on the Slipway

### Tamsin

They came early.

That was the first thing Tamsin understood, standing in the yard's doorway with a mug of chicory going cold in his hand. Not at the deadline Meridian had circled on their calendars, not when the Kestrel sat finished and waiting for the morning tide. They came now — two nights early, while half the hull's caulking was still curing and the yard was full of shavings and pitch and every flammable thing a shipwright owned.

He smelled it before he saw it. Tar smoke where no lamp burned. Then a bloom of orange low against the black, down by the drying sheds, and a second one catching at the sawdust pile like a struck match in a hayloft.

"Fire!" His voice cracked on it. "FIRE!"

The yard woke in pieces. Wren's shutters banging open above the loft. Old Josiah the caulker hauling his deaf brother out of the tar shed by the collar. The disgraced navigator, Pell, stumbling into the moonlight with his trousers over his nightshirt and his sextant clutched to his chest like a child.

Tamsin dropped the mug and ran.

The drying sheds were already lost — that was his read of it, one long breath, the way he'd once read water before a dive. You couldn't save everything. You chose what lived and you committed. The sheds were kindling; let them go. The workshop with the deed chest and all of Hale's patterns was stone-footed timber, still savable if they got ahead of the wind. And the *Kestrel* —

God. The *Kestrel*, fresh-caulked, her hull slathered in pitch, moored at the slipway not thirty feet from a burning sawdust pile with the harbor wind pushing the fire straight toward her.

"Wren!" He caught the boy's arm as he flew past. "Bucket lines to the workshop. Wet everything. Pell — the harbor chain, wake the watchhouse, tell them Meridian men set this, tell them tonight."

"And you?"

Tamsin was already running for the slipway. "I'm going to get her loose."

* * *

### Wren

Wren had never fought anything bigger than himself before, and fire did not fight fair. It moved like something thinking. It found the gaps between boards. It climbed.

He stood hip-deep in the bucket line, passing pails hand to hand from the rain cistern, and watched sparks come off the burning sheds like a flock of evil birds. One landed on his sleeve and he beat it out against his ribs without breaking rhythm, because if he broke rhythm the line broke, and if the line broke the workshop went, and in the workshop was the chest.

The chest. Hale's deed, Hale's letter, the paper that said Tamsin Okafor belonged to something.

"FASTER," Josiah bellowed, though half his line couldn't hear him, and it didn't matter because they could feel the urgency coming up the chain of hands like current through wire.

A beam collapsed in the sheds and the whole sky went orange. Wren felt the heat on his face from forty feet away, and understood, in a small cold place under his fear, that whoever set this hadn't meant to give them a chance at all.

Then a spark must have gone somewhere wrong, because he looked up at the loft window — his window, over the workshop — and saw flame licking out of it.

"The roof!" someone screamed. "The roof's going!"

And Wren was moving before he decided anything, out of the line, through the workshop door into a room gone thick and brown with smoke, coughing, eyes streaming, feeling along the wall by touch because the world had shrunk to nothing but heat and dark. The chest was under the workbench. Three steps. He counted them like a dive. One — his shoulder hit the bench. Two — his hands closed on iron handles hot enough to sear. Three —

He hauled it up and the ceiling groaned overhead, and a knot of burning lath came down across his back like a brand.

He didn't remember screaming. He remembered the weight of the chest, and the door being very far away, and then Josiah's arms around him, dragging him backward into air, the chest still locked in his grip so hard they had to pry his fingers open on the cobblestones.

His shirt was burnt through. The skin underneath was a red ruin from shoulder to spine, and he lay there gasping smoke while Josiah wept over him, and Wren held up the deed chest with both shaking hands like an offering.

"It's safe," he rasped. "It's safe."

* * *

### Tamsin

The *Kestrel* wanted to live. That was what Tamsin thought, waist-deep in black harbor water at the end of her mooring line, knife in his teeth. Every ship he'd ever known had a temperament, and this one had been fighting her own death since the day they'd started on her — shedding sabotage planks, taking caulk, holding.

But she was tied to the slipway, and the slipway was becoming a wall of fire.

The mooring cleat on shore was already ringed in flame. Above the waterline her pitch was beginning to soften, bubble, darken. Ten minutes, maybe less, and she'd catch like a lamp.

He couldn't untie the shore cleat — couldn't reach it through the fire. So it would have to be the ship's side: the ring bolt low on her hull, below the waterline, fouled with weed and crusted paint, seized tight as sin. Someone would have to go under and cut her free at the waterline itself, blind, in October harbor water.

Someone who could hold his breath.

His mother had taught him in another life, off another coast, counting down his limits in bubbles. *The sea takes what panics. It gives to the patient.* He spat the knife into his hand, filled his lungs the way she'd shown him — belly first, then chest, then one more sip at the top — and went under.

The cold hit like a blow. He'd forgotten how much colder north water was than the diving grounds of his childhood; it squeezed his skull, made his ears scream. He followed the hull down by touch, hand over hand along barnacled planking, feeling the fresh caulk under his fingers — their work, three weeks of their lives — until he found the ring bolt, furred with growth, the mooring line's eye seized around it with corrosion and time.

He set the knife's edge against the shank of line and sawed.

Lungs beginning to burn. Saw. The line was hemp over an iron core of old cable, and it did not want to part. His vision pulsed at the edges. He thought — absurdly, underwater, drowning-adjacent — of his mother's pearl hanging above a workbench now, hung there just this morning by his own hands, and of the bag packed by the door that he had not yet unpacked.

Saw. Saw. The core gave with a snap he felt more than heard.

The line fell away. Through the murk he saw the *Kestrel* shift, sigh, begin to drift — but drifting wasn't enough. Drift took her along the burning quay, broadside, a lantern of pitch. She needed to go *out*, to deep water, and she needed someone steering.

He surfaced gasping on her far side, hauled himself up her trailing line hand over hand, hooked an elbow over her low stern rail, and screamed into the smoke at the figures on the slipway:

"Pell! Get the launch! We push her off the seaward quarter — NOW!"

* * *

### Pell

Pell had been navigating other men's ships for twenty years and blamed for most of them, and he had never once in that time done anything that mattered until tonight. He got the yard's leaky launch crewed with two caulkers and a boy, and they pulled for the drifting *Kestrel* through falling ash, and Tamsin — soaked, lips blue, half-dead — was already aboard, throwing his weight on the sweep.

"She'll answer," Tamsin shouted. "She wants to go."

She did. That was the uncanny thing. With the fires roaring astern and her own pitch skin blistering, the *Kestrel* came round to the offshore breeze like a horse smelling home, and Pell stood at her tiller with no instruments, no chart, nothing but the wind on his ruined face and the glow of the burning yard behind him, and steered her out.

Deep water. Past the harbor stakes. Far enough that when the slipway fully went, when the sheds and half the yard went up in a column of orange that turned midnight to noon, she sat in the dark sea like a patient animal, scorched along her rail but unburnt, riding the swells.

They let go anchor in twelve feet of clean water, and nobody spoke. The boy was crying. The caulkers were signing to each other, fast and fluid, and Pell realized they were praying.

On the dock, tiny with distance, the bucket line still fought — and held. The workshop stood, blackened, smoking, standing.

"We don't know," Tamsin said quietly, beside him at the rail. His voice was ash. "We don't know yet whether any of it held. Whether they'll believe us. Whether Meridian doesn't just burn the story down with everything else."

Pell watched the firelight play on the water and thought about all the voyages he'd plotted that had never been believed, either.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we sail her past the bar in front of the whole harbor, and we make them believe."

Below them, at anchor, the *Kestrel* creaked and settled — alive, saved by sacrifice with no promise attached, held in deep water like a held breath.

And on the quay, through the thinning smoke, a man in a company coat stood watching the yard that refused to burn — and Tamsin knew him, and knew whose coin was in his pocket, and knew that by morning tide, one of them would hang for this.

It would not be him.
