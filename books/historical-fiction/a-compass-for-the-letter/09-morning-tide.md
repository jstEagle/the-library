## 9. Morning Tide

I did not sleep. None of us did.

We sat in the yard through the smallest hours, black-faced and blistered, passing around a kettle of Wren's terrible chicory while the smoke lifted off the slipway in gray ribbons. The fire had taken the drying shed, the fence line, half the timber rack, and a good bite out of the western wall. It had not taken the workshop. It had not taken the *Kestrel*.

At some point I realized I was still holding the diver's knife, my knuckles white around it, and made myself put it back in my belt like a man who was not waiting for the world to end again.

Wren slept sitting up against the deed chest, arms folded across the lid like a mother over a cradle. She'd come out of the smoke with her eyebrows singed to nothing and the chest strap still gripped in one fist, coughing so hard the caulkers had pounded her back in turns until she could breathe again. When she woke she said only, "Got it," and went back to sleep with her cheek on the wood.

The east went pale. Then gold. And with the light came the tide.

* * *

I heard her before I saw her — the creak of fresh caulking taking the water, the groan of a hull remembering its shape. We'd worked four nights on her, and the last twelve hours straight, and there were places where my hands moved without consulting me at all. But she floated. Sat high and honest at her moorings, patched planking dark against the older oak like scars that hadn't decided their color yet.

Old Corvin, the disgraced navigator who hadn't touched a chart in nine years, came down the ladder with his collar singed and his eyes clear for the first time since I'd known him. He looked at the *Kestrel* the way other men look at a horizon.

"She'll make the bar," he said. "If you're asking."

"Meridian says she'll break apart on it," I said. "That's the story they've been selling since before dawn. Fire, panic, unseaworthy vessel lost with all hands. They sent a runner over an hour ago to tell the harbormaster's office."

Corvin smiled, and it changed his whole ruined face. "Then let's go ruin the story."

* * *

By seven o'clock the word had gone through the harbor the way word does — by gulls, by gossip, by the particular electricity of a crowd that smells blood. Half of Corvane seemed to have found reason to be on the seawall or in small boats or leaning from the tavern balconies along the quay. They'd all heard about the fire. Now they wanted to see what burned.

Meridian had sent men too. I saw the clerk — Mr. Aldous Vane, neat as a ledger line even at seven in the morning — standing near the customs house with two of the company's own officers, talking low and fast, arranging faces into sorrow. He'd offered me my freedom once, in a room that smelled of ink. He'd offered me my own signature in exchange for walking away. I wondered if he knew yet that the saboteur I'd caught had led me straight to him, that I'd spent the evening before the fire copying names out of the yard's night log by lamplight while the crew worked above me.

He didn't know. You could see it in how comfortable he looked.

The *Kestrel* warped off her moorings at half past seven with Corvin at her helm and six of us aboard, because a ship sailing to prove a point should not look empty. Wren stayed ashore with the apprentices, standing in the black scar of the gate with her arms crossed, chin up. The deaf caulkers stood either side of her. Behind them the whole ragged company of Hale's yard lined the broken fence, and I thought: that is what Meridian never counted on. They priced the lumber and the insurance and the debt. They never once priced the people.

The tide took us out past the mole, past the fish-fleets coming in with the morning catch, and every boat we passed turned to watch. Word travels faster than sail. By the time we cleared the inner harbor, the fishing fleet had fallen in behind us at a respectful distance — twenty little hulls flying their nets dry, come to see whether Meridian Trading Company told the truth about anything at all.

The bar lay ahead, that long white stitching of broken water where the harbor meets the open sea, and beyond it the swell came in heavy and green. My mother had read water like scripture. She used to say the sea lies twice — once about how deep it is, once about how kind — but never about whether a thing will hold together. I stood at the bow with my hand on the forestay and felt the hull speak up through the wood: tight, tight, tight.

"Stand by," Corvin called, and the deck came alive under our feet, and we went at the bar on the flooding tide exactly as he drew it.

She rose on the first swell and dropped, and held. Rose on the second and dropped, and held. On the third — the big one, the one where Meridian's story said she'd open like a dropped plate — she climbed it clean, hung a moment against the sky with water sheeting off her forefoot, and came down the far side into open sea as steady as a woman walking home.

The fishing fleet cheered. Actually cheered, hats thrown, a sound I have never heard from fishermen, who are not a cheering people.

Corvin put her about and brought us back over the bar slower, showing her off, letting every glass on the seawall see her pass twice. As we came up the channel toward the customs wharf, a launch put out from it flying the revenue pennant, and standing in the bow was Customs Officer Maren Quill — a narrow, unhurried woman who had watched the whole business from the seawall with the expression of somebody doing arithmetic.

She boarded us at the mooring, walked the deck stem to stern, ran her thumb along the new planking, and asked Corvin for the repair manifest. He gave her the true one. Then she looked at me.

"You're the Okafor boy. The one who carried Hale's letter."

"Tamsin." I said my name the way I'd been learning to say it all week, all the way down. "Tamsin Hale, now, if the deed holds."

Something moved behind her eyes. "Mr. Vane filed a report this morning at six o'clock. Before the ship sailed. Describing her loss in considerable detail." She let that sit in the salt air. "A man cannot honestly report the loss of a vessel that is, at the moment of reporting, visible from his window."

"No, ma'am," I said.

"I'll want your account. The apprentice girl's account. The night log you kept." She glanced back toward the shore, at the scorched yard, at the crowd thickening along the quay, at Vane still standing by the customs house door with his face beginning, very slowly, to understand. "And I'll want the name of the man you caught with the auger in the planking."

"Garrick Sowle. Meridian rigger, ten years on their books. He's being kept somewhere warm and fed, because Wren wouldn't let us tie him to a post, and he'll talk if you ask him about the paymaster's signature."

"And whose signature would that be?"

"Vane's," I said. "In the company book. I copied it myself."

Officer Quill closed her little notebook. For just a moment, the arithmetic left her face and something warmer replaced it. "Hale's yard has stood forty years," she said. "It would be a shame to lose it to a filing error and a box of matches. Go home, Mr. Hale. I'll be at your gate by noon."

* * *

We came ashore to a crowd that parted for us, and I will be honest: I have never in my life walked through a crowd that parted. In Corvane a diver's son walks the edges, carries nothing, looks at no one. But the fishwives were telling anyone who'd listen that the *Kestrel* had crossed the bar like a bride, and someone had already chalked LIARS in letters three feet tall across the boarded window of the Meridian counting house, and when I came up the lane to the scorched gate of the yard, Wren broke from the others and hit me at a dead run, hugging hard enough to crack a rib, smelling of smoke and chicory.

"You didn't sink," she said into my shoulder.

"We aim to please."

"The customs woman stopped by already. She looked at the burn line and then she looked at the fence post where Sowle was tied and she laughed. Laughed! Like a normal person!" She pulled back, holding me at arm's length, and her face did something complicated. "There's a man here to see you. Been waiting since you sailed. Says he knew Bramwen Hale thirty years."

I followed her inside, past the black streaks on the stones, past the apprentices hauling charred timber into a pile to be sorted later. A man sat on an upturned keel by the workshop door — old, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once been enormous, with hands folded on his knee like a judge. He stood when he saw me, and I saw Bramwen in the set of his jaw, dead these two weeks and still somehow standing in front of me in another man's bones.

"So," the man said. "You're the one he wrote to." He reached into his coat and brought out a paper, folded twice, soft with handling. "Bram sent this to me in the spring, when he first learned what Meridian meant to do. Told me if anything happened to him, I was to bring it to whoever stood in the yard when the smoke cleared." His eyes went over the scorched walls, the exhausted crew, the chest under Wren's arm. "Looks like that's you, boy."

"What is it?"

"That," he said, "is the part Bramwen didn't put in the letter you carried. That's everything Meridian did to earn this fire — names, dates, ships, twenty years of it. Enough to hang them in court or bury them in debt, whichever comes first." He held it out. "Question is whether you've got the stomach to use it. Because they'll know by tonight that the ship sailed and the story died, and men like Aldous Vane do not go down alone."

I took the paper. It weighed almost nothing.

Behind me the harbor bell rang eight times, and out past the bar, small and white in the morning glare, the *Kestrel* stood out for open sea — proof, floating, that some things hold.
