## 10. A Compass for the Letter

The inquiry lasted nineteen days, and Tamsin counted every one of them the way he'd once counted tides.

He did not attend. There was no reason to. The customs officer — Halloran, a gray man with gray hands who had watched the *Kestrel* cross the bar that morning with tears he pretended were wind — came to the yard himself, twice, to take testimony. He sat on an upturned sawhorse in the middle of Bramwen Hale's workshop, which was now Tamsin's workshop, though Tamsin still could not say the words even in his own head, and asked questions in a voice like sandpaper drawn slowly across oak.

Tell me about the letter. Tell me about the insurance. Tell me what you found in the water under the slipway at three in the morning with a knife between your teeth.

Tamsin told him. All of it, in order, leaving nothing out — not the clerk Voss's smooth offer of his indenture papers, free and clear, if he walked away; not the saboteur he'd followed through the dark to the paymaster's door; not the burn scars on his palms from hauling the Kestrel's lines while the yard glowed orange behind him. Wren sat beside him for the second testimony, her knee bouncing, correcting him when he undersold himself. *He held his breath four minutes,* she said. *I counted.* Halloran wrote everything down in a hand so small it looked like it was trying to disappear.

On the nineteenth day, word came up from the harbor on the lips of a fish-peddler who traded gossip as freely as mackerel: the Meridian Trading Company had folded its ledgers and fled its offices in the night, its directors scattering like cockroaches under a lifted stone. The insurance syndicate had refused payment. Halloran had produced sworn testimony from six witnesses, including a disgraced navigator who once worked for Meridian herself and knew exactly how their books were cooked. There would be arrests. There would be, the peddler said gleefully, a hearing at the Exchange, and everyone in Corvane intended to attend and eat roasted chestnuts while important men ruined themselves publicly.

And there was one more thing. Halloran had sent a packet to the yard, marked urgent, sealed with the customs house wax.

Wren brought it up the hill at a dead run, waving it over her head like a captured flag.

* * *

Tamsin waited until evening to open it, when the apprentices had gone home to their lodgings and the yard had settled into its familiar dusk sounds — the tick of cooling timber, the gulls arguing over the harbor, old Dedric the deaf caulker humming tunelessly to himself two bays down, packing oakum into a fishing skiff because he could not bear idle hands any more than Tamsin could.

The packet contained two documents.

The first was a copy of the inquiry's conclusion, brief and brutal. *The vessel Kestrel, seaworthy and properly crewed, completed passage over the harbor bar in conditions her owners' own surveyors had certified as unsurvivable. The undersigned finds no basis for any claim of accident, and every basis for suspicion of deliberate fraud. The Meridian Trading Company is hereby remanded to the Crown inquiry. Signed, R. Halloran, Principal Customs Officer, Port of Corvane.*

Beneath it, in Halloran's disappearing handwriting: *Your name carried weight in that room, boy. Weight is not always a burden.*

The second document was Tamsin's indenture papers.

Seven years old, the parchment soft and foxed at the corners now. *Tamsin Okafor, orphan, minor, bound to the Meridian Trading Company for a term of ten years or until majority plus service, in consideration of board, lodging, and instruction in maritime trades.* His mother's mark was on it — not a signature; she'd never learned to write — but a small pressed circle where she'd thumbed the wax. A pearl-diver's thumbprint, ridged like a shell.

Below it, stamped across the whole face of the contract in red ink large enough to be read across a room:

**VOIDED — TERM TERMINATED BY ORDER OF THE CROWN INQUIRY, PORT OF CORVANE.**

Tamsin stood in the doorway of the workshop for a long time, holding it, letting the last light of the day fall across the paper.

Ten years. He'd been bound for ten years, and they had ended not with freedom purchased or bargained for, but simply — declared. Struck through. As if the whole architecture of his life, the careful arithmetic of carrying only what could be lifted, had been a sum someone else had finally gotten around to checking, and found false.

He was free.

He set the paper down on the workbench, and his hands were shaking, and he did not know why.

* * *

He had owned one bag for eleven years. Canvas, salt-stiffened, its strap mended twice with fishing line. Everything he possessed fit inside it with room to spare, and that room had always been the point — the slack space that meant he could be gone in ninety seconds, over the yard fence, down to the water, vanished before anyone thought to miss him. His mother's pearl in its oilcloth wrap. The diver's knife. Two shirts, a coil of good cord, a whetstone, forty-one coins in a knotted sock. A dead man's letter, unburnable, unanswerable.

That night, after Dedric went home and the lamps along the slipway guttered out one by one, Tamsin carried the bag into the workshop and set it on the workbench under the hanging lantern.

He untied the strap.

It felt like opening something that should have stayed shut — a hull breach, a sealed hold. Eleven years of habit screamed at him. *Leave it packed. You never know. You never, ever know.* But he had watched a ship built by dying hands cross a bar she was never meant to survive, and he had learned something in the water under her that night, four minutes into a breath-hold that should have killed him: there is a moment past the fear when the body stops fighting and simply does what it was made to do. His hands knew how to unpack this bag. He had simply never let them.

He laid everything out on the bench in a row, like cargo inventoried for manifest.

Two shirts. Cord. Whetstone. The knife, its handle worn to the shape of his grip. Forty-one coins. The letter, softened at its folds now from handling. And last, the oilcloth bundle, which he unwrapped with the care of a man defusing something.

His mother's pearl.

Small, imperfect, gray-pink, worth almost nothing on any market in Corvane — a diver's pearl, pulled from a shallow bed on a working day, kept not for value but because it was the first good one she'd ever brought up and she'd promised it to her son. She had died three years later with her lungs full of harbor water and her hands empty, and the company had given Tamsin her thumbprint on an indenture contract and called it fair trade.

He looked around the workshop. Hale's tools still hung on the wall in their careful rows — adzes and augers, planes sharpened to a whisper, the long saws racked like instruments. Hale had owned all of this and none of it had ever left the building, not once in forty years, and Tamsin had understood that once as weakness. Now he picked a nail out of the tin on the bench, held the pearl up to the lantern light, and drove the nail into the main beam above the workbench, right where Hale used to hang the plumb line.

He hung the pearl from it on a loop of cord.

It turned slowly in the lamplight, gray and small and luminous, throwing a tiny warped reflection across the ceiling like a second moon. Above the bench. Where he would see it every morning. Where it would watch him work.

"You're home," he said to it, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it.

* * *

The deed was on the drafting table, where it had waited since the night Hale died — actually waited, Tamsin realized now, in a drawer Hale had shown no one, beneath a false bottom, next to a will written in a steady hand despite the blood the shipwright had been coughing up onto everything else. Hale had known he was dying for months. Hale had also known what Meridian intended for the Kestrel, because a man who builds ships learns to smell rot in a hull before anyone else does. The letter had not been sentiment. It had been strategy. A dying man's last launch, setting out on the only tide he had left.

But the will required one more thing. Not a mark. Not a thumbprint, the company's currency, the diver's currency, the mark of someone who cannot sign and therefore cannot refuse. It required a name. Written. In full. By the hand of the heir.

Wren had hunted down the town's only scrivener and made him explain the letters one by one until Tamsin could form them without shame. They had practiced on scrap pine shavings with a carpenter's pencil, night after night during the repair work — *T*, tall as a mast; *a*, round as a hull section; the double *m* like waves following each other in. Wren had been a merciless instructor. *Again. You're rushing the k. Again.*

Now he dipped Hale's pen in ink and drew the paper close.

*Tamsin Okafor Hale.*

Okafor — his mother's name, the name she'd carried from the islands, the name the company had never bothered to record because recording it would have acknowledged she mattered. Hale — not inherited, not owed. Chosen. Taken the way you take a tool from a dead man's wall: with respect, and with intent to use it.

He signed, and blew on the ink, and set the pen down, and it was done. The deed said what it said. The workshop stood, scorched along its western eaves, its slipway black, half its timber smoke-darkened but sound — standing because a deaf caulker and a runaway apprentice and a disgraced navigator and a boy who owned one bag had fought for it with wet blankets and buckets and bare scorched hands.

Home was not a thing you could carry lightly or leave behind in ninety seconds. Home was the weight itself. And he had spent eleven years learning to survive by carrying nothing, and it had taken him one fire to learn that some things are worth drowning for.

Dedric appeared in the doorway at some point — Tamsin hadn't heard him, never would hear him, and had long since stopped being startled. The old caulker looked at the pearl swinging above the bench, then at the signed deed, then at Tamsin. He tapped the doorframe twice, their oldest signal, the one that meant *good*, and grinned with his three remaining teeth, and jerked his chin toward the harbor.

Tamsin followed him out onto the slipway.

The *Kestrel* was back at anchor in the outer roads, riding easy, her repaired hull sitting high and honest in the water. She'd taken a charter — a coastal trading run, legitimate, contracted through the customs house itself now that Halloran held her papers. Tomorrow she'd load. Next week she'd sail north.

And beyond her, further out, past the bar, another sail was coming in with the evening breeze — a small coaster flying no colors Tamsin recognized, running quiet and deep-laden, the way ships run when they're carrying something they don't want examined.

Halloran's note had included a postscript Tamsin hadn't mentioned to anyone yet. *One item unresolved. Meridian's books list a second vessel insured under the same fraudulent schedule. She left Corvane before the collapse. If you learn anything of a ship called the Pale Mercy, the Crown would be grateful.*

Tamsin watched the unnamed coaster slide toward the anchorage, deep in the water, wrong somehow, and felt the old instinct wake in him — the diver's instinct, the reading of what moves beneath a surface.

"Well," he said to Dedric, "we're going to need more oakum."
