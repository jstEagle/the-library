## 2. Bridge Out

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most bureaucratic of days. Nothing that happens on a Tuesday is ever good news dressed as an adventure; it is always good news dressed as a form. The county engineer nailed his placard to the post at the foot of the bridge with four nails and the particular satisfaction of a man who has been authorized to ruin someone's autumn.

I read it twice, because reading things twice is my profession.

NOTICE OF CLOSURE — VALLEY CROSSING. By order of the County Commission, the seasonal bridge over the Alder River will close to all traffic effective October the fourteenth, pending emergency repairs to the western piling. Repairs will continue through the winter months. The crossing will reopen at such time as the river permits. Travelers are advised to complete their business on this side of the water before the stated date.

October the fourteenth was eleven days away. The bridge did not ordinarily close until the middle of December, when the ice made it suicidal, and even then there was a fortnight of foolhardy traffic after that, farmers gambling their wagons against their necks. Two months early. Emergency repairs to a piling that had stood, as far as anyone in the valley could remember, since before anyone in the valley could remember.

I drove the notice home in my head the way I drove everything else — carefully, without commentary, arriving before I was expected.

* * *

Miss Calloway was in her study, which she called the writing room when she wished to flatter herself and the study when she wished to intimidate visitors, and since the only visitor we had that season was a man come to sell us lamp oil, it was mostly the study. She sat at the desk with her spectacles pushed up into her gray hair, drafting something by hand first, which she only did when the letter mattered or when she was angry, and often both.

"The bridge closes on the fourteenth," I said from the doorway. I had learned long ago not to soften news for Edith Calloway; she took softening as an insult, the way other women took spilled tea.

She did not look up. "Closes."

"Two months early. Emergency repairs. Western piling." I recited it the way she dictated to me, flat and complete. "River's rising. They're advising everyone to finish their business on this side before then."

"Whose business?"

"Ours, presumably. Anyone's. The wording is admirably vague."

Now she looked up, and I saw her do the arithmetic behind her eyes — the same arithmetic I'd already done, though I suspected our sums came out differently. Mine ran to flour and lamp oil and whether Marta's stove wood would last. Hers, I would learn later, ran to people.

"They mean to leave us here," she said. Not alarmed. Almost pleased, the way she was pleased whenever an institution revealed its indifference in writing. "The county. They've decided a handful of households above the river are cheaper to forget than a piling is to fix properly."

"It's a temporary closure."

"Everything is temporary, Dominic, until it isn't." She pulled her spectacles down onto her nose and regarded me over them. "How deep is the pantry?"

"I haven't counted. I'll count."

"Count everything. Flour by the pound. Oil by the quart. Wood by the cord. If we are to be forgotten, I should like to be forgotten accurately." She returned to her page, then stopped, pen hovering. "And send word to the wagon company. I want to know what they'll still carry across before the fourteenth, and what they'll charge for the privilege."

I wrote it down because that is what my hands were for. What my hands were not yet for, though neither of us knew it, was anything else.

* * *

Marta counted better than either of us. She counted in her head, standing in the cold cellar among the crocks, and she counted aloud only what she wanted heard, which is a skill I admired without yet understanding that I would need it myself before the winter was out.

"Flour, two hundred pounds if the miller honors the order. He won't honor all of it. Sugar less than Miss Edith thinks. Apples—" she paused at the bins, and something crossed her face that I mistook at the time for arithmetic and understood later for grief. "Apples we have. More apples than God intended anyone to eat."

The orchard had gone strange that year. Blight in the east rows, fruit dropping half-ripened, the whole south slope smelling of fermentation by September's end. A dying orchard is a particular kind of wealth — abundance rotting in place — and we stood in the cellar that afternoon surrounded by exactly that: crates of fruit nobody wanted, in a house full of food nobody could reach.

"How long can we last?" I asked. "If the thaw comes late."

Marta wiped her hands on her apron with great finality, as though the apron itself were responsible for the answer. "If the thaw comes late, we last until it doesn't." She looked at me. "You should ask her again about leaving. She listens to you more than she admits."

"She doesn't listen to me. She listens to herself and mistakes me for the echo."

"Then be a louder echo."

I have been called many things in my life, but never that, and I confess the word lodged somewhere under my ribs where I kept the small sharp objects.

* * *

Edith refused to leave. This should surprise no one who knew her, but at the time I still harbored the naive belief that self-preservation operated on everyone eventually, the way gravity operates on dropped crockery.

The wagon company sent back word — carried across the river by a boy on a mule, since apparently even our telegrams now required livestock — that they would make one final supply run on the thirteenth, and thereafter nothing until the repairs concluded, likely spring. They offered passage out for persons as well as goods, at a rate Edith called extortionate and paid without blinking, because Edith's relationship with money was that of a general to ammunition: spent freely, hoarded strategically, never discussed.

"You'll take the berth," she told me in the study that evening. Not asked. Told, in the voice she used for dictation, which brooked no emendations.

"I don't need—"

"Don't interrupt a sentence you haven't heard the end of. You'll take the berth on the thirteenth, go to Millbrook, stay with my cousin Adela — you remember Adela, the one who disapproves of me — and handle the correspondence from there. The bank letters, the lawyer, the suffrage committee. All of it crosses the river more safely in your head than in any mailbag, and you know it."

It was true. Half the reason Edith kept me was that my memory was better than the mails and more discreet than either. Letters got lost. Letters got opened. Dominic Marchetti got cold, wet, and mildly carsick, but she arrived intact, along with everything she carried between her ears.

"And you?" I said.

"I," she said, "am staying."

"Through the flood season. In a house cut off by a rising river, with a blighted orchard, and—" I almost said something else. I didn't know yet what the something else was. The barn still held nothing but apples and mice. "With whatever winter does to the roads."

"Dominic." She set down her pen and gave me the full weight of her attention, which was considerable, having been exercised on judges and aldermen and once, famously, a bishop. "I have spent forty years being told when to sit down and when to leave the room. The county posts a notice and expects me to obey it the way the county expects obedience from everyone it considers furniture. Well. I am somebody's furniture too, am I not? Yours. You drive me. You read for me. And I am informing my furniture that we are not going."

There it was, laid out plain: the philosophy we shared, spoken aloud for the first time, and it sounded different in her mouth than it had ever sounded in mine. In my mouth, being furniture was a comfort — safe, unseen, outside the weather of other people's history. In hers it was an accusation aimed at everyone else.

"I'm not your furniture," I said. It came out smaller than I intended.

"No," she agreed pleasantly, taking up her pen again. "You're much more useful. Furniture can't take dictation."

* * *

The river rose the way bad news rises — gradually enough to ignore, then suddenly enough to drown in. By the ninth of October the water had climbed past the second stake the county had driven into the near bank, and the current had gone brown and muscular, hauling branches downstream at a pace that made the bridge pilings look less like architecture and more like prayer.

I walked down to look at it on the evening of the tenth, because I had developed the habit of checking the river the way other people check the sky. The ferryman was pulling his boat up onto the mud with the air of a man closing shop forever. He saw me watching and called across the distance:

"Last crossing's tomorrow noon, miss! After that you swim or you wait!"

"Wait for what?"

He shrugged with the entire geography of the valley in the gesture. "Spring, I expect. Or better luck."

I stood there longer than the errand required. It is difficult to explain to anyone who has not felt it, that specific chill — not the temperature, though the wind off the water had teeth — of watching a door close while knowing you have chosen, technically, voluntarily, to remain on the wrong side of it. Eleven days ago the closure had been a notice, a form, someone else's logistics. Now it was brown water eating the stakes, and I was inside the form.

We could still go. That was the thing. The berth existed. The wagon company would take me across at noon, and Edith — well, Edith had made her position clear, but positions could be besieged; I had seen her abandon them before when the siege was conducted correctly. Marta had a sister in Millbrook. There was time. There was, as of that evening, exactly enough time, which is the most dangerous amount of time there is.

I did not yet know that within a week the arithmetic would change entirely — that the question of who left would stop being about flour and heart trouble and start being about gallows and fevers and a boy I hadn't met. On the tenth of October I still believed confinement meant canned peaches and boredom.

But I remember standing there, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that should have warned me: *the letter-writer stays because staying is her story. Nobody has ever asked the driver.*

Nobody had asked me. That was the arrangement. I had built the arrangement. I had built it brick by brick over six years — invisible, indispensable, sealed — and I had believed it load-bearing.

The river rose another inch while I watched. Somewhere behind me, up the hill, Edith was dictating something furious to the County Commission, and I would transcribe it tomorrow, and seal it, and carry it nowhere, because the mails had stopped, and the words would sit in the desk drawer growing stale like everything else on the wrong side of the water.

* * *

On the twelfth, Marta found the inventory discrepancy.

It was a small thing. A sack of flour moved from the north shelf to the west shelf, repositioned, she said, against damp. A candle stub missing. Nothing. She mentioned it to me in the kitchen with her back turned, stirring something, and her voice had a carefulness in it I recognized because I invented that carefulness — the tone of a person reporting facts while omitting the fact that matters.

"Damp gets in off the stone," she said. "I've shifted things."

"Marta. We inventoried three days ago. Nothing was damp three days ago."

"Stone is unpredictable." She stirred harder. "You want to count candles, count candles. But count them tomorrow."

I let it go, because it was a candle stub, and because loyalty in a household runs downward as well as upward, and because some instinct older than my philosophy whispered that the day was coming when I would need Marta's silences more than her bookkeeping.

I did not know yet whose silence she was keeping, or how large it was, or that it slept thirty feet from the kitchen door among the apple crates, breathing shallowly around a wound.

That night the river took the third stake. Edith worked late by lamplight, drafting letters she could no longer send, and I sat across from her reading them as she spoke, sealing each finished page out of habit, sealing letters addressed to a world that could no longer receive them — and if that image struck me as an omen, I was too tired, and too comfortable in my invisibility, to read it correctly.

The wagon came at noon the next day. One berth. One chance.

And the barn, when I went out to fetch apples for the journey that afternoon, had learned to keep secrets of its own.
