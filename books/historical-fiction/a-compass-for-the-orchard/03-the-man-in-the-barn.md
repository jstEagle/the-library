## 3. The Man in the Barn

The apples were going soft on the branch, which was the orchard's way of filing a complaint.

I had been sent down to the cellar with a lantern and a basket because Marta wanted enough fruit for a dozen jars of preserves, and because Edith Calloway did not send anyone anywhere — she issued assignments the way a general issues orders during a war she is confident of winning. *"Dominic, the apples. Before the frost takes them all and we're reduced to buying jam like ordinary people."* Ordinary people being, in Edith's cosmology, a species somewhere below the moles.

So I went. It was late afternoon, the kind of October light that makes everything look like it's already been remembered rather than lived, and I took the path past the kitchen garden, through the gate that hung crooked no matter how many times the handyman swore at it, and around to the barn.

Now, a word about barns. A person who has spent four years being furniture learns things about houses that their owners never know. I knew which floorboard in Edith's study creaked under the left heel only. I knew that Vivian's childhood room still held a rocking horse nobody had moved since 1906, dusted faithfully by Marta, who never explained herself either. And I knew the Calloway barn the way you know a face you've studied in a mirror — every shadow accounted for, every corner assigned its proper function. Apples in the north crates. Harness on the east wall. Feed for the hens in the bin with the loose lid that Marta kept threatening to fix and never did.

Which is why I noticed immediately that the feed bin lid was not merely loose. It was *arranged*.

Furniture notices these things. Furniture has nothing else to do.

I stood in the doorway with my basket and my lantern and I ran the arithmetic. Nobody had been out here today but me, and I hadn't opened that bin since Tuesday. The hay in the far stall had been shifted — not disturbed, *shifted*, by someone who understood the difference. There was a smell underneath the apple-and-hay smell that didn't belong: sweat, and something medicinal, and the faint iron tang that I would learn, over the following weeks, to recognize before I could name it.

A sensible woman would have gone back to the house. Would have told Edith there were rats in the feed bin, and let the machinery of the household — the shotgun in the pantry, the dogs, the general principle of property — handle whatever it was.

I have never once in my life been accused of being a sensible woman, mostly because nobody has ever thought about me long enough to accuse me of anything. That is, as you'll learn, both my profession and my affliction.

I lifted the lantern higher and said, to the empty-looking barn, "I can see the hay's been moved."

Nothing. A hen complained somewhere above me, philosophically.

"The lid," I said. "On the feed bin. It's been set back at an angle nobody sets things unless they mean to come back for them."

Silence, of the kind that isn't silence at all but listening.

I should tell you what I was thinking, or claim I was thinking something noble. I wasn't. I was thinking that this was the most interesting thing that had happened to me in four years, and that I hated myself a little for how much I wanted to see what came next. History happens to letter-writers, I had always told myself. Drivers watch it go by from the front seat, hands on the wheel, eyes on the road. But there is a version of a driver that history does catch, eventually, and she is the one who stops the car and gets out.

I set the basket down. I walked to the far stall, and I pulled aside the hay.

He was smaller than I expected. That's the first thing. Men who make trouble are supposed to be large; that's practically the job description. This one was folded into the space between the stall wall and the sacks of bran like a letter folded into an envelope too small for it — knees up, one arm cradled against his chest, a coat that had once been good draped over him like a tarpaulin. His face was gray under the brown, and there was a bandage around his ribs, visible where his shirt had come untucked, stained in a way that made my stomach drop through the floorboards.

He looked up at me, and I watched him do it — watched him read my face.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my whole religion then. In four years of reading Edith Calloway's letters, I had learned that a face is just a document written in haste. Most people write themselves plainly without knowing it. Edith wrote herself in cipher. And this man, this stranger bleeding into our hay, looked at me for perhaps three seconds and read me the way I read other people's correspondence — completely, instantly, and with a fluency that frightened me more than the blood did.

Because what he found there, apparently, was something he could use.

"You're the driver," he said. His voice was roughened, like paper that's been handled too much. "The one who comes and goes. They don't notice you."

"They don't," I said, because lying to a wounded man seemed beneath even me.

"Good." He exhaled, and the exhale cost him; I saw him pay for it. "Then you can keep it that way."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that in a healthier man might have become a smile. "You haven't. But you put your basket down before you came looking, and you lowered your voice when you crossed the threshold, and you're standing between me and the door without realizing you did it. So you've already decided something. You just haven't signed it yet."

I want you to understand what that sentence did to me. That was Edith's trick. That was the trick I had spent years admiring from three feet away, across a desk, watching her take the measure of county commissioners and bishops and editors with two observations and a pause. And now it was being done *to* me, by a man hiding in our feed storage, and I discovered that being read is nothing like reading. Being read is being opened.

"My name is Tomás Reyes," he said. "And I'm going to ask you for something enormous, and I'd like to do it before you decide to run inside."

"Don't tell me your name." I heard myself say it and was surprised. "Names are evidence. You don't give evidence to someone you've known ninety seconds."

That stopped him. He studied me again, slower this time, the way you reread a paragraph that doesn't behave the way you expected.

"You read letters," he said. Not a question.

"I drive a car."

"For the suffragist. Calloway. She dictates; you write it down; you seal them." He shifted, winced, went on anyway. "Half the valley knows her letters get read twice — once by you and once by whoever they're for. My people used to count on that. On her mail getting through when ours got confiscated."

*"Her mail,"* I said, "is none of my business after it's sealed."

"That's a lie," he said, almost gently. "You just told me yourself. You read everything. You have to — you're the one holding the pen."

The lantern flickered. Somewhere outside, a crow said something rude to another crow. I became extremely interested in a knot in the stall board.

"It's not the same thing," I said finally. "Reading a letter isn't doing anything about it. Reading is just — standing nearby while words happen."

"And burning a wage ledger?" he said. "Is that standing nearby?"

I looked at him. Really looked. Under the grayness and the stubble and the eyes that had been awake too long, he was maybe thirty. Maybe younger. Younger than Edith's son would have been, if she'd had a son instead of a daughter in Switzerland or wherever Vivian was marinating these days. His right arm was cradled wrong, and when I let my gaze travel down to it, he didn't hide it. He let me look, deliberately, the way you show a witness your open hands.

"You're hurt," I said. Brilliantly. Four years of proximity to one of the great minds of the age, and that was the best I could produce.

"There were three of them and one of me and a staircase involved. The staircase took their side." He tried the smile again; this time it arrived, thin and crooked. "They work for Hollis & Vane. The mill owners. If you've driven through Millbrook, you've seen their smoke."

"I've seen their smoke."

"Two weeks ago we organized a walkout. Three hundred men. It lasted nine days, which is eight days longer than anyone bet on." His voice stayed level, reciting facts, and I recognized that too — that flatness, that carefulness with facts. It was how Edith dictated when a subject cut close to the bone. People who feel deeply often speak in inventories. "Thursday night they came for the ones whose names they knew. I got out with some papers. They got my shoulder with a rifle butt and my ribs with a boot. The papers matter more than the ribs, if you're wondering where my priorities sit."

"What papers?"

He looked at me for a long moment.

"You really are going to make me earn everything," he said. "All right. Ledgers. Wage ledgers, going back six years. Proof of what Hollis & Vane paid and when and to whom, including the winters they shorted men and called it 'adjustments.' Names of every man who ever signed a grievance. If those pages reach a newspaper or a lawyer, it's not a strike anymore. It's a case."

"And the mill owners' men want them back."

"The mill owners' men want *me* back, preferably in a condition where I can't testify. The papers they'd settle for burning." He paused. "I already burned one of them. The biggest one. To keep it off their side."

Something in the way he said it — too quickly, the way you step over a hole in the dark — filed itself away in me without my permission. I was learning, even then, to keep a copy of everything.

"So you ran," I said.

"I ran. Through Millbrook, then along the river, then I saw this roof and remembered whose it was." He tilted his head toward the house, invisible beyond the barn wall. "Calloway. The woman who writes letters that make judges nervous. I figured if any barn in the county had a sympathetic attic, it was this one."

"You guessed."

"I gambled. There's a difference, but it's small and it's measured in luck, and I've had about as much of that as a man can use." He moved then, trying to straighten against the wall, and the movement tore a sound out of him that he strangled halfway — I heard it die in his throat, and I thought, absurdly, of Edith swallowing the end of sentences when Vivian's name came up.

I crouched down. I don't remember deciding to. My knees simply conducted their own foreign policy.

"How bad?" I asked.

"Ribs are cracked, probably. Not punctured — I can breathe, mostly. Shoulder's bruised to the bone but the arm works." He demonstrated, flexing fingers, and I noticed the knuckles were split and scabbed. "It's the fever risk that'll kill me, if anything does. Wound like this goes bad in a barn in October, you've got days."

Days. The word sat down between us like a third party.

I should describe what I felt, and I will try, though feeling was never my strong suit — observation was. What I felt was fear, first, clean and animal: this man was contraband, and possession was a crime, and I was now, by virtue of one crouch, an accessory. Underneath the fear, though, was something worse and warmer. Recognition. Because I knew what it was to be unreadable on purpose. I knew what it was to fold yourself into a space too small for you and wait for someone powerful to pass by without noticing. I had been doing it for four years in a better coat.

He watched all of this happen on my face. I could tell he watched, because his own face changed as mine did, tracking me like a man following text under his finger.

"Please," he said.

Just that. No speech, no case. All his inventory-keeping abandoned at once, and one word left standing. And I understood suddenly that he had saved it — that everything before had been argument, strategy, the ledger-talk and the name-dropping, and *please* was the thing underneath, the true draft, the one you swear you'll never send.

"I'm not going to tell anyone," I said. "Yet."

"*Yet.*"

"Yet. Because I need you to hear the shape of what you're asking. There are five people in that house. Marta, who feeds everyone and misses nothing. The handyman, who owns a shotgun and a grudge against trespassers in equal measure. Mrs. Calloway, who—" I stopped, because I genuinely didn't know how to finish that sentence, which unnerved me more than the blood had. "Who is not what the newspapers say she is. And Mr. Aldous, the groundsman. And me."

"And you," he repeated.

"I'm the one who just found you. Which means from this moment forward, everything that happens to you happens through me. Food, water, word to a doctor if it comes to that, warning if someone comes looking. I'm your whole supply line, Mr. Reyes. You've made a driver into a smuggler, and you did it in under ten minutes, and I'd admire it if I weren't so busy resenting it."

The smile came back, and stayed longer this time. "You'd have made a fine organizer," he said.

"I'd have made a fine nothing. That's the entire point of me." I stood, knees cracking in protest, and picked up the basket. "Apples. I'm supposedly here for apples, and Marta counts, and if I come back short she'll come looking, and Marta finding you is a coin toss I'm not prepared to flip."

"Marta," he said, and filed the name away. I watched him file it. Organizers collect names the way I collect sentences.

"Stay down. Stay quiet. Don't move after dark except to—" I gestured vaguely at the rear of the barn, and felt my face heat like a schoolgirl's. "You know."

"I know."

"And if anyone comes to the barn, you're in the loft behind the hay, not here — here's the first place anyone looks, I don't care how cleverly you arranged that bin lid. The loft ladder's on the north side. Can you climb it?"

"With complaints."

"Complaints are free. Screams aren't." I turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in my chest since the moment he read my face. "Why me?"

He blinked. "What?"

"Out of everyone. You've been here — how long? Since Thursday? Two days? And the household has walked past this barn a dozen times. Aldous oils the harness at seven every morning. And you lay low, you waited, and the moment the *driver* walks in, you spend your whole reserve of luck on her. Why?"

Tomás Reyes leaned his head back against the stall wall and considered me, and I had the strange sense of being read one final time — the last page of the document, the signature block.

"Because everyone else in this valley is somebody's daughter or somebody's man or somebody's debt," he said. "But you — you walked in here with a lantern and a basket and no story at all. Nobody's watching what you do, so nobody can predict it. You're the freest person I've seen in weeks." A beat. "Also, you put the basket down before you came looking. I told you. You'd already decided."

I carried the apples back up the path in the failing light, and I counted them twice at the kitchen door, and Marta counted them a third time, because Marta trusts arithmetic more than she trusts people, and I passed inspection. I remember that. I remember standing in Marta's warm kitchen, smelling onions and vinegar, listening to her complain about sugar prices and frost, answering in my usual half-sentences — *yes, no, the north rows are done* — and feeling like a forged document. Like a letter with the seal broken and pressed carefully back down.

Edith found me at supper. She looked at me across the table over her glasses, the lamplight catching the silver at her temples, and she said, "You were a long time with those apples, Dominic."

"The tree fought back," I said.

"Hm," said Edith Calloway, who had built a life out of hearing the sentence underneath the sentence. And she returned to her plate, and I returned to mine, and neither of us said anything else.

But I want it recorded — here, now, in the only testimony I trust — that when she said *hm*, my heart did something complicated and disloyal. Because it occurred to me, for the first time in four years of being the reader of everyone's letters, that I might not be the only one in that house who could read.

And I was right. Though I wouldn't learn how right until much later, and by then the river would be up and everything would be different.

That night I took a heel of bread, a wedge of cheese, and the last of Monday's cold chicken wrapped in a cloth, and I carried them down through the dark to the barn, moving the way furniture moves — silently, apologetically, without witnesses.

From the loft, in the dark, a voice said, softly: "Knew you'd come back."

"Don't flatter yourself," I whispered. "You're just the most interesting thing that's happened to me in four years."

"God help you," said Tomás Reyes, "if that's true."

I handed up the bundle through the slats, and his fingers brushed mine, warm with fever already, and I stood in the straw-smelling dark and understood that I had just sealed a letter of my own — addressed to trouble, postmarked tonight, no way to recall it.

Above me, he unwrapped the chicken. Outside, far off toward Millbrook, a dog began to bark, and kept barking, and did not stop.
