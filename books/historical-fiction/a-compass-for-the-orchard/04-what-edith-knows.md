## 4. What Edith Knows

I have been hiding things for sixty-one years, and I will tell you the first rule of it: a secret is not kept by silence. Silence is merely the wall. The secret is kept by the ordinary. You must go on demanding your tea at four o'clock exactly, complaining about the damp in the east wing, correcting your driver's parallel parking — you must be so relentlessly yourself that no one thinks to look behind you.

So when I found the man in my barn on the second Tuesday of October, I did what any woman of sense and long practice would do.

I went inside and rang for tea.

* * *

Let me back up, because I can see you forming opinions already, and I'd rather you form them with the facts in hand.

The morning had begun badly, which is to say normally. Dominic had driven me into town for the county meeting about the bridge — a spectacle of men in wool coats explaining to a room of women why nothing could be done, performed with the solemnity of priests announcing that God has taken up residence elsewhere. I asked three questions. Each one was answered with a longer version of *no*, delivered more slowly, as though I were a foreigner who might improve with volume. The river was rising. The repairs could not wait for thaw. The crossing would close within the fortnight, two months ahead of schedule, and after that we would all be residents of an island with a dying orchard on it and a winter's worth of flour, if Marta had counted honestly, which she always does, which is its own small mercy.

On the drive home I said nothing for six miles, which Dominic correctly interpreted as fury rather than peace. She is good at reading me. That is what she is *for*, though I have never once told her so. A driver should be furniture — comfortable, silent, positioned where you need her — and Dominic has made herself the finest piece of furniture in my household. I sometimes think if I died mid-sentence she would simply wait, engine idling, until someone else needed driving somewhere.

It was after luncheon that I walked out to the barn. Not for any dramatic reason. The storm two nights previous had thrown a branch through the south window of the cider room, and I wanted to look at the damage before Vivian's surveyor — if she ever actually sends one, or if that telegram was only another of her instruments — could price it against me.

The man was in the far stall, among the apple crates, and he heard me before I saw him. I knew because the crates went quiet. Apples do not hold their breath; men do.

I stood in the doorway and considered my options the way one considers a menu in a language one half-remembers. I am seventy-three years old. My heart, according to Dr. Prewitt, is "holding, given everything," which is doctor for *no promises*. My knees object to stairs. And here was a young man — twenty-five, perhaps thirty, dark-haired, with a bandage around his left forearm that he had wrapped himself, badly, the way men wrap wounds when there is no woman nearby to scold them — pressed back against the wall with his whole body saying *please* before his mouth opened.

"Don't," he said. "Please don't."

I looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped through the broken window onto the flagstones, keeping time.

"You're bleeding through that bandage," I said. "And you've knocked two crates over trying to become part of the scenery, which never works. Men always try it. It never works."

He stared at me. I imagine he had rehearsed a dozen speeches for the mill owners' men, and not one word for a small elderly woman in a grey coat holding an umbrella like a bishop's crozier.

"I haven't got money worth stealing," I continued, "and if you've come for the silver, you'll want the house, not the barn, and you'll want to come after dark, and frankly you'll want to hurry, because my cook counts the spoons." I set down the umbrella. "Sit down before you fall down. Who stitched that arm?"

"Nobody. I—"

"It shows. Wait here."

And I walked back to the house, and I did not tell Dominic, and I did not tell Marta — though Marta, being Marta, knew within the hour anyway, because Marta knows everything that passes through her kitchen's orbit the way the tide knows the moon — and I sat in my study with a cold cup of tea and thought about 1879.

* * *

You will forgive me if I do not give you 1879 entire. Some chapters of a life are written to be read and some are written only so they can survive being survived. But I will say this much: there was a winter, and there was a name that was not mine, and there was a loft above a print shop in Manchester where I lay awake listening for boots on the stair, and there was a woman named Mrs. Oakes downstairs who brought me bread and never once asked a question whose answer might endanger us both. She fed me for eleven weeks. When the danger passed, I thanked her, and she said something I have carried since: *"I didn't help you, miss. I just declined to notice you."*

Declining to notice. It sounds like cowardice put in better clothes. It is not. It is the hardest verb in the language. Anyone can shout; anyone can turn a man in; noticing-and-declining requires you to carry the weight of what you know, alone, indefinitely, without the relief of either action or innocence.

So: I declined to notice Tomás Reyes. And then I noticed everything else, because that is how the arrangement works. I noticed that Marta's stew portions grew mysteriously generous on certain evenings. I noticed that the lantern in the barn burned later than thrift allows. I noticed that Dominic — my furniture, my driver, my reader of letters — had begun to take the long way to town, past the mill road, and came back with her jaw set in a way I recognized from mirrors.

That last one worried me. Not because I doubted her. Because I couldn't afford to be wrong.

* * *

Which brings us to Thursday, and the test.

I want to be clear that I am not proud of it. Testing people is what cowards and mothers do, and I have been both. But the telegram from Vivian sat on my desk like a coiled thing — *arriving before the crossing closes to settle the estate, Mother, do keep well* — and every sentence of it was a key turning in a lock I had built myself. The deed includes the barn. Vivian knows property the way sharks know water. If she arrives with her surveyor and her grievances and finds a wounded organizer in my hayloft, she will not hesitate, because hesitation is the one inheritance I failed to leave her. She will call the constable across the river, or worse, she will make a bargain of it, and either way the girl will have found the one lever under my whole life.

So I needed to know whether my household could keep a shape under pressure. And there was only one member of it I hadn't yet weighed.

Dominic drove me to the village Thursday afternoon — the last legal week of the bridge, though the men had not yet posted the final notice — and on the return I began, in my most conversational voice, the kind I use for discussing weather with vicars, to talk about loyalty.

"Do you find," I said, watching the orchard rows slide past, brown leaves going to rust, "that you notice things you wish you hadn't?"

She glanced at me. She has excellent eyes, my driver. Brown, level, patient as held breath. "Things, ma'am?"

"In general. On the roads. In houses. People are careless in their own territory. They forget that a driver sees everything — the comings and goings, the visitors who arrive by the back gate, the lights that burn late in outbuildings." I smoothed my glove. "I've always wondered what a person in your position does with all that. All that seeing."

A silence of perhaps forty yards.

"I forget most of it," she said.

"Do you."

"Yes, ma'am. Most of it." Her hands were steady on the wheel, and I mean that literally — I watched them — and I confess I admired her for it even as I resented the steadiness. "The rest I keep where it doesn't trouble anybody."

"And if keeping it troubled somebody? If forgetting it endangered somebody?" I turned fully toward her then, and gave her the full weight of my attention, which is not a gentle thing to receive. "If a person saw something, Dominic — something that other people might pay dearly to learn — what would that person owe? To the one who might be hurt by telling? Or to the truth itself?"

Another silence, longer. The wheels ticked over a rut. Somewhere behind us the river was doing its slow patient arithmetic against the pilings.

"I think," she said finally, and her voice had changed — not louder, but deeper, the way a floor changes when you step off the carpet onto board — "that a letter isn't finished until it's sealed. And sealing it is a choice. You choose who reads it. Nobody owes every reader everything."

Well.

I sat back. For a moment neither of us spoke, and the orchard went on being autumn around us, indifferent and gold, and I understood two things at once, the way one understands lightning and thunder as a single event arriving in installments.

The first was that she knew. Of course she knew. My furniture had found the man days ago and had folded the knowledge away with the same neat hands that folded my letters, and had told no one, and had lied to no one, and had simply — declined to notice. Mrs. Oakes's verb, performed by a girl of twenty-some years who believes herself to be nobody.

The second thing was that I had just shown her my hand. Every card. *If forgetting endangers somebody.* She was too intelligent to miss it, and I have never once mistaken her for anything less than intelligent. I had spent a lifetime teaching myself to reveal nothing, and in one afternoon of vanity and fear I had revealed the shape of everything.

We came up the drive. The house stood against the grey sky the way it always does, patient, a little shabby, keeping its counsel. Marta was at the kitchen window pretending to scour a pot. In the barn, under the hay, a man with a badly wrapped arm was listening to his own heartbeat and hoping.

Dominic stopped the motorcar precisely where I like it stopped. She came around and opened my door, and offered her hand, and I took it, and neither of us said anything, and it was the most complete conversation I have had in years.

Inside, I hung my coat and rang for tea, because the ordinary is the wall, and walls must be maintained.

But at my desk afterward I drew out the drawer where I keep the letters I have never sent, and I took out the thinnest one, the one addressed in my own hand to *Miss V. Calloway, care of the Hotel Bellingham, London*, rewritten each autumn for nineteen years and sealed with nothing, and I held it a long while over the candle I keep for sealing.

I did not light it. Not quite yet.

Because tomorrow, or the day after, the wires would fail with the bridge, and then the only news that reached this house would be whatever walked or rode or swam to it — and I had a sudden, sick certainty, sitting there in the smell of wax and apples and old paper, that what was coming for us would not wait for spring.

Somewhere downriver, my daughter was packing.
