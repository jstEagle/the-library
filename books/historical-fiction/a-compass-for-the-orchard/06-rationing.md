## 6. Rationing

I have kept a kitchen through two wars' worth of rumors, one flood, and the entire span of Edith Calloway's marriage, so I did not need the county's notice on the bridge to tell me what was coming. You can smell a siege before you see it. It smells like flour and arithmetic.

"Inventory everything," Edith said that first morning of our confinement, standing in my kitchen doorway in her good gray coat as though she were inspecting troops. "Everything, Marta. I want to know how long we last."

"How long *who* lasts," I said, because I am old and she permits me liberties she'd hang a younger woman for.

"The household."

The household. That word again. Four souls in the big house, one in my cottage with her grandson's fever cooling across a river I could no longer cross to reach him — but we'll come to that. A household is a convenient shape. It has walls. It holds exactly the people who are supposed to be inside it.

Or so I said, out loud, to Mrs. Calloway, and then I went to my storeroom and began counting what would keep us honest until thaw.

* * *

An inventory is an act of faith performed with a pencil. Here is what faith looked like on that Tuesday:

Flour, forty-one pounds. Sugar, nine. Salt, plenty — salt is the one mercy of a poor year. Potatoes, four sacks, sound but for the soft ones at the bottom of the third sack, which I sorted out and set aside for reasons that will become apparent. Apples, more than God in His generosity or the previous owner in his stinginess had any right to give us: the orchard had gone unpicked down the far rows because the hired crews never came after September, and now those apples hung there in the cold like a debt nobody intended to collect. Onions, half a sack. Dried beans, eleven pounds. A ham I had been saving against Christmas and would now have to save against nothing at all, because Christmas does not exist when the bridge is out; Christmas is one more thing on the far side of the river.

I wrote it all in my ledger book, the fat one with the cracked spine, and I will tell you something about cook's ledgers that no one believes until they've kept one: they are the truest history ever written. Mrs. Calloway writes letters that go out into the world and change laws, maybe. My ledger just says *flour, forty-one pounds*, and it means it.

Dominic came in while I was halfway down the sacks. The driver. The quiet one, always at the edge of every room like a chair someone might need. She stood with her hands in her coat pockets and watched me count and then said, without preamble, "There's more mouths than you counted."

Now. A lesser woman would have played dumb. I have been playing dumb professionally since before Dominic Marchetti was born — it is the oldest weapon in the kitchen arsenal, older than fire, and I have seen whole empires defeated by a cook saying *I'm sure I wouldn't know, ma'am*. But there was something in the girl's voice. Not accusation. Something worse. Something like an offer.

"I count four," I said.

"You count four," she agreed. "And the apples in the barn have been moving."

The apples in the barn had been moving. Lord. Of course they had been moving. I'd noticed it myself three days earlier — a crate shifted, a bruised windfall where no windfall should be, the particular disorder of a man trying to eat quietly. And I had done what any sensible woman does with knowledge that is dangerous to hold. I had put it down and walked away from it and told myself the mice were large this year.

"He's hurt?" I asked.

"Not badly. Badly enough. He took a knock getting over the fence and it's not healing clean."

We stood there among my sacks like two conspirators in a play where nobody has written the conspiracy yet. Then Dominic did the thing that decided me, though neither of us knew it was a decision. She picked up my pencil from where I'd set it on the flour bin, and she made a small mark in the margin of my ledger, and she said:

"So we count six."

Not five, mind. Six. Because the girl included herself. That's the part people miss about complicity — it doesn't usually start with a lie. It starts with an arithmetic correction.

* * *

Here is how I fed Tomás Reyes for the first month of our captivity, and I record it here precisely because it was so unremarkable:

I cooked four portions. I have cooked four portions for thirty years; my hands do not consult me. But a portion is not a fixed thing, it is a suggestion. A ladle held high gives less. A ladle scraped shallow gives less still. Bread cut thin at the ends and generous in the middle can lose half a slice per plate without any single plate noticing its own diminishment. Potatoes boiled in their jackets shrink honestly and blame the water.

I took my own portion down by degrees, the way you take a hem up — a little here, a little there, invisible unless you're the one wearing it. I was the one wearing it. Cooks understand something that generals don't: hunger distributed evenly across one person is called sacrifice, and nobody sees it happen.

The soft potatoes went into a pail. Ends of bread, the rind off the ham before I cooked it — you render flavor from rind either way — a cup of beans from the eleven pounds, measured against my palm so that even my ledger couldn't catch it. The pail lived under a sacking in the woodshed, and from the woodshed it made its short criminal journey to the barn.

I told myself stories while I did it. That's what rationing is, mostly, storytelling. I told myself he was eating the orchard's apples anyway, so the rest was only charity toward a man already stealing. I told myself Edith Calloway, of all women, would be the last person alive to begrudge a fugitive a heel of bread. I told myself my grandson needed his grandmother healthy in the spring, and a grandmother who faints at her stove feeds nobody.

All of it true. None of it sufficient. Guilt does not read your arguments and concede; guilt just sits at the table with you, taking up a chair.

* * *

Dominic caught me at it on the ninth day. Or rather — she didn't catch me, she *joined* me, and there is a difference the size of a life.

I had carried the pail out through the side garden at dusk, the hour when the light makes everyone innocent, and I heard footsteps behind me and my heart went into my throat like a swallowed plum. But it was only her, and she was carrying her own pail.

"What's yours got," I whispered, as if whispering in an open field helps anything.

"Soup," she said. "From my bowl. While everyone was looking at the telegram board." She paused. "It's cold soup."

"He'll take cold soup."

"He'd better," she said, with a stiffness that I recognized, because I had worn it myself forty years ago feeding men off a boat in San Francisco harbor, "take it gratefully."

And there it was. The girl had crossed her line and was standing on the far side of it holding a pail of cold soup and pretending to be annoyed, which is how the young carry tenderness — like a package too heavy to lift properly, so they drag it.

We walked to the barn together. That was new. Until then it had been separate crimes, hers and mine, each believing ourselves alone in them, and I want to say plainly what it felt like to walk beside another person in a shared sin: warm. Warm and guilty, like brandy taken from a bottle you're supposed to be guarding. Two pails between us, four hands, one secret, and the mathematics of my ledger suddenly wrong in a way I found I didn't mind.

Tomás was sitting up against the apple crates when we came in, in the dark that smelled of frost and fermentation. He'd rigged a candle stub somewhere above, I never learned where, and it gave just enough light to see that his color was bad and his shoulder was being held together by hope and a strip of bedsheet. He looked at the two of us, then at the two pails, and something went out of his face — not suspicion, its opposite, the terrible softening of a man who has stopped having to be ready.

"You shouldn't," he said. "You should not do this."

"Eat your soup," I said.

"It's your food."

"It's the household's food, and you're in the household now, whether anybody voted on it or not." I handed him the bread ends. "Chew. You look like a man who swallows whole out of habit, and habits like that cost a body."

He ate. Slowly at first, watching the door between bites — a man cannot help it, they train that into you young if you're the wrong sort — and then faster, forgetting himself, and I felt that stupid swell in the chest that cooks get when somebody eats their food like it matters. Thirty years at a stove and it still works on me. Every time.

Dominic stood back by the crates with her empty pail and asked him, low, "How's the shoulder?"

"Lying to itself."

"That makes two of us," she said, which surprised a laugh out of him, a small dry one, quickly smothered — you could see him catch it, afraid of the sound carrying. And I thought: *there*, that's what confinement does, it turns laughter into contraband along with everything else.

He tried to refuse again — he was a refuser, this one, I'll give him that, some men would have eaten us blind within the week and slept fine — but I shut him down with the oldest tool in my trade. I told him a fact. "Every pound of flour in that storeroom is counted," I said, "and every count is a decision about who deserves it, and I have been making decisions about who deserves food since before your mother was born. You don't get a vote. Eat."

* * *

Walking back through the dark, Dominic said the thing I'd been waiting for her to say.

"Marta. Your portions. You're going shorter. I've watched you at supper."

"I've cooked for worse odds."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I admitted, "it's a defense. There's a difference."

She stopped by the woodpile. In the dark I could hear her thinking, which is a thing about that girl — she thinks audibly, like a clock in another room.

"We should split it," she said finally. "Whatever you're giving up. Split it between us. Half from your portion, half from mine."

"And what will you eat, girl? You're thin as a fence rail."

"I'm invisible. Invisible people need less." She said it lightly, the way you say the truest things, and I heard under it the whole strange philosophy of her — the pride she took in being furniture, in passing through rooms unread — and for the first time it struck me not as wisdom but as slow starvation of another kind. But I wasn't her keeper. I was everybody's keeper, which left no staff for the position.

"Split it," I agreed. "But we weigh fair, and I do the weighing. I've known drivers to cheat with their thumbs."

That earned me a breath of a laugh, and we shook on it like horse traders, there by the woodpile in the dark, and that was the treaty of the pail, ratified, unwitnessed, binding until famine or thaw.

* * *

For a week it worked. God forgive me for writing that sentence, because I know now what a week of things working is worth — it is worth exactly one week.

The household ate at seven. Edith at the head, reading or dictating; Vivian not yet arrived, thank the Lord, though the wires were spitting static about her coming; Dominic serving and observing in her furniture way; and me going in and out with dishes, keeping my eyes down and my counts up. Nobody noticed the missing ladles. Nobody noticed the thinner middles of the loaves. Secrecy, I learned again, is mostly a matter of nobody looking, and nobody looks at a kitchen twice.

On the eighth night Edith looked.

She looked at my hands, actually, while I was ladling the bean pot, and she said, mild as milk, "Marta's lost weight."

The table went quiet the way a forest goes quiet. Dominic's spoon stopped. And I did what I was built to do: I lied with a straight spine. "The stairs," I said. "Thirty years of stairs."

Edith looked at me a moment longer than comfort allows, and I had the sudden crawling certainty — which I have never confirmed and never will — that she knew. That she had known since the bridge notice went up, possibly since before it, that behind her house in her barn a hunted man was eating her orchard, and that her silence had simply been waiting to see whose company it kept. Two keepers of secrets in one household circle each other politely; I have seen it in hens.

"Well," she said at last, returning to her letters, "portion yourself sensibly. Winter punishes heroines."

Winter punishes everyone, ma'am, I did not say. Winter had already punished my daughter-in-law with worry and my grandson with fever, both of them across a river that was rising instead of falling, and every night I sat with my ledger and my shortened belly and did sums that had no answer: how much food, how much river, how many weeks, how much boy.

* * *

It was Dominic who brought the news, near midnight, knocking at my cottage door with her cap in her hand like a penitent.

"The wagon company," she said. "A man rode up to the closed end of the bridge road and shouted across the water. Final run before the ford goes under — one berth out, maybe two if the horses swim well, and after that nothing until thaw. Proper nothing. Months."

I stood in my doorway in the cold and understood, before she finished explaining why this mattered tonight of all nights, exactly what was coming for us. Arithmetic. The kind done on people.

"One berth," I repeated.

"One berth," she said. "And Marta — your grandson. Across the river. If the fever comes back, if the ford drowns—"

"Don't," I said.

"—then there's no doctor, no medicine, no—"

"*Don't.*" My voice cracked like a plate. I gripped the door frame. Thirty years I have kept this household fed and I could not keep one sentence from breaking over me. "You think I haven't done that sum? Every night since the bridge notice? I've done it in flour, girl. I know what a boy weighs in bread."

She had the grace to go silent. Down the hill the river muttered in the dark, louder than it had any right to be, as if it had opinions about our bookkeeping.

"There's a meeting tomorrow," she said softly. "Edith's called one. All of us. About the berth."

"All of us," I said.

"All of us," Dominic confirmed, and neither of us said the sixth name, but we both heard it sitting there in the dark between us, patient as a mouse in a wall — the man in my barn, eating my stolen bread, whose name nobody had spoken aloud in this household yet and whose fate was now going to be weighed on the same scale as a sick child and a failing heart.

I shut my door and banked my fire and took out my ledger, and by candlelight I drew a line under the week's column. Six mouths, I wrote. One berth. And beneath that, because a ledger must balance or drive you mad, I wrote the only honest entry left: *God help whoever does the sums.*

Tomorrow, it seemed, that would be all of us.
