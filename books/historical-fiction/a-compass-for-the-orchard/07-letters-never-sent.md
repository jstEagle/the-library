## 7. Letters Never Sent

The rain had been at the windows for three days, patient as a creditor, and Edith Calloway had decided — in the way she decided things, which was to say instantly and without consultation — that her correspondence room wanted sorting.

"It wants sorting" was Edith's phrase for *I want you to do it*, and by now I spoke that dialect fluently. She stood in the doorway of the small back room where eleven years of letters lived in cedar boxes and biscuit tins, and she looked at the shelves the way a general looks at a map of a country she has already won.

"You know my system," she said.

I did. Her system was: everything in a box until the box is full, then a new box, labeled only when some future historian's suffering amused her. There were boxes from the Denver campaign, boxes from the year she'd picketed the governor's mansion in a coat she'd had made specially with hidden pockets for pamphlets, boxes from seasons so long past that the ink had gone the brown of old tea. Somewhere in all of it were replies from senators, threats from editors, one pressed violet from a woman whose name even Edith had promised never to say aloud.

"Keep nothing," Edith said. "Read what you must to sort it. Burn what's dead."

"Burn what's dead," I repeated, and we both pretended that was an ordinary instruction and not an obituary notice for several hundred pieces of paper.

She left me to it. That was the arrangement between us, refined over a decade: she made the noise, and I made the silence around it. She wrote history in her strong slanted hand while I sat across from her reading every word before the wax went on, and neither of us ever remarked on this. I was furniture. Furniture that could read.

Here is what I believed, in those days, when I still believed comforting things: that history happened to letter-writers, not to drivers. That the people who got remembered were the people who got written about, and the safest place in the world was one step to the side of the page. Edith dictated; I drove; the horses did the actual work; and none of us would ever hang in anybody's portrait gallery. It seemed like a fine ambition at the time. I want that on the record. It seemed like a fine ambition.

* * *

Sorting correspondence is archaeology conducted on a living civilization. You learn the strata quickly. The top layer is urgent: petitions to sign, a furious exchange with a Mrs. Harriet Pelham-Grice over whether suffragists ought to smoke in public (Edith's position: yes, and worse). Beneath that, the middle years — thick bundles from the eastern papers, clippings she'd saved of her own worst reviews, because Edith believed you should keep your enemies where you could hear them chewing. At the bottom, the sediment: receipts, a lock of hair I chose not to investigate, a child's drawing of a horse done in charcoal on butcher paper, signed *V.*

I held that one a long time.

The rain kept up its work at the glass. Downstairs Marta was making something with onions, which meant the household was either celebrating or bracing, and with the bridge closed and the river climbing, I'd have bet on bracing. In the barn — well. In the barn was a thing I was trying very hard not to think about while I handled other people's secrets, since thinking about one secret tends to wake all the others like dogs in a yard.

I put the drawing in my apron pocket instead of any box. That was my first theft of the week, though I didn't know yet it would have company.

By the second day I had burned two full boxes' worth of dead paper in the kitchen stove while Marta watched me with the expression of a woman deciding how many questions she can afford. The ash smelled like every year from 1902 to 1908. Senators I'd driven Edith to confront. Editors who'd called her a hyena in petticoats. One entire folder of letters from a man named Gerald who had loved her, apparently, with tremendous stamina and no success whatsoever.

It was on the third afternoon, reaching into the deepest shelf, that I found the tin.

Not a biscuit tin. A tobacco tin, black, painted over, with a strip of leather tied around it the way you'd tie something you intended to come back for but somehow never did. It was heavier than paper alone accounts for. And it wasn't in Edith's system at all — it was outside it, behind the boxes, which meant it wasn't filed. Filed means dealt with. This was hidden, and there is a difference, and I knew the difference in my hands before my head caught up.

I should have set it down. Let me say that plainly, since later events gave everyone cause to ask why Dominic Marchetti, professional keeper of other people's privacy, opened a tin that was not addressed to her. I should have set it down. But the rain was falling, and the river was rising, and somewhere under my ribs was a cold spot the size of a barn door that I hadn't been able to explain to anyone, and when a woman spends eleven years reading letters that are never meant for her eyes, she begins to wonder what it would feel like to read one that isn't meant for anyone's.

I untied the leather.

* * *

There were nineteen letters in the tin. All addressed to the same person. All unsealed, unstamped, unsent.

*Vivian,* they began, every one of them, in handwriting that started each year confident and ended each page smaller, as though the words wore out partway through their journey and had to be carried the rest of the way.

They were dated. That was the first thing I noticed, because I am a reader of postmarks and dates the way other women read palms. One per year. Nineteen years. The earliest was dated two months after a date I knew without being told — Vivian would have been seventeen then, and there is always one date in a family's calendar that needs no explanation.

I am going to tell you what I did, and it will sound orderly, and it was not. I read them in order, because disorder felt like a greater trespass than the reading itself. Nineteen letters. Each one begun the same way, each one a fresh attempt at a sentence that had failed nineteen times.

*My daughter,*

*I have read your article in the Ledger and find your argument sound and your tone unforgivable...*

*Your aunt tells me you've been ill. I would come if I thought my coming wouldn't be the illness...*

*I marched today. Four hundred women and I thought, as I always think, that you would have counted them better than I did, you always counted things better than I did...*

*I have bought the orchard. You will think this is sentiment. It is not sentiment, or it is not only sentiment. Trees keep their own records...*

Year after year. The letter changed as the writer changed — early ones sharp with grievance dressed as praise, middle ones trying humor like a coat that didn't fit, late ones stripped down almost to nothing, almost to just the salutation and a weather report and a signature, as if Edith Calloway, who had faced down governors and newspapermen and once, memorably, a bull in the Capitol Square, could not get further than *it has been a wet autumn here* to the daughter she had not spoken to in two decades.

And each year she rewrote it. Not revised — rewrote. New page, new attempt, the old draft folded away into the tin like evidence against herself. She never mailed any of them. She never stopped writing them. Do you understand what that is? That is a woman standing at a door for nineteen years, knocking, and never once trying the handle, because she is afraid of what she'll find on the other side — no one, or worse, someone.

Edith Calloway, who feared nothing that walked on two legs, was afraid of a stamp.

I sat on the floor of the correspondence room with my back against the shelf and the last letter in my lap, and I confess I wept. Not elegantly. Wryness fails me here and I won't pretend otherwise; you may have me dry-eyed at funerals and steady at gunpoint, but give me a fearless woman writing nineteen letters to a girl who is now a stranger, and I will come apart like wet newsprint.

Because I recognized it. That is the shame of it. I have never had a daughter, never had a quarrel worth twenty years, never had anything that large to fail at — and still I knew the shape of that tin. Everyone keeps one. Everyone has the drawer, the box, the envelope gone soft at the corners from handling, the words that can be written and rewritten forever precisely because they will never be sent. Unsent letters are the only safe letters. They cannot be answered. They cannot disappoint you at breakfast. They stay exactly as brave as you made them, forever.

History happens to letter-writers, I had told myself. But Edith had taught me better without meaning to: history also happens to people who don't send the letter. It happens slower, and indoors, and it looks from outside like nothing at all.

* * *

The latest letter was dated this past spring. I read it four times. It was short even by the standards of the recent vintage.

*My daughter,*

*It has been a wet spring. The apples are coming in well. I am told my heart is not what it was, which will surprise no one who has ever tried to hold onto it.*

*I enclose nothing. I ask nothing. I write only to keep in practice, in case I ever grow brave, which I do not expect.*

*Your mother,*

*E. C.*

*I am told my heart is not what it was.* I had driven her to Dr. Pruett in February. I had waited in the hall. Nobody had told me anything; nobody ever told drivers anything. I had assumed gout. Gout, at least, announces itself. A heart can fail politely, privately, the way good women are supposed to do everything.

I folded the letter along its existing creases — worn soft, nineteen springs deep, creases that said *handled often, sent never* — and I put the whole bundle back in the tin and tied the leather exactly as I'd found it, because a lie is easiest to sustain when you leave no fingerprints on the truth.

Then I sat with the tin in my lap and thought about what a person does with knowledge like this.

Option the first: say nothing, return the tin, carry it forever. This was my specialty. Eleven years of saying nothing. I could win medals in saying nothing.

Option the second: burn them, as instructed. *Burn what's dead.* But they weren't dead. That was the whole horror of them. Every other scrap in that room was finished — answered, answered badly, outlived. These were mid-sentence. Nineteen unfinished arguments, nineteen unhealed wounds, still bleeding annually on the first wet week of spring. Burning them would be mercy or murder and I could not determine which, and it was not my determination to make.

Option the third, which arrived unbidden and stayed like an unwelcome guest: Vivian was coming. The telegram had come through on the last day the wires could be trusted, before the line went down past Miller's Ferry — *ARRIVING BY FERRY BEFORE CROSSING CLOSES STOP COMING FOR WHAT IS MINE STOP V.* And what was hers, legally, in whatever mood the county clerk was in when he drew up the deed, included the orchard, the house, the land, and — my stomach performed a slow deliberate turn — the barn.

The barn, where at this moment a wounded man lay healing among the apple crates, breathing as quietly as he could, keeping his silence the way you keep a coal alive overnight.

Edith didn't know I knew about Tomás. I didn't know, until this hour, that she knew too — though I suspected it now, the way you suspect weather. Two keepers of secrets in one house, circling, polite as dance partners. And now a third secret, the oldest and heaviest of all, sitting in a tobacco tin in my lap with the rain coming down.

Three secrets, one household, one closing bridge. It occurred to me, sitting on that cold floor, that secrets behave like water. Contain them separately and they're manageable. Let them find each other, and you get a flood.

I heard Edith's cane on the hallway boards before I saw her. Nineteen years of not knocking at one door had made her a great knocker at all the others; she rapped twice at the correspondence room and entered without waiting, because waiting was for people with fewer convictions.

"Well?" she said. "Is it sorted?"

"Two boxes burned," I said, and my voice came out level, and I marveled at myself even as it did. "Three more to go."

She looked at me a moment too long. Edith missed nothing; she had simply spent decades perfecting the art of not revealing what she'd caught, and I recognized the technique, having borrowed it myself any number of times. Her eyes went — I would swear to this — to the deepest shelf, where the gap where the tin had sat was now filled by a box of Gerald's hopeless correspondence. Then back to my face. Nothing moved in her expression except everything.

"The Pelham-Grice file," she said at last, "can burn entire."

"Yes, ma'am."

She turned to go. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and did not look back at me, and said, in the voice she used for dictation, light as a comment on the weather:

"Some letters are drafts, Dominic. A draft is not a lie. It's a thing not yet true." And then the cane went tap, tap, tap down the hall, and I was alone with the rain and the ash-smell and the tin behind the boxes, understanding that I had just been read as thoroughly as I had ever read anyone.

That night I couldn't sleep. Toward the small hours I heard a floorboard in the hall, and then, faintly, the back door, and then nothing — and in the morning there were three fewer apples in the cellar basket than Marta's inventory allowed, and a smear of rain-mud on the kitchen threshold shaped like a man's boot.

Someone was feeding the barn. Someone besides me, now.

I was still standing at the window doing that arithmetic when I saw them, far off across the flooded low field, at the edge of the far bank where the road ran gray under the rain: riders. Four of them, maybe five, moving slow along the fence lines, stopping at the neighboring farms, knocking at doors.

Not mail riders. Mail riders don't ride in pairs, and they don't look into barns.
