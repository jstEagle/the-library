## 8. The Mill Men Ride Out

From the loft of the Calloway barn, through the gap where a board has warped away from the frame, I can see the river, the ford, and the far bank where the road bends toward town — which means I can see the mill men coming two hours before anyone on this side of the water knows they've ridden out.

I have decided to think of this as a strategic advantage rather than what it is, which is a man lying in hay, counting other men's horses.

There are five of them today. Yesterday there were three. They ride up to the Herrera farm first, because the Herreras are closest to the crossing and because Amado Herrera has a loud voice and a reputation for answering questions with questions, which the mill men find insolent and I find admirable. I watch them dismount. I watch them stand in the yard with their hats on, which is how you know they mean business; a man who takes off his hat in your doorway is asking, and a man who keeps it on is telling. These ones are telling.

I cannot hear what they say at this distance. That is probably mercy. What I can do is watch Amado's arms, which start crossed and then uncross slowly over the ten minutes or so the conversation lasts, and by the end they are hanging at his sides like he's forgotten what arms are for. Then one of the mill men — the big one, Kesselring, who used to check the tally books at the mill and had hands like shovels — reaches into his coat, and my whole body goes tight as a knot before I understand that what he's pulling out is paper. A flyer, maybe. A notice. He hands it to Amado, and Amado holds it the way you hold something dead, by one corner, and then the mill men remount and ride on toward the Delgado place.

They are looking for me. Let's not dress it up. Somewhere between here and the mill, four days' hard riding back the way I came, there is a burned building and a missing man and a wage ledger that no longer exists, and every man on those horses is being paid — I would bet my remaining dignity on it — by the day plus expenses. They will question everyone on the far bank, then work their way to the crossing, and when the county closes the bridge for repairs — and they will close it; I watched the surveyors come through last week with their instruments and their long faces — the search will stop at the water. The river becomes a wall, and I become a thing on the wrong side of it.

Which is the whole trick, isn't it. Get trapped. Trapped men can't run, so nobody chases them.

I did not use to think in these terms. Eight months ago I thought in terms of pamphlets and meetings and the beautiful arithmetic of the union — one man's grievance times four hundred men equals a strike. I was good at the arithmetic. I could make a room full of exhausted pickers feel like an army. What I could not do was make an army out of men whose names were written in a book owned by the people they were striking against, and this is the part of the story I have been rehearsing in my head every night since the fire, the part where I explain to myself exactly what I did, and why the explanation does not hold its shape in the morning.

* * *

She comes at dusk, the way she always comes. Dominic. The driver. The woman who is furniture.

I figured her out early, in the three days I've been in this barn. She comes to fetch apples from the cellar crates and she moves through this barn like she owns the silence of it, like silence is a country she holds citizenship in and I am only visiting. She never looks up at the loft. For two days I let that be. On the third day I coughed, deliberately, and she went still — completely still, the way prey goes still, except that wasn't right either, because after the stillness she looked directly up at me with no fear in her face at all, only assessment, like she was reading me off a page. Deciding whether I was worth the trouble of pretending not to have seen.

She brought bread after that. Not asked for, not discussed. Left at the foot of the ladder with such precision that I understood the bread was a sentence, and the sentence was: *I see you, and we will not speak of it.*

Today she climbs the ladder.

That's new. She comes up over the edge with a tin plate — beans, actual salt in them — and sits down across from me with her back against a post, and says nothing, and I understand that the nothing is mine to fill or not fill, and I am so tired of my own company that I fill it.

"You had visitors," I say. "Across the river."

"I saw." She breaks a piece of bread in half and hands half to me, though the bread is already mine, technically hers, this economy of ours where everything is borrowed from the same kitchen. "Five riders. They stopped at the Herrera place."

"Amado Herrera has a big mouth."

"Amado Herrera," she says, "has four daughters and a mortgage, and told them nothing anyway. I could tell by his shoulders." She eats a little. Watches the last light go off the water. "They'll reach the ferry landing tomorrow."

"Then I should be gone before—"

"Where." It is not a question with a question mark. It is a question with a trapdoor. "The bridge closes in ten days. The ford is waist-deep already. Where exactly do you propose to be gone *to*?"

I look at the river. The river looks back, gray and patient, and does not answer either of us.

"Miss Calloway knows," Dominic says, still watching the water. "You should know that. If you don't already."

My chest does something complicated. "How long has she known?"

"Longer than I have." A pause, and then, with the particular dryness that is her entire soul compressed into tone: "She asked me to make sure the barn cat wasn't eating your share."

I laugh. It comes out of me sideways and surprised, the first real laugh since the night of the fire, and it hurts my ribs, which are various shades of yellow-green from a boot I received outside Pueblo while running. Everything costs something lately. Even laughing bills you afterward.

* * *

Here is what I did. Since she is sitting there and since the light is going and since a man alone with his account books eventually has to audit himself.

The Delgado Mill kept a wage ledger. Big book, canvas-bound, older than the current owner. Every picker, every hauler, every man and woman on seasonal labor, entered by name with their rate, their debts against the company store, their deductions — including the deductions that were not legal, the ones the company invented, the two cents a pound skimmed off the top that over eleven years came to more than any of us would earn in a lifetime. The ledger was the proof. We had copies of pages — I made them myself, wetted paper and a glass pane and lamplight, weeks of work — but copies can be called forgeries. Copies can be disputed. The original, produced at a hearing, subpoenaed by a lawyer with a law degree and a willingness to be hated, was a hammer.

We were going to take it. Not burn it — take it. Hand it to the state labor board, get our hearing, watch the mill owners choke on their own arithmetic. There were four hundred names in that book. Four hundred people who'd been robbed in amounts small enough that each theft looked like rounding error and large enough that together they built the owner's second house in Denver.

The plan failed on a Tuesday. There was a raid — someone talked, and I have spent some bad hours wondering if the someone knew they were talking — and the owners' men came to the boarding house where the ledger was hidden under my floor, and I got it out the window with them coming up the stairs, and I ran for the mill yard with the book inside my coat, and they followed, and there was a moment — I remember it with terrible clarity, the way you remember anything you're ashamed of — there was a moment when I stood by the boiler house holding a hammer I'd picked up off the ground, and I understood that I could not outrun them carrying what I was carrying, and that if the ledger stayed intact and stayed theirs, it was not just useless, it was a list. A list of everyone who had signed the strike circulars, everyone who had attended the meetings, everyone whose name appeared next to a debt or a deduction or a note in the margin in the owner's handwriting — *troublemaker* beside certain entries, I'd seen it, beside the name of a girl of nineteen named Ruth Okafor who'd had the nerve to ask about the scale weights.

A list of four hundred names, sitting in the hands of men who had boots.

So I burned it. In the boiler, in front of them, while one of them hit me and another swore and the third just watched with his head tilted like I was an interesting kind of weather. I burned the proof of the crime along with the record of the victims, and I ran, and I have been running for six weeks, and I am alive.

And Ruth Okafor's stolen wages are ash now too. And Amado Herrera's. And every one of the four hundred. The men who beat me can't prove I struck them. But the women and men who were robbed can't ever prove they were robbed either. I took the evidence out of the owners' hands, yes. I also took it out of history's. Both things are true. I have been carrying both things true for six weeks and they weigh different amounts depending on the hour.

* * *

I tell some of this to Dominic. Not all of it. The middle part — the floorboard, the window, the boiler — yes. The part about Ruth Okafor, the name specifically, no. Some weights you don't hand to a stranger, even a kind one.

She listens the way she seems to do everything: without interrupting, without performing listening, just receiving. When I finish, the barn is dark except for the gray square of the window gap, and somewhere down the road a dog is making a complaint about the century in general.

"They'll keep searching?" she asks.

"Until the bridge closes. Then they'll assume the river solved their problem." I rub my face. My hands smell like hay and horse and six weeks of not staying anywhere. "It worked, is the ugly part. I'm free. Nobody can hang me for a fire they can't trace, and nobody can prosecute the mill with a ledger that doesn't exist, so the mill will simply continue. Everyone robbed stays robbed. I stay unrobbed of my life." I hear my own voice go flat. "That's the trade. That's what it cost. I bought my skin with four hundred people's proof."

Dominic is quiet for a long time. Long enough that I think she's going to offer comfort, which I could not have borne, and I'm arranging my face to refuse it when she speaks instead.

"When Miss Calloway dictates letters," she says, "I read them. Before sealing. Every one."

"That's—" I begin, and stop, because I genuinely don't know if that's trust or trespass.

"It's neither. It's habit." She turns the plate in her hands. "And last month there was a letter to the county registrar about land assessments, and there was a line in it — an accurate line, a legally useful line — about who held title to this orchard before Miss Calloway bought it, and under what conditions the previous sale occurred. I read it twice. Then I sealed it." Her jaw works. "I almost didn't seal it. That's all I'll say. Except to tell you this, since you're doing arithmetic tonight: whatever you burned, burning it didn't un-rob anybody, but keeping yourself alive didn't rob them either. The robbery was already done. You just couldn't fix it, and you couldn't bear to leave the weapon behind, so you destroyed the weapon." She shrugs, awkward, as if the philosophy embarrasses her on its way out. "Whether that was right, I don't know. But I know it isn't the same as stealing from them."

"It feels the same."

"Yes," she says. "I imagine most true things feel like that. Like the wrong thing, wearing a coat."

Down at the house a lamp goes on, then another. Marta lighting the kitchen, Edith the study — the whole small constellation of a household that contains, as yet unknown to most of it, a fugitive in the attic of its barn. From the far bank comes the sound of hooves on the ferry plank; the mill men giving up the day, crossing back toward town, one of them turning in the saddle to look across the darkening water at this side, this valley, this orchard, this exact patch of dark where I am.

Tomorrow they ride the near fields. Tomorrow they knock on doors I can see from this loft. And tomorrow, or the day after — the wires are degrading, Dominic said, the lines down somewhere north of the pass — a telegram is due that I know nothing about and cannot dread properly, though I sense it coming the way animals sense weather: the daughter is coming home, and the property she comes to claim runs fence line to fence line around the barn where I am hiding, and the men on those horses will not need to be told where to look.

Dominic goes down the ladder with the empty plate. At the bottom she pauses.

"The Herreras fed a lie to five armed men today," she says. "For a stranger they've never met. Remember that, when you're auditing yourself."

Then she's gone, and I'm alone with the arithmetic again — but it adds up differently now, or at least the columns won't stay put, and I sit in the hay watching the far bank until the last lamp out there goes dark, and I do not sleep, and I do not much mind.
