## 9. Vivian at the Gate

The ferryman knew my name before I gave it, which told me everything about how the valley talked.

"Mrs. Ashworth," he said, not asking, holding the gunwale steady while I stepped down into his boat with my valise and my good boots ruined anyway by the wet planks. "Didn't expect anyone crossing today. Water's been climbing since Tuesday."

"Then I've timed it well," I said. "You'll be the last."

He laughed as if I'd made a joke. I hadn't. I had read the notice pinned to the county office in Millbrook — BRIDGE CLOSED FOR EMERGENCY REPAIRS, CROSSING BY FERRY AT OWNER'S RISK — and beneath it, in smaller type, a date that meant the ferry itself would drown its route within the fortnight. My mother's letters never mentioned any of this. My mother's letters never mentioned anything; that was rather their defining feature. Eleven years of envelopes postmarked from this valley, each one containing a page of weather and a sentence of nothing, addressed to me with the careful formality of a business correspondence between strangers who had once shared a table.

*Dear Vivian. The apples came in well this year. I trust the city treats you kindly. Your mother, E.*

I could have set fire to the whole bundle and lost nothing but paper.

Now there was a telegram instead — the last one to get through before the wires went slack, the operator later admitted — and it said, in that clipped impersonal prose that telegrams achieve so much better than mothers: *VIVIAN COMING STOP WILL SETTLE MATTER OF ESTATE STOP E.*

*Will settle.* As though eleven years were a drawer she could finally open and empty, as though the estate were a matter like a matter of freight. Well. She was half right. I had come to settle. Whether she would enjoy the settlement was another question entirely, and one I found I did not care about, or told myself I did not care about, which on the ferry felt like the same thing.

The river was high and brown and moving with the unhurried confidence of something that intends to win. The ferryman talked. He was one of those men who narrates geography as though he invented it: the ford, the orchard on the rise, the mill downstream with its new chimney, the low fields that would go under if the rain kept up. He said the Calloway place still brought fruit to market, though the trees were old. He said Mrs. Calloway kept to herself. He said it in the tone people use when they mean *and we let her*, and I understood that my mother's isolation had been a local amenity, maintained by mutual agreement, like the church clock nobody bothered to correct.

"And you'd be her daughter," he said again, as if confirming it to himself. "Thought you'd be older."

"I'm thirty-four."

"Well." He shrugged. "People imagine things."

People did. For eleven years the valley had presumably imagined me — the daughter who left, the daughter who didn't write, the unnatural daughter — while I imagined this place right back: a gray house in a gray orchard where a gray woman outlasted every argument we ever had simply by refusing to have them. I had constructed my mother's whole world from silence, the way you can reconstruct a face from its negative. It gave me some satisfaction, stepping onto the near bank, to see how wrong I'd been. The light was gold. The hills were almost tender in the haze. It was a beautiful country, and she had hidden in it for a decade, and I hated it immediately and completely for making hatred difficult.

* * *

The gate was new. That stopped me longer than it should have.

Someone had replaced the old latch with an iron one, oiled, swinging true, and beyond it the drive had been graveled within what looked like a season. There is a particular kind of information in maintenance. Neglect tells you a household has given up; upkeep tells you it has decided something. I stood at my own childhood gate like a surveyor at the boundary stone of a property she means to take, and I suppose that is precisely what I was, valise in hand, hat pinned at the severe angle Miriam at the salon called "the widow," which I affected because it made men underestimate the conversation to follow.

I walked rather than took the drive's curve, cutting across the grass at the diagonal, because I wanted to measure the land the way I'd learned to measure everything in my husband's offices: not as scenery but as asset. Twelve years keeping the books of Ashworth & Vale had taught me that every estate is a confession written in fences. Where the wire sagged, money had run out. Where it ran straight and tight, someone had fought for the line. A property is just a family argument conducted in posts and rails, ongoing.

So I read the orchard as I walked it. The rows nearest the house: pruned within the year, competent, no more. The rows on the south slope: neglected two seasons, maybe three, the suckers taking over, the fruit small and knuckled on the bough. There was a barn below the rise, sound roof, fresh paint on the doors — again, new, again, deliberate — and I noted the discrepancy without yet understanding it: a household letting its trees starve while spending money on a barn it didn't fill. Discrepancies are how books tell the truth. Ledgers lie politely; discrepancies shout.

And there was the third discrepancy, standing in the yard beside a motorcar with mud to its axles: a young woman in a driver's coat, watching me come across the grass with the fixed, polite blankness of a servant deciding what I am going to be to her.

She didn't come to meet me. That was the first thing. She let me close the distance, which is either insolence or strategy, and when I reached her she said, "Miss Calloway," as though reading me off a manifest. "We weren't told the ferry was still running."

"We weren't told the bridge wasn't," I said. "It seems we've both been misinformed. You're the driver?"

"Dominic Marchetti."

"Marchetti." I let it sit. "Italian."

"My father was."

"And your mother?"

"From here."

From here. Everyone in this valley was from here; it was the whole of the valley's character. But she said it flatly, guarding something, and I filed it. I do not pretend to be a warm woman. I know what I am, which is exact, and exactness begins with noticing what people protect.

"Is my mother in the house?"

"She's in the parlor. She knows you're coming?"

"The telegram said so."

"Then she knows," said Dominic Marchetti, and there was something in the way she said it — not doubt, exactly. A test performed in reverse. As though she were checking whether *I* knew something, and had decided from my answer that I didn't.

I would think about that exchange a great deal in the weeks to come, though I couldn't yet have said why.

* * *

My mother received me in the parlor the way monarchs receive ambassadors from minor powers: seated, upright, gracious, and utterly unyielding in every particular that mattered.

She had aged, of course. Everyone ages; only vanity finds it worth remarking. What struck me was that she had aged *deliberately* — the way some women choose gray hair as one chooses a costume. Edith Calloway at sixty-three had arranged herself into a monument of her own choosing, spine straight as a fencepost, hands folded, the famous composure polished to the point of parody. Once, as a girl, I had believed that composure was strength. Now I recognized its engineering. I had sat across from bankers and land men and my late husband's lawyers, and I knew composed when I saw it, and composed was what you built when you could not afford to feel anything in public.

"Vivian." She did not rise. "You look well. City life agrees with you."

"Mother."

"You'll stay in the blue room. Marta has aired it. Your father kept it as it was, but there's no sense in that now."

My father, dead nineteen years, deployed in the first forty seconds as a load-bearing wall. I sat down opposite her without being asked, which won us nothing; she had trained me too well for tactics to surprise either of us.

"I read the telegram," I said.

"Yes."

"You wrote *will settle*. I've come to settle it. The deed, the accounts, the disposition of the property. I've retained a man in Millbrook to review the boundaries — the eastern line runs through land the mill company has wanted for years, and I intend to know whether we hold it clean or whether you've let it erode through inaction, as you've let everything else—"

"Vivian."

"—erode. I'm not here for a reconciliation. We're past that, and I'd think less of you if you pretended otherwise. I'm here because you are a woman of sixty-three with a failing—"

The pause was very small. Her hands did not move. But something behind her eyes closed, briefly, like a curtain drawn against weather, and I understood that I had guessed correctly about the doctor's letter — the one her solicitor had mentioned to mine, the discreet correspondence of discreet men, *her heart, you understand, nothing definite, but* — and that she knew I knew, and that neither of us would say it aloud, because we were, whatever else, well matched in our cowardices.

"I see," she said quietly. "Marta will show you the blue room."

"Marta needn't. I remember where it is."

"Of course you do."

And there — only there, only for a second — the monument cracked. Not into tenderness. Into something older and worse: recognition. Two women who each remembered the same room from the inside, who remembered the wallpaper and the loose board and the view of the orchard where the light came at six in June, and who had agreed, across eleven years of unmailed pages, to remember nothing together. I picked up my valise because if I did not stand immediately I was going to sit in that parlor and grieve her in front of her, and I had not crossed a flooding river to do her that courtesy.

* * *

I walked the boundaries that afternoon because boundaries don't lie and everyone else in that house did, in the aggregate, by existing so politely around whatever it was they existed politely around.

Three days I had been raised in that house, eighteen years, and I had forgotten how large the property felt when you measured it with your feet instead of your grievances. I walked the eastern line to the stone marker at the creek, found it intact, found the mill company's survey stakes just beyond it — recent, bright-flagged, presumptuous — and pulled one up, and stood it back down on our side of the line, which is the sort of declaration a stake is for. I walked the southern fence where the orchard starved, and counted the dead trees — nine, ten, eleven — and did the arithmetic of yield loss in my head until I made myself stop, because the numbers were doing what numbers always do for me: standing in front of the feeling like hired men.

Because here is what I had not put in any ledger: I had come home to a dying orchard and a dying mother and a household of strangers keeping secrets, and some part of me — the part I keep in the locked drawer, under the deeds — had hoped the place would be ugly. Ugly I could work with. Ugly forgives me for coming. But the light through those old trees at five o'clock was the exact light of my seventh summer, of my father lifting me to reach a branch, of everything before my mother chose a movement over a child and then chose silence over apology for eleven years, and the beauty of it was an accusation, and I had never in my life been accused by anything I couldn't answer back.

At the top of the rise behind the barn I stopped, because from there you can see the whole shape of the holding: house, yard, orchard, the river beyond running high and brown toward the ford, the far bank where the county bridge stood open like a broken tooth. And below me, close enough that I should have heard something — a door, a footstep, anything — the barn sat with its new-painted doors shut in the middle of a working afternoon.

I stood there longer than I can honestly defend. The discrepancy ticked. A household spends money on a barn it doesn't use, keeps its motorcar muddy, posts a driver who reads people like documents, feeds an orchard nothing, locks a gate it has newly repaired. None of it cohered. Estates always cohere, once you find the figure hidden in the columns; that is the entire discipline of accounting. Somewhere under all this politeness there was a number that made the books balance, and I intended, professionally, to find it.

I started down toward the barn doors.

I want it recorded — by me, to me, since no one else keeps my accounts — that I was not snooping. I was conducting an inventory. That is what I told myself then and what I will tell myself always, because it is nearly true, and nearly true is the only kind of true I have ever been able to afford.

My hand was on the iron latch when the kitchen bell rang across the yard, twice, three times — the summons pattern, the one Marta used to call the field hands in, the one I had not heard in twenty years and recognized instantly, the body remembering what the heart refuses. And through the door I heard, or thought I heard, a floorboard shift inside the barn — one soft creak, quickly stilled, the sound a person makes when they are holding themselves motionless.

I stood with my hand on the latch of my inheritance, listening to it breathe.

Then I let go, and turned, and walked back toward the house across the gold-lit grass, because there is a time to force a lock and a time to let the discrepancy ripen, and my mother had taught me patience after all — her one bequest, delivered by example. Whatever the barn held, it would keep. The river was rising; the bridges were closing; soon this whole valley would be sealed in ice and silence with its secret, and none of us, whatever we pretended, would be able to leave.

Supper, I decided, would be instructive.

I was right. It would cost me more than I imagined.
