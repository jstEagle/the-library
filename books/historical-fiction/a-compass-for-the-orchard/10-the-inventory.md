## 10. The Inventory

Vivian Calloway arrived at breakfast on the third morning with a leather portfolio, a fountain pen, and the expression of a woman who had rehearsed being disappointed and found the world cooperative.

"I'll want a full accounting," she said, buttering toast she did not eat. "Everything on the property. Structures, livestock, stores, equipment. My mother's affairs have been run like a rumor, and I intend to make them into facts."

"Good morning to you too," said Edith, from the head of the table, in the tone of a woman who had spent seventy years making other people's certainty look foolish.

"I'm serious, Mother."

"You're always serious. It's your most exhausting quality."

Marta refilled the coffee and caught my eye — one flick of the glance, quick as a card trick. We had developed a whole language since the fever boy crossed under the apple crates: a lifted eyebrow meaning *the barn*, a pause at the door meaning *not yet*, a certain way of setting down a plate that meant *he ate well last night*. None of it was spoken. All of it was deniable. I had thought, in my old life, that invisibility meant freedom. I was beginning to understand it could also mean enlistment.

"I'll need someone who knows the property," Vivian went on, uncapping the pen. "Someone who's been everywhere, seen everything." She looked up, and her eyes were her mother's eyes wearing a stranger's arithmetic. "The driver. You've been here two years?"

"A little over," I said.

"Then you'll do the inventory with me. Room by room. Today."

There is a particular sensation that arrives when a person who has spent her life being furniture is asked to become an accomplice. It is not fear, exactly. Fear has edges. This was more like standing on the bank of a river you'd always assumed you couldn't swim and realizing the water had already reached your knees.

"Certainly, miss," I said.

* * *

We started with the house because Vivian believed in starting where the money was. She moved through the parlor like an auditor through a church, touching nothing, pricing everything. The piano: forty dollars if it survived the damp, thirty if not. Edith's books: catalogued by shelf, valued at pennies apiece, which told me Vivian had never watched her mother read one. The silver from Edith's own mother: weighed, tested, entered in a hand so upright it seemed to be standing at attention.

I carried the ledger. That was my role — the person who holds things. For two years I had held doors open, held letters, held my tongue. Now I held a ledger while a woman priced her inheritance out loud as though grief were a line item she'd simply forgotten to include.

"The orchard itself," Vivian said at the window, looking out at the rows gone brown and ragged past their season. "How many trees still bearing?"

"Fewer every year," I said. "The blight took the north block in '11. Your mother's been letting it go rather than fight it."

"Why let it go?"

"Because trees aren't the only thing that can be mortgaged," said Edith from the doorway, arriving with the timing of weather. "Some days I chose the trees."

Vivian wrote something. I did not see what. There is a version of this woman, I understood even then, who writes everything down and shows no one — a private ledger behind the public one, and God help whoever appears in it.

* * *

By afternoon we had done the cellar (preserves enough until February if we were honest about February, which we were trying not to be), the carriage house (one motorcar that would never start again, two sleighs for a valley that no longer froze reliably), and the well house, where Vivian tasted the water and spat it back into the bucket with the first honest expression I'd seen on her.

"That's brackish."

"That's the river," I said. "It's coming up under everything now."

She wrote that down too. She was writing down the flood before it happened, which I suppose is what surveyors and daughters do.

And then we came to the barn.

* * *

Tomás was in the loft. He had been in the loft since dawn; Marta had carried his breakfast up in a lard pail, and he knew better than to cough, better than to shift his weight on boards that talked. From below there should have been nothing to find but hay dust and the smell of apples going soft in their crates.

Vivian stood in the doorway with her portfolio and looked around the way she'd looked at the parlor, and I felt the lie assemble itself inside me before I had decided anything — felt it arrive fully built, the way a sneeze arrives, or a conviction.

"Barn's contents are mostly agricultural," I said. "Apples in storage — maybe sixty crates, though half will rot by solstice. Ladder, tack, the press for cider, which hasn't worked since 1909. Tools on the wall. Hay above, less than we'd like."

She looked at me. "That took you four seconds."

"I've walked through it every day for two years."

"Read me the crates," she said. "Count them properly. My mother's ledgers claim eighty."

So I counted. Out loud, crate by crate, moving down the rows with my back to the loft ladder, my voice steady as a metronome, and every number I called was both true and a wall — true about apples, and load-bearing against discovery. Sixty-one. Sixty-two. Behind me I heard Vivian's pen, heard her boots on the packed earth, heard her stop once near the tack room and stand there long enough that the silence got a texture, and I kept counting. Sixty-three. Somewhere above us a man was breathing the way people breathe when they know their lives depend on the patience of strangers. Sixty-four.

"Tack room," Vivian said finally.

"Two saddles, one cracked. Harness for the pair. Blankets, oil, a box of horseshoe nails."

She opened the door herself. I watched her do it and did not move to help, because helping meant caring, and caring meant having something to hide. This is the first law of lying, which nobody teaches you because the people who know it are busy lying: the liar must be slightly bored. Boredom is the costume innocence wears.

Inside the tack room: exactly what I had listed, because Tomás was not in the tack room, and because Marta and I had spent Tuesday night moving everything he might have touched — a bedroll, a tin cup, a boot — into a cavity behind the south wall where the foundation had settled and left a gap wide as a coffin. I had lain awake afterward listening to the house settle and thinking: *this is what I am now, a woman who knows where a fugitive sleeps.*

"All accounted," Vivian said, and wrote it.

"Yes, miss."

She closed the tack room door and turned, and for a moment she looked at me directly, and I want to say something about that look. It was not suspicion. Suspicion would have been easier. It was *assessment* — the look of a woman deciding what a thing was worth, and I understood that I had just been appraised along with the apples, entered somewhere in that upright hand, and that the entry might read either *loyal servant* or *liar*, and that Vivian Calloway possibly could not tell the difference, and neither, increasingly, could I.

"You're very good at this," she said.

"At what, miss?"

"Knowing things." She tucked the pen back into the portfolio. "Most people describe a place. You recite it. Like you've read it somewhere."

"It's just driving, miss. You learn roads by heart or you get lost."

"Mm," she said, and walked out of the barn into the flat gray light, and I stood alone among the counted crates with my heart going like a bird in a chimney.

From the loft, very softly, came a sound. Not words. Just a knock of knuckle on wood — twice — the way one traveler signals another across dark country.

I did not knock back. But I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, and then I went out and shut the door behind me, gently, the way you close a book you intend to deny having read.

* * *

Here is the thing that frightened me, and I record it plainly because it deserves plainness: the lying had been *easy*. Not strained-easy, not white-knuckled. Easy the way my own handwriting is easy. Sixty-four crates, two saddles, one cracked harness — each falsehood had come out of my mouth with the same weight and temperature as the truth, and no one in the world could have sorted them, because they were sorted nowhere, not even in me.

All my life I had believed that reading other people's words kept me safe because it kept me outside them. History happens to letter-writers; drivers only carry the envelopes. But standing in that barn I understood the mechanism at last, the whole dirty engine of it. Reading letters had taught me every sentence a person can be made of — the pleading kind, the proud kind, the kind written at midnight and regretted by post. Two years of Edith's correspondence had been an apprenticeship after all, just not the one I thought. I had studied how words conceal. I had memorized the architecture of the sealed envelope. And now the apprenticeship was over and the work had begun, and the first thing I had built with it was a wall between a hunted man and a counting woman.

Invisibility curdles. That's the word for it. Milk sits too long and turns without asking anyone's permission, and what was nourishing becomes dangerous, and you cannot un-sour it, you can only decide what to do with it. I had spent two years being unseen, and I had told myself it was humility, and perhaps some of it was. But a hidden person is a person with a hidden shape, and Vivian had nearly found mine today, and the part of me that had lied fluently, boredly, perfectly — that part had *enjoyed* it. Let the record show.

At supper Marta set down the stewed apples and lifted one eyebrow. *The barn.* I gave the smallest nod. *Held.*

Edith talked about the war in Europe, which had gotten itself started without anyone in this valley's permission, and about the bridge, which the county now promised by thaw, and about Vivian, whom she discussed in the tone of a general reviewing an enemy's uniforms. Vivian corrected her twice and took notes once. I poured the coffee and said nothing, and my silence had a different weight than it used to. Before, my silence had been empty. Tonight it was furnished.

Afterward, on the porch, Vivian lit a cigarette — the first I'd seen her smoke — and looked out toward the black line of the river.

"The inventory's incomplete," she said, not turning around.

"Miss?"

"There's a discrepancy. Eighty crates in the old ledgers, sixty-four on your count, and rot doesn't account for sixteen in six weeks." She tapped ash into the dark. "I'm not accusing anyone. Ledgers drift. People miscount, or they count twice, or—" she paused, and the pause had teeth— "or they take things. Or hide things. I grew up watching my mother hide things; I know the household habits." She dropped the cigarette and ground it under her heel. "I'll count the barn again myself tomorrow morning. Early. Before the light's good."

She went inside. I stayed on the porch a while longer, listening to the river rise in the dark like something clearing its throat, and did the only arithmetic available to me: sunrise was at seven, and Tomás's fever had broken only yesterday, and the gap behind the south wall was three steps from the tack room door.

Sixteen crates' worth of discrepancy, walking back into the barn at first light.

I went inside to find Marta in the kitchen, and to begin, for the first time in my life, planning something instead of carrying it.
