## 11. Floodwater Rising

I have buried one husband, two employers, and a sourdough starter I loved more than any of them, so I am not a woman who panics easily. But when I stood at the kitchen window on the fourth morning of the rain and watched the river walk up the lane like it owned the place — which, historically speaking, it has always suspected — I will admit my hands went still on the bread dough for a full three seconds. Three seconds is a long time in my kitchen. In three seconds I can judge a person's whole character by how they hold a teacup.

The low fields went first. The hay meadow that old Mr. Calloway used to mow in June, the one Edith pretends not to care about because caring about hay is not what suffragists do, disappeared under brown water sometime in the night, so quietly that we only knew it had happened when the fence posts began to float past the orchard wall like a procession of small wooden mourners. By noon the water had found the far pasture. By evening it was licking at the roots of the lowest apple trees, the Bensons, forty years planted, and I watched those trees stand in moving water to their knees and thought: there it is. There is the winter decided.

Rain does something to a house that no amount of experience prepares you for. It makes the walls smaller. We had been confined before — the bridge closing early had already turned the estate into an island with delusions of self-sufficiency — but confinement by notice posted on a board is one thing. Confinement by weather, rising daily, measurable in inches against the gatepost, is another entirely. A notice is bureaucracy. Water is intent.

Miss Vivian walked the boundary that second morning with her umbrella furled despite the rain, using it instead as a pointer, tapping fence stones, muttering elevations to herself. She had the surveyor's maps out on the parlor table every night, and she'd drawn lines on them in red pencil, and I am not a stupid woman, whatever the priest said about me in 1889, and I could see what the red lines meant. They meant: this much of your mother's kingdom is going under. She did not seem grieved by it. She seemed, if anything, vindicated, as though the river were simply another relative confirming her opinion of the family property.

Dominic drove the coach down as far as the rise above the ford that afternoon, against everyone's advice and therefore obviously, and came back soaked to the collar with a look on her face I have learned to read over these months. Dominic reads other people the way I read a recipe — thoroughly, silently, taking notes. Being read back is uncomfortable. "It's bad," she said, wringing out her hat in the scullery. "The ford's maybe four days. Five if the rain slackens."

"Five days until what?"

"Until the ford drowns. Until nothing crosses in either direction until thaw." She hung the hat on its peg with the particular care of someone who would rather throw it. "March, they told me at the crossing keeper's. Earliest."

March. I did the arithmetic right there, standing in my own wet footprints. It was the middle of November. Four months. I counted in my head the way I count everything: in meals. Four months of meals off a cellar I had inventoried twice already and both times come up with a number I didn't like.

"Well," I said, "at least the rain keeps the dust down."

Dominic almost smiled. That girl's smiles are like the sun in this country — rare, and when they come everyone pretends not to notice but everybody feels warmer anyway.

* * *

The well went brackish on the sixth day.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all a cook has when the world is ending. It wasn't sudden. Tuesday's water tasted faintly of iron, the way it does after heavy weather, and I blamed the pipes. Wednesday it tasted of iron and something older, something mineral and sad, like sucking on a coin from a drowned man's pocket. Thursday I drew the bucket and held it to the light and saw the faintest cloud in it, a haze like breath on glass that wouldn't wipe away, and I stood there in the well house with my arm aching and understood that the river had gotten into the aquifer, that the flood hadn't stayed politely outside the walls of our lives but had crept underneath, into the very ground we drank.

There is no locking a door against water that comes from below.

We had the cisterns, thank God and old Mr. Calloway, who built for siege whether he knew it or not — two stone cisterns off the roof-gutters, holding together perhaps six weeks of careful water if the rain kept filling them, which it obligingly did, being the same rain that was drowning us. But cistern water wants boiling, and boiling wants fuel, and fuel wants counting, and suddenly everything in this house wanted counting, and I am the one who counts.

So I made my lists. I sat at the deal table in the kitchen after the others had gone up and I made lists by candlelight like a general planning a campaign, except my war was against hunger and my soldiers were forty-one jars of apples, nine sacks of flour going hard at the edges, a ham, half a ham, a crock of lard, onions sprouting in their net because I'd been soft-hearted and bought too many in October, twelve pounds of salt pork, beans, beans, more beans, and — tucked behind the farrier's crate where I thought no one looked — the small private hoard I had been shaving off our rations for five weeks now and carrying out to the barn after dark.

I looked at that last item a long time, the way you look at a thing that could hang somebody.

Because here is what the lists don't say. Here is what no inventory records. Every evening, when the house settled and Dominic was seeing to the horses and Miss Vivian was upstairs resenting her mother in the approved style, I took a heel of bread, a piece of cheese or a spoonful of the stew, sometimes an apple, and I walked through the rain to the barn, and I climbed partway up the ladder to the loft, and I set the food on the top rung without looking up, because some courtesies survive even treason.

Tomás Reyes never asked for more than I brought. He thanked me in a voice gone quiet and rough from disuse, and once he said, "You shouldn't. If they catch you—"

"If they catch *you*," I said, "they won't ask what you ate," and he had no answer for that, and neither did I, and that was the whole of our friendship: bread on a rung in the dark, and both of us knowing the price.

Rationing, the cookbooks will tell you, is a matter of proportion. Rationing is nothing of the kind. Rationing is theft, performed daily, against yourself, on behalf of someone whose name you cannot safely say. I took Tomás's share from our plates — thinner stews, bread cut closer, the ham saved and saved and saved until I feared it would mummify out of sheer neglect. Nobody complained. That was the strange mercy of it. Edith ate what was put in front of her with the abstraction of a woman eating letters rather than food. Vivian noticed the portions shrinking and said only, "Mother's household economy leaves much to be desired," and pushed her plate away half full, which nearly ended me. I fed her leavings to no one. I could not bear the waste and I could not bear to say why.

And Dominic — Dominic began carrying things out with me. I never asked. One evening in the third week there were simply two sets of footsteps in the wet grass, and she kept pace beside me to the barn door and waited while I climbed to the rung, and on the way back she said nothing except, "The horse liniment bottle's empty. I refilled it with the good brandy. If anyone asks, the horses are drinking very well this winter."

That girl. I have raised sons, God rest them all at a distance, and I know the type that thinks virtue must be loud to count. Dominic is the other kind, the kind that commits small crimes in complete silence and calls it furniture. I squeezed her arm in the dark, which is as close as either of us comes to a speech.

But rationing cuts both ways, and the water was the thing that frightened me, more than the food. You can stretch bread. You cannot stretch water you're afraid of. I boiled everything twice, and I caught rain in every pot and basin in the house, and I lay awake listening to the river and thinking about the boy.

My grandson, Emilio. My daughter's boy, eleven years old, across the river at her sister's place with a fever that started as a cough and became, according to the letter that took nine days to arrive by whatever unreliable grace still moved between the banks, "something in the chest." Nine days old, that news. The doctor over there was attending worse cases closer to town; the letter said that too, in my sister-in-law's blunt hand, meaning to comfort and achieving arithmetic instead. And the ford had perhaps four days left, and then nothing, no crossing, no medicine carried over, no boy carried back, until March.

Four days. I counted them the way I counted everything, and for the first time in my life the counting gave me no comfort at all, because I could see exactly how many meals stood between my grandson and the rising water, and none of them were enough.

* * *

The wagon man came on the fifth day, which was one day later than Dominic's estimate and one day less than hope.

I heard the horse first — an unfamiliar horse, and after five weeks of knowing every hoof on this ground the way you know your own heartbeat, an unfamiliar hoof is like a shout in church. Then the bell the crossing companies use, hand-bell, brass, cracked on one side so it sounds like the bell has opinions. I went out onto the step with a carving knife still in my hand from the onions, because a woman alone in a kitchen learns to carry her tools.

He was a squat man in oilskins on a big gray horse, with a wagon behind him and a face like a man who had given up expecting good news and was merely delivering the alternative. Rain ran off his hat brim in ropes. Behind him, down the rise, the ford showed itself one last time — brown water running fast and thick over the crossing stones, already deep enough to curl around the axles of anything foolish enough to try.

"Marta?" he called. "Cook? Company says there's a Mrs. Calloway here."

"There's several people here," I said. "None of them are coming with you."

"That's not—" He swung down, stiff, and stood stamping in the mud, and produced from inside his coat a paper folded in eight, which he unfolded with the ceremony of a man unveiling his own grievances. "Notice. Last run of the season. Ford goes under tomorrow, day after at the outside — surveyor's word. Company's making one final trip out tonight while there's light, one wagon, and I'll tell you straight, madam, because you look like a woman who'd hear it anyway: one berth."

"One berth," I repeated.

"One seat. One passenger. Room for one person and what they can carry on their lap." He had the decency to look embarrassed about it, which put him a full class above most men in his trade. "Company's position is that a berth's no good to a dead passenger, and two passengers is a dead wagon, and a dead wagon's no use to anybody, and besides the insurance—"

"I know how insurance talks," I said. "I've heard it talk at funerals."

He had the paper in his hand, the official notice with its printed border and its signature, and he read out the terms in a voice gone flat with repetition: departure at four o'clock, from the rise above the ford, final crossing of the season, one berth, first claim to the party presenting this notice, no exceptions, the company regrets.

No exceptions. The company regrets. Somewhere in some office in town a clerk had typed those words with a straight pen and believed them, and here they stood dripping in my gateway, meaning that of everyone in this house — everyone in this house and its barn — precisely one person was getting out before the water closed over the road until spring.

He waited while I took the notice. My hands were steady. I noticed my hands being steady the way you notice a stranger in the room.

"Who else knows?" I asked.

"Knows?"

"About the berth."

"Nobody knows yet. You're the first house on the route. Calloway's the name on the ledger, so it's yours to pass along." He remounted, gathered the reins, and then paused, because even men like him have one decent moment per delivery. "Get whoever it is ready by four. I don't wait. Waiting's how wagons drown."

Then he was gone, bell clanking its cracked opinion down the lane, and I stood on my own step with the paper in my fist and the rain coming down and the knife still in my other hand, and I did what I had done all my life with unbearable information: I went in and got supper on.

* * *

Supper was barley soup with the last of the fresh onions, and bread that I cut thin and toasted, because toast lies about quantity. We ate by lamplight in the kitchen, all five of us, because the parlor chimney smoked in rain and Vivian had declared the dining room "melancholy," which it was, being full of her mother's history. Edith sat wrapped in a shawl with her hands around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Her heart — I have known about Edith's heart longer than Edith has, because I watch what people leave on plates and how they climb stairs, and stairs have been costing her more all autumn. Tomás's bread sat on the rung in the barn, going soft in the damp; I'd take him his portion after. Dominic sat where she always sat, at the edge, half in shadow, watching everyone read the room the way she probably read it better than anyone in it.

I put the notice on the table between the bread board and the salt.

"What's that?" Vivian said, reaching.

"It's a berth," I said. "One berth. On the last wagon out. Tonight, four o'clock. After that the ford drowns, and nobody crosses this river again until the thaw. Not out. Not in. Not medicine, not letters, not anything." I kept my voice level, because a voice is like pastry — handle it roughly and the whole thing falls. "The company sends one seat. Not one each. One."

The silence had a texture to it. I have cooked through silences for forty years and I can tell you what they're made of. This one was made of arithmetic.

Vivian read the paper twice, fast, lips moving slightly, and then set it down with the air of a woman declining a bribe. "Well, it can't be me. Obviously. I've only just arrived, and I'm not leaving with the deed unsettled." Which was true and also a performance, and I let it pass, because among the things this household has learned is that Vivian's reasons are always half true and the useful half is usually the honest one.

Edith looked at the paper for a long time. Longer than reading takes. Her thumb moved along the edge of it, and I saw her do the sum I had seen her refuse to do for weeks: the stairs, the shawl, the tea untouched, the distance to March.

"No," she said finally, and it was almost gentle. "I didn't leave for the cause and I won't leave for the weather. Besides—" and here she smiled, that terrible bright smile that has carried her through forty years of platforms and arrests, "—someone should stay to keep the flag flying."

"The flag," Vivian said, "is going to be underwater."

"Flags dry, Vivian."

And then there were three of us at that table who had not spoken, and I watched the silence turn toward us like a searchlight. Because here is the thing that none of them could say and all of them knew, or half knew, the way this house knows everything halfway, in glances and unopened doors: the berth was not really a question of five people. It was a question of three.

It was Tomás, hiding in my barn with a price on his head and the mill men's patience wearing thin on the far bank, who would hang if the thaw brought the law across before it brought forgiveness. It was Emilio, eleven years old and burning with fever on the other side of that same drowning water, who needed the wagon not to escape it but to reach it — the last crossing, the only crossing, the boy carried over to the doctor in town before the road went under forever. And it was Edith, who would say no with a smile, and mean it, and die a little faster for meaning it, and whose no did not make her less of a candidate, only a harder one to save.

Three desperate people, one berth, and a deadline of four o'clock.

And I stood there with the soup ladle still in my hand like a fool, understanding suddenly that the notice had been delivered to *me*, that first claim was mine to pass along, that somewhere between my kitchen and that wagon at four o'clock somebody was going to have to decide — and that everyone at this table was going to look at everyone else, and wait, and that the waiting would land, as waiting always does in this house, on the quiet ones.

Dominic had not touched her spoon. She was looking at the notice, and then at Edith, and then — briefly, terribly, like a woman checking a wound — at the dark window that faced the barn.

"Four o'clock," I said, to no one, to everyone. "Someone had best pack."
