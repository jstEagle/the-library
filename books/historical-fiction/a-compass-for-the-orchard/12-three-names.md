## 12. Three Names

The wagon company's letter came with the last of the mail, and I read it four times before the meaning settled into me like cold water into boots.

*Mrs. Calloway — Due to rising water at Miller's Ford, we will make one final supply run this season, departing Thursday next. We regret we cannot guarantee return service until spring thaw. Should you require transport for any person, one berth is available on this run. Kindly confirm by Wednesday noon.*

One berth.

I sat in the driver's seat of the motorcar with the engine off, because the engine was the only private room I had left. Rain ticked against the roof. Through the windshield the orchard stretched away toward the river, rows of trees going brown at the edges like paper left near a lamp, and beyond them the water was doing something rivers are not supposed to do in October — climbing.

I had read thousands of letters for Edith Calloway. Love letters and legal letters, letters of condolence composed the way other people composed grocery lists. I knew by now that most human catastrophe arrives in an envelope with a printed return address. But this was the first piece of correspondence I'd ever held that was addressed, in effect, to me alone: *kindly confirm.* Confirm what? The letter didn't say. It didn't have to. One berth on the wagon out, and somewhere in this house, three people who needed it more than I did.

I put the letter in my coat, where it lay against my ribs like a coal.

* * *

I want to be precise about how the arithmetic began, because I would like to claim I resisted it longer than I did.

It began that same evening, in the kitchen, with Marta counting beans. Not idly — she counted them the way a priest counts rosaries, each bean lifted and set down with intention, and her lips moved around the numbers as though they were prayers she didn't believe in anymore.

"Forty-one days," she said, not looking up. "If I stretch it. Forty-two if nobody gets sick."

"And if somebody does?"

Marta set down a bean. "Then less."

The boy's name was Rafael. He was seven years old and he lived across the river with Marta's daughter, in a house on low ground that appeared on no map worth trusting, and two days earlier a neighbor's message had come over the water by the ferryman's last trip: *the child has fever, the doctor says watchful waiting, the doctor says there is nothing else to say.* Watchful waiting. I turned that phrase over for a long time that night. It sounded like something you'd write about weather. It did not sound like something you'd write about a seven-year-old whose grandmother was counting beans forty miles from his bedside.

Marta never asked me for anything. That was the thing I kept coming back to. She fed Tomás Reyes out of her own bowl, thinner each week, and said nothing. She mentioned Rafael exactly once, in exactly that flat inventory voice — *my Lilia's boy has the fever, they say it's gone to his chest* — and then picked up another bean. And I understood, sitting there in the warm bread smell of that kitchen, that she had already done the arithmetic herself. She had gotten to my name and found it wanting, or found it unwritable, and closed the book.

She never asked. Which meant the asking was mine to volunteer for, and volunteering, I have learned, is just asking with the courage inverted.

Then there was Edith.

I heard her heart before anyone named it. She took to pausing on the stairs between the landing and the hall — not resting, she'd have shot me for *resting*, but pausing, hand flat against the wallpaper as though checking whether the house was level. Once I came upon her at the parlor window and she didn't hear me for a full ten seconds, which for Edith Calloway was equivalent to unconsciousness. When she did turn, her face assembled itself so quickly I almost believed I'd seen nothing.

"Dominic. Tell me, do you hear that humming?"

I listened. There was nothing but the river, which had developed a voice lately, a low continuous remark like a committee meeting held underwater.

"I don't hear anything," I said.

"Exactly," said Edith, and looked back out at the dark, and I stood in the doorway hating myself for the comfort of the lie and hating her more, far more, for needing it.

Dr. Prather had said it plainly enough when he'd made his last crossing in September: *the valve is failing, Mrs. Calloway; I won't dress it up. If it deteriorates, you'll want to be within reach of the hospital in Millbrook.* Within reach. The bridge had since removed itself from our lives like a servant giving notice, and Millbrook might as well have been the moon. Edith had thanked him for his frankness and seen him to the gate, and I had watched her walk back up the path at half her usual speed, and I had told myself: she is tired. She is seventy-one. Everyone walks slowly in October.

That is the thing about arithmetic done on human lives. Every figure requires rounding, and every rounding is a small act of cowardice.

And the third name. Well.

Tomás Reyes slept in the loft above the crates with a wound in his side that Marta dressed twice weekly with boiled linen and a stubbornness I admired, and every day he stayed was a day the mill men's patience grew richer across the water. They had come to the far bank twice now, and both times the river had been our alibi, but rivers end their generosity eventually, and so do Octobers. If he were caught — and caught he would be, once the water fell and the roads opened and the world resumed its appetite — there was a courtroom in Millbrook and a judge whose last three labor cases had ended the way such cases ended. Tomás himself put it without decoration, one night in the barn, handing me back an empty bowl.

"They don't hang men for arson anymore," he said. "They find something else. A resisting charge. A fall down the courthouse stairs." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever been party to. "But yes. In every way that matters to me, Miss Marchetti, your wagon berth is a rope."

I hadn't known he knew about the berth. I hadn't told anyone. Which taught me something about how visible my invisibility had become.

* * *

Wednesday morning, Edith summoned me to the study to dictate correspondence, and I went carrying the wagon letter in my coat like contraband.

She dictated two letters — one to a suffrage contact in Seattle, one to a seed merchant complaining about blight with a savagery I enjoyed transcribing — and then she set down her pen and looked at me with the particular directness she reserved for interrogating senators.

"You've had the wagon letter two days."

Not a question. My hand stopped on the page. "You've seen it."

"Marta saw it first. Marta sees everything first; it's why she's still alive." Edith folded her hands. "One berth, Dominic. And I imagine by now you've made your list."

"There's no list."

"There is always a list. I made one at your age, though mine had different names on it and the same arithmetic underneath." She rose, walked to the window — no pause on the way, or perhaps a half-pause she disguised as adjusting her sleeve — and stood looking at the orchard. "I will save you the suspense regarding one line of it. I am not going anywhere."

"Edith—"

"I know what's wrong with me better than Prather does; I've known since July. If the heart means to stop, it will stop in Millbrook or it will stop here, and here has better views and worse gossip." She turned. "What I want to know is what you intend to do about the other two. Because you hold the contract, and therefore you hold the decision, and I have watched you carry other people's words for three years without ever once being asked to carry a consequence. It's time someone watched how you bear it."

"That's cruel."

"Yes," she agreed placidly. "It generally is."

I should have argued. I should have said something with teeth in it. Instead I sat very still with the pen drying in my hand, and what I felt — beneath the anger, beneath even the fear — was a horrible flicker of gratitude, because for the first time in three years Edith Calloway was treating me not as furniture but as a person with weight, and it had taken a drowning valley to do it. Self-erasure, I discovered, is a habit like any other, and being dragged out of it hurts the way a cramped limb hurts when the blood comes back.

"I haven't decided," I said finally.

"Good," said Edith. "Decided people stop looking. Keep looking."

* * *

I kept looking all through Wednesday afternoon, and what looking amounted to, in practice, was walking.

I walked down to the barn under the pretext of checking the crates, and Tomás was mending harness he had no right to mend, his hands moving with the competence of a man building a case for his own usefulness, and he didn't look up when I came in but said, quietly, "Thursday?"

"Thursday."

"And the berth goes to—"

"I don't know yet."

He nodded as if that were an answer and not an absence. Then he said, "For what it's worth — if it comes to me against the boy, choose the boy. I've had seven years of running. He's had seven years of anything." And went back to the harness, declining further discussion, leaving me with the peculiar cruelty of having one candidate argue for his own subtraction from the ledger.

I walked up through the orchard, where the apples were dropping uncollected into the grass with soft regular thuds, the whole hillside performing its small funeral at once, and I thought about the phrase *one berth available* and how much of history is conducted in that grammar. Somebody always holds the pen. Somebody always writes *regret* into the formal notice. And the person who receives it reads it five times and calls it logistics.

I walked back past the kitchen window, where Marta was making bread, and through the glass I saw her stop, mid-motion, one hand pressed flat against her breastbone — the same gesture as Edith on the stairs, as if grief ran in the household's bloodstream — before resuming her kneading. Rafael's age. Seven years old, coughing in a low house across a rising river, while his grandmother portioned out forty-one days of beans.

By evening I had stopped pretending I was deciding and admitted I was simply circling the truth I'd already landed on, the way you circle a cold pool before going in.

Because here is what the arithmetic actually showed, once I stopped rounding:

Edith refused to go. That line could be struck — not by mercy, not entirely, but by her own flat refusal and my own cowardly relief at honoring it. One name gone.

Tomás would not take the berth while any alternative existed, and would poison whatever life he lived afterward with the knowledge of the boy he'd displaced. Struck, by his own hand. Two names gone.

Which left Rafael, seven years old, across the river, whom none of us could deliver to safety except by the single berth on a single wagon on a single Thursday — unless.

Unless.

There was a fourth name, wasn't there? There was always a fourth name, the quiet one, the one that had spent three years perfecting the art of not appearing on lists. Dominic Marchetti, driver, reader of letters, furniture. I had reserved that berth in my own mind from the moment I'd read the letter — I could admit that now, standing in the wet orchard in the dark — reserved it the way you reserve oxygen, without thought, as a birthright. Escape. Out. Across the water to Millbrook and a boarding house and a life that happened to other people again, instead of this closing trap of apples and secrets and dying hearts.

The boy needed the berth. The boy got the berth. And I — I would stay, and be drowned in with the rest of them, and it would be noble, and nobility, I suspected, was mostly what people called it when they couldn't afford the ticket.

Except.

Except that smuggling Rafael onto that wagon would require deception — the wagon men searched cargo coming off a property with a fugitive on it, however casually; a seven-year-old under apple crates was a crime with a child's body inside it, and I would be the one committing it, with my name signed at the bottom like everything else I'd ever signed. And except that Marta would need to know, and consent, and forgive me for deciding her grandson's fate without asking her — the exact sin I'd spent the day condemning in myself.

I stood in the dark between the apple rows until my hands went numb, and the river muttered its committee meeting below, and I understood at last what Edith had meant about consequences versus words. I had read everyone's desperate letters for three years and believed reading them made me safe from them. But a decision is also a kind of letter. It has a recipient. It cannot be unsealed.

Inside, the lamplight was yellow in the kitchen windows, and Marta was setting out plates, and Edith's silhouette crossed the parlor curtain once, upright, unhurried, lying to us all with her posture.

Tomorrow I would tell Marta. Tomorrow I would forge a manifest, lie to the wagon men, put a feverish child under apple crates, and give up the only exit I would have until spring — if spring came, if the ford held, if the river granted its permission.

Tonight I went in to supper and passed the study door, and Edith called out without looking up from her desk:

"Well, driver? Is the list finished?"

I stopped. And what came out of me surprised us both — not deference, not wit, but the flat voice of a woman reading terms aloud before signing.

"It's finished. And you're not going to like what's written on it."

Edith set down her pen and turned in her chair, and for the first time all season, she looked afraid.
