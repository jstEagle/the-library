## 14. Confrontation in the Parlor

I rehearsed it eleven times on the walk from the barn to the house. Not the words — the words were simple enough, four of them, the kind of sentence a child could carry: *Edith, we have to talk.* What I rehearsed was the walking itself, the arrangement of my feet, because in nine years of driving that woman I had never once entered a room intending to be seen.

The parlor fire had been fed twice that morning, which told me Marta knew something was coming too. Houses this size develop nervous systems. The logs crackled with the particular self-consciousness of fuel burned for an occasion.

Edith sat by the window with her mending basket, though she hadn't threaded a needle since October and the basket was pure theater. She'd been staging tableaux of domestic calm all week — the mending basket, the half-finished chess game against herself, the seed catalog dog-eared at the sweet pea page. A woman building an alibi out of upholstery.

"Dom." She didn't look up from the catalog. "You've been hovering at doors for three days like a moth with grievances. Sit down before you wear a groove in my floorboards."

"I need to ask you about Aldous Wren."

The page turned. Sweet peas, I noted. She turned the page as though the question had been about sweet peas.

"What about him?"

"You bought his orchard in 1906."

"I did. It's a good orchard. Was."

"Two winters before you bought it, he stopped paying his pickers in cash. Paid them in scrip redeemable only at his own store, where prices ran three times what they should. When they complained, he locked the storehouse. There are men in this valley who watched their children go hungry through two winters so that Aldous Wren could buy a second piano." I heard my own voice doing something I didn't recognize. It had stopped being furniture. "And you knew. You bought the land knowing, and you never said one word about it, not once, not in nine years of dictating letters about justice with your spine straight as a church steeple."

* * *

Let me back up, because I can hear how that sounds — how it sounded even as I said it, like a stranger borrowing my mouth.

Tomás had told me everything the night before, up in the loft, by the light of a shielded lantern he'd rigged from a jam jar. He'd held nothing back, which I suspect was its own kind of test. He told me about the ledger he'd burned at the mill — the wage book, the debt rolls — and then, quieter, in the voice men use for the part that actually hurts, he told me what else had been in those pages before the mill's clerks copied them into their own books years ago: records going back past the mill entirely. Land transactions. Labor contracts. Aldous Wren's orchard accounts, 1904 through 1906, itemized with the tidy cruelty of a man who believed arithmetic absolved him.

"And your Mrs. Calloway," Tomás said, "her name is in there twice. Once as a bidder. Once as the final signature."

"She wouldn't have—"

"Read it however you want," he said, and pulled the blanket Marta had smuggled up over his shoulder like a man pulling a door shut. "But names don't end up in ledgers by accident. Somebody wanted her name in that book. And somebody signed."

I had gone down the ladder with the lantern still burning behind me, which was unlike me, and stood in the frozen yard looking up at the stars as if they might countersign some other version of events. They declined. Stars are terrible correspondents; they answer every letter the same way, with silence and distance.

So: eleven rehearsals, four words, and then I opened my mouth and forty of them came out instead.

* * *

Edith set down the seed catalog. She did it carefully, aligning its edge with the arm of the chair, the way she aligned everything, and when she looked up at me her face was doing something I had never seen it do in nine years.

Nothing.

That's the thing I couldn't get past, later, when I turned the scene over the way I used to turn over letters. Not anger. Not shame. Not even the cool political deftness she deployed on county supervisors and Methodist ministers, the smile that made powerful men feel they'd been patted on the head by history itself. Just — nothing. A face with the lamps out.

"Wren paid market price for labor," she said, "when he paid at all. That's not a defense. It's a fact, and facts are the only currency I trade in anymore."

"The scrip wasn't market price."

"No," she agreed. "It wasn't."

"You knew."

"Yes."

Just that. Yes. No varnish, no pivot into principle, no *but consider the larger movement*, none of the architecture of justification I had spent nearly a decade watching her build around harder truths than this. She let the word sit in the parlor air between us like a stone someone had dropped through a window.

I found, to my horror, that I was waiting for her to explain. Some old trained part of me — the part that read her sentences before sealing them, that had learned her rhythms the way a pianist learns a score — kept expecting the second clause. Edith Calloway did not speak in single syllables. Edith Calloway spoke in periods of twelve seconds or more, each one load-bearing.

The second clause didn't come.

"Do you know what I thought," I said, "when I first came here? I was nineteen. I thought you were the bravest person alive. You'd stand up in front of men who owned the mills and the banks and the sheriff, and you'd tell them the truth to their faces, and you'd make it *land*, and I used to think — I'm driving her carriage and I'm nobody and I'm safe, but she's out there in the open taking the shots, and somebody has to be willing to do that."

"Somebody does," Edith said.

"And the whole time—" My voice did something embarrassing. I steadied it against the mantelpiece the way you steady yourself against anything when the floor tilts. "The whole time you were standing on ground you got from a man who starved children. And every speech, every letter, every time you said *justice* — I was reading those words, Edith. Before anyone else. I sealed them. I carried them. I believed I was carrying something clean."

"Was it?" she asked. "Clean?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." She rose from the chair — slowly; the heart trouble showed in it now, in the way she used the armrest, in the pause at full height — and went to the window. Below, the orchard lay under its first serious snow, rows of bare trees like a congregation waiting to be told something. "You want me to say I was young, or ignorant, or deceived. None of those are true, so I won't insult us both. I knew what Wren was. I also knew that his creditors wanted the land fast and cheap, that no one else would bid against the scandal, and that whoever bought it would either continue his arrangements or end them. I ended them. The first spring I owned it, pickers were paid in coin."

"At your rates. Which you could afford because you bought poisoned land at a poisoned price."

"At rates I set," she said. "Yes."

"And the families who starved? The winters before? Does buying the field after the fact settle the account?"

She was quiet a long moment. Snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, Tomás was no doubt listening at the base of the wall, because he listened everywhere now, the way hunted things do, and I felt a brief mad urge to throw open the window and shout down into the snow: *Ask her yourself. She'll give you the same four minutes of honesty she gave me. It's the honesty that's unbearable.*

"Survival is not a confession," Edith said finally, without turning around. "Every woman in this valley survives by means she'd rather not discuss. The mill girls take wages from men they despise. Marta works for a wage I set, in a house she couldn't refuse. You, Dominic — you survived nine years by making yourself invisible in the home of a woman whose money came from exactly the kind of man you now find unforgivable. Did you investigate my holdings when you took the position? Did you ask where the salary came from before you decided your conscience was clean?"

"That's not the same."

"It isn't," she admitted. "You're right. It's smaller, and smaller is not the same as innocent. But it rhymes." Now she turned, and there was something in her face at last — not the nothing, but worse, a kind of weary recognition, as though she'd been waiting nine years for this conversation the way one waits for weather. "You've built yourself a story, Dom. The driver who reads the letters, keeps her hands clean, stays outside history. And it's worked, hasn't it? Right up until a man in the barn handed you a page from a ledger, and now the whole neat little structure is on fire, and you've come in here to put the match in my hand because it's easier than holding it yourself."

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"I read every letter you ever dictated," I said.

"I know."

"Did you ever once dictate one that told the truth about Wren? To the papers, the league, anyone?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the truth about Wren was also the truth about me, and I had work to do that required me to remain upright in public opinion." She said it flatly, a schedule read aloud. "I chose. That's what choosing is. You weigh what you can lose against what you can change, and you pay, and you keep working, and you do not confuse your payment with your innocence. If that makes me fallen in your eyes, Dominic, then fall. But understand what you're falling *from*. It isn't purity. Purity was never on offer in this house or any other. It was a story you told yourself about me because you needed one woman in the world to be worth believing in."

I want to report that I answered her well. I want to report that I said something with edges, something worthy of the moment, something that would look fine in a letter.

What I did was stand there while my faith — nine years of it, patient and unexamined and warm as a banked coal — cracked straight down the middle with a sound I heard more in my chest than my ears. Because she was right, and being right was the cruelest thing she could have done. I had needed her to be clean. I had needed the letters to be clean. And now I understood that I had spent nine years curating a saint, the way I curated her correspondence, keeping only the pages that flattered, sealing away the rest unread in my own heart.

"I have to think," I said. It was a small sentence. It was all I had left in inventory.

"Take the time you need," Edith said. "We have until thaw, and then perhaps less." Something moved across her face then — quickly, gone — and I was too cracked open to read it properly, though later I would turn it over and over: not guilt, exactly. Fear. She had glanced toward the window, toward the road, toward the ferry landing beyond the flooded fields, as if the conversation had reminded her of a clock somewhere.

I went to the door. My hand was on the latch when she spoke again, softer, and this time the steel was gone and what remained was almost — almost — the woman from the early years, the one who'd taught me to drive in the rain.

"Dom. Whatever you decide about me — the boy in the barn stays warm. That order stands regardless of what you think of me now."

"It's not your order to give anymore," I said, and heard, as I said it, that it was true, and that saying it had changed something structural in the house, the way removing one brick changes a chimney.

I closed the door behind me gently. Gentle, even now. Old habits seal themselves.

In the hall, snow-light through the tall windows, my breath loud as a bellows. And it was there — standing in that corridor between the parlor and the rest of my life — that I heard the front gate hinge cry, and looked out through the frosted pane, and saw Vivian Calloway coming up the drive through the falling snow with a leather document case under her arm and a surveyor's rolled map in her gloved hand, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who already knows what she owns.

She was early. She was weeks early. And she was heading, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, not toward the front door —

— but around it, toward the barn.
