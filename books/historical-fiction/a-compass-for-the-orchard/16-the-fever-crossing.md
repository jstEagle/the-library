## 16. The Fever Crossing

There is a particular arithmetic that happens in the dark, when a house is asleep and one woman is awake with her boots on the wrong feet. I had done sums all my life — miles to town, pence per pound of nails, the number of Edith's letters against the hours of daylight left to post them. But I had never done this sum: one berth, three names, and my own name written last on the list in pencil, as if even my arithmetic couldn't be bothered to make me permanent.

The wagon company's man had come to the gate two days after the ford was declared drowning-distance from safe. He was a red-faced fellow named Oakes who spoke as though the river were an employee who'd disappointed him personally. One final run, he said. The wagon would leave at dawn on Thursday, cross at the narrows while the water still permitted it, and after that the valley would keep its own counsel until thaw. One berth for a passenger. The rest of the wagon's capacity belonged to apples, because apples were what the county would still pay to haul.

Edith could not go. She would not say so in those words, but she had begun keeping a chair by every window in the house, as if planting herself at intervals, and a woman who does that has already decided where she means to finish. Marta's grandson, Rafael, lay fevered across the river at his mother's house, and Marta had taken to standing at the gate each evening looking west with an expression I recognized from letters I'd read and never answered: the face of someone composing an appeal they know is unaddressed. And Tomás — Tomás would hang. That was not melodrama. The mill men had stopped pretending they wanted him merely questioned.

And me. I had reserved the berth in my own name eleven days earlier, quietly, through the wagon office before the wires degraded, because I am not a fool and never have been. Invisibility has its uses; one of them is that nobody thinks to ask whether the furniture has made plans. I had imagined myself on that wagon seat with everything I owned in one carpetbag — which, being everything I owned, weighed almost nothing — riding out over the narrows while the orchard shrank behind me into something that had happened to other people.

I want to be honest, which is a new habit for me: I did not give up the berth in a rush of noble feeling. I gave it up the way a tooth comes out — badly, at night, with my own hand on the pliers.

* * *

It was Marta who broke me, though she never knew it, or has never said so. Wednesday evening she came down from the boy's mother's place across the fields — no, she couldn't cross; the low road was gone by then; she came back from the high bank where she'd stood shouting across the water like a gull, receiving news secondhand from a neighbor with a strong voice and no tenderness in it. *Still fevered. Not worse. Not better.* Not worse, not better — the two most expensive words in any language.

She came into the kitchen and began, without a word, to wrap biscuits in cloth. Not the household biscuits. Her own ration, the portion she had been quietly shorting herself since October, which I knew about because I had helped her steal flour for Tomás and had watched her do the same arithmetic then. She wrapped four biscuits, then unwrapped one and put it back, then wrapped it again anyway.

"Marta," I said.

"He is seven years old," she said. "He will eat this in the wagon."

I looked at the cloth bundle and understood, with the slow cold of water finding the seam in a boot, that she was not asking me anything. She was informing me of a plan that required a wagon, and there was exactly one wagon, leaving in ten hours, carrying apples and one passenger, and the passenger was me.

"You can't go with him," I said. "There's one berth."

"I know what there is." She tied the cloth with the vicious neatness of a woman who has tied a thousand things and been undone by all of them. "A seven-year-old can ride in a crate among apples if somebody lifts the lid now and again. A seven-year-old cannot cross a flooded river alone, and his grandmother cannot swim, and his mother cannot leave the other children, and I" — here her voice did something I had never heard it do; it dropped all its iron and became simply old — "I cannot carry him there, Dominic. My legs are done. God is punishing my knees for my opinions."

So there it was. The sum I had been avoiding resolved itself without my consent: the boy went in a crate, the crate needed a lifter, the lifter needed a berth, and the berth had my name penciled in it like a placeholder in a draft letter — *and so forth, regards, etc.*

I said, "Let me get the crate," because it was either that or start crying, and crying in Marta's kitchen is a firing offense.

* * *

We worked by lantern in the barn, past midnight, and I will tell you that smuggling a child is materially easier than smuggling a grown man, but infinitely heavier. Tomás came down from the loft when he heard us and asked no questions, which is the finest manners I have ever seen in a fugitive. He reinforced the crate bottom with slats from an apple box and bored air holes with an awl, spacing them like constellations, and when Rafael's mother's message had come saying the boy's fever had turned him dreamy and pliable — which is the only mercy fevers extend — we counted on the dreaminess to keep him quiet.

Rafael arrived just before dawn, carried down the high bank on a door taken off its hinges by two neighbors who then looked at me with the particular blankness of men who wish to owe nobody anything. He was small. That was the thing that undid me, in the end. All my dread had built a creature of consequence — a cause, a symbol, a stone in the scale. What came off that door was a hot, damp, seven-year-old weight who smelled of camphor and said, when Marta woke him, "Are we going in the apple wagon, Nana?" as though it were a birthday.

"In the apple wagon," Marta said, and her voice was iron again, welded back together overnight. "You are going to be apples until I say otherwise."

He laughed. A fevered child laughing in a barn at four in the morning is a sound that will follow you, I find, into any room you enter for the rest of your life.

We settled him in with blankets and the biscuit bundle and a jar of water with a corked spout Tomás had rigged. Then Marta looked at me over the crate lid and said, "You're coming too. You'll lift the lid at the halfway tree."

"The halfway tree?"

"The big cottonwood. You'll know it. Everyone knows it." She said it as though it were settled, and for a moment I let it be settled, let myself imagine the two of us riding out, the boy alive beneath us, the river declining to kill anyone that day. It was a beautiful thirty seconds. Then I heard Oakes's voice in my head, red-faced and literal: *one berth, missus. The wagon's contracted for one.* Contracts are the only scripture I've ever kept faith with, and I knew its commandments by heart.

"Marta," I said. "One berth. I checked the contract twice. I wrote the contract's cousin, practically."

"Then you'll stand on the back rail holding the tarpaulin like a hired man. Oakes won't count what he doesn't pay for."

"He'll count."

"Then don't be countable." She took my wrist. Her hand was a bird's foot with a smith's grip. "Dominic. I am asking. I have never asked anyone living for anything, and I am asking."

And there it was — the whole architecture of my life laid open like a letter read over someone's shoulder. Eleven years of being unaskable, uncountable, furniture with a fine memory. I had believed it was safety. Standing in that barn with lantern light in the knot holes and a fevered child breathing applescented air under my feet, I understood it had also been cowardice wearing safety's coat, and that the coat had never fit at the shoulders.

"All right," I said. "But if Oakes throws me in the river, you're explaining it to Edith."

Marta almost smiled. "Edith will want to know why you didn't swim better."

* * *

Oakes came at gray dawn with four horses and a driver named Pell who chewed tobacco like it owed him money. We loaded crates — Tomás hauling them out of the barn with a laborer's flatness of expression that I admired and feared in equal measure — and Oakes checked his manifest twice, licked his pencil, and said, "One passenger. Miss Marchetti, is it? You'll ride front with Pell."

"Change of plan," I said, and heard my own voice do something new: lie, fluently, in daylight, to a man with a ledger. "My employer requires her driver back by Friday. I'm traveling as cargo supervision. Put me wherever there's room."

"Cargo supervision isn't a thing."

"It is if you're paying me," I said, and looked at him the way Edith looks at county clerks, and Oakes — bless his small imagination — decided that whatever was happening was above his pay grade, which is the only prayer most men ever say. He waved me toward the tail of the wagon.

Vivian watched from the porch. She had been watching since first light, arms crossed, surveyor's eyes taking inventory of a departure. As I climbed onto the tailboard she came down the steps, crossed the frozen mud in sharp-heeled boots, and stood below me with her breath steaming.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I'm supervising cargo."

"You're leaving." She said it again, flatter, and I realized it wasn't a question and wasn't quite about me. Everyone left, Vivian had decided long ago; her whole grievance with her mother was a departure she hadn't witnessed. She looked up at me, and for one unguarded second I saw not the antagonist but the daughter, the one whose unsent letter sat in a drawer upstairs rewritten twenty times. "Take care of the boy," she said, so quietly that only I could hear it, and walked away before I could answer — which meant she had known about the crate all along, and had chosen, like everyone in that mad household, to know it silently.

Edith did not come out at all. But as the wagon pulled through the gate I saw her at an upstairs window, one hand against the glass, and I raised my hand, and she did not raise hers, and somehow that was more honest than waving. Goodbye, said her stillness. Or possibly: *come back.* With Edith the two were always difficult to distinguish.

* * *

I will not describe the whole crossing, because some things deserve to keep their privacy. I will describe the halfway tree.

The narrows were running brown and muscular, higher than Oakes liked — he stood in the shallows up to his knees swearing at it, then swore at the horses, then at the county. The water hit the floorboards at the deepest channel and stayed there, an inch of moving cold that found every gap in my boots. I stood on the rail with the tarpaulin half over my shoulders like a woman playing at martyrdom, and between swells I knelt on the crate and lifted the lid.

Rafael looked up at me. His fever had broken sometime in the night — Marta had felt his forehead at the gate and gone very still, and then very busy, which is how Marta celebrates — and what looked out at me was not a sick child but a curious one, seven years old and riding in a crate full of apples, having the best story of his young life.

"Is it true the river ate a bridge?" he said.

"It's true it's chewing on one."

"When it spits it out, will there be a new bridge?"

"Probably a better one," I said. "Counties are lazy but they're vain."

He nodded gravely and pulled the lid down himself, and I stood up on the rolling, groaning wagon with riverwater in my boots and thought, absurdly, of every letter I had ever sealed on Edith's behalf. All those words dispatched safely out of other people's mouths, folded shut, wax pressed down, history kept at arm's length. I had spent eleven years believing that sealing a letter was the same as finishing one.

The far bank rose ahead, mud-slick and real, and Pell hauled the horses up it, and the wheels caught, and we crested, and Marta was already scrambling to the crate, and I pried the lid, and Rafael climbed out into his grandmother's arms smelling of apples and camphor and entirely, outrageously alive. Marta held him and did not cry, because Marta would sooner die, but her hands shook on the biscuit bundle as she fed him, and Oakes pretended to inspect a harness buckle very thoroughly, because even Oakes knew what he was looking at.

Then the wagon went back for its return crossing, and I stood on the bank beside a woman and her grandson and watched the river take my exit.

It didn't happen dramatically. Rivers rarely bother. The wagon made the channel fine, was halfway up the shallows on our side of the water, and then the current, which had been merely rude all morning, simply leaned its shoulder in. The near wheel lifted. Pell shouted. The horses lunged, and the wagon swung broadside like a door, and four hundred crates of Calloway apples went into the flood in a slow red-brown avalanche, bobbing away downstream like the orchard itself deciding to emigrate. The team tore free and dragged Pell ashore. Oakes stood hip-deep in wreckage, watching his livelihood float to the sea, and said, with terrible calm, "Well. There's the season done."

No more crossings. The ford was drowned, the wagon was kindling somewhere below the narrows, and the valley closed around us like a book.

Marta took Rafael's hand. "You'll stay with us," she said to me, in the tone she used for facts. "Until thaw. You'll drive nothing, you'll carry nothing, you'll sit in that kitchen and drink my coffee and complain about it."

"Marta—"

"You gave your berth to a child," she said. "Do not insult it by moping."

I laughed, and the laugh cracked something, and for a moment I stood on that muddy bank with water running out of my boot heels and let myself feel the size of the door that had just shut. No coach. No clean exit. No returning to being furniture in some other house, invisible and unaskable, history happening politely to other people. Every road out of that valley now ran through spring, and spring ran through the people I'd chosen, and the choosing had been done in a barn at four in the morning by a woman with her boots on the wrong feet.

We walked up the high road toward the ferry landing and the long way home, the three of us, Rafael chattering about bridges. Behind us the river kept the valley, and ahead of us, somewhere past the ridge, Edith was sitting by a window with a heart that was quietly failing, and a drawer full of letters she'd never sent, and a daughter in the east wing making maps of everything she meant to take.

And in my breast pocket, folded smaller than it had any right to be, was a sheet of paper I had started writing three nights earlier and had told no one about — not addressed yet, not signed, barely begun. *To the County Clerk,* it said. *Regarding the property of the late H. Calloway orchard, and certain matters of record concerning the winters of 1909 and 1910.*

Tomás's burned ledger had ghosts, and I had heard them speak, and the hearing we'd convened in that kitchen had judged them — but judgment whispered inside a flooded valley is not the same as testimony the world can read. The river had just made certain I couldn't leave. It had not made certain I couldn't write.

I put my hand in my pocket and felt the paper, warm from my body, and kept walking.
