## 17. Edith's Collapse

The frost came in the night like a thief who had learned patience, and by morning every apple still hanging in the orchard wore a coat of glass. I saw it from the kitchen window while I waited for the kettle, and I thought — the way a cook thinks, which is to say the way a general thinks with worse equipment — *there goes the last of the cider apples, and there goes Edith Calloway's excuse to keep walking out to the trees every day.*

She walked out anyway. Of course she did. I have known her forty years, and in all that time the woman has treated her own body as an unreliable employee: kept on out of sentiment, given light duties, never consulted in matters of importance.

I heard her come back. That was the thing. Edith Calloway did not come back from anywhere quietly. She came through doors the way weather does, announcing herself, demanding tea, pronouncing judgment on something — the county, the river, the century. That morning she came through the back step slowly, one hand on the frame, breathing like she'd climbed the stairs of a courthouse instead of crossing forty yards of frozen grass.

"Your garden's dead," she told me, as if reporting a scandal among people we both knew.

"It'll come back," I said.

"So will I," she said, and sat down harder than she meant to on the kitchen bench, and that is when I knew she was lying, because Edith Calloway never lied about anything except the things that mattered most, and she always lied about those beautifully. This lie was clumsy. Clumsy lies are the dying kind.

I gave her tea with a spoonful of the good honey I'd been rationing for the fever — Marta's grandson's fever, though thank God and the wagon that sank, the boy was across the river and mending, and there was nothing left between us and the spring but cold and each other. She held the cup in both hands and looked at me over the rim with those pale eyes that had frightened three generations of sheriffs.

"You've gone gray in the face," I said.

"I've been gray in the face since 1898, Marta. It's called dignity."

"It's called sitting down before you fall down."

She drank her tea. Her hands were steady, but her breath made small hitching sounds at the top, like a hinge that wanted oil. Neither of us mentioned it. We were both too well brought up, which is a flaw in my character I have never forgiven my mother for instilling.

* * *

By noon she had taken to her bed "for the warmth of it," which fooled nobody, least of all the bed. I sent Dominic up with broth at two o'clock and she came down at four with a look on her face I had not seen there before. Dominic reads faces the way other people read newspapers — thoroughly, silently, and without moving her mouth — so whatever she'd read upstairs had been worth reading.

"How is she?" I asked.

"She dictated a letter," Dominic said. She hung her coat on its peg with great precision. "For Vivian."

"The old one?"

"There's only ever been the one," Dominic said. "Rewritten. She wants me to write it out fresh. Clean copy."

"And will you?"

Dominic looked at her hands. Forty years I've watched this girl — not a girl anymore, but the eyes never age past the year you started minding other people's business for them — and I have never once seen her hesitate over a pen. Now she stood in my kitchen holding her own fingers like borrowed tools.

"She asked me why I never sealed certain letters," Dominic said finally. "Back when I worked for her. Years ago. She said she chose me because I noticed which ones went out heavy." She almost smiled. "I thought she hired me for my driving."

"What did you tell her?"

"That history happens to letter-writers, not drivers." She took the coat off the peg again and put it back on, which was unlike her, then took it off again, which was more unlike her. "Marta, the letter she's dictating isn't the letter in the drawer. It's the same words. She's had them by heart for thirty years. But the way she said them—" She stopped. Dominic does not stop mid-sentence; finishing sentences is how she hides. "It sounded like a confession being rehearsed."

"Well," I said, stirring a pot that needed no stirring, "she's got things to confess. More than that girl knows, and less than Tomás knows, and exactly what that burned ledger would have told anyone with the stomach to read it." I hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. The house was getting to all of us — too small, too cold, too full of true things nobody had consented to hear.

Dominic looked at me for a long moment. Two keepers of secrets, circling politely, right to the end.

"Soup's on the stove," I said, which is what I say instead of everything.

* * *

That evening the frost deepened and so did she. I sat with her while Dominic wrote out the fair copy in the parlor, the scratch of the pen coming faintly up through the floorboards like a mouse doing sums. Edith lay propped against pillows she'd embroidered herself decades ago — *HOME*, they said, in blue cross-stitch, because the woman had never once resisted the opportunity to be ironic.

"Marta," she said. "You've been stealing from your own plate to feed that organizer for two months. Don't argue. I count the butter."

"You count nothing. You're a sentimental tyrant with a good nose."

"I count the butter." Her breath caught, steadied. "It was well done of you. Don't let it become a habit. Generosity is like sherry — medicinal in small doses, ruinous as a practice."

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

Something moved behind her face then — quick, dark, the shape of a thing surfacing and diving again. "I tell myself many things," she said. "That's the trouble with a long life. You accumulate explanations the way a river accumulates drowned trees. Eventually you're mostly debris, flowing."

"You're not debris."

"No," she agreed placidly. "But I'm not far upstream of it."

I should have argued with her. That is my single regret of that night — not that I failed to save her, because no power under heaven or above it was going to save her heart, which had been running on principle and stubbornness long after the muscle itself had resigned — but that I didn't argue. I smoothed her blanket instead. Cooks smooth blankets. It is the gesture we fall back on when the truth has gotten too large for the room.

"Send Dominic up," she said, as I rose to go. "And Marta. Whatever you hear in the next hour — and you will hear it, you hear everything in this house, you always have — remember that I meant to burn it myself. Every winter for thirty years, I took it out to do it. And every winter I found a reason. Remember that about me. Not the reasons. The taking it out."

I remembered it later. God help me, I remembered it, and it changed nothing, and I am glad of nothing so much as that I tried to make it change something anyway.

* * *

I heard it from the kitchen, as predicted. Not the words — the words came later, from Dominic's white mouth, in fragments — but the shape of it. A voice rising, falling, breaking at intervals like ice underfoot. Once Edith laughed, a short terrible sound, the laugh of a woman hearing her own eulogy delivered by the wrong priest. Then quiet. Long quiet. Then Dominic's footsteps going to the stove, and the sound of paper catching, and a silence afterward so complete I could hear the frost forming on the windows.

When Dominic came down she went straight past me to the sink and ran the tap over her wrists. Her face was the face of someone who has done a thing she believes correct and cannot locate a single feeling to support the belief.

"She asked me to burn it," Dominic said to the window. "After I read it aloud. All of it. To her face, and she listened to every word like she was checking it against a ledger." Her voice did something I had never heard it do — cracked down the middle, young. "Marta, it named names. Dates. Two winters. What the pickers were fed, what they weren't. What the wage book showed before Tomás ever touched it. She bought the orchard knowing. She said so, in her own words, in her own hand, and then she stood there in her own bed and watched me feed her own confession into the fire."

"Why did you do it?" I asked, though I knew.

"Because she asked." Dominic shut off the tap. "Because she was dying, and she asked, and I have spent eleven years doing exactly what she asked with exactly her words, and my hands didn't know any other way to finish the sentence." She dried her wrists on a towel with slow, furious thoroughness. "I hated every second. I hate that the ashes smell like nothing. A whole truth, Marta — wages and winters and women who starved picking apples none of us will ever be hungry enough to understand — and it smells like nothing. Like a burnt page. That's all a person amounts to, once the paper's gone."

I took the towel from her and made her sit and put soup in front of her, because that is my liturgy and it was all I had. She ate maybe half. Somewhere overhead a floorboard creaked, and we both looked up like guilty animals, though Edith was past creeping about.

"She said mercy," Dominic said at last, quietly. "She said, *this is the only mercy I have ever been able to afford, and I could only afford it because I won't be here to collect.* And then she thanked me. She thanked me, Marta. For burning the proof. What do you do with gratitude like that? Where do you put it?"

"Nowhere," I said. "You set it on the step and you let the frost take it. Some gifts are just debts wearing their Sunday clothes."

She nodded, and stared into her bowl, and I saw the exact moment the grief arrived — not grief for Edith, or not only; grief for the version of herself that had believed reading other people's letters kept you clean. Eleven years of being furniture, and in one evening promoted to accomplice. Invisibility curdling, as I'd watched it curdle in her for months, into something with her fingerprints all over it now.

* * *

Edith died at dawn, which she would have considered tidy of her. The first hard frost still on the fields, the sun coming up red over the water, the whole valley looking like the inside of a furnace about to be banked for winter. I was holding her hand. She squeezed it once — a command, unmistakably, even through the failing of everything else — and then let go in the way of letting go that is not sleep.

Vivian was downstairs within the hour. I will say this for the woman: whatever she was, she did not pretend to be more. She stood in the doorway of the sickroom in her sharp city clothes, looking at the bed where the woman who had unmade her childhood lay arranged and finished, and her face did not crumble. It calculated. Grief and arithmetic arriving together, holding hands, the family resemblance to her mother so strong in that moment that I had to leave the room.

Dominic told her about the letter — that there had been one, that it existed, that it was gone. I don't know what passed between them precisely; I was in the kitchen starting bread that nobody would eat, because bread is what I know how to do when the world tips over. But I heard Vivian's voice rise once — *"You burned it? She made you— and you just* did it*?"* — and I heard Dominic answer in a voice flat as a frozen pond:

"Yes. And I'd do it again. And I hate that. Both of those are true, and you can decide which one makes me yours or hers or nobody's, Miss Calloway, but you'll have to decide it without my help."

Then, quieter, Vivian: "Burned how completely?"

And Dominic, after a pause I felt in my teeth: "Completely. Why?"

No answer came down. Just footsteps — Vivian's, going toward the study, where the desk was, where the drawers were, where thirty years of rewritten drafts had lived until one of them finally left the world by fire. And I stood in my kitchen with flour on my hands and the dawn coming up red, and I understood, with the particular clarity of a woman whose whole life is noticing what's missing from an inventory, that the letter was not entirely gone.

Tomás Reyes knew what that letter said. He'd found the drafts in the barn loft's old correspondence crate months ago, before he was a fugitive, when he was just a man sleeping rough among another man's apple boxes. He'd read it. He'd never said a word — but he knew. And Vivian, standing in her dead mother's study, was at this moment beginning to suspect that somewhere in this frozen, flooded, lawless household, buried in some crate or some conscience, there might survive a copy of exactly the document that could ruin her inheritance or redeem it.

And the only tribunal this valley was going to get — with the bridge down, the law across the river, and four of us plus a fugitive trapped together until thaw — would convene, if Dominic had her way, in my kitchen. Before my stove. Over my table.

I put my hands flat on the dough and pushed down, and began, as one does, to plan the seating.
