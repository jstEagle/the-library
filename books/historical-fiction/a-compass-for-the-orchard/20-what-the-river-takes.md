## 20. What the River Takes

The river had been talking to itself all week.

I'd grown up on a river in Sonora that did the same thing — a low muttering under everything, like a man rehearsing an argument he intends to win. My grandmother said you could hear a river change its mind before it did it. I thought of her often that spring, standing in the barn loft at dawn with my hands wrapped around a mug of Marta's coffee substitute, which tasted like punishment but was hot, and listened to the Calloway fork of the river stop muttering and start declaiming.

Three weeks since the hearing. Three weeks since the household had ruled my fire justified and my person conscripted, and I had gone from fugitive to foreman without ever being asked whether I wanted the promotion. The orchard had needed me, which is the only kind of offer anyone makes in this valley. Pruning to do, grafts to cut before the sap ran, rows to walk with a knife in your pocket and an opinion about every tree. I had opinions about every tree. It turns out this is what I am for.

Vivian found me among the Gravensteins on the fourth morning of the melt.

I want to say something generous about how we had arrived at our arrangement honestly, two adults who had weighed their secrets and signed a treaty. The truth is less dignified: we avoided each other the way two dogs avoid the one fence line where they both know they are outmatched. She held my silence about her mother in her pocket like a knife she hadn't decided against. I held hers about me. We were the two most dangerous people on the property and we spent our mornings forty yards apart, pretending to admire the same trees.

That morning she stopped at the end of my row and stood there in a wool coat too fine for mud, watching my knife work with the expression of a woman auditing a receipt.

"You cut too close," she said.

"The tree disagrees."

"Trees don't have opinions."

"Then they're the only ones in this house who don't."

Something moved across her face — not a smile, nothing so surrendered as that, but the place where a smile keeps its coat checked. She looked down the slope toward the river, and I saw her do the arithmetic everyone on the property had been doing all week: the water was higher than the fence posts, higher than the second rail, and still climbing, and the boathouse — her mother's boathouse, built by a previous owner out of lumber he'd never paid for, though nobody knew that yet except me and the dead — sat low on its pilings right in the throat of the current.

"Should someone move the boats?" she asked.

"The boats are already gone. Marta's nephew took them up to the high rack Tuesday."

"Then what's in the boathouse?"

"Rope. A crate of Edith's papers I haven't sorted. The good saw." I shrugged. "Nothing that can't drown."

She looked at me like I'd said something obscene. This, I understood, was the difference between us. I had watched a ledger burn and felt clean. She had watched her inheritance flood and felt robbed. Both of us standing in the same orchard, both of us certain the other one didn't know what things cost.

"I'm going to walk the levee," she said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't asking permission, Mr. Reyes."

"No," I said. "You weren't."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the levee that she did not, because I had grown up on rivers and she had grown up on ledgers: a levee doesn't fail where the water is loudest. It fails where the water goes quiet, because quiet means the water has found a way through and no longer needs to argue. There was a stretch below the boathouse where the current bent inward and made a smooth green tongue of water, flat as poured milk, and every hour that tongue got wider and quieter, and every hour I liked it less.

I went back to the trees. This is the sin I will carry, small as a prune stone: I let her go, because she hadn't asked, and because I was angry, and because a man who has been hunted learns that the safest distance between himself and other people's trouble is the whole width of an orchard.

I lasted eleven minutes.

* * *

The sound carried uphill before the sight did — not a scream, worse than a scream, the groan of green timber giving up its argument with physics. Then Dominic's voice from somewhere near the kitchen garden, sharp as a whip crack: *"Tomás!"*

I ran. I ran the way I hadn't run since the night at the mill, when running was the whole of my religion, and I came down the slope fast enough to see it all laid out like a diagram: the boathouse listing, one piling snapped and standing in the water like a broken tooth; the roof folded at the middle; and Vivian on the downstream corner, hip-deep, both hands locked on the ridge beam as the structure leaned over her with the slow terrible patience of everything the river does.

She wasn't drowning yet. She was about to be pinned, which is worse, because pinning takes longer.

"The rope!" I shouted, and didn't wait to see if anyone heard, because there was no time for rope anyway — the lean was accelerating, the whole roof swinging on its one living piling like a door deciding to shut. I went into the water upstream of her, let the current slam me against the wall of the boathouse, grabbed the eave, and hauled myself along it hand over hand with the water trying to peel me off like fruit skin.

"Let go," I told her.

"It's holding me up!"

"It's going to hold you *under*. On three. One—"

She let go on two. Everyone lets go on two; I have decided to believe this is faith rather than bad counting. The roof came down where she'd been with a sound like God closing a book, and the wave it threw knocked us both off our feet, and then the river had us.

I have nearly drowned twice now, and I will tell you the thing nobody believes: it is orderly. The water is not malicious. It simply has somewhere to be and you are in the way, and it handles you the way you handle a sack of grain — efficiently, without hatred. I got an arm around Vivian. She got both hands around me, which was a problem, because a drowning person holding on is two drownings wearing one coat.

"Breathe when you can," I yelled, which is advice that sounds insane until you need it. "Don't fight sideways. Let it take us to the bend."

The bend was the willow stand, and the willows were drowned to their shoulders but their branches were in the water, and I put out one hand and closed it on a branch thinner than my wrist and felt the whole tree take my weight and *hold*, which is more than I have ever been able to say about any institution.

We hung there. The river pulled. The willow, indifferent and heroic, pulled back. And then there were boots in the shallows — Marta's nephew, and behind him Dominic, waist-deep and white-faced, hauling on a coil of the good saw-roping she must have grabbed from the boathouse wreckage, throwing it with a sidearm motion that told me she had done this before or dreamed it enough times to matter.

"Loop it! Under the arms!" she was shouting, and I thought, absurdly, *she reads letters, where did she learn knots* — and then the rope hit the water beside us and there was no room in my head for anything.

They pulled Vivian out first. Of course they did; she was lightest and highest-born and nearest. When they pulled me, the river argued for one last moment, got its fingers into my collar, and then gave me up with the petulance of a creditor accepting partial payment. I came out onto the mud on my hands and knees and coughed up half the tributary system of the county, and above me Dominic crouched down, dripping, her hands shaking so badly she had to wrap the rope around them to make them stop.

"You," she said, "are a very stupid man."

"He saves people," Marta's nephew offered, "it's his whole thing."

"Shut up, Emilio."

* * *

Vivian sat wrapped in blankets by the kitchen stove for four hours, refusing tea, refusing dry clothes, refusing everything except the right to stare at the middle distance with her jaw set. I dried out more slowly by the barn fire, in my own company, which suited us both. But near evening she came out to the barn doorway, and we looked at each other across the smell of apples and wet wool, and she said:

"You came in after me."

"You were going under."

"I've done the sums, Mr. Reyes. You had nothing to gain and your life to lose. Men of my acquaintance do not make that trade." She paused. "Men of my *mother's* acquaintance certainly don't."

"Maybe I miscalculated."

"You don't strike me as a man who miscalculates."

"Then maybe," I said, working a knot loose that didn't need loosening, because a man needs something to do with his hands while he says true things, "I have decided this is mine now. The orchard. The river. All of it, including the parts that try to kill me. You can't inherit a place by deed alone, señorita. Somebody has to be willing to fish it out of the water."

She stood in the doorway a long time. Behind her the sky was doing its enormous spring-evening business, pink and indifferent, and somewhere downhill the river had gone back to muttering, satisfied for now.

"My mother bought this land knowing what the last owner did," she said quietly. "Dominic told me. After the hearing." Her chin came up. "I am keeping the deed anyway."

"I know," I said. "I'd think less of you if you didn't. Land isn't an apology. It's just land. What you do with it —" I gestured at the rows, darkening now, ten thousand bare trees waiting on the thaw — "that part hasn't been written yet."

"And you intend to write some of it."

"I intend to prune some of it. Writing is Dominic's department."

That earned me the thing I'd seen checking its coat three weeks ago: not quite a smile, but close enough that a man could warm his hands at it.

"Foreman," she said, testing the word. "Through the thaw, per the verdict. After that we renegotiate." She turned to go, then stopped. "Mr. Reyes. If you had gone under today — if the willow hadn't held — would anyone have known? About the ledger? About why you ran?"

"Emilio knows. Dominic knows. Marta knows." I shrugged. "You know. That's more witnesses than most men get."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She left. I stayed by the fire until it burned down to a red argument, and I thought about my grandmother on her river, and about the difference between a sentence and a sentence — the kind you serve and the kind you say — and how I had come to this valley carrying the first and was somehow, tree by tree, letter by letter, building myself the second.

I was still thinking it when Dominic appeared in the doorway with a lantern and an envelope in her hand, and her face was the face of a woman who has done something irreversible and cannot decide whether to be terrified or proud.

"Tomás," she said. "There's something I've done. I think you should sit down."
