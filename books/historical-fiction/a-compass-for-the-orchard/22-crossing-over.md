## 22. Crossing Over

The new bridge opened on a Thursday, which felt appropriate. Thursdays had always been the day Edith dictated her most dangerous letters — the ones to editors, to legislators, to men who needed reminding that women could count votes. She would have appreciated the symmetry. She would also have complained about the county's workmanship, and she would not have been wrong. The planks rang hollow when the wagon crossed them, like a drum skinning over water, and every board seemed to say *temporary* in a language only bridges speak.

I drove the first wagon across because nobody asked me not to.

That is the strange inheritance of a year spent being furniture: even now, with everything changed, people still look through me at the road ahead. I let them. I have learned there are worse fates than being underestimated, though I intend to spend considerably less time enjoying this one than I used to.

* * *

The morning of the crossing, Marta packed me a basket as though I were going to war instead of to a coach station forty miles off. Bread, hard cheese, a jar of her quince preserves — the last of them, made from fruit she'd hoarded through winter with the cunning of a woman who had fed a fugitive out of her own ration bowl for four months and never once been caught by anyone but me.

"You'll eat properly," she said, pressing the basket into my hands with the authority of a general surrendering territory. "You never eat properly."

"I ate properly for eleven years in your kitchen, Marta."

"And got thinner every year. Like a candle." She looked me up and down, this woman who had taught me more about courage than any pamphlet Edith ever slipped under my door. "You're doing the right thing. Going."

"Am I?"

"No." She said it flatly, and then, seeing my face, softened by exactly one degree, which for Marta was a flood of tenderness. "There is no right thing. There is only the thing you can live inside. Go find out which one yours is."

I kissed her floury cheek. She allowed it for two seconds, then swatted me toward the door like a cat that has decided affection has gone on long enough.

* * *

Vivian was waiting by the barn, wearing riding clothes cut so precisely they seemed less sewn than negotiated. Behind her, Tomás was mending a crate — his sentence, his refuge, his work — and though he kept his head down, I saw him listening the way he always listened, with his whole spine.

"You're really going," Vivian said. Not a question. Vivian did not ask questions; she issued observations and waited for you to fail to contradict them.

"The coach leaves at eight."

"I know. I read the schedule." Of course she had. She'd probably memorized the coachman's name and his debts. "I want you to understand something before you go." She straightened, and for a moment I saw Edith in her so clearly it hurt — the same jaw set against the same stubborn air. "I spent my whole life believing my mother's silence was aimed at me. That she kept things from me because I wasn't worth telling. It took a burned ledger and a flooded valley to learn she kept things from everyone. From herself, most of all." Her chin lifted. "You were the only person she ever let read her. Don't waste that."

"I don't intend to write anything worth wasting," I said.

"Good," said Vivian Calloway, heiress, survivor, bargainer. "Then write something worth keeping."

Tomás stood as I passed him. He didn't offer his hand; he offered something better. He took an apple from the crate he was mending — a small, scarred thing, half its skin bruised from the flood season — and pressed it into my palm.

"For the road," he said. "From the orchard. So you remember what you saved."

"I didn't save the orchard, Tomás. I saved a boy and lost a wagon."

"You testified," he said simply. "The rest of us just survived. You testified." He turned back to his crates before I could answer, which was a mercy, because I had nothing in my mouth but the taste of that apple, and no words would have improved it.

* * *

Edith's grave sits on the rise above the river, where the ground stayed dry even in the worst of the flood. I stopped there on my way to the bridge, as I had stopped every morning since we put her down, and found that today the habit had grown teeth.

I took out the letter.

It had taken me three nights to write and one night to rewrite, and it was addressed to the county clerk, and it contained everything: the ledger's ghosts, the starved pickers, the hearing in the kitchen with its crooked verdict and its honest arithmetic, the boy smuggled across the river under apple crates, the wagon that sank behind him, the berth I gave away. My testimony, signed Dominic Marchetti, late driver to Edith Calloway, present author of her own correspondence. Eleven years of reading other people's words, and here were mine, in ink I'd bought myself, saying things no one had dictated.

My hand shook holding it. Not from cold. The morning was mild, almost tender, the kind of spring morning that makes liars of pessimists.

Here is what I know about fear: it feels exactly like readiness. The same quick heart, the same bright edges on the world. For eleven years I mistook the one for the other and called my terror professionalism. Standing at that grave with my own words in my fist, I finally understood the difference — fear wants you to stop; readiness knows you can't.

I mailed the letter at the crossroads box before the coach came, before I could reconsider, dropping it through the slot the way you drop a stone down a well — listening all the way for the splash that doesn't come.

* * *

The coach station at Miller's Ford smelled of axle grease and other people's tobacco. My trunk went up top — one trunk, small, mostly paper — and I climbed aboard and chose a window seat with the particular greed of a woman who has spent a decade sitting facing backward on a driver's bench, watching everyone else arrive.

The coachman checked his watch twice, sighed once, and then held the door open for a latecomer: a young man in a clerk's coat, breathless, apologetic, carrying a satchel stuffed with letters. He sat beside me, thanked me for the window in a voice still cracking at its hinges, and immediately began sorting his mail with the anxious devotion of a man who loves his work and suspects it doesn't love him back.

"What's all that?" I asked, nodding at the satchel.

"Correspondence for the county office. Petitions, complaints, testimonies." He glanced at me sideways. "There's talk of an inquiry. Something about an orchard, a ledger that burned, wages owed to families dead and gone. A driver gave a deposition, they say. A woman." He said *a woman* the way people used to say *the bridge is out* — as a fact of weather, remarkable and true.

"Yes," I said. "They say that."

The whip cracked. The horses leaned into their collars, and the coach rolled out onto the new bridge, and the planks rang their hollow wooden ring beneath us, and the river ran green and swollen and indifferent underneath, carrying winter away toward the sea.

Halfway across, I looked back once. Not at the orchard — I knew it by heart, every row, every scarred tree, every grave on the rise — but at the small group of figures standing at the far end of the span: Marta with her arms crossed against a wind that wasn't cold, Vivian with her surveyor's eyes already measuring the next fence line, Tomás with an apple crate balanced on his shoulder like a man carrying both his punishment and his home.

They did not wave. Neither did I. We were past the age of waving, all of us. But Marta raised one hand — not a wave, a salute, brief and soldierly — and I raised mine, and then the road bent and took them from me the way roads do, gently, permanently, without malice.

The young clerk beside me had fallen asleep against the window, his satchel of letters rising and falling with his breath. I looked at it a long time. All those words traveling to places their writers couldn't go, carried by strangers, opened by hands they'd never meet. Once, that would have sounded like my life's ambition. Now it sounded like a stage I was glad to be done with.

In my pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, I carried three things: the apple Tomás gave me; the stub of pencil Edith had chewed for thirty years and left to me in a will that named, among her effects, "my reader"; and a fresh sheet of paper, blank, bought with my own last coins.

Blank pages terrify people who have spent their lives filling them for others. I am finding that they don't terrify me at all.

The coach climbed out of the river valley and onto the open road, and the morning opened with it — wide, unproven, entirely unwritten. Somewhere ahead was a city, a desk, a newspaper office with a bell over its door. Somewhere ahead was whatever a woman becomes when she stops being the person who carries the words and starts being the person who writes them.

I took out the pencil. I took out the paper.

And I began.
