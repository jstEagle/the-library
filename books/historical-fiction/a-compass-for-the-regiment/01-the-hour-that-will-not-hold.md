## 1. The Hour That Will Not Hold

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Emrys Dravenmoor would remember later, because Tuesdays were the days he cleaned the escapements, and cleaning the escapements was the closest thing his life had to a ritual.

He was bent over the bench with a jeweler's loupe pressed to his eye, teasing a speck of brass dust from the pallet stones of a merchant's carriage clock, when the workshop door opened and let in the cold. Master Hollis didn't knock. Masters didn't. The door simply opened, and the world came through it wearing boots.

"Put that down," Hollis said. "You're wanted at the observatory."

Emrys kept his loupe at his eye a moment longer, mostly out of principle. The speck came away on the tip of the peg wood, perfect as a harvested seed. "Wanted by whom?"

"By the regiment." Hollis said the word the way other men said *weather* — flatly, with an eye already calculating what it would cost them. "A rider came up from the garrison this morning. The master clock's losing time. Four minutes a day, they say, regular as tithe."

"Four minutes a day." Emrys set down the peg wood and finally removed the loupe, leaving a small red ring around his eye that he knew from experience made him look either scholarly or ridiculous. "That's not possible."

"Then you'd better go up and tell the mountain that."

"I have the Carrow clock to finish. And the vicar's regulator promised for Sunday—"

"The vicar can tell time by his conscience like everyone else." Hollis was already turning away, which was how he ended conversations. He stopped at the door, though, and half-turned, and for one unguarded moment Emrys saw something in his master's face he couldn't immediately classify. Not worry, exactly. Something adjacent to it. "They asked for you by name, boy. Not for me. You. Think on what that means while you're climbing."

Emrys thought about it for the whole climb, and by the top of it he had decided it meant nothing at all.

***

The observatory sat above the valley like a hat someone had set down and forgotten — a pale stone dome and its attendant buildings, perched on the shoulder of Mount Kelderan where the air went thin and honest. It had been built sixty years ago by the same regiment whose banners flew below in the garrison town, back when the founders wanted their valley to have not only the finest soldiers but the finest hour in the province. The master clock in its tower kept the time for everything: the garrison drills, the mail coaches, the mill wheels, the school bells, the astronomer's star-charts. When the master clock spoke, the whole valley answered.

Emrys had seen it twice before in his nineteen years, both times from the outside, once from a considerable distance while pretending not to look at it. Clockmakers were not invited to the observatory. Clockmakers were sent for, when something broke, and sent away again after. The charter — the regiment's founding charter, that old vellum gospel every schoolchild in the valley could recite — gave the commanders stewardship of the mountain and all its instruments, and the commanders guarded their stewardship the way dragons guard gold: not from malice, but from constitution.

The mule track switchbacked up through pines still dark with last night's rain. Emrys climbed with his tool roll across his back and his collar up and his breath coming in white rags, and he resented every step of it, and he knew — in the small, cold accounting office of his heart where he kept such ledgers — exactly why he resented it.

Because he was good. Because he was very good, better than good, and everyone knew it including himself, and none of it signified. Nineteen years old, six years an apprentice, hands so steady that Master Hollis let him do the delicate work and then took the credit for it at the guild dinners. A head full of teeth and levers and the particular music a movement makes when it is true. And nowhere, in all that competence, anywhere to *go*. History happened in the capital, where the parliament sat and the newspapers were printed. History happened to colonels and commanders and men with names carved into the regimental monuments. History did not happen to apprentice clockmakers from Wrenlow Street who spent Tuesdays cleaning escapements.

His father had understood that, Emrys thought. His father — dead eight months now, and the grief still arrived sideways, ambush-fashion, at odd hours — his father Gareth Dravenmoor had been a man things *happened to*. Not grandly; his father had never left the valley except twice. But there had been a current running through him, a sense that he was party to some larger transaction, that when he sat late at night over his bench with the lamp turned low, working on commissions he never discussed, he was touching the edges of something with weight to it. Emrys had inherited the hands and the lamp-late-hours and, apparently, the summons to the mountain. Whether he'd inherited the current too remained to be seen.

The observatory compound resolved out of the mist: the great dome, the long low residence, the instrument sheds, and the tower — square, squat, crowned with the clock's external dial, four feet across, painted white with black figures so it could be read from the valley floor. From up close, the dial looked different than it did from below. Smaller. Wearable, almost. Like a watch on the wrist of the mountain.

A servant in regimental grey met him at the gate without asking his name, which unsettled him more than any question would have.

"You're expected," the man said. "This way."

***

The clock room occupied the tower's second storey, and Emrys stopped on its threshold the way other men stopped at the edge of consecrated ground.

He had worked on fine clocks. He had worked, once, under Hollis's supervision, on a cathedral regulator with a nine-foot pendulum and a case of figured walnut, and he had thought then that he would never see its equal. He had been wrong. The master clock stood against the tower's inner wall like a piece of architecture that had wandered indoors — eleven feet of case, glazed along its length, the movement within gleaming with the soft dull bloom of metal that is polished weekly by people who are paid to care. Three weights hung in their channels, patient as executed men. The pendulum swung below with a beat so slow and deep Emrys felt it in his sternum before he heard it with his ears.

*Tick.* And the mountain leaned on the second. *Tock.* And the valley obeyed.

"Apprentice."

The voice belonged to a broad man in the grey-and-crimson of the regiment, silver braid at the collar, a moustache groomed to architectural standards. He extended a hand. "Lieutenant Sasson. Observatory liaison. You're young."

"You're observant," Emrys said, and then heard himself, and winced inwardly, and watched the lieutenant decide to find it amusing. Insolence, apparently, was tolerated from tradesmen the way barking was tolerated from dogs.

"The problem," Sasson said, leading him to the case, "is four minutes a day. Losing. Steady. For five weeks now. Our previous contractor examined it in the spring and pronounced it the finest movement in the province. Now walk with me and tell me what you see."

Emrys walked. He looked. And the looking took him over, the way it always did, the resentment draining out through his fingertips and into the work the way heat drains into metal.

The escapement was a gravity arm of unusual design — he'd read about this construction, never touched it — and it was clean, oiled, sound. The train ran true. He checked the beat error by ear, eyes closed, and found it nearly perfect. He inspected the suspension for wear, the crutch for play, the pallets under magnification. He weighed the pendulum bob in its cradle and found no evidence of slipping rating nuts. The barrel was wound full. The temperature compensation, a mercurial column in the bob's stem, was intact and correctly proportioned.

Two hours passed. Sasson watched him work and offered nothing, which Emrys grudgingly respected. At the end of it Emrys straightened, wiped his hands on his apron, and delivered the verdict that had been assembling itself in him joint by joint.

"There's nothing wrong with it."

"Four minutes a day," Sasson said.

"Yes. I believe you. But the machine isn't losing time. Every component is within tolerance. Better than tolerance — this movement was built by someone showing off." He looked at the great gears again, and felt the familiar ache, the one that had nothing to do with ambition: the ache of meeting a superior mind through its handiwork. Whoever had built this clock had poured a whole life into it. "If a clock loses time steadily, it's friction or torque or temperature. If it loses time erratically, it's interference or damage. This loses time steadily with no cause. Which leaves—"

He stopped.

"Which leaves," Sasson prompted.

"Which leaves the possibility that the clock isn't malfunctioning at all," Emrys said slowly. "It's being *adjusted*. Someone's slowing it deliberately. Rating nut tampered with and restored, or — no, even simpler — the pendulum's effective length altered by degrees too small to see, then corrected whenever an examiner comes." He frowned at his own words. They tasted wrong. "But that's absurd. Who would slow the master clock? Half the valley sets its day by this thing. The mail, the mills, the garrison—"

"The garrison," Sasson repeated, in a tone Emrys couldn't read. "Yes." The lieutenant consulted a pocket watch — a heavy gold thing that must have cost a sergeant's yearly wage — and snapped it shut. "You'll stay the night. We keep rooms for contractors. Tomorrow you'll open the weight channels and examine the maintaining power. Take your time. Be thorough." He paused at the door, and his voice changed by one degree, the way ice changes just before it cracks. "And apprentice — whatever you find in this room stays in this room. Those are the commander's standing instructions regarding the observatory. All of it."

The door closed behind him. The pendulum swung. *Tick*, said the clock, to no one in particular, and the mountain leaned on the second.

Emrys stood alone with the finest machine he had ever touched, and for the first time in his life he noticed the quality of the silence around it — a listening silence, a silence with attention in it, like a held breath. He told himself it was the altitude. He unpacked his tools anyway, and began, methodically, to check every measurement a second time, because the hands needed occupying, and because somewhere beneath the arithmetic of his trade a small cold voice had begun to whisper something he did not want to hear:

*A clock doesn't lie by accident. A clock lies on purpose, or someone teaches it to.*

***

Night came early to the mountain, as if summoned. Emrys ate alone in a stone-flagged room while a silent woman brought bread and cold mutton and asked nothing, and afterward he lay in a narrow bed in a narrow room listening to wind work at the shutters, and slept badly, and dreamed of his father's bench with the lamp burning low and his father's hands moving over some commission he wouldn't name, faster and faster, as though racing a deadline only he could feel.

He was at the tower before dawn, and he told himself it was diligence.

The weight channels first, then. He opened the access panels at the base of the case, three panels of joined oak, each fitted so finely the seams vanished under his thumb. Inside, the weights descended in their wooden channels on the winding drum's schedule, and the channels were lined with lead sheeting to damp vibration — expensive, careful work, sixty years old.

Emrys put his lamp inside the channel and looked, and looked again, and felt the hair rise on his forearms.

There, scored faintly into the lead lining, in a hand small enough to require him on his knees with the lamp held high: marks. Groups of scratches. Tallies.

Not random. Not wear. *Records.* Rows upon rows of them, some fresh enough to show bright metal at the stroke's heart, others gone brown with years — years of years, the oldest ones faded almost back into the lead. Someone had been kneeling where he knelt, night after night, keeping count of something in the one place in the observatory where no one but the clockmaker would ever think to look.

And beneath the most recent group of tallies, in the same cramped hand, a single word had been cut into the lead with a point finer than any knife — a scriber's point, a watchmaker's point.

Emrys read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused the assignment.

The word was a date. Six years old. And next to the date, three letters: *A.V.*

He knelt there a long while, the lamp trembling just enough to make the shadows breathe, while above him the great clock ticked out its false, beautiful, stolen seconds, and the mountain leaned on the hour, and history — which had never once happened to Emrys Dravenmoor, which had always happened elsewhere, to other people — quietly climbed through the window and sat down beside him in the dust.

Below, far down the mountain road, small and late and out of place, a figure was walking up through the grey dawn toward the observatory gate.
