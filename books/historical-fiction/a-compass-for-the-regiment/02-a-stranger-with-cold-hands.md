## 2. A Stranger with Cold Hands

The light failed early on the mountain, and it failed completely. Emrys had been warned about this in the workshop — *you'll lose the sun by four up there, boy, and your wits shortly after* — but the warning hadn't prepared him for how total it was, how the dusk came down the valley like water filling a bowl, and how the observatory's single lamp in the east window seemed less like a light left burning than a signal that someone was awake and watching.

He spent the last hour of daylight checking the clock's escapement for the fourth time, which was a way of admitting he had found nothing. A clock loses four minutes a day for reasons: a worn pallet, a fouled bearing, a pendulum rod swelling with damp, a weight cable slipping its groove. This clock lost them for no reason at all. Every surface was clean. Every adjustment was true. He had measured the beat against his own pulse until his pulse felt unreliable, and the clock had gone on losing time with the serene indifference of a thing that knows something he doesn't.

"You'll want to come down before dark," said Cobbett, the observatory caretaker, appearing in the doorway with his coat already on. Cobbett had the gift of leaving rooms as if he'd never fully entered them. "Lamp oil's scarce, and you've a bed at the inn."

"I'll stay a while longer."

Cobbett shrugged the shrug of a man paid by the month, not by the outcome, and went out. Emrys heard his boots on the gravel, then the gate, then nothing but wind working at the shutters of the transit room and the tick of the master clock — that deep, deliberate tick, slower than any clock had a right to be, as if it were counting something other than seconds.

He should have gone down too. That was the thought he would carry afterward, the one he turned over on cold nights: *I should have gone down the mountain at dusk like a sensible apprentice and let the mystery keep until morning.* But there is a particular loneliness to being twenty years old and good at your work in a town that has decided what you are, and Emrys Dravenmoor had discovered that he preferred the observatory at night to the inn at night, because at the inn he was the Lockridge boy's apprentice, sent up the hill because old Marlowe's hands had grown unsteady, and here he was simply the only person who understood what he was looking at.

So he stayed. And because he stayed, he was standing at the clock case when the knock came.

It was not a loud knock. That was the first strange thing about it, though he didn't understand why until later. The road up from the valley ended at the observatory gate, and anyone arriving after dark knocked loudly, because the building was stone-walled and the wind was constant and a soft knock was a knock that did not expect to be answered. This one was quiet. Two raps, patient, spaced like the beats of a pendulum.

Emrys stood very still.

"Hello?" he called, and hated how young his voice sounded against the stone.

"Forgive the hour." The voice through the door was thin and careful, the voice of a man rationing his breath. "I'm looking for the clockmaker."

"There's no clockmaker here. There's an apprentice." Even as he said it, Emrys was unbarring the door, because the alternative — leaving a man on the mountainside in the falling dark — was not something his upbringing allowed. "The master clockmaker is Marlowe, in the valley. I'm—"

"Emrys Dravenmoor," the man said.

And there it was. The second strange thing, arriving without ceremony, the way the worst things do. No one knew Emrys was coming to the observatory except Marlowe, the regimental quartermaster who'd signed the work order, and Cobbett. Marlowe had told him at the last moment; the summons itself had come only two days prior, a note in a quartermaster's hand. There was no chain by which a stranger on the mountain road could have his name.

The man in the doorway was gaunt in the way that made you think first of illness and second of age, and even then you'd be wrong about both. Forty perhaps, perhaps fifty, hard to say when a face has been starved and weathered past its decades. He wore a plain traveling coat gone shiny at the elbows, and his hands — he held them folded in front of him like a man entering a church — were white at the fingertips where the blood had withdrawn, and he shivered once, violently, and then controlled it.

"Cold hands," he said, with a small apologetic smile. "It's the altitude. May I come in?"

Everything in Emrys said no. He would spend years trying to reconstruct what exactly had said no — some animal ledger weighing the man's thinness against the isolation of the place, the lateness of the hour against the politeness of his manner — and every entry in the ledger would come back wrong, insufficient, because the truth was simpler and worse: the man knew his name, and curiosity is a stronger door-opener than caution is a door-bar.

"You'd better," Emrys said, and stepped aside.

* * *

The stranger walked into the clock room the way no visitor ever did — without looking up at the great dial, without the involuntary pause people gave the master clock, that twelve-foot face of brass and enamel that dominated the observatory and, in a sense that Emrys was only beginning to grasp, dominated the valley entire. The regiment set its watches by this clock. The garrison bells relayed its hours down the mountainside, and the valley towns relayed the garrison's hours, and so this machine, losing four minutes a day for no reason Emrys could find, was quietly desynchronizing the lives of several thousand people, one missed minute at a time.

The stranger looked instead at the case. At the base of it. At the join of the panels near the weight channel, low down, where a kneeling man might reach.

"You've found nothing wrong with it," he said. Not a question.

"Nothing mechanical."

"And yet it loses time."

"Four minutes a day. Give or take."

The stranger nodded slowly, as if Emrys had confirmed something he'd carried a long way. Then he turned, and in the lamplight Emrys saw his eyes properly for the first time and had to stop himself from stepping back. They were the eyes of someone who had spent years watching horizons for the shapes of pursuit. Restless, hooded, kind around the edges despite everything.

"My name is Pell," the man said. "I'm sorry for the theater of it — arriving like this, knowing your name. I needed to be sure of you before you were sure of me."

"Sure of you how?"

Pell did not answer directly. Instead he reached inside his coat, moving with the exaggerated slowness of a man who knows how frightened people are of sudden gestures, and drew out a package wrapped in oilcloth. It was roughly the size of a prayer book, tied with waxed cord, and it was stained along one edge with something dark that Emrys chose not to identify. Pell laid it on the workbench between them with the gentleness of a man setting down an infant.

"Do you know whose hand this is?" he asked, and began unwrapping the cord.

"Pell. Whatever you're—"

"Whose hand, Mr. Dravenmoor."

The oilcloth fell open. Inside were letters — a dozen or more, closely written on paper of varying ages, the oldest foxed and browned at the corners, the newest still white-edged. Emrys did not want to look. Some instinct told him that looking was a threshold and that thresholds don't swing back. But his eyes were twenty years old and trained on fine detail, and they looked anyway.

The handwriting was upright, economical, formed under pressure — the hand of a man taught to write dispatches that clerks might read by candlelight in bad weather. Emrys knew it the way everyone in the valley knew it. It was reproduced on the monument in the square. It was engraved on the commemorative tankards sold at the founding-day feast. It hung behind glass in the regimental hall over the list of the fallen, above the name ALDOUS VANE, COLONEL, and beneath the words HE GAVE HIS HOURS FOR OURS.

Every child in the valley learned that hand. Every child learned the story that went with it: the hero of the ford at Callow Bridge, the colonel who held the pass for three days with half a battalion, the man who had ridden out six years ago on the anniversary of the founding and died in an ambush on the high road, within sight of the observatory, so that even his death had seemed arranged by providence to complete the regiment's legend.

"This is a forgery," Emrys said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "A good one, but the ink's wrong for six years old, and—"

"The newest ones are three weeks old," Pell said. "The oldest are nine years. Read the date on the third letter. Then tell me about the ink."

Emrys picked it up. His hands, he noticed distantly, were not shaking. The paper was right — the slight tooth of it, the way it took the light. The date read the spring nine years past, when Vane had been alive and celebrated and, by all official record, still two years from his grave. The ink had aged honestly. The letters had been written across years, stored, carried, kept dry in oilcloth by a man who understood that they were worth more than his life.

"This is impossible," Emrys said.

"Yes," said Pell, with terrible mildness. "It is generally agreed to be impossible. And yet Colonel Vane wrote the last of these three weeks ago, in some discomfort, by the light of a tallow candle, and asked me specifically to bring them to the observatory and put them in the care of the clockmaker's son."

"The clockmaker's—" Emrys set the letter down. "My father was a wheelwright."

"Was he," said Pell. It was not a question either.

* * *

They stood facing each other across the workbench with the letters between them, and outside the wind rose a register, and the master clock ticked its slow wrong tick, and Emrys became aware that his heart was beating faster than the escapement — faster than any honest escapement — and that Pell could probably hear it in the silence.

"I don't know what this is," Emrys said finally. "Smuggling, or madness, or a test of some kind. But whatever it is, I'm not part of it. I came up this mountain to repair a clock. That's the whole of my commission. Tomorrow I'll find what's wrong with it, fix it, and go home, and whatever you're carrying, you can carry it back down the way you came."

"Of course," said Pell. He did not move toward the letters. He did not, Emrys noticed, move toward the door either. "May I ask you one question first? Then I'll go, and trouble you no more."

Emrys waited.

"When you examined the clock — the great case, the movement, the weight channel — did you notice the panel at the base, second from the left? Did you notice that the screws have been turned more than once? That the paint over the slots is newer there than anywhere else on the case?"

Emrys opened his mouth to say no, and found the memory waiting: a flicker of attention, three hours ago, a small wrongness he had filed and not pursued. Newer paint. Screws with bright, unworn slots in a case otherwise untouched since its making. He had seen it and thought *odd* and moved on, because odd things that don't affect the mechanism are not a clockmaker's business.

"I see that you noticed," Pell said gently. "You're your father's son after all."

"Don't." The word came out harder than Emrys intended, and it surprised them both. "You don't know anything about my father. He died when I was eleven. He was a tradesman. He fixed wheels and hinges and whatever else people brought him, and he was buried in the parish yard with half the valley at the funeral because everyone liked him, and if you think you can come up my mountain and—"

"—and what?" said Pell, still gently. "I only know what I've read. I've spent six years reading nothing else. Your father's name appears in these letters forty-one times. Would you like me to count again? I have. Forty-one times, Mr. Dravenmoor. Aldous Vane has been writing to a dead man for nine years, hoping that someday the letters would reach the son, because the father made him a promise, and promises, he says, are the only currency that holds its value in exile."

Emrys's mouth was dry. "Exile."

Pell smiled — a small, exhausted, genuinely apologetic smile, the smile of a man apologizing for weather. "Yes," he said. "That is the word he uses. Though in fairness, most people use the word *grave*."

The clock ticked. Four minutes slow today. Eight tomorrow, if nobody stopped it.

"Read one," Pell said. "Just one. The shortest. Then I'll leave, tonight, down the mountain, and you'll never see me again unless you choose otherwise. That is the whole of my request, and I apologize for the hour, and for the cold hands, and for the burden. I apologize for the burden most of all."

And Emrys, who had spent his whole life believing that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capital cities and on battlefields and in the fine houses down the valley — Emrys reached across the bench and picked up the topmost letter, because refusing had begun to feel like a thing done by other people too.

The handwriting was unmistakable. It leapt off the page, the hand from the monument, the hand from the regimental hall, the hand engraved on a dead hero's memorial tankard — and it was fresher than the bread on his breakfast table.

*To whoever keeps the hours truly,* it began.

Emrys read the first line. Then, without deciding to, he read the second.

At the bottom of the page, in the margin, in a smaller, cramped script he had seen ten thousand times on the backs of invoices and the flyleaves of the few books his father had owned — a script he had watched form itself, stroke by stroke, sitting on a stool beside a workbench at the age of seven — were three words of annotation.

His father's hand. Correcting the colonel's draft.

The letter slipped from his fingers onto the bench, and Pell, who had been watching his face the way a man watches a fuse, said quietly:

"Now you understand why I came at dusk."
