## 3. Handwriting of a Dead Man

The stranger's name was Pell. He gave it the way a man sets down a card he expects to be refused.

We stood in the doorway of the observatory while the wind came off the mountain in long grey shoves. Behind me the master clock ticked on in its losing way — four minutes adrift by now, maybe five, patient as rot — and I found myself oddly grateful for the sound. It was the only ordinary thing left in the room.

"You'll want to shut the door," Pell said. "Not for warmth."

I shut the door. I want that recorded plainly, because everything afterward grew out of it: I shut the door when a stranger told me to, and I did it without thinking, the way you obey a foreman or a father. Later I would tell myself he'd forced nothing on me. That would be a kindness I didn't deserve and couldn't afford.

He unwrapped the oilcloth on my workbench with the care of a man handling something that had already been dropped once too often. Inside were letters — perhaps twenty of them, tied in bundles of five with waxed thread, each bundle wrapped again in linen against damp. The paper was good stock, valley-made, the kind the regimental quartermaster bought by the ream. The ink had faded unevenly; some letters were months old, others recent enough that the brown-black still shone faintly where the pen had pressed hard.

And the hand itself —

I have spent my life reading hands. A clockmaker learns handwriting before he learns faces, because every mechanism carries its maker's script in the shape of its wheels: this man cut his escape wheels proud, this one filed his arbors shy. Writing is no different. Colonel Aldous Vane's hand was famous in the valley the way his face was famous — reproduced on commissions, on the founding-day proclamations, on the memorial plaque in the garrison chapel that named him dead. A tall, slanted, disciplined script, upright as a drawn bayonet, with a single vanity: the crossbar of every *t* swept slightly upward at its right end, like a man lifting his chin.

The letters on my bench lifted their chins.

"Six years," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "He's been dead six years. There's a plaque."

"There is," Pell agreed. "Brass. Very handsome. They paid for it out of the mess fund, which I'm told he'd have found funny." He set one letter apart from the rest, as if marking a place in a book. "Read it. Not all of them. Just that one. Then tell me what you want to do, and whatever you say, I'll walk back down the mountain tonight and you'll never see me again."

It was a lie, of course. We both knew it. Men who walk away and are never seen again don't climb mountains in November to ask clockmakers for favors. But I took the letter, because my hands wanted something to do, and because — I will be honest here, since honesty is the whole of this account — because some part of me had already decided to read it the moment I saw the handwriting, and everything since has merely been the machinery catching up to the decision.

*To the keeper of the observatory,* it began. *Or to whoever keeps the clock.*

*You do not know me, and if the fates are kind you never will. I write this knowing it may reach no one, or reach someone who burns it, and both outcomes serve better than the truth sitting in a drawer where men like Halewick can find it. I ask only that these pages be kept where time itself can vouch for them. A clock does not lie unless a man teaches it to. Put them inside the case. No soldier of this regiment will open it. Their fear of the mechanism is older than any of them, and fear, unlike courage, keeps excellent order.*

*Tell the clockmaker this much and no more: your father mended more than clocks.*

I read it twice. The second time was not for comprehension. It was because my eyes kept snagging on the last line the way a wheel snags on a bent tooth, and I needed to prove to myself that it said what it said.

"My father," I said, "has been dead two years."

"I know," Pell said gently. "He knew that too. He wrote it anyway. Hope is not efficient, Mr. Dravenmoor. It just refuses to stop."

"Don't call me that." It came out sharper than I intended. "Don't use my name like we're acquainted."

"You were summoned here by name," Pell said. "Someone in the valley knows who you are. I merely followed the same road they did."

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and let no reader flatter himself that he'd have done otherwise until he has stood in a cold room holding a dead man's living words: I should have handed the letters back, walked down the mountain, and reported the whole business to Commander Halewick within the hour. That was the lawful act. That was the sensible act. Halewick was generous to tradesmen; he'd have paid me for the information and thrown in a commendation, and I'd have gone back to my bench with a story to tell for the rest of my small, orderly life.

Instead I said: "Why the clock?"

Pell blinked. For the first time since arriving, he looked genuinely uncertain, as though he'd rehearsed every answer except the one to an honest question.

"Because they're afraid of it," he said. "Because in six years of searching — and they have searched, Mr. Dravenmoor, they've turned over every chapel, every granary, every grave in this valley, including mine, which was a novel experience — no one has ever opened the master clock's case. Halewick's own quartermaster ordered it sealed after a man died in there. Fell against the escapement during winding. There was blood. Since then it's superstition. The soldiers think the machine takes a tithe." He smiled without humor. "Superstition is the finest lock ever devised. It needs no key and cannot be picked, only believed."

"A man died in the case?"

"Slipped from the ladder. Caught his coat on the great wheel." Pell looked at the clock's tall case, dark against the shuttered windows, and lowered his voice as if the thing might hear. "They say you can still see the mark on the fourth wheel. Can you?"

"There's a discoloration," I admitted. "Oil and iron. It's not blood. Blood doesn't survive cleaning."

"No," Pell said. "Very little survives cleaning. That's rather the colonel's point."

* * *

I want to describe the refusal properly, because it was real. I did refuse. I stood there with the letter in my hand and told him no in words that were, I believe, entirely sincere at the moment of speaking.

"This isn't my affair," I said. "I came to mend a going-train. I'm an apprentice. My master sent me up here because his knees won't take the road, and in three days I go back down, and none of this—" I held up the letter— "none of this follows me. Find a priest. Find a lawyer in the capital. Find anyone whose trade isn't gears."

"Gears are precisely why we came to you."

"That's an argument for someone else, then."

"Is it?" Pell asked. He didn't move from his chair — he'd taken the one by the stove uninvited, another liberty I permitted because objecting felt like admitting how unsettled I was. "Who else opens that case? Who else could hide papers inside a machine so that no one finds them, and retrieve them later, and know the hiding hasn't broken the going? You think this is a favor we're asking? It's a craft. There is exactly one man on this mountain who has it, and he is standing in front of me refusing."

"The clock is losing four minutes a day," I said, mostly to change the subject, partly because the problem had been gnawing at me for two days and saying it aloud was a comfort. "There's nothing wrong with it. I've been through the train twice. Temperature compensation is sound, the pendulum's true, the escapement's clean. Something is stealing four minutes and I can't find the thief."

Something crossed Pell's face then — quick, quickly suppressed, but I was watching him closely by then, the way you watch a suspicious movement in a train you've already checked once. It wasn't surprise. It was confirmation.

"Four minutes," he repeated.

"A day. Give or take."

"And you can't account for it."

"Not yet."

"Then perhaps," Pell said, very quietly, "the clock is telling you something, and you haven't learned its language yet."

I didn't understand him then. I want that clear. Whatever I suspected in that moment — and I suspected something, some dissonance in his tone that a clockmaker hears the way a musician hears a flat note — I did not understand what the clock had been doing, or why, or for whom. Understanding came later, in Chapter Six of my life, if chapters are how these things must be told, and it changed everything. In this room, on this night, I understood only that a stranger knew more about my clock than I did, and that he was afraid of me learning it too fast.

* * *

He stayed the night in the astronomer's old quarters below — Isolde Marchmont's rooms, though she was away at the university town that week, a fact I mention now because it matters later and because even then her absence felt like a held breath. I sat up with the letters. He offered them freely; that was part of his method, I see it now. A man guards what's forced on him and reads what's given.

I read until the candle guttered. Vane wrote well — a soldier's prose, plain and load-bearing, no ornament he hadn't tested under weight. He wrote about roads and rations and the names of men buried in places the maps got wrong. And threaded through it all, recurring like a refrain, was the charter.

*The founding charter grants the commanders authority over the valley in perpetuity.* Everyone knew the charter; schoolchildren recited its preamble. *Everyone knew* it had been drafted by the first commander's council and signed under the mountain oak in the year the regiment stood down. What no one questioned — because questioning it would have been like questioning the height of the mountain — was its authenticity.

Vane questioned it. In letter after letter, in the oblique language of a man assuming interception, he circled the thing: *the ink sits wrong*, he wrote once. *The vellum is younger than the date. A document should age honestly or not at all.*

I told myself I was reading a madman. Dead heroes make convenient prophets; grief invents conspiracies the way standing water breeds mosquitoes. I told myself this with real conviction, sitting alone at two in the morning in a mountain observatory with a dead man's correspondence spread across a workbench, and I almost believed it.

Almost. Because near the bottom of the third bundle, folded around a draft — a draft of what, I didn't know yet, a draft of clauses, legal clauses, in a fine formal hand — there were marginal corrections.

Small ones. A crossed-out *shall* replaced with *may*. An inserted phrase in tighter script: *in perpetuity, subject to the assembly's consent* — struck through, the insertion abandoned. And beside one clause about the commander's power to levy labor from the valley households, a note in the margin, tiny, hurried, the pen pressed hard:

*This exceeds the council's grant. I will not cut this stroke. Find another hand if you must have it.*

Find another hand.

I know my father's writing the way I know my own pulse. I had learned my capitals tracing his. He had died with ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles that the undertaker had remarked on it, embarrassed, as though it were a deformity. Josiah Dravenmoor, watchmaker of Thistlefall Row, third-generation, honest as a plumb line — that was the man I'd buried, that was the name on the workshop door, that was the inheritance I'd carried like a coat that fit badly but was mine.

His handwriting was in the margins of a draft of the founding charter.

I sat with that for a long time. The candle burned down and I let it. Below me, through the floor, I could hear Pell turning over restlessly, a man sleeping badly among strangers. Above me the dome ticked and settled with the cold. And the master clock went on losing its four minutes a day, faithful to some appointment I couldn't yet imagine.

Here is the thing about complicity that no one tells you: it doesn't arrive wearing a mask. It arrives wearing your own face. I did not feel corrupted. I felt awake — wider awake than I had felt in years, the way a mechanism feels when a stuck tooth finally frees. All my life I'd believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, to men with names on plaques. And now history was lying across my workbench in my father's handwriting, and the only question was whether I would keep being a man things happened to.

I bundled the letters. I wrapped them in the oilcloth. And I heard myself say, to the empty room, in a voice that sounded disgracefully calm:

"He wouldn't have cut the stroke."

That was the whole of my deliberation. Not justice, not the colonel's honor, not the valley's freedom — those came later, dressed in nobler clothes. At the bottom of it, in the cold and the candle-dark, was a boy's stubborn loyalty to a dead man's marginal note, and the terrible relief of finally having a reason.

At dawn Pell climbed the stairs, looked at the oilcloth bundle in my hands, and asked nothing. That, more than anything he'd said, decided me. A man who asks nothing has already counted you as his.

"The case opens from the back panel," I said. "There's a clearance behind the great wheel — six inches deep, dry, and the weight channel runs past it. Papers wrapped in waxed linen will sit safe for years. The trick is getting them past the fourth wheel without fouling the train, and the trick to that is timing the placement to the beat."

"Which is?"

"Which is that I do it while the clock runs," I said, "between the fourth and fifth seconds of every swing. Miss the beat and the wheel takes your fingers. That's how the last man died."

Pell went pale. "Then don't—"

"It's my clock," I said, and heard, even as I said it, whose cadence I was borrowing. "If anything in this valley is mine, it's that."

I opened the case that afternoon, with the letters squared and wrapped and my heart going harder than any escapement I'd ever serviced. The mechanism breathed out its smell of oil and old iron. And as I reached into the shadow behind the great wheel, my fingers found something that should not have been there — something thin and metallic, wedged against the frame, deliberate, placed, maintained.

Someone had been inside this clock before me. Someone had put something there on purpose.

And it was moving.
