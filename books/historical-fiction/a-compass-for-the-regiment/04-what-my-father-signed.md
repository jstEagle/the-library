## 4. What My Father Signed

The workshop smelled of my father for eleven days after he died — oil and iron filings and the particular sweetness of the boxwood he used for his smallest tools. On the twelfth day it smelled of nothing but dust, and I knew I could not put the sorting off any longer.

Master Corvane had given me leave without my asking for it, which was unlike him. "Go home, boy," he'd said, not looking up from the escapement he was boring. "Grief is like a mainspring. You can't work around it. You have to let it down all the way or it'll snap on you at the worst hour." Then he'd bent back over his bench as if he hadn't said anything worth remembering, which was also like him.

So I went home to the narrow house above the tannery lane where I had been born, and where my father, Josiah Dravenmoor, had lived and worked and saved and kept his secrets for forty-one years, thirty of which I could remember, and none of which I understood as well as I had thought.

The house was small enough that sorting his effects should have taken an afternoon. It took four days because I kept stopping — because a coat on its peg still held the shape of his shoulders, because his chair still leaned at his angle against the table, because grief does not move in a straight line but in circles, tightening slowly toward some center you dread reaching.

On the fourth day I reached the center.

* * *

It began with the chest under the floorboards of his sleeping room. I only found it because I tripped over the loose board carrying an armful of linens, and swore, and knelt to press the board back into place, and felt the give in it that meant it was made to give.

My father was a careful man. Careful with money, careful with tools, careful with his hands and his words and, apparently, with what he buried beneath his own bed. The chest itself was unremarkable — plain oak, iron-bound, no lock, which struck me even then as strange. A locked box says *secret*. An unlocked one says *I have nothing to hide*, which is a different kind of lie.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, were three things.

The first was a roll of drafting instruments finer than any my father had ever used at his bench. A set of ruling pens with nibs of graduated fineness, their reservoirs still faintly stained with ink gone brown as old blood. A rock crystal burnisher. A bone folder worn smooth as river stone from years of handling. And a perspective frame of brass, adjustable, expensive — the kind of tool that belonged to a professional limner or scrivener, not to a clockmaker who kept his accounts in a cheap hand and never wrote a letter longer than an invoice.

The second thing was paper. Practice sheets, dozens of them, each covered in the same line repeated over and over, growing more assured sheet by sheet. A flowing, formal script with flourished capitals and a peculiar double-curve in the descenders. I knew that hand. Every soul in the valley knew that hand, though almost none had seen it up close. It was reproduced on the regiment's letterhead, carved into the lintel of the assembly hall, stamped on the seals that granted leases and settled disputes and told men what they could do with the land their grandfathers had cleared.

It was the hand of the Founding Charter of the Regiment.

I sat down on my father's bed, hard, and looked at the practice sheets, and did the arithmetic that grief always forces on you eventually: how long must a man rehearse a hand before he can write it so perfectly that two centuries of lawyers and heralds cannot tell the difference? Months. Years, perhaps. The early sheets were stiff, the curves uncertain. The later ones were flawless. Somewhere between them lay my father's patience, and my father's talent, and my father's silence.

The third thing was the ledger.

* * *

You have to understand what the charter means in our valley. Not what the schoolbooks say it means — *the covenant between the founders and the crown, the compact of service and protection* — but what it means when a farmer wants to fell a tree on his own land and must petition the commander's steward for leave. What it means when the regiment's tithe comes due each autumn and no one has ever once asked whether the amount is just, because the charter says the commanders' rights are *ancient and inviolable*, and ancient and inviolable things are not argued with. They are inherited, the way weather is inherited, the way hunger is.

Colonel Aldous Vane had died six years ago — the hero of Marrenfeld, the man whose name every recruit swore on, whose statue stood in the square with its hand forever gesturing toward some horizon of duty. His death had been the great mourning of my boyhood. I was thirteen when the news came down the mountain. I remembered the bells, all of them, rung together, and how the sound had seemed less like sorrow than like something enormous shifting its weight.

And I remembered my father standing in our doorway, listening to those bells, with an expression I had never been able to name until now. It was not grief. It was recognition. It was the face of a man hearing a debt called in.

* * *

The ledger was small, bound in grey boards, its clasp broken. My father's own writing inside — the neat, cramped, unlovely hand he used for invoices. Columns of dates going back twenty-two years. Payments received from the Office of the Commander's Steward.

Not wages. Wages appear weekly; these came irregularly, sometimes twice in a month, sometimes nothing for half a year. Sums varied. Beside several entries, in place of amounts, stood only a symbol — a small circle with a cross through it, drawn quickly, like a signature abbreviated past legibility.

I turned pages until my hands shook. The last entry was dated eleven months before his death. The sum was the largest in the book.

I want to say I wept. I didn't. I sat very still in the failing light of my father's room with the ledger open on my knees, and I felt something worse than tears: I felt everything I knew about myself rearranging. I was the son of a clockmaker. I was an apprentice with steady hands and a reputation for patience. I was a nobody from the tannery lane who believed, insofar as I believed anything about history, that history happened to other people — to colonels and commanders and men whose names got carved in lintels. My father had mended hinges and cleaned the workshop clocks of the gentry and been polite to everyone, and I had thought that smallness was his whole story.

But my father's hands had touched the founding charter of the Regiment. My father's patience had built the document that built Commander Halewick's power — Halewick, whose grandfather had been Vane's brother-in-arms, whose family had ruled the valley on the strength of a covenant my father had *made*, in a dead man's borrowed hand, at a dead government's price.

The charter was forged. My father forged it.

And I had spent my whole life believing we were too small to matter.

* * *

That night I dreamed of the bells again — all of them ringing together for a dead colonel — and woke before dawn with my father's voice in my head, saying the thing he said whenever I rushed a job: *Slow is smooth, boy. Smooth is true.*

I lit the lamp and went back to the chest.

There was more in it that I had missed, folded inside the oilcloth lining. A single sheet, older than the rest, foxed at the edges — a draft, half-finished, of a clause I recognized. *The rights of command shall descend unbroken upon the lawful heirs of the founders, in perpetuity, neither to be questioned nor revoked.* The same clause that Halewick had read aloud, I would learn later, at every founding-day feast of his stewardship, in a voice pitched to carry to the back of the hall.

In the margin of this draft, someone else had written corrections. Small, precise annotations in a different ink — a lawyer's or a scholar's hand, tightening the language, smoothing the legal seams. And beside one correction, where the annotator had queried a phrase, my father had replied in the margin in his own cramped script:

*As you wish. It must read as if it were written by men who feared God more than lawyers.*

And below that, in the annotator's hand, a single line:

*He will thank us both, when it is done. — A.V.*

A.V. Aldous Vane.

I read it four times. The lamp guttered. Somewhere on the tannery lane a dog began to bark at nothing, and stopped, and the silence after it was the loudest thing in the valley.

Vane had known. Vane had commissioned it — or at least collaborated, corrected, blessed the work. The hero of Marrenfeld, the martyr, the man the bells had rung for — his hand was there in the margins of the forgery alongside my father's.

Unless.

Unless the hand in the margins was not the author's but another's altogether. Unless Vane had discovered the work rather than directed it, and these notes were something other than approval. I was a repairer of mechanisms; I knew that the same mark on a dial — a scratch, a stain — can mean manufacture or sabotage, depending on when it was made and by whom and why. Context is everything. Provenance is everything. I had proof that my father helped forge the charter. I had no proof at all of what Vane's handwriting meant, except that it existed, and that it had been hidden under a floorboard for twenty years by a man who hid everything carefully.

*Slow is smooth. Smooth is true.* He had taught me that. He had also taught me, without ever saying it, that a mechanism can be made to lie — that a clock can be adjusted to flatter its owner's sense of time, that books can be balanced however the balancer pleases. He taught me honesty by being dishonest, and craftsmanship by concealing his greatest work, and love by leaving me this chest to find, because surely — surely — a man as careful as Josiah Dravenmoor did not leave loose floorboards by accident.

He wanted me to know. That was the part that undid me, finally, kneeling on cold boards at first light with the draft in my hands. He could have burned all of it. He had months to burn it, dying as he did by slow degrees, alone in this room. Instead he left it where a grieving son would trip over it. He left me the truth the way you leave a watch to a child: knowing it will need winding, knowing it will break, trusting the child to fix it anyway.

I did not know yet whether to bless him or curse him. I suspected I would spend the rest of my life doing both.

* * *

What I did next, I did the only way I knew how: methodically.

I inventoried. Every practice sheet dated where possible, ordered by progression. Every ledger entry transcribed. The drafting instruments cleaned and examined — and here I found one more thing, because examining mechanisms is what I am for. The handle of the largest ruling pen was hollow, capped with wax colored to match the bone. Inside the handle, rolled tight as a wick, a strip of paper.

Words in my father's hand, written fast, unlike all his other writing:

*If you have found this, then you know what I was. Do not trust the charter to speak for itself. Documents lie unless someone living stands behind them. Find the man behind it. If he lives, he owes me a truth, and you owe him nothing but your ears. — J.D.*

Find the man behind it. *If he lives.* My father had been dead eleven months. Colonel Vane had been dead six years. Whoever the man behind the charter was — whoever *A.V.* was — he was bones on a hillside or ash in an urn, and whatever truth he owed was owed to nobody.

I repacked the chest. I put it back under the floorboards. Then I pulled it out again, because hiding it felt like complicity in my father's concealment, and I had decided — in the way one decides such things at nineteen, absolutely and without evidence — that I would hide nothing, ever, from anyone.

I locked it instead in the workshop's tool safe, behind the gear blanks, where a thief would pass it ten times over looking for silver. Then I ate nothing, packed my kit bag, and walked the four miles up the valley road to Master Corvane's shop to ask for my apprenticeship duties back, because work is the only prayer I know.

Corvane took one look at me across the bench and put down his file.

"You've found something," he said.

"I've found my father wasn't who I thought."

"No one ever is." He wiped his hands slowly, watching me. "Was he less?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then you're not finished finding." He nodded at the wall where the shop's regulator clock ticked away the honest seconds. "Mind you don't judge him by the hour you're living in. Judgments keep poor time."

I might have answered. I would have, I think — I had three weeks of silence stacked up behind my teeth and a question burning through all of it. But I never got the chance, because at that moment the shop door opened and a boy in regimental livery stepped in out of the cold, stamping snow off his boots, and handed me a sealed note.

The seal was the Commander's Own — the crossed circle of the founding charter, embossed in black wax.

"From the steward's office," the boy said. "Commander Halewick requests the clockmaker's apprentice at the mountain observatory. There's a master clock there that's losing time, and no one can say why."

I broke the seal with a thumb that did not shake, though I felt it shake somewhere deeper, in the wound the chest had opened.

The note was courteous. Generous, even — offering payment beyond anything my father's debts had left me hope of, offering the honor of the commission, offering, between its lines, the warm regard of Commander Halewick for the son of Josiah Dravenmoor.

*Your father rendered the Regiment service of the highest order,* the last line said. *It would please me to see that service honored in his son.*

Service of the highest order. I stood in the doorway holding the letter, feeling the weight of the tool safe at my back, the ledger and the draft and the marginal note signed *A.V.* all of them suddenly very close, as if the floorboards of my father's room stretched under the whole valley and everything buried in them was listening.

Halewick knew. Or Halewick suspected. Either way, the commander was summoning the forge's son to tend his most public clock, with courtesy laid thick as fresh paint over whatever lay beneath.

I folded the note into my breast pocket, where it lay against my heart like something ticking.

"I'll come," I told the boy. "Tell the commander I'll come."

And behind me, honest as a conscience, the regulator clock marked the second in which my small life ended and my dangerous one began.
