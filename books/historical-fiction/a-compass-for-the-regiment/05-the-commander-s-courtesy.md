## 5. The Commander's Courtesy

Commander Halewick believed in the moral utility of a good breakfast. A man who ate slowly, he had observed across thirty years of command, was a man plotting something. A man who ate well — bread torn rather than cut, butter laid on thick, coffee taken black and without ceremony — was a man at peace with his circumstances. And a man at peace with his circumstances, Halewick had found, could be led anywhere.

So when the summons went down the valley for a clockmaker, and up the mountain road came not the master Brackenridge but an apprentice with ink-stained cuffs and his father's narrow shoulders, Halewick ordered the good breakfast laid: the white tablecloth, the preserved cherries, the ham that came up from the low country at ruinous expense.

He dressed for it too. Not full dress — that would be vulgar, and vulgarity was a form of doubt — but the undress uniform with the silver chain of office, the boots Bracken's saddler kept polished to the shine of a dark mirror. He checked himself in the glass of the east window, where his reflection floated over the valley like a saint over a parish, and allowed himself one moment of honest appraisal.

Sixty-one years old. Still straight. Still the face from the engravings — the jaw they called granite in the newspapers of the capital, though granite weathered, and Halewick did not. He had buried a wife, outlived two superior officers, and governed this valley longer than most of its inhabitants had been alive. The charter hung in the great hall behind him as he turned from the window, framed in gilt, signed by hands sixty years dead, and it said what it had always said: *the commander of the regiment is the law of the valley, its rents its own, its justice his to administer.* He had read it aloud every founding day since he inherited it, and each year the reading came more easily, the way a key turns best in a lock it has made.

A knock. Corporal Vess, young, earnest, slightly out of breath from the stairs.

"The clockmaker's arrived, sir. Down at the south gate. Young one. Apprentice."

"Name?"

"Dravenmoor, sir. Says — " Vess hesitated. Halewick liked Vess; he hesitated only when it mattered. "Says his father worked the observatory clock. Before."

Halewick let the word sit a moment. *Before.* The valley had many words like that, small doors that opened onto rooms nobody entered twice. Before the war. Before the ambush. Before Colonel Vane rode into the trees one October morning and came back to them as a flag-draped box and a monument.

"Dravenmoor," Halewick said pleasantly. "Yes. I knew his father. Show him up when he's washed the road off himself. Feed him first — the kitchen, not the servants' hall. I want him comfortable." He smiled at Vess, who straightened as men always did under that smile, as if it were a decoration being pinned on. "And Vess — find out how much the old man left in debts. Discreetly."

Vess went. Halewick poured his coffee and stood at the window and thought about clocks.

* * *

The observatory had been his idea, whatever the histories said about Vane. That was one of the quiet jokes Halewick kept for himself, the way other men kept snuff or scripture. The monument in the square bore Vane's face; the schoolbooks bore Vane's name; the founding-day toast was drunk to Vane's memory before it was ever drunk to the living commander. Let them have the dead man. Halewick had the charter, and the charter had teeth, and teeth were what a valley required if it was to be governed rather than merely inhabited.

But the observatory — the observatory was useful in a way monuments were not. A mountain that told the time. Trains in the low country set their schedules by it; the postal service; the insurance houses; two banks. Every noon the ball dropped on the mast and the whole province adjusted itself, and every adjustment passed, however invisibly, through the authority seated in this house. Men trusted the clock because they trusted the regiment, and they trusted the regiment because the charter told them to. It was the most elegant instrument of rule Halewick had ever possessed, and he had not even had to build it. He had only had to keep it running.

Which was why four minutes a day had become, over the past month, the single largest fact in his life.

He had handled it correctly, he believed. No panic. No public word. He had ordered Brackenridge up the mountain, and when Brackenridge's hands had begun to shake — age, or drink, or conscience; with craftsmen the three were often indistinguishable — he had arranged for the apprentice instead. A boy. A dead man's son, which was either an inconvenience or a gift, depending on the boy, and Halewick had built a career on assuming the best of people until they showed him otherwise, at which point he assumed the worst efficiently.

He heard the footsteps before he saw them: light, quick, the cadence of someone climbing stairs they considered above their station. Good. A little awe would save everyone time.

Emrys Dravenmoor came into the long room looking exactly as a clockmaker's apprentice ought: travel-worn coat, tool-roll over one shoulder, eyes going immediately — involuntarily — to the light, to the windows, to anything mechanical. His father's coloring but not his father's stillness. The old man had stood like furniture. The boy stood like a held breath.

"Commander." A correct bow, half a degree too deep.

"Mr. Dravenmoor." Halewick crossed the room with his hand already extended, took the boy's hand in both of his, and gave it the warmth of a hearth. "You honor me. Sit — no, there, where the light is. You'll take ham? You'll take coffee? You've climbed a mountain before ten o'clock; you've earned both, and my cook will be wounded if you refuse her cherries."

"I'm grateful, Commander. I didn't expect—"

"No one expects courtesy anymore. It's the century's great failing." Halewick sat opposite him, unfolded his napkin, and began, unhurriedly, to make the meal happen around the boy whether he wanted it or not. Bread torn, not cut. Butter laid on. He watched the apprentice eat and noted, with satisfaction, that he ate quickly and thoroughly. Hungry men were honest men, or at least cheaper to buy.

"You knew my father," Emrys said, when the plates had stopped moving.

"I did." True, and therefore the best kind of sentence to say. "A careful craftsman. Quiet. The sort of man who'd spend three days on a component no one would ever see, because *he'd* see it." Halewick shook his head with the proper weight of memory. "I was sorry at his passing. This valley doesn't notice the deaths that matter most."

"He never spoke of the regiment."

"Modesty. It was a generation's failing — all of them modest, all of them silent, and then they die and we're left inventing their stories for them." Halewick refilled the boy's cup himself, which the kitchen staff would report to half the barracks by evening, and that too was intentional. *The commander poured his coffee.* Small mercies, publicly witnessed, compounded like interest. "You've seen the observatory yet?"

"Not inside."

"Then you've had the worst journey in the world and no reward. Finish your cup. I'll walk you up myself."

* * *

The path from the house to the observatory was forty minutes of switchback through pine, and Halewick walked it at the boy's pace, which gave him forty minutes to be generous.

He pointed out the quarry that had cut the foundation stone ("my grandfather's contract — the family has always believed in building for a hundred years"). He named the wildflowers. At the shoulder of the mountain he paused, breathing theatrically, and let the valley open below them — the river, the roofs, the regimental parade ground green as a billiard table, the smoke of the mills — and watched the boy take it in.

"My whole world," Halewick said quietly. "When I was your age I couldn't wait to leave it. Fought to. Bled to." He touched his ribs, where the old wound lived, an honest gesture worn smooth by use. "And now I can't climb this path without thinking I'd defend every field of it with what's left of me. That's what the charter means, Mr. Dravenmoor, whatever the lawyers in the capital mutter. Not power. *Keeping.* Someone must keep a place, or it comes apart. You'll learn that, with mechanisms. Leave a clock alone a season and see what entropy does to it."

"A clock keeps itself, mostly," the boy said. "If it's built true."

Halewick laughed, warm and easy, and stored the sentence away in the room where he kept everything anyone said to him. "Well. We'll see what yours thinks."

The observatory door was unlocked, because locks suggested fear and Halewick feared nothing he could name. Inside, he became a different register of host: the enthusiast. He walked the boy past the great transit instrument, the meridian marks, the star charts pinned like trophies, narrating in the voice of a man showing off a mistress's portrait. He saved the clock for last, as one does.

It filled the end wall of the round room like an organ in a chapel — brass and walnut and the pale faces of three dials, pendulum swinging behind glass with the patience of something that would outlast everyone present.

"There she is," Halewick said. "Sixty years. She's lost her appetite lately, I'm told — four minutes a day, drifting like a drunkard home from the fair. Brackenridge swears it's beyond him." He spread his hands, the picture of a commander defeated by machinery. "I confess I don't understand her insides and I don't pretend to. That's your kingdom now, Mr. Dravenmoor. Anything you need — tools, lodging, food, an extra lamp burning till dawn — you ask, and it appears. Consider this house yours."

"That's generous."

"Generosity is just stewardship with warmth in it." He watched the boy look at the clock, and saw the look he wanted: not greed, not fear — fascination. Craftsmen were the easiest men in the world to manage. Give them a problem with teeth and they forgot there was such a thing as politics. "One request. One." He let it come lightly, almost as an afterthought, turning toward the dial as he spoke so the boy would read his profile rather than his eyes. "While you work — the mechanism, the weights, all of it — keep the room to yourself. My officers are curious men, and curiosity plus instruments equals breakage. If anyone comes asking to see the works, send them down to me. I'll entertain them. It's what I'm for."

"Yes, Commander."

"And Mr. Dravenmoor." Halewick moved to the window, hands clasped behind him, gazing out with the mild absent air of a man remembering scenery. "There was a second matter. Small. Housekeeping, really."

"Sir?"

"A traveler was reported on the valley road yesterday. Gaunt fellow, grey coat, walking with purpose and asking after people by name." A pause, calibrated. "Pell, they call him. Do you know the name?"

He heard the silence before the answer — felt its length, its texture, the way one feels a gear tooth that hasn't meshed. Then: "No, Commander."

"Mm. Probably a peddler. They wander farther this time of year." He turned back with the smile restored, sunny, unburdened, a man discussing weather. "Still — roads are changeable in autumn. If a stranger finds his way up here, wanting access to the clock, or to you, or carrying anything you can't account for — you'll tell me at once. Not from suspicion, mind." He stepped close and put a hand briefly, paternally, on the boy's shoulder. The wool of the coat was thin under his palm; underneath it the shoulder was all wire. "From protection. There are men in this valley whose business is mine to mind, and a boy alone on a mountain shouldn't have to mind it for them. I look after the people in my keeping, Mr. Dravenmoor. Ask anyone. It's the whole of my method."

The boy looked up at him, and for a heartbeat Halewick read what moved behind those eyes — calculation, or fear, or the beginning of something braver and stupider than either — and he smiled more warmly still, because whatever it was, it was better unremarked.

"I understand, Commander."

"Of course you do. Your father would have. Now —" he clapped once, briskly, the audience concluded — "the case looks sound to me, but I'm a soldier; I admire things I can't judge. Start wherever the trouble lives. And when you find it —" he paused at the door, hand on the frame, and delivered the last line the way he delivered all his best lines, lightly, looking at the mountains —

"— find it all. Clocks are honest things, Mr. Dravenmoor. It's why I trust them more than men. Whatever she's been trying to tell us these past weeks — listen to her."

* * *

He descended alone, faster than he'd climbed, and the warmth fell away from his face in stages, like cloud crossing the sun, until by the gate there was nothing on it at all.

Corporal Vess was waiting, properly.

"Well?"

"Pell was seen at Millbrook ford, sir. Day before yesterday. Bought passage downriver — the boatman remembers the grey coat. Heading for the low road."

"Carrying?"

"Satchel, sir. Heavy, the boatman thought."

Halewick nodded slowly and looked back up at the observatory, a small grey shape against the enormous sky, its mast bare against the wind. Four minutes a day. A dead colonel's handwriting somewhere in the world, moving along rivers. A clockmaker's son with his father's hands standing beside a machine everyone feared to open.

Charming, all of it. Manageable. He had governed this valley for twenty-two years by the simple principle that problems, like guests, responded to hospitality — fed, warmed, and quietly watched until they revealed what they had come for.

"Double the watch at the ford and the ferry," he said. "Quietly. And Vess — if our young clockmaker sends any message down the mountain, I want to know its contents and its address before it goes further than the gate."

"And if he asks why, sir?"

Halewick settled his cloak on his shoulders and permitted himself, at last, alone among his own, the smile that was not for anyone's comfort.

"Tell them it's for his protection," he said. "It's what I'm for."
