## 6. Four Minutes of Silence

The letters weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing that unsettled him — he'd expected contraband to feel heavier, the way guilt was supposed to. But wrapped in their third layer of oilcloth, tucked against his chest beneath his coat, they were lighter than a pocket watch. Lighter than the lie they carried.

Emrys climbed the observatory stair in the blue hour before dawn, when the mountain path was still stiff with frost and no one with sense was abroad. He had told himself a dozen versions of the story on the way up. *I'm only inspecting the escapement again. I left a tool behind. The master sent me to check the temperature coefficients.* Each version was true enough to survive a casual question and false enough to rot in his mouth.

Pell had gone before first light — Emrys had watched from the workshop window as the gaunt man folded himself into the grey of the valley road and disappeared, walking with the unhurried gait of a man who had practiced not being remembered. Before leaving, Pell had said only: *"He asked for you by name, Mr. Dravenmoor. Not your master. You."* As if that settled anything. As if being known by a dead man were an honor instead of a sentence.

The observatory door was unlatched. Isolde Marchmont would be asleep in her quarters below the instrument room — she kept strange hours, he'd learned, retiring at dusk and rising at midnight to catch the stars at their steadiest. He had four hours, maybe five, in which the building belonged to him and the clock.

Inside, the great mechanism filled the stairwell with its patient sound: that deep, metronomic breathing he had come to know over three weeks of repairs. *Tick. And tick.* Most people stopped hearing it after a day. Emrys had stopped hearing it after an hour, the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

Tonight he listened to it properly for the first time.

Four minutes a day. Losing time like a leaky bucket loses water — steadily, invisibly, without any single moment you could point to and say *there, that's where it went wrong*. He had torn down the escapement twice. He had re-poised the pendulum, cleaned every pivot, checked the suspension spring under magnification. The mechanism was immaculate. Someone had built it to survive a century, and it was failing anyway, politely, by fractions.

A clock doesn't lie, his master liked to say. A clockmaker does, but a clock never.

So if the clock was losing time, either the clock was broken in a way he couldn't find —

— or someone was making it lose time.

He set his lamp on the workbench and stood a long while in the circle of its light, listening to the case breathe. Then he took off his coat, unwrapped the letters, and got to work on the one act he could not undo.

* * *

The weight channel ran down through the heart of the case like a well shaft, a wooden conduit two hand-spans square, descending fourteen feet into the base of the cabinet where the driving weights hung on their cables. It was the one part of the clock no one touched. Weights were dumb iron; you wound them up once a week and let gravity do the rest. In three weeks of work Emrys had never once seen anyone else so much as open the access panel at the channel's midpoint — a slim mahogany door, finished to match the case, held by two brass turn-buttons that had never been turned. There was a fine film of dust along the top edge, unbroken except by a single smear, old and half-erased, as if someone had wiped it clean years ago and then stopped bothering.

They fear the mechanism, Pell had said. They fear it the way sailors fear the sea: respectfully, from a distance.

Emrys turned the buttons. The little door swung open on hinges that needed oil but worked.

Inside, the channel was dark and smelled of linseed and cold pine. The weights hung below him somewhere in the dark, motionless between ticks, and above him the great wheel train turned with its slow thunder he felt more than heard. He measured the space with his thumb and forefinger — a gap between the cable guide and the wall of the channel, maybe three fingers wide, running the length of a forearm. Dry. Dark. Safe from damp, safe from beetles, safe from men who feared mechanisms.

He wrapped the letters in a final layer of waxed linen, tied it with waxed thread, and slid the bundle into the gap. It fit the way a book fits a shelf made for it. He told himself that was coincidence. He told himself a lot of things.

Then he closed the panel, turned the buttons, and brushed the dust back over the seam with a clean camel-hair brush until the door might never have been opened at all.

His hands were steady. That frightened him more than shaking would have. Somewhere in the last three weeks, without noticing the moment it happened, he had become a man who could hide treason inside a clock with a craftsman's calm.

He sat down on the workbench stool and discovered he didn't want to leave yet. Partly it was the hour — too late to meet anyone on the path, too early to seem innocent. Partly it was something else. The letters were hidden, but the question wasn't. Why did a perfect machine lose four minutes a day?

He pulled the lamp closer and opened the case's rear service panel, the one his master's keys had admitted him to on his first day, and began — this time — at the bottom of the clock instead of the top.

* * *

It took him two hours to find it, and when he found it, he laughed out loud — a single ugly bark in the empty room that scared him more than the finding did.

The pendulum rod was steel, three feet of it, terminating in a cast bob of brass the size of a man's head. Regulation was done by a knurled nut at the base of the bob: turn it up, raise the center of oscillation, quicken the beat; turn it down, slow it. Standard. Textbook. Every apprentice learned it before they learned to file a tooth.

Except the rating nut on this clock was a lie.

The nut moved freely — he'd turned it himself in week one, verified it seated properly, calculated the rate change per revolution, and found the clock stubbornly ignoring his arithmetic. Now, working upward from the bob with the lamp angled close, he saw why. The nut's threads engaged nothing. The actual regulating screw had been cut short, decades ago, ending a quarter-inch above where the nut bottomed out. Below the decorative nut, hidden inside the bob's lower skirt, was a second arrangement entirely: a small sliding weight, a brass ring riding on a smooth unthreaded rod, held in place by a tiny spring clamp. To move it you had to reach *inside* the bob, through a slot cut in the skirt and disguised — beautifully, wickedly disguised — as a casting flaw.

Someone had rebuilt this pendulum so that ordinary regulation showed honest effort and changed nothing, while the true control sat concealed where only a hand that knew to look would ever find it.

Emrys lay on his back on the floor with the lamp wedged against the case and worked two fingers through the slot. The clamp was stiff. He needed a thin screwdriver and a great deal of care. When he finally released it, the ring slid freely, and he could see the fine scratches along the rod where it had traveled — hundreds of adjustments, thousands maybe, worn bright as a scar.

He slid the ring all the way up and clamped it. The pendulum's period shortened by a whisper. On the bench, the seconds dial began, imperceptibly, to gain.

Four minutes a day, returned to the world. He sat there watching the dial the way you watch a pot you've just brought back to a boil, and the questions came in slowly, one at a time, each heavier than the last.

Who. When. How long.

He answered the last one first, because it was written in front of him. The scratches weren't uniform. Under the lamp, with a loupe pressed to his eye, he read them the way his father had taught him to read wear on brass — the oldest ones were oxidized dull, years deep; the newest were bright. The adjustment had been made many times over many years. This was not sabotage. Sabotage is once, and angry. This was maintenance. Someone had been tending this falseness like a garden.

And the *why* — the why arrived not as a thought but as a memory of his apprenticeship arithmetic. Four minutes a day. After a week, nearly half an hour. After a month, two hours. The observatory clock was the regulator clock — the clock the town clocks were set by, the clock the regiment's duty rosters answered to, the clock whose time was carried down the mountain every noon by the time-ball and the flag signal. If the master lost four minutes daily and was quietly corrected — reset to true time at intervals — then between corrections it drifted, and everything downstream of it drifted too.

Every bell in the valley that struck the hour slightly wrong. Every guard rotation that began late. Every patrol that returned after dark believing itself punctual. Every ledger entry, every log line, every recorded hour of every recorded event for years — stamped with a time that was softly, systematically false.

You could make things happen in that fog. You could move men and goods and papers in the slack minutes. You could be seen in one place at a recorded time and be elsewhere in the true one. Or the reverse. An alibi is just a time with witnesses, and if all the witnesses' watches are wrong in the same direction, by the same amount, controlled from one room on this mountain —

The machine became a witness while he sat there. That was the only way he could describe it. One moment it was a mechanism, beautiful and neutral, a thing he understood completely; the next it was an archive, a confessor, the longest-serving officer of the regiment, present at every hour of every day for thirty years, keeping its account in the only language it knew. And someone had taught it to lie.

Not someone. His fingers knew before he did. He was looking at the slot in the bob, at the disguised casting flaw, at work of this quality — and he recognized the hand. Not the literal hand. The *style*. The confidence of the cut, the economy of it, the way the deception was finished so that it looked like honest imperfection. His father had the same trick with filed surfaces: make the repair look like wear, because wear draws no questions.

No. He shut that thought in a box and sat on it. Plenty of first-rate hands had passed through this mountain. It proved nothing. It proved nothing.

The sky beyond the east shutter had gone from black to iron. In less than an hour, Isolde Marchmont would climb to the instrument room for her midnight observations, and she would pass the clock, and she might glance at the regulator dials as astronomers do, because stars keep their own time and astronomers notice everything.

Emrys stood, closed the rear panel, and gathered his tools. Then he stopped.

There was one more thing to check, and he checked it because he was the sort of man who checked. The correction schedule. Observatories kept a rate book — a daily log of the clock's error against the stars, corrected each clear night by transit observation. If the clock had been slowed deliberately, the rate book would show it, unless the rate book had also been tended like a garden.

The book lived on the shelf beside the transit instrument, bound in green cloth, columns ruled in a fair hand. He opened it to the current year and read down the daily entries. Error: 3 minutes 51 seconds slow. Corrected. Error: 4 minutes 2 seconds slow. Corrected. Page after page, year after year, the same modest drift, the same tidy correction — always small, never alarming, never allowed to accumulate past the point where a stranger might ask why. Whoever wrote these entries knew exactly what they were doing and exactly how much falseness a routine would bear.

He flipped backward through the years. The entries changed handwriting twice, three times, but never changed character. Ten years. Fifteen. He reached the earliest volume, the first rate book, its spine cracked and its pages foxed, and turned to the opening entries in a young, careful script.

The date was the founding year of the charter. The first entry, in ink gone brown as dried blood, read:

*Error: nil. Clock set true by A.V.*

A.V. Aldous Vane had set this clock himself, the year it was built. Vane, who wrote letters from a living grave about charters and forgeries. Vane, whose promise to a nine-year-old boy had something to do with telling the time truly.

And directly beneath, dated six days later, in the same careful hand but pressed harder, the strokes gouged: *Error: 1 minute 44 seconds slow. Cause unknown. Under investigation.*

*Cause unknown*, repeated entry after entry, growing weekly, until the investigations stopped and the corrections became routine and the routine became the clock's character. Someone had begun slowing it within days of Vane setting it true — begun, perhaps, the very week the charter was signed into power.

Emrys closed the green book and put it back precisely where it had lived, spine to the wall, angle unchanged. Through the floor he could hear a door, footsteps, the small sounds of Isolde Marchmont waking into her long night. In twenty minutes she would stand in this room with her star-charts and her sharp, sleepless eyes.

He had hidden the letters from Halewick's men inside the one place they would never search. It occurred to him now, coldly, climbing the stairs toward the instrument room where lamplight had just bloomed, that he had no idea whether the person who tended those entries for fifteen years was still here to tend them —

or whether she was already coming up the stairs to ask him why the clock had suddenly started keeping perfect time.
