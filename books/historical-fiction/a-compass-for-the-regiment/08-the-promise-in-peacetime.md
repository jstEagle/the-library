## 8. The Promise in Peacetime

The letters would not stay closed.

Emrys had read them twice through the night — Vane's careful hand marching across page after page, a dead man explaining himself with the patience of someone who had expected to be doubted — and when dawn came grey through the observatory's east windows he had folded them back into their oilcloth and found his hands shaking so badly that the folds came out wrong. He refolded them. He refolded them again.

Then he did what he always did when his mind refused to be governed. He went to the clock.

The great mechanism filled the tower's heart like a ribcage of brass, and it was losing its four minutes with the same maddening regularity as before, tick by stolen tick, and there was comfort in that. A fault you can name is a fault you can face. He opened the access panel and put his hands among the wheels and let the work pull him down and away from everything the night had handed him.

It almost worked. It worked for perhaps an hour.

What broke it was small: a gear blank in his coat pocket, left over from the workshop, that he'd carried up without thinking — a disc of soft brass he'd meant to file at some idle moment. He took it out to worry at while he thought, and his thumb found the faint score marks across its face where he had begun, years ago, cutting teeth into it with a jeweler's saw, badly, and his father's voice said *no, boy, from the center out* in memory so vivid that Emrys stopped breathing.

He knew those words. He knew them not as a lesson but as a morning. He knew the smell of them — oil and woodsmoke and, underneath, the cold iron smell of the regiment's riding cloaks hung to dry by the workshop stove.

Not his father's cloak. Another man's.

The memory came up out of him the way water comes up through chalk — not all at once but everywhere at once, soaking through.

* * *

He was nine. It was the winter of the armistice year, when the regiment had come down from the passes and the whole valley smelled of woodsmoke and celebration and horses that were not used to being stabled in a town. His father's workshop had been given over, for three weeks, to repair of the regiment's instruments — chronometers, surveyor's dials, one shattered barometer that a lieutenant still mourned — and so the workshop that was usually quiet except for the tick of mending clocks had been loud all day with boots and laughter and men calling each other by ranks instead of names.

His father had hated every minute of it. Emrys remembered that clearly, even then: the way his father had drawn into himself like a clock case closing, working later, speaking less, watching the officers with an expression Emrys had no word for yet. He had a word for it now. It was the look of a man standing in a room where something he built is about to be weighed.

But the boy had loved it. The boy had loved everything about those weeks — the red coats drying by the stove, the smell of pipe smoke, and most of all the tall officer with the grey eyes who came on the fourth day carrying a broken carriage clock and stayed two hours past the fixing of it.

Colonel Aldous Vane. Though nobody had needed to tell Emrys the name; the whole valley spoke it like weather. The hero of the pass. The youngest colonel in the regiment's history. The man whose portrait hung in the guildhall looking nobly off at something no one else could see.

In person he was thinner than his portrait and he laughed more. He sat on the workshop stool like any tradesman waiting on a boot heel, and when nine-year-old Emrys crept close enough to watch the disassembly, the Colonel moved over and made room on the bench.

"Do you know what this is?" Vane asked him, holding up a small wheel between finger and thumb.

"A gear."

"A gear. And do you know why it has as many teeth as it does?"

Emrys had shrugged. So that it fit the other gears, he'd said, or something equally childish, and Vane had smiled — not the polite smile adults gave children before turning back to adult things, but the other kind, the rare kind, the one that said *wrong answer, good thinking.*

"It has as many teeth as it needs," Vane said, "to keep a promise. Every wheel in a clock is promising something to every other wheel. Turn me once, I'll turn you so many. Carry this hour faithfully to that hour. A gear is just a promise made solid. That's all a clock is, in the end — a machine for keeping promises about time." He set the little wheel in Emrys's palm and closed the boy's fingers around it. "And a man who breaks a promise about time is worse than a man who breaks a promise about money. Money you can make again. An hour, once lost or once lied about, is gone forever."

Emrys's father, at the far bench, had looked up sharply at that. Emrys remembered that too — remembered wondering, in the way of children, why Papa looked frightened, and then forgetting, because Vane was already saying:

"Well. If you're going to stand there looking at my clock like that, you may as well learn to cut one. Come here. Mind your fingers."

* * *

They cut a gear together over three afternoons.

Vane turned out to know the craft properly — not the shallow competence of a gentleman dabbler but real knowledge, the kind that comes only from doing. Later, much later, Emrys would understand where a career soldier had learned to cut clockwork: siege works, gunnery timing, the long bored winters of peacetime garrison. But at nine he only understood that the tall officer held the jeweler's saw correctly and corrected Emrys's grip gently and never once made him feel small.

"From the center out," Vane said, guiding the first cut. "Always lay out your truth from the center. Mark the middle first. Everything else is measured from what you decide the middle is. Get the middle wrong and no amount of skill at the edge will save you."

His father watched from across the shop the whole third afternoon, filing the same place on the same bracket for over an hour, and said nothing until Vane rose to leave.

"Colonel," his father said. "A word. Privately."

Vane glanced at the boy. "Run along, lad. Count the teeth on that wheel for me. I'll want the number tomorrow."

So Emrys had counted — forty-two, though he counted four times to be sure — sitting on the stairs that led up to the loft, close enough to hear the low voices in the shop below and far enough that the words arrived blurred. He caught his own name once. He caught the word *charter*, spoken by his father in a voice Emrys had never heard him use, tight and quick, and then a longer stretch he couldn't parse at all, and then Vane's voice, level and unhurried and suddenly nothing at all like the laughing man of the workbench:

"—then we will have it out properly. In daylight. Whatever it costs."

Something else, lower. Then Vane again, clearer, almost amused, but the amusement had a blade in it:

"You mistake me, Master Dravenmoor. I have spent eleven years keeping soldiers alive with honest time. I don't intend to spend the rest of my life profiting from a dishonest hour."

Boots on the stair. Emrys bent frantically over his counting. Vane came up past him, paused, and looked down at the wheel in the boy's hands.

"Well? How many?"

"Forty-two."

"Forty-two," Vane repeated, gravely, as if confirming something of state. "Good. Forty-two is a true number." And then he had crouched, red coat pooling around him, so that his eyes were level with the boy's, and whatever had passed in the shop below was gone from his face entirely, and he was the laughing officer again — mostly. There was something underneath. Even at nine, Emrys had seen it.

"I'm going away tomorrow," Vane said. "Back up the valley, then on to the capital for the winter sessions. I may be gone some time. So I want to leave something with you, and I want you to take it seriously, because I am going to ask you to take it seriously."

Emrys had nodded, terrified and thrilled.

"When you are grown — and you will be grown sooner than either of us likes — you will be the clockmaker here. This valley will come to you to know the time. Not the King's men, not the church bell, not the sun. You. And one day — I don't know when, I don't know how, and I may not be there to explain it — someone will ask you for the true hour, and giving it will cost you something. It may cost you a great deal." Vane held out his hand, palm up, and after a moment Emrys understood and laid the little brass wheel in it. Vane turned it over once and gave it back. "Keep that. Cut the rest of its teeth yourself, when your hands are ready. And remember: from the center out. Decide what the truth is first, and measure everything else against it. Can you promise me that? If ever you are asked — you will tell the time truly."

Nine-year-old Emrys, who had promised his mother to eat his greens and his father to sweep the shavings and the neighbor to return a borrowed auger, none of which promises had survived the week, made this promise and felt — for the first time in his short life — that a promise could have weight. That some words were load-bearing.

"I promise," he said.

"That's settled, then." Vane stood, and ruffled the boy's hair with a hand that smelled of oil and cold air, and went down the stairs and out into the snow, and the bell above the door rang behind him, and that was the last anyone in the valley saw of Colonel Aldous Vane alive.

Officially.

* * *

Emrys came back to the present with the gear blank in his fist and the imprint of its teeth cut into his palm.

He stood a long time in the cold heart of the clock tower, listening to the mechanism steal its four minutes, and let the two ends of his life meet.

The officer at the bench. The word *charter* in his father's mouth, spoken like a confession trying not to be one. *We will have it out properly, in daylight, whatever it costs.* And six months afterward — he could count it now, the seasons matched — the ambush in the pass, the massed drums, the empty coffin under the monument, the whole valley in black for a hero who had, apparently, walked out of his own funeral and kept walking.

His father had never spoken Vane's name again. Emrys had assumed it was grief, or the general silence men maintained about heroes. Now he understood it was something closer to fear. His father had known what Vane knew. Had perhaps been the reason Vane knew it. And when Vane had demanded it be brought into daylight, his father — the craftsman whose hands had made the lie, whose ledger Emrys had found folded inside a folio of escapement drawings, payments entered in his own neat cipher — his father had chosen the dark, and the dark had swallowed him within three years of the Colonel's supposed death. A fever, they said. Emrys had always privately believed his father had simply drunk himself into the grave, and had built a modest shrine of filial forgiveness around that belief.

Now the shrine was rubble. And underneath it was not a monster, which might have been easier, but a frightened man — a man who took money to forge a charter and then spent twenty years flinching whenever the regiment's band played in the square.

*I don't intend to spend the rest of my life profiting from a dishonest hour.*

Emrys looked down at the gear blank. Forty-two teeth, half-cut, abandoned for thirteen years in a drawer of his father's things — his father had kept it, Emrys realized, all that time. Kept the boy's unfinished lesson the way other men keep unpaid debts.

He had told Pell the letters could not go in the clock. He had argued, hedged, resisted — and then he had hidden them anyway, deep in the weight channel, because a marginal note in a dead man's draft had his father's handwriting in it, and refusal had curdled into complicity somewhere in the night and he hadn't noticed exactly when.

That wasn't enough anymore. He could see that now, standing inside the machine his father's generation had taught him to revere. Hiding evidence was not telling the truth. It was only hiding it more carefully.

*If ever you are asked — you will tell the time truly.*

Nobody had asked him yet. That was the thing that steadied him, oddly. Nobody had come to the tower and put the question plainly, and so the promise was still unspent, still his, still possible. He could carry these letters another mile or another year and still say honestly that no one had asked. There was comfort in that. There was also, he noticed, shame in it — the specific shame of a man measuring exactly how long he can postpone his own oath.

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, in the yard, hooves on frozen gravel — the observatory got few visitors, and none of them at speed.

Emrys closed the access panel, pocketed the gear blank, and went to the window.

Two riders. Regimental cloaks, Halewick's blue facings. Not coming at a gallop — that was somehow worse — but at the deliberate trot of men under orders to be courteous. The taller of the two raised a hand to the tower as if greeting an old friend.

Emrys's stomach dropped through thirteen years and landed, cold and certain, at the bottom of the stair. He thought of the letters in the weight channel, rustling their paper whisper with every swing of the weights — *they'll hear it, Pell had warned, no, they won't, unless—* — and he thought of Vane's voice saying *from the center out,* and he understood, finally, that the question he had been waiting for was not going to arrive politely phrased.

It was climbing his stairs right now, wearing the regiment's blue.

He put his hand flat against the clock case, as if to shield it, and went down to open the door.
