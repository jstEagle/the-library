## 9. Roadblock at the Ford

The satchel was heavier than it should have been, and that was the point of it.

Pell had weighted it himself, three nights before, in the back room of the tannery where he'd been sleeping on a cot that smelled of lime and blood. He'd filled the leather with a copybook's worth of foolscap — pages and pages of careful script in Vane's hand, or close enough to Vane's hand to pass at arm's length — and beneath them he had packed river stones wrapped in cloth until the thing weighed as much as a sleeping cat. A man carrying nothing walks like a man carrying nothing. A man carrying secrets carries his shoulders differently. Halewick's soldiers would know the difference even if they couldn't name it, so Pell gave them shoulders to read: heavy, guarded, precious.

What mattered was not in the satchel at all. What mattered was sewn into the lining of his coat, flat as a psalm and twice as damning: eleven pages copied from the colonel's letters, plus the draft clause Emrys Dravenmoor had identified as his father's work, plus Isolde Marchmont's star-charts folded to the size of a prayer card. The true cargo rode against his ribs like a second heart.

*If they take the bag,* Vane had said, *they must believe they've taken everything.*

Pell had been a courier for thirty years — first for a wool merchant, then for anyone who paid, then for men whose names he made a point of forgetting — and in thirty years he had learned one law above all others: never carry the truth and its decoy in the same pair of hands. He was old enough now that his hands shook on cold mornings anyway. Best they shake over stones.

He left the valley before dawn by the secondary road, the one that followed the Aldern up through the beech woods instead of down through the regimental town. It added two days. It also added two fords, a charcoal-burner's track, and forty miles of country where a man might plausibly be nobody at all. He walked with a carter's stoop and a peddler's bundle of tinware strapped atop the satchel, because a courier carrying pots looked like commerce, and commerce was invisible.

By noon of the first day he had begun to believe his own disguise. That was the danger. He knew it was the danger, and he believed it anyway, the way a man believes the ice will hold because it held yesterday.

* * *

The ford at Marlebridge was not supposed to be watched.

It was a bad crossing — knee-deep gravel, a current that shoved sideways, a history of drowned sheep — which was precisely why the secondary road used it. Anyone wanting the capital took the bridge at Thurlow, where there was a tollhouse, an inn, and a sergeant who stamped papers without reading them. The ford was for smugglers, lovers, and men avoiding tolls. Pell had crossed it nine times in his life without seeing another soul.

This time there were six soldiers and a fire.

He saw the smoke first, thin and blue above the beeches, and stopped with one hand on a trunk like a man admiring trees. Down the bank, horses stood hipshot in the shade. A corporal sat on a stone eating bread. Two men were wading the shallows with poles, prodding the gravel bed with what looked like idle thoroughness, and a sixth stood at the far side checking the papers of a drover whose cattle lowed at being delayed.

They weren't hunting a man. They were filtering a road. Every soul who came to the water would be turned out, opened up, read. And behind Pell, four miles back, the road forked only once, and that fork ran past the regiment's own sawmill.

His mouth went dry in a way no amount of planning cures.

He considered the river itself. The Aldern here ran fast and cold with snowmelt; a strong swimmer could go down a mile and come out below the sentries' sightline, but a fifty-two-year-old courier with a coat full of paper did not swim well, and wet paper was no paper at all. He considered the woods, but the beeches stood open to the ground this time of year, leafless, and a lone walker moving fast uphill draws every eye a sentry owns.

So he did what couriers do, which is to say he became boring. He unshouldered the bundle of tinware, sat down on a root within clear view of the path, and began, ostentatiously, to eat bread and cheese. A peddler resting. Nothing in the world more natural. He let them see him from a distance for ten minutes, a small gray man doing nothing, until he was scenery. Then he shouldered up and walked down to the water like a man with a schedule he resented.

"Oi." The corporal didn't stand. "Crossing?"

"If the water lets me." Pell pitched his voice tired and local. "Got pots to sell in Ashcombe by Friday."

"Ashcombe." The corporal chewed. "That's past the hills. Long way to carry kettles."

"Long way to carry anything. That's the trade." Pell smiled with half his face. "You want one? Copper, holds heat like a grudge."

Something flickered across the corporal's expression — not suspicion exactly, but the boredom of a man who has been standing at a freezing ford since morning checking farmers for contraband and would rather talk about kettles than do it. That flicker was worth more than gold. Pell pressed it gently. "Bad business, all this? Saw your fire from up the track."

"Checking papers. New orders." The corporal said it in the tone of a man reciting words he'd been told to recite, and then, because bored men volunteer, added: "Looking for a courier, if you can believe it. One man. Whole valley turned upside down."

"One man," Pell repeated, and heard how carefully his own voice stayed stupid. "What's he done?"

"Dunno. Papers is papers." The corporal waved at the drover ahead, still fumbling at the far bank. "Get in line. You'll be checked same as him."

*Of course I will,* Pell thought. *I counted on being checked.* The plan had always assumed inspection; the plan had simply hoped the inspection would find stones and bad handwriting. But hope, he reminded himself, wading toward the soldiers, is just fear wearing its good coat.

The soldier at the water's edge was young, chapped red at the cheeks, and held Pell's papers upside down for a full three seconds before turning them around. Peddler's license, issued in a county Pell had actually worked, aged and stained appropriately — he had aged it himself with tea and a warm oven, a trick his father had used for excise dodging and which Pell had inherited along with his hands. The boy read slowly, lips moving.

"Name."

"Pell. Tom Pell."

"You the Tom Pell off the valley road? The one we're—"

"I'm a Tom Pell with a license and eleven kettles," Pell said, "and I'm losing daylight."

It was too sharp. He heard it land — heard the boy's eyes come up off the page — and understood in one cold instant that he had spent thirty years being forgettable and had just made himself memorable. The boy looked at him longer. Looked at the bundle of pots. Looked at the satchel beneath them, at the way Pell's shoulder sat under its weight.

"That's a heavy bag for pots."

"The pots are light. The stock book isn't." Pell set the whole load down on the gravel, unhurried, a man with nothing to hurry about, and while his hands were down there anyway, busy, legitimate, he worked the strap loose. "Look in it if you like. You'll get gravel dust on the kettles and I'll get an argument from my wife, but look if you've got to."

The offer disarmed better than any refusal. The boy hesitated — and in that hesitation, fate leaned over the board.

Because that was when the drover's cattle chose to bolt.

Whether it was the cold water on their hocks, or the crack of a pole against a flank, or simple cow malice, half the herd swung upstream in a bawling brown flood, straight through the shallows where the two pole-prodding soldiers stood, and those two men went down in the gravel with a splash that soaked everyone within ten feet. The corporal shouted. The boy at the papers turned his head — one heartbeat, two — and Pell moved.

He did not run. Running is what hunted things do. He picked up his bundle, stepped backward off the path with a mumbled curse about cattle and idiots, and walked — briskly, bent, a peddler going round — into the alder scrub at the river bend. Ten steps. Twenty. The shouting behind him resolved into orders. Then a whistle, one long note, the kind of sound that means *stop that man*, and Pell dropped the pots — sorry, pots, you deserved better — and ran after all.

* * *

The river saved him, which felt like a joke with teeth.

There was a place below the bend where the Aldern cut against the far bank and deepened between roots, and he had noted it an hour earlier with a courier's habit of noting exits, though he had honestly expected to need none. He went in off a deadfall with his coat clutched high and let the current take him downstream, boots dragging, breath sawing, the cold closing on his chest like a fist. Fifty yards. A hundred. The shouting faded behind the noise of water. He came out on a shingle spit where the channel split around an island, lay flat among the stones with his face in the gravel, and breathed until breathing became voluntary again.

They found the satchel within the hour. He heard them — voices on the wind, the whinny of a horse, someone laughing with the particular laugh of men who have caught something. He lay among the stones and listened to his own decoy being carried away in triumph, and felt nothing so much as a strange, cold pride. Let them open it by firelight tonight. Let them read pages of beautiful, meaningless forgery and find the river stones beneath. Let some clerk in the regimental town spend a week comparing that script against known samples and conclude they had seized the whole conspiracy in one wet bag.

Let them think the war was over.

Pell peeled off his boots, poured out the water, and wrung his stockings like a washerwoman, teeth chattering. Against his ribs, inside the oilskin, the true pages were damp at one corner and otherwise untouched. Eleven sheets of testimony. A dead man's living hand. He pressed them flat between his palms as if warming a bird, and thought, not for the first time, that courage was mostly logistics conducted at speed.

He had lost the pots, the satchel, and his cover. He had kept the truth. On balance — he inventoried himself, joint by joint — on balance he was ahead.

Then he counted what remained of the day, and stopped feeling ahead. Ashcombe was two days' walk. The grain merchant who would carry the packet the final leg left the valley market at week's end; miss him and the testimony sat in Pell's coat through another week of searches, another tightening net, while somewhere behind them Halewick read forged letters by lamplight and smiled. Ahead of Pell lay forty miles of country that now knew a courier was running it. His face, he suspected, was being described at this very moment in barracks and tollhouses: *small, gray, sixtyish, talks too much about kettles.*

He got up, wrung out his coat, and turned his back on the ford.

* * *

He walked the rest of that day and most of the night, off the roads entirely, following the charcoal-burners' track along the ridge where the ground was soft and printed nothing. Twice he heard horses below him and stood still in the dark until they passed. Once he slept, badly, ninety minutes in the lee of a fallen beech, waking at every owl-note convinced it was a whistle.

Near dawn, in a hollow where mist pooled like milk, he allowed himself the luxury of thinking about Emrys Dravenmoor — the clockmaker's boy grown into a serious young man with his father's hands and, God help them all, his father's conscience to redeem. Pell had carried messages for liars and cowards and one or two genuine villains, and he had long ago stopped believing in people. But the apprentice had looked at a draft of the charter and recognized his own father in it, and had not looked away. Most men would have burned it and called the burning loyalty. The boy had called it evidence.

*You're the last honest hands in this valley,* Pell had told him, and meant it, and now the honest hands were holding a master clock together while Pell himself staggered through fog with the whole truth sewn against his ribs like a wound that hadn't decided whether to kill him.

He measured his remaining courage the way he measured everything now: in hours. Three days to Ashcombe. Three days of being the most wanted man between the mountains and the plain. He was fifty-two years old, soaked to the skin, and carrying a dead hero's testament in a coat that smelled of river.

Somewhere behind him, he knew, the soldiers would be opening the satchel right about now — and somewhere beyond that, Commander Halewick would be reading pages of flawless false testimony and believing, for a few golden days, that he had won.

Pell pulled the wet coat tight, put the ridge under his feet, and started walking.

Three days. After that, either the truth reached the capital — or the stones in a leather bag would have to testify in his place.
