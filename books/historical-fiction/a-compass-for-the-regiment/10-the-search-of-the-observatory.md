## 10. The Search of the Observatory

They came at half past nine in the morning, while the fog still held the valley like a hand around a throat.

I knew before I heard the boots on the stair. I had known since dawn, when Pell's note arrived folded inside a delivery of lamp oil — *they have your name, boy, and they have a description that fits you badly, which is worse* — and I had spent the hours since doing the only useful thing I could think of: winding the clock.

Not because it needed winding. Because my hands needed somewhere to be that was not shaking.

The master clock stood in the center of the observatory's great room like a patient ox, eleven feet of oak and brass, its weights descending in their channels behind panels of latticed glass. Somewhere inside those channels, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged between the counterweight and the oak lining, lay forty-one letters in the handwriting of a dead man. Every hour, the great weight sank past them by exactly as much as it had risen the night before. Every hour, paper sat six inches from iron, and said nothing.

I had learned to hear it anyway.

There — as the escapement ticked and the train wheels turned — the faintest whisper of movement, softer than breath, softer than doubt. No one could possibly hear it over the clock's own voice. I told myself this. My hands did not believe me.

The boots reached the landing. Three sets, heavy, unhurried, with the particular rhythm of men who had been told they had all day and intended to use it. Then a fourth set, lighter, and I recognized it and felt something in my chest turn over — not fear, or not only fear. Recognition.

Sergeant Brack.

He had come to the workshop twice that winter to have a watch regulated, and he had paid promptly and complained about the price and called me "boy" in the way that wasn't an insult so much as a habit. He had stood three feet from my father's bench and talked about his wife's rheumatism. He was a man who searched houses for a living and apologized for the mess afterward.

"Master Dravenmoor." He filled the doorway, and behind him came two soldiers whose faces I did not know and would not forget — one broad and bored, one thin with quick eyes that went everywhere and rested nowhere. And behind them, unannounced and unexpected, Commander Halewick himself.

He looked better than he had at our first meeting. Rested. Amused. He wore civilian wool today, grey, cut close, the uniform of a man who wanted you to forget he owned one.

"Don't rise," Halewick said, though I hadn't. "We're told there's been a theft from the regimental stores. Lamp oil, candles, a quantity of paper. Nothing dramatic — but paper is paper, and in these hills paper has a way of becoming pamphlets, and pamphlets have a way of becoming trouble." He smiled. "You'll forgive the intrusion. We're searching every building above the treeline. Yours is simply next."

"Next after what?" I asked.

"After the chapel ruin," Brack said, before anyone could stop him. Then his jaw tightened, aware he'd said more than the script allowed.

Something cold walked down my spine. The chapel. Vane's chapel. They had gone there first, and if they had gone there first—

But Halewick's expression gave nothing away except patience. If they'd found a dying man in the ruined chapel, we would not be having this conversation; we would be having a different one entirely, and I would already be in a cell. So: empty. Or emptied. Pell, then, had moved him. Or failed to, and both of them were dead on a mountainside, and I was performing calm arithmetic on corpses.

"The observatory is the astronomer's domain," I said. "Miss Marchmont keeps the keys."

"Miss Marchmont is in the valley," Halewick said pleasantly. "Purchasing silver nitrate. She left instructions that you were to admit us. Didn't she mention it?"

She had not, which meant either she didn't know, or she did know and had chosen to be elsewhere when it happened, and I couldn't decide which possibility frightened me more.

"Well," I said, stepping aside. "Mind the threshold plate. It's original 1811 brass and Miss Marchmont will have your pension if you scratch it."

That got a huff out of the broad soldier. Even Brack's mouth twitched. Halewick laughed outright, warm as brandy.

"There it is," he said. "Your father had that same gall. A tradesman's insolence, but clean. Come along, gentlemen. Master Dravenmoor will give us the tour, won't you? You know the instrument better than any of us."

And that was the trap laid open on the table, and I walked into it with my eyes wide, because refusing the tour would have been its own confession.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it does not shout. It sits at conversational volume, sips tea, asks about the weather. For four hours I conducted my own interrogation in the voice of a man showing off his trade, and underneath it, minute by minute, the clock counted down the distance between me and the rope.

They started in the east rooms — the sleeping quarters, the chart library, Isolde's small office with its ink-stained desk. I narrated. That was my strategy and I committed to it fully: a tour so thorough, so technical, so suffocatingly detailed that it would leave no silence for them to think in. Silence was the enemy. In silence, a man's ear detaches from politeness and starts listening to rooms.

"So this is the transit room," I said, walking them through. "Meridian telescope, fixed north-south. She observes stars crossing the wire and records the instant against the clock. Which is why the clock matters — every time in every almanac we publish comes off that dial. Lose four minutes a day and your star charts are fiction. Lose four minutes a day and the valley's mills ring noon whenever they feel like it."

"Which is why the commander wants it fixed," Brack said.

"Which is why I'm here," I agreed.

The thin soldier opened Isolde's desk drawers without asking. I watched his quick fingers lift a letter knife, weigh it, put it back. He found her observation logs and read three pages upside down, fast, and I felt sweat start at my hairline until I remembered — she logs *times*, not contents. Columns of figures. Hours and errors. The most incriminating document in the valley, disguised as bookkeeping. If you didn't already know what the numbers meant, they meant nothing.

He put the log back and moved on.

In the corridor outside the chart library, Halewick fell into step beside me, close enough that I could smell bay rum and something under it, leather and horse.

"My clerk tells me you visited the regimental archive last month," he said lightly. "Requesting the founding charter. To check a measurement."

"To check a measurement," I confirmed. "The charter specifies the observatory's boundary by chain-lengths from the old boundary oak, and the oak came down in the storm two winters back. Miss Marchmont disputes where the new line runs. We thought the charter might settle it."

"And did it?"

"It settled nothing. The description contradicts itself. Whoever drew it up measured from the oak and then described the line as if measuring from the river." I shrugged. "Old documents do that. They're written by committee."

Halewick looked at me for a long moment, and in that moment I understood that he was not testing whether I had seen the forgery. He was testing whether I had seen it and *understood what it cost to see it*. His eyes were quite kind. That was the worst thing about them.

"Committee," he repeated. "Yes. History is written by committees, Master Dravenmoor, and then it is maintained by families. I try never to resent that arrangement. Resentment is expensive." He patted my shoulder once, twice, like a man settling a horse. "Onward."

* * *

They worked through the morning with a thoroughness I had to admire even as it peeled years off my life. The broad soldier went through the storeroom shelf by shelf, tapping sacks, opening the salt boxes, running a blade along the seam of the flour barrel. The thin one climbed into the roof space with a lantern and stayed up there so long I began composing the sentence I'd say to Isolde about her insulation being disturbed, just to have words ready, just to keep the machinery of speech oiled.

And all the while, the clock ran.

I heard it the way you hear your own heartbeat when you're lying awake at night — too well. Each swing of the pendulum, each click of the escapement, and threaded through it, real or imagined, that whisper of oilcloth settling as the weight descended another inch. By ten o'clock the weight had dropped past the topmost letter. By eleven, past the middle of the bundle. If anyone opened the weight channel now, they would not find paper near the mechanism's working parts, where a search might be casual — they would find it deep in the channel, deliberately placed, deliberately hidden, and no explanation on earth would cover it.

"Master Dravenmoor." Brack appeared at my elbow in the south gallery. He spoke quietly, without the others hearing. "A word of advice, freely given."

"Sergeant."

"When the search reaches the clock — and it will, because it's the only thing in this building worth searching — don't hover. Men hover over the thing they're afraid of. Stand away from it. Yawn at it, if you can manage a yawn." He was looking at the clock as he said it, at the great case with its brass face, and his voice stayed level as a spirit level. "My mother kept her savings in a hymnal. Everyone assumed the strongbox. She died rich and nobody ever knew. It's not the hiding place that fails, lad. It's the face you make standing next to it."

"I don't know what you—"

"No," he said. "You don't. Nobody ever does." And he raised his voice back to parade volume and called across the gallery: "Kestrel! Leave the lady's charts alone, they're beyond either of us!"

* * *

They reached the great room at half past one.

I had rehearsed this the way you rehearse falling: knowing the knowledge wouldn't help. The broad soldier — Kestrel — circled the clock once, professionally, the way a farrier circles a horse. He knocked on the case panels. Solid oak answered him, dense and dumb.

"Runs loud," he said.

"She runs true," I said. "Or nearly. Four minutes slow a day, which is why I'm here. Knocking won't tell you anything except that oak is oak."

"What's in the channels?"

"Cast-iron weights. Two hundred pounds each. They drive the going train and the strike train. There's a pulley system at the head of each channel and a buffer at the foot." I said it fluently, and hated how fluent it sounded, and made myself add, carelessly, "You can look if you want. Mind the glass."

It was the correct move and I knew it and it nearly killed me. Refusal would have focused every eye in the room on the one panel I refused. Permission spread attention thin. But watching Kestrel slide back the lattice glass and reach his arm into the west channel — his fingers passing within inches of the oilcloth, feeling along the oak lining for loose boards — was like watching a man pat down a wound to see if it's deep.

His hand stopped.

Everything in me stopped. The pendulum did not stop; the pendulum is indifferent to human affairs, which is the whole point of pendulums, and I clung to that indifference like a rail in flood.

"Something here," Kestrel said. "Rough patch on the lining."

"Wax," I said. "The joiners sealed the channels against damp with beeswax and rosin. It goes lumpy with age. Half the clocks in the county have it. If it bothers the run I scrape it smooth, but it's cosmetic." I crossed the room — casually, yawningly, thank you Sergeant Brack — and leaned past him and rapped my knuckle on the spot. "Feel. It's hard all through. Wax doesn't sound like paper, and it doesn't tear like paper. Go on, dig it out if you like. I'll reseal it tonight."

He dug at it with a thumbnail. Beeswax, actual beeswax, the seal I had laid over the lining joint three days ago for exactly this reason, flaked and crumbled under his nail. Behind it: oak. Oak, and beneath the oak — six inches further down the channel, past the depth of any fingertip, riding slowly downward with two hundred pounds of iron — the truth.

"Huh," said Kestrel, and lost interest, because men lose interest when a mystery resolves into wax, and because I had given him permission to lose interest, which is the kindest thing you can do for a searching man.

Halewick had not lost interest. He stood by the south window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the clock face rather than the clock body, and after a while he said, conversationally:

"Do you know what I like about that machine, Master Dravenmoor?"

"It tells the time."

"It *insists* on the time." He turned. "Every other institution in this valley bends. The law bends to the charter, the charter bends to necessity, necessity bends to whoever holds the pen that week. But that clock—" he nodded at it almost fondly, "—that clock does not bend. Four minutes slow, you say. Someone made it slow. Deliberately. Year after year, a little theft of time, and the machine recorded it faithfully, because that is all a faithful machine knows how to do. Record." He smiled at me. "Machines make excellent witnesses. That's their tragedy. They cannot lie, and they cannot testify. They can only wait until someone learns to read them."

My mouth was dry enough to grind grain in. "That's very philosophical for a man hunting stolen candles, sir."

"Candles today," Halewick agreed. "Who knows tomorrow." He glanced at his pocket watch — his own watch, gold-cased, keeping whatever time he preferred — and snapped it shut. "Gentlemen. We've done this floor. The cellar?"

* * *

The cellar took an hour. It contained coal, spare line, three crates of instrument packing straw, and Isolde's reserve of mercury in stoppered flasks, which Kestrel examined with the reverence due to anything that might explode. I gave them the lamp, told them which steps were rotten, and stood at the top of the stairs listening to the muffled percussion of a search, and let myself, for ninety seconds, feel the fear properly — let it move through me like weather — because I had learned that afternoon that fear postponed becomes panic, but fear spent promptly becomes only tiredness.

When they came up, the thin soldier had straw dust on his coat and nothing in his hands.

"Clean," Brack reported.

"Clean," Halewick echoed. He put on his gloves, finger by finger, taking his time. At the door he paused and looked back at me with that terrible warmth.

"You'll have the clock right soon, I hope. True time. It matters to me more than you'd guess — a valley ought to agree on the hour. When everything else is argued about, the hour should be the one thing we share." He settled his hat. "Oh — and Master Dravenmoor. If a stranger should come asking for the clockmaker by name — a gaunt fellow, cold hands, polite manner — send for me before you speak to him. Consider it a professional courtesy. Men in your position can't be too careful about strangers."

"I'll remember it, sir."

"I'm sure you will." And he went down the stairs, and the boots went after him, and the door closed, and the fog swallowed the hoofbeats, and the observatory was silent except for the one honest voice it had.

I stood in the great room until my legs decided standing was over. Then I sat down on the floor, right there, in the dust Kestrel's boots had raised, with my back against the case, and felt the pendulum swing through the wood behind my shoulder blades — tick, tock, tick, tock — the machine breathing, the letters riding down in the dark, the whole day held together by oak and brass and one sergeant's strange mercy.

Brack had known. Not known — suspected, perhaps, or simply read the fear off me and chosen, for reasons of his own, to teach me how to hide it better. A hymnal. His mother's savings in a hymnal. I turned the gift over and could not tell if it was kindness or a hook, bait dangled to see what I'd do with it.

Outside, the light was going copper behind the ridge. Isolde would come up from the valley within the hour, smelling of silver nitrate and pretending she hadn't arranged her absence perfectly. There would be decisions to make — the letters could not stay in the channel much longer; the weight would reach the bottom of its descent tomorrow night and the rewinding would bring hands to them again; Pell's route to the capital needed a second courier in case the first was taken; and somewhere on a frozen mountainside an old man was waiting to learn whether his testimony still lived.

But all of that was tomorrow's work. Tonight there was only the clock, and me, and the four minutes of lie I had promised myself I would someday erase.

I got up. I fetched the key to the winding crank. And as I lifted it from its hook, I saw, on the floor by the threshold plate — the original 1811 brass I'd joked about — a slip of paper that had not been there when the soldiers arrived.

Folded once. No name. In a hand I did not recognize:

*They searched the chapel yesterday. It was empty, and they know it was empty, and an empty chapel frightens Halewick more than a full one. He is doubling the ford patrols tonight. Whatever you are sending to the capital must travel by Thursday or not at all.*

I read it three times. Then I fed it to the lamp, watched it curl, and went to wind the weights — carefully, gently, easing the great load upward so the oilcloth bundle rose with it, back toward the dark at the top of the channel, back toward safety.

Thursday. Two days.

I had a clock to make honest and a road to choose, and suddenly, terribly, no time at all.
