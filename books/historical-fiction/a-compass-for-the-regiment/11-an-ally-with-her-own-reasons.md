## 11. An Ally with Her Own Reasons

Isolde Marchmont had learned long ago that the best way to be left alone was to be indispensable and slightly frightening. The soldiers who climbed the observatory road brought her their superstitious awe like an offering — the woman who talked to stars, the woman whose instruments could see a man's sins written in the sky — and they left her instruments alone, which was all she had ever asked of the regiment.

She had been at the eyepiece for three hours when she noticed Emrys Dravenmoor in the transit room, standing too still beside her meridian circle, holding one of her star-charts as though it were something that might bite.

"You're reading it upside down," she said, without turning from the telescope.

He turned the chart around. "The declinations are wrong," he said.

"They're not wrong."

"The clock says otherwise." He set the chart down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a thing he has decided not to throw. "Your transit times are off by four minutes against the master. Every night. For — how long?"

Isolde swung the telescope aside and looked at him properly. He was younger than she'd expected, this clockmaker's son; there was ink on his cuff and a stubbornness in his jaw that reminded her unpleasantly of someone she'd once tried to save. "Two years," she said. "Two years, four months, and eleven days. I keep count."

"Of what, exactly?"

"Of every hour that never happened." She crossed to the cabinet where she kept her working notebooks — not the fair copies she let Halewick's clerks inspect, but the real ones — and took out a ledger bound in grey cloth, unmarked. She laid it between them. "Every observation I've made since the spring of '46 is timed by the stars, not by your master clock. Stars don't take orders."

Emrys didn't touch the ledger. He was looking at it the way he'd looked at the chart — as if the numbers were rearranging themselves into a shape he didn't want to see. "You knew the clock was losing time."

"I knew the clock *couldn't* lose time. Not four minutes a day, not steadily, not without a mechanical cause. Your father built that escapement, boy. It doesn't drift. It's made to be *drifted*."

Something moved behind his face — grief or recognition, hard to tell them apart at that distance. "You've been recording falsified hours for two years," he said slowly. "Why didn't you report it? Why didn't you repair it yourself? You're no fool."

"No," Isolde agreed. "I'm not."

* * *

She poured tea because her hands wanted something to do, and because it is easier to confess over cups than across a bare table. She gave him the chipped blue one, the guest cup, and sat down opposite him with her own hands wrapped around the heat.

"My brother served under Vane," she said. "Tomas Marchmont. You'll find his name in the regimental rolls if the rolls haven't been cleaned. He died at the ford action — the same ambush where the colonel fell, according to the official account."

Emrys went very still. "According to."

"According to." She drank. "Tomas wrote to me for six years. Every letter came through channels so circuitous I sometimes thought he was writing from the moon rather than from a garrison forty miles away. Then the letters stopped, and a year later a parcel arrived with his effects and a note in a clerk's hand telling me he'd fallen in the colonel's disgrace. That's what they called it. *The colonel's disgrace.* As though my brother had been killed by a stain."

"And you believed the record."

"I believed nothing. That's rather the point." She set down her cup. "When the clock began to lie, I started to write down exactly how it lied, night after night, because a discrepancy that steady isn't decay — it's intention. Someone wanted certain hours to be uncertain. Someone wanted a shadow over the observatory's testimony, so that when the day came that anyone asked questions about dates, about times, about where men were and when — the answer would always be *the clocks can't be trusted.*"

"Do you know who slowed it?"

"I know who benefits. In this valley those have been the same people for a hundred years." She watched him absorb that. "But here is what I don't know, clockmaker: why you're asking. You came up that mountain to fix an escapement. Now you stand in my transit room looking like a man who has read something he shouldn't. So either you're Halewick's creature sent to test me, in which case this tea has been wasted on you — or you found something inside that case that frightened you more than the commander does."

Emrys looked at the ledger again. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped as though the walls were listening, and perhaps in this valley they were.

"There are letters inside the weight channel. Written by Colonel Vane."

The silence after that had texture. Isolde heard her own pulse in it, slow and even, the way she'd trained it to be during long nights of observation.

"Vane is dead," she said.

"Vane is alive. And the letters say the founding charter is forged. They say—" His voice caught and steadied. "They say my father helped forge it."

There it was, then. She had wondered, these past two years, whether the son knew what the father had done; she had half hoped he never would, for the sake of whatever peace he'd built himself. Grief reassembling as betrayal — she knew that architecture intimately.

"Say the rest," she said quietly.

"He wants the record honest. He wants the clock repaired so the hours can be trusted again. And he needs proof that will survive him, because—" Emrys stopped.

"Because they're hunting him."

"How did you—"

"Because I've spent two years watching a commander prepare for a dead man's return." She rose and went to the window. Below, the valley lay in evening smoke, the regimental compound a geometry of order, the ford a thin bright line in the river. "Halewick doubled the patrols on the secondary road last month. He's had the archive sealed for 'restoration.' He asked me — politely, twice — whether my instruments could distinguish between two men of similar build at extreme distance. What would you call that, if not preparation?"

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight, and Isolde found herself talking more than she had talked in a year, because Emrys listened the way craftsmen listen — completely, without performing sympathy.

She showed him the ledger. Two years of nightly entries: the star-time, the clock-time, the difference, growing from seconds to minutes as whoever tampered with the mechanism grew bolder. Beside each entry, in her small fierce hand, notes: *patrol returned 22:14 by clock, 22:10 by stars. Dispatch logged at midnight, actually 23:56.* Hours shifted, always slightly, always conveniently.

"It's an alibi machine," Emrys said at last, wonder and disgust in equal measure. "The whole observatory."

"It's worse than that. It's a *witness* that's been bought." She tapped the ledger. "But bought witnesses can be un-bought. Correct the escapement and every entry in this book becomes provable — my star-times against your mechanism's true rate. Two independent records agreeing on when the lies began."

"They'll destroy the ledger."

"They'll try. Which is why there are three copies." She allowed herself the smallest satisfaction. "One in the capital, mailed in installments to a solicitor who believes he's cataloguing astronomical data. One in the lining of my winter coat. And one you may burn, if it comes to that, so they find ash and nothing else."

Emrys stared at her. "You've been building this case alone for two years."

"I've been *surviving* alone for two years. Building is what happened along the way." She took the ledger back, held it against her chest. "Understand me, clockmaker. Tomas is dead either way. Vane may be dying — gaunt men hiding in chapels generally are. I am not doing this for vindication. I'm doing it because a woman who watches the sky for a living cannot spend her whole life pretending she didn't see."

He nodded slowly. "And if I refuse? If I walk down the mountain tomorrow and tell Halewick everything?"

"Then I'll tell him about you first." She said it without heat, almost kindly. "Wary means wary, Emrys Dravenmoor. Trust is earned in this valley, usually posthumously."

To his credit, he laughed — a short, startled sound, as if surprised to find laughter still available to him. "Fair," he said. "Fair enough."

* * *

It was near midnight when the sound came — hooves on the switchback road, more than one horse, moving fast. Isolde was at the window before Emrys finished rising. Lanterns below, swinging through the pines. A patrol, climbing toward the observatory at an hour when no patrol had business climbing.

"That's the third time this week," she murmured. "They're searching somewhere tonight."

"The chapel?" Emrys whispered.

"Or testing whether anything runs downhill to warn us." She blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, counting lanterns — five, then six — while Emrys pressed his ear to the floor as though he could hear through stone whether the letters in the weight channel rustled. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, or the day after, they'll come in daylight with papers instead of lanterns. When they do, you'll be beside that clock keeping them talking, and I'll be at my telescope, and neither of us will look at the other. Can you do that?"

"Yes," he said, and then, quieter: "Isolde. Why help *me*? You don't know me. My name is on the forgery as surely as my father's."

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because saying it aloud settled something in her own chest.

"Because two years ago I decided I'd rather die certain than live comfortable," she said. "And because tonight, for the first time since the letters stopped coming, I'm not the only one in this valley who knows." She held out her hand, rough from cold brass and cold nights. "Allies, then. With our own reasons. Keep yours, and I'll keep mine, and we'll meet in the middle at the truth."

He shook it. His hand was warm; hers had forgotten warmth was possible.

Below them, the lanterns paused at the tree line, hung there a long moment like low stars, then turned back down the mountain.

Isolde watched them go and thought: *soon*. Whatever Halewick was hunting, he was hunting it closer now. And when he ran out of mountain to search, he would come for the clock — and for everyone who had ever touched it.

"We should sleep," she said. "Tomorrow I'll teach you to read my charts properly. If we're going to hang a commander with astronomy, you'd best learn which end of the sky is up."

Emrys smiled in the dark. But at the door he paused, and said, softly, as if the words cost him:

"There's something else. Pell — the man who brought the letters — took copies toward the capital yesterday. By the ford road."

Isolde felt the cold come up through the floor stones. "*Yesterday?*"

"This morning, early."

She was already reaching for her coat. "Then God help him," she said, "because Halewick's patrols were posted at the ford before dawn. I saw them myself."
