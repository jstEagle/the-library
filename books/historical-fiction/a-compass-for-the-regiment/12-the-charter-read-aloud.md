## 12. The Charter Read Aloud

Commander Halewick had read the charter aloud eleven times, and he had never once grown tired of it.

There was a trick to it, of course. There was a trick to everything. You did not simply stand at the head table and recite; you *performed* the founding, you gave the valley its origin story in your own voice, and by the final clause every man and woman in that hall understood — without being told, without anything so crude as a threat — who the charter's living instrument was.

He checked himself in the long mirror an hour before the feast. Grey coat, black facings, the silver chain of office lying flat against his chest. Fifty-one years old and still straight through the shoulders. His father had worn this same chain at fifty-one, and his grandfather before him, and somewhere behind them all the first Commander Halewick had taken the charter from the hands of the founders themselves. Inheritance. That was the word the valley had forgotten how to say with pride. He intended to teach them again tonight.

"Commander." Lieutenant Sorrel at the door, immaculate, faintly nervous. "The hall is full. The clockmaker has arrived."

"Good," Halewick said, and meant it.

That was the thing his officers never quite understood about him: he liked people. Genuinely. He liked their faces when they came into his hall and found warmth instead of iron. Fear was efficient but crude, like a hammer used on every nail; hospitality got a door opened for you, and once a man had eaten at your table he would hesitate a long while before calling you a tyrant. The clockmaker — young Dravenmoor, the son of old Dravenmoor — was exactly the sort of man hospitality was made for. Talented, obscure, hungry for recognition without knowing he was hungry. A certificate, a few forgiven debts, a place at the high table among his betters. Men had surrendered far more than letters for far less than that.

And if the boy proved stubborn — well. The charter gave him means for that too.

* * *

The hall had been dressed since noon. Banners of the regiment along both walls, the old colours faded to the colour of weak tea, and above the head table the framed charter itself, glass-fronted, lit by four candles arranged so no shadow fell across the script. Halewick had supervised the candle placement personally years ago. Documents were like faces; they needed light to be believed.

He worked the room before the meal the way he worked everything — thoroughly and smiling. He touched the shoulder of the miller's second son, promoted last month. He complimented Mrs. Calloway on her plum preserves and accepted a jar with the gravity of a man accepting tribute. He paused at the table where the observatory party sat — Astronomer Marchmont, upright and cool as ever, her glass of wine untouched; and beside her, the clockmaker.

Emrys Dravenmoor looked, Halewick thought, like his father around the eyes, and nothing like him anywhere else. Old Dravenmoor had been a stooped, apologetic man who kept his gaze on the floor as though apology were owed to it. The son sat straight and watched the hall with an expression Halewick recognized because he wore a version of it himself: the look of a competent person assessing a room full of incompetent ones.

"Master Dravenmoor." He put out his hand and let the boy take it. "The clock is the best it has run in twenty years. I am told four minutes lost each day, and now — nothing. You have my thanks."

"You have my work, Commander," the boy said. "It's what it's for."

Halewick laughed, warm and easy, and let his thumb press once, briefly, over the back of the young man's knuckles. "Your father would be proud. Sit. Eat. Tonight you will hear the charter read properly — most people only ever hear fragments."

"I've never heard it at all," Dravenmoor said.

"Then you are in for an education." Halewick smiled and moved on, and behind him he felt the boy watching him, which was fine. Let him watch. Watching cost nothing.

* * *

The feast was good and the wine was better, and by the time the plates were cleared the hall had reached the low, contented hum that was the true foundation of his authority — not the garrison, not the courts the charter let him convene, but two hundred bellies full of roast mutton who wanted nothing tonight except to feel that the world was ordered and old.

Sorrel brought the reading stand. The candles were trimmed. The hall quieted by degrees, the way water stills.

Halewick rose, unhooked the frame's clasp, and lifted the charter out into candlelight.

He knew every word of it. He had known them since he was nine years old, kneeling on a chair in his father's study while his father traced the lines with a stick and said, *this clause is why we eat, boy; this one is why they obey*. But he had learned long ago never to rush the opening. He held the vellum up so the whole hall could see the age of it, the foxed edges, the great seal hanging fat and brown at the foot.

"The Charter of Founding," he announced, "granted to the Regiment of the Vale by those who raised it from nothing, and confirmed by the Crown entire. Read aloud each year upon this day, as it has been read since the first founding-feast, so that none may claim ignorance of the law under which they live."

He began to read.

He was a good reader. That was not vanity; it was craft. He gave each clause its weight, pausing where pause flattered the sense, letting the archaic words roll. *"Let there be constituted within the Vale of Ardenne a standing regiment, whose commanders shall hold, exercise, and administer all powers civil and martial therein..."* Heads nodded along the tables. This was the air they breathed. *"…and let the commander's word stand in place of writ and warrant throughout the bounds of the Vale…"*

Halfway down the second column, something happened that had never happened in eleven readings.

Someone laughed.

Not loudly. Not even a full laugh — a breath of it, quickly strangled, the kind of sound a man makes when his own body betrays him. Halewick did not stop reading. He had commanded too long to stop reading. But his eyes lifted over the top of the vellum and swept the hall with unhurried precision, the way a man checks a fence line, and they found the source without difficulty.

Emrys Dravenmoor sat at the third table with his hand pressed flat against his mouth.

It was not mockery. Halewick knew mockery; he collected it. This was worse. The boy was staring at the charter in Halewick's hands as though he were watching a man walk confidently into deep water — that stricken, suspended attention, the hand over the mouth, the slight forward lean of someone who wants to shout a warning and cannot think how to begin. And beneath the horror, absurdly, something almost like recognition. As if the words on the page were familiar to him in some way they could not possibly be.

Halewick returned his gaze to the text and continued, voice level, resonant, filling the hall. *"…and let the rolls of levy, tithe, and muster be kept to the commander's keeping alone, none other to inspect or audit the same…"*

But now a small cold engine had started up inside him, running underneath the performance. He catalogued facts as he read, the way he always did, the reading becoming almost secondary — a cover for thought.

Fact: the clockmaker had repaired the master clock, which had run slow for years, and no one had ordered it slowed through him. Fact: Pell, that grey little ferret, had been seen twice on the valley road, and once asking after the observatory. Fact: old Dravenmoor had died owing the regiment money, modest debts, easily forgiven or easily called in. Fact: his son had spent three weeks alone in the observatory with a mechanism no soldier would open, because soldiers feared mechanisms the way farmers feared weather.

Fact: the boy had just heard clauses he should have been hearing for the first time, and had reacted like a man hearing his own name read from an indictment.

*"…and these powers shall descend undivided to the commander's heir lawfully named, from generation unto generation, none to revoke, amend, or appeal the same, save by consent of the commander holding office."*

There. The keystone clause. The one that made the charter less a document than a dynasty. Halewick delivered it slowly, savoring it, and let his gaze rest — openly now, warmly, the way a host rests his gaze on a guest he intends to remember — on Emrys Dravenmoor.

The boy met his eyes.

For one moment across the candlelit hall, with the charter's seal swinging gently at the edge of Halewick's vision and two hundred people sitting in respectful silence, the clockmaker and the commander looked at each other, and Halewick saw the boy understand that he had been seen. Colour climbed his throat. He dropped his gaze to his plate and pressed his hand harder against his mouth.

Halewick finished the reading beautifully. *"Given under hand and seal at the Vale of Ardenne, in the year of the founding."* He set the vellum back into its frame, closed the glass, and led the toast — *to the Founders, to the Regiment, to the Vale* — and the hall roared its answers, and the musicians struck up, and everything proceeded exactly as it had proceeded for six generations.

Except that nothing proceeded at all.

* * *

He found the boy afterward, naturally. He made a point of finding him, drawing him aside near the banner-hung wall with two cups of the good brandy, pressing one into his hand.

"You laughed," Halewick said pleasantly.

Dravenmoor went white. "Commander, I— it wasn't—"

"At the keystone clause. The descent clause." Halewick watched his face with genuine interest, the interest of a craftsman examining unusual grain in ordinary timber. "Most people find that clause dull. It's the longest one. You found it — surprising?"

"I didn't know what it said," the boy managed. "I've never heard it read. I told you."

"You did tell me." Halewick smiled and drank, and let the silence do a little work. "Your father heard it read many times, you know. He sat where you're sitting, some twenty-odd feasts running. A quiet man. Very careful with his tools." He tilted his head. "You have his eyes. And apparently something of his ear."

"My father was a clockmaker."

"Yes," Halewick agreed warmly. "A very fine one. The regiment valued him highly." He let the past tense sit there between them, doing its quiet work, then patted the boy's arm. "Drink your brandy. It's a founding-day tradition, and I'd hate for you to break tradition on your first feast. You'll find, Master Dravenmoor, that traditions here are very well protected."

He left him then, because leaving them alone with the fear was worth ten interrogations, and rejoined his officers at the head table, radiant, gracious, dispensing toasts.

But the cold engine did not shut off. It ran on through the music and the dancing, turning over its facts, and near midnight it arrived at a conclusion that sent him to the window with a cup of wine he no longer tasted.

Below, past the courtyard wall, the mountain rose black against the stars, and near its summit one light burned — the observatory, steady and small, like an eye that had recently remembered how to see.

Old Dravenmoor had been careful with his tools. Careful men kept copies. Careful men kept *proofs* — proofs of payment, proofs of practice, proofs of the drafts that preceded any finished script. Halewick had spent thirty years assuming the old man's silence was purchased and permanent. But silence purchased can be inherited unpaid, and sons are not fathers, and somewhere in a workshop or an attic or — his jaw tightened — inside a clock case that no soldier of his would willingly open, there might lie paper with his family's whole inheritance written on it as forgery.

"Sorrel."

His lieutenant appeared instantly. "Commander."

"How many men can you spare at dawn?"

"For what, sir?"

Halewick turned from the window. Down the hall the music played on, and at the third table Emrys Dravenmoor was rising to leave early, pale, apologizing to his neighbours, gathering his coat — and the astronomer Marchmont, Halewick noted, rising to leave at precisely the same moment, though her quarters were in the opposite direction from the town road.

"Dawn," Halewick said, "we ride to the observatory. All of us. With the charter's authority." He smiled, because Sorrel expected it. "Consider it an inspection. The clock has been running too well lately. I want to know why."
