## 13. A Meeting Above the Snowline

Pell came for me an hour before dawn, and he did not knock. He simply stood in the doorway of my room above the workshop until I woke, a shape darker than the dark, with a knapsack already packed in his hands.

"Today," he said.

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You climbed into your boots before you finished the sentence." He was right. I had been dressing in my head for three days, since Isolde put her star-charts on the table and said, in her flat astronomer's voice, that a man who would forge hours would forge anything else he liked. "Bring nothing that marks you as a clockmaker. No tools. Your hands will do."

"Where are we going?"

"Up," Pell said. "Above the snowline. Where dead men keep warm as best they can."

* * *

We left by the drovers' path, which is not on any regimental map because the drovers paid good money over two generations to keep it that way. The valley lay below us in blue shadow, the smoke of Halewick's barracks rising perfectly straight in the windless cold, and I thought: all of it — the fields, the tithes, the marriages made and unmade at the commander's pleasure — all of it rests on paper my father ruled and inked with a forger's patience. Every chimney smoking under a lie.

Pell walked like a man reading a page only he could see. He did not talk. Twice we stopped while he listened to something I couldn't hear, and once we flattened ourselves behind a drystone wall for ten minutes while a patrol's lanterns swung along the valley road far beneath us, patient as ferry lights.

"Why you?" I asked when we moved again. "Why does a living legend send a stranger with cold hands instead of a soldier?"

"Because soldiers are what they look for." Pell shifted his knapsack. "I was his batman. Twenty years. I carried his kit, his letters, and for six years now his guilt, which weighs more than both." He glanced at me sideways. "He asked for you by trade, you know. Not by name. *Send me the clockmaker's son.* He said you'd come."

"He never met me."

"Didn't he?"

I had no answer to that. The memory from childhood — the kind officer, the gear-cutting lesson, *promise me you'll tell the time truly* — has its own weather, and it closed over me then like cloud over the peak.

* * *

The chapel found us before we found it. One moment there was only scree and the white line where snow persists through summer; the next, a gable stood against the sky, roofless, its stones furred with lichen the color of old bronze. Below it, half-sheltered by a fallen wall, a lean-to of canvas and slate. A thread of woodsmoke.

"He knows we're coming," Pell said. "He always knows. He counts our pace from the sound of boots on scree. Old habit — he could tell a regiment by its rhythm once."

My heart was going hard enough that I suspected he could tell mine from the valley floor.

The man who rose from the lean-to was not the man from the engraving above the barrack gates. The engraving shows Colonel Aldous Vane at forty: broad, gold-braided, jaw like a verdict. The man in front of me was sixty-some and thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a shepherd's coat over a soldier's posture that illness had bent but not broken. His face carried the ruin of what the portrait kept — same grey eyes, but one of them clouded. A cough lived in him; I could hear it waiting.

"Well," he said, and his voice was quieter than legends, rougher. "The clockmaker's son."

"Emrys Dravenmoor."

"I know." He studied me the way I study a movement — looking for what drives it. "You have his hands. Thomas's. I cut a gear wheel with those hands once, when you were small enough to believe officers were a species of weather."

"You did," I said. My voice came out strange. "I've remembered it wrong my whole life. I thought I dreamed him."

"No one dreams their father accurately." Vane gestured to a stool by the fire. "Sit. You've walked six hours and your opinion of me is climbing with every step — awe curdles fast above the snowline. Let's get it over with while it's still warm."

* * *

I will set down what happened at that fire plainly, because everything afterward depended on it.

I expected a plea. I had rehearsed refusals the whole climb — I would tell him the risk was too great, that Halewick's men were searching houses, that I was one man with a sick mother's debts and no taste for martyrdom. All of it true, all of it ready.

Vane asked for none of it.

He poured me tea that tasted of pine needles and asked first about the clock. Not whether I'd hidden the letters — he assumed that, having ordered it. He asked about the escapement, the pendulum's period, whether the losing of four minutes daily was drift or design. When I told him what I'd found in the weight channel — the file-marks, years of them, someone slowing the great clock deliberately, hour by falsified hour — he closed his eyes as if the news confirmed a wound he'd been keeping dressed.

"So it's been done to the mechanism itself. Not just the record books." He opened his eyes. "Then the books can be mended, Emrys. Books burn. But a machine that lies — that takes longer to forgive."

"What do you want from me?" I asked. "Rescue? I don't have the means. Testimony carried to the capital? Pell is doing that."

"No." He leaned forward, and the cough stirred in him and he mastered it, the way you master a spring under tension. "I am dying. We needn't pretend otherwise; I've had two winters more than the doctors gave me and this one will be my last regardless of how the inquiry falls. I don't ask to be rescued. I ask for something smaller and harder." He held my gaze. "Put the observatory clock right. True time, kept truly, visible to every soul in the valley. Then whatever happens after — the inquiry, the charter, my name or Halewick's version of it — there will exist one public thing that cannot be lied to. Hours are the last honest currency, and he has debased them. Give the valley back its hours, and men will start asking what else has been clipped."

"That's all?"

"That is everything." He smiled, and for a moment the engraving looked out of his ruined face. "Empires have fallen for less than a corrected clock."

I heard myself say: "My father forged the charter."

"Yes."

"And you still keep a promise to him."

Vane was quiet a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, snow let go of a stone and hissed down the slope.

"Your father was the finest hand I ever saw," he said finally, "and he knew it, and it ruined him by inches. He forged the charter for money when he was young and frightened, and spent thirty years paying for it in a currency nobody watched — teaching an apprentice honestly, loving his family badly, correcting other men's errors with a precision he denied his own. I found out what he'd done because I found the draft in the regimental archive with corrections in his margin — you know this. What you don't know is that I went to him before I went to anyone else. I expected to denounce him. Instead we sat up one whole night in your father's workshop, and he wept, and showed me how the forgery worked, stroke by stroke — because a confession should be exact, he said, or it's just noise. And at dawn he asked me to do what he couldn't: make it right after he was gone." Vane's clouded eye caught the firelight. "So you and I, Emrys, are executing the same testament. His. Yours is just written in a different hand — though I'm told it resembles his."

I looked at my hands. They looked like hands.

"The letters in the clock case," I said. "There's one addressed to me."

"There is. You'll read it when you're ready, not before. A dying man doesn't get to schedule another man's grief." He settled back, and the effort of the long speech took its toll visibly, like a spring running down. "Now. Tell me about the four minutes. Not the mechanism — the meaning of it. Who slowed it, and when, if you can tell."

And so, above the snowline, in a ruined chapel, I sat with a dead man and talked shop for an hour: false minutes, dark windows, alibis built out of missing time — and it was the most honest conversation of my life, conducted entirely in the grammar of gears.

* * *

He walked me as far as the broken door when I left, moving slowly, one hand on the wall. Pell had gone ahead to scout the descent. The light was failing gold and violet, and the valley below had begun to prickle with lamplight — Halewick's valley, lit by Halewick's permission.

"Emrys." Vane stopped me at the threshold. Up close, the illness in him smelled like cold ash. "One more thing, and then you go and don't look back at the chapel, whatever you hear afterward. That's an order. I still outrank everyone in that valley, even unlisted."

"I understand."

"Do you?" He gripped my wrist — a soldier's grip still, startling in its strength. "When they come for you, and they will, and they offer you your father's debts and a certificate and call it mercy — remember that mercy bought with a lie is just wages. Halewick pays them. He thinks he owns what he pays for. You don't owe him a thing he can purchase." His grip tightened. "Tell the time truly. That's the whole of it. Everything else is commentary."

"Yes, sir," I said — and realized I had never called any living man that before.

He let go. He stood in the ruined doorway with the last light on him, straight-backed inside the ruin of himself, and I went down the scree without looking back, as ordered, though my neck ached with not doing it.

Behind me, faintly, I heard him begin to cough, and then stop himself, and then begin again.

* * *

Pell caught up with me at the treeline. We descended most of the way in the dark, by his counting of paces rather than any path I could see, and we did not speak until the valley floor was under our boots and the observatory's silhouette stood against the stars on its shoulder of mountain — my clock inside it, my letters in its weight channel, my promise in it now like a mainspring.

"He asked you for the clock," Pell said. It wasn't a question.

"He asked me to make it true."

"Hm." Pell walked a few steps. "He asked me for something different, six years ago. He asked me to carry word to your father that he lived. Your father died three days before the letter arrived." He stopped and faced me. "So when the colonel says a thing must be done before winter, boy, you believe him about the winter."

"I believe him."

"Good." We reached the fork where the drovers' path split — one branch toward my master's workshop, one toward the observatory road. Pell pointed at neither. "Because here's the thing you need to know before you sleep tonight. While we were on the mountain — I checked at the ford on the way up. There were soldiers at the observatory gate this evening. Asking questions. Showing a sketch."

"A sketch of what?"

"A sketch," Pell said quietly, "of you."

Below us, high on its shoulder, the observatory's lamp burned steady — the lamp that was only ever lit when the clock needed attention, the signal Isolde and I had agreed on for danger.

It was burning now.
