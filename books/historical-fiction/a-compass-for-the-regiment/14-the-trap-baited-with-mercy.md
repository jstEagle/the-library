## 14. The Trap Baited with Mercy

Commander Halewick believed in hospitality the way other men believed in scripture: absolutely, and as a weapon.

He had the table set in the small dining room of the garrison house rather than the great hall — a private kindness, he wanted it to seem. White cloth. Two candles. The good silver that had belonged to three commanders before him and would belong to three after, because nothing in this valley truly changed hands; it merely passed through them. He had the cook prepare the trout from the high streams and open the bottle of red from the case he kept for occasions that required warmth he did not feel.

The boy — and Halewick thought of him as a boy, though he was what, twenty-two now, twenty-three? — was due at seven. Emrys Dravenmoor, son of Josiah Dravenmoor, journeyman clockmaker, debtor, and dead man's tool.

Halewick poured himself a measure of the wine and did not drink it. He stood at the window instead and watched the light fail on the valley. From up here the regiment's lands looked orderly almost to the point of beauty: the fields in their long stripes, the roads running where roads had been told to run, the river bending obediently between its banks. Sixty years of charter had done that. Sixty years of a single document, signed by founders whose names were carved into the chapel wall, granting the commander of the regiment authority over land, labor, tolls, and law from the snowline down to the ford.

He had read the charter more times than any living man. He had read it aloud every founding day for eleven years, and each reading had settled into him like sediment, until he could not distinguish between his belief in the words and the words themselves. That was what inheritance was, Halewick had long ago decided. Not property. Certainty.

And certainty was under threat.

Pell had been seen on the valley road twice in a fortnight. The satchel they'd taken at the ford had been a disappointment — copied pages, plausible ones, but copies, and no originals, and Pell himself gone quiet in his cell in a way that suggested a man who had given up exactly as much as he intended to. Halewick was not a fool. He had been fooled before, and once had been enough to teach him that a clever enemy leaves you holding something and calls it victory.

So the question was no longer Pell. The question was the clockmaker's son, who had been up at the observatory for six weeks tinkering with a clock nobody needed fixed that badly, who had dined twice with the astronomer Marchmont — a woman Halewatch watched the way one watches a door left ajar — and who had, ten days ago, climbed above the snowline with a guide who did not file his name at the garrison gate.

Above the snowline there was nothing. That was the official position. Above the snowline there were ruins, scree, and weather, and no reason for an apprentice clockmaker to go there unless someone had told him otherwise.

Halewick turned from the window when the knock came. He arranged his face into its most generous setting — the one that had made farmers weep with relief at rent hearings, the one that had made young officers loyal past the point of sense — and called, warmly, "Come in, come in."

* * *

Emrys Dravenmoor looked worse than the last time Halewick had received him. Thinner. There was ink or oil worked deep into the skin around his nails, and a rawness about his eyes that Halewick recognized with professional interest: the look of a man who has learned something in the last month and has not yet decided what it will cost him.

"You honor me, Commander," the boy said, and hesitated at the threshold the way people always did in this room. The room did that. It was furnished to make guests feel they had wandered somewhere slightly too fine for them.

"Nonsense." Halewick took his hand — cold fingers, cold even at the palm — and drew him to the table. "Sit. You've been eating observatory food, I can see it. Dried meat and pride. Sit, sit."

They sat. Halewick served him the trout himself, which surprised the boy visibly, and poured the wine, and talked — easily, pleasantly, for a quarter of an hour — about nothing. The weather. The price of wool. A story about his own apprenticeship in the cavalry, forty years gone, a horse that hated him and a sergeant who taught him patience. He watched Dravenmoor relax by degrees, the way metal relaxes under heat, and he felt the old satisfaction, the deep professional pleasure, of handling something delicate correctly.

Then, only when the plates were cleared and the second glasses poured, he said:

"Your father owed me four hundred and twelve marks."

The boy went still. Not dramatically. Just the small stillness of a man recalculating everything in the room.

"Josiah was the finest craftsman this valley ever produced," Halewick went on, turning his glass by its stem. "I say that without flattery, and you know I have no reason to flatter you. But craft is not arithmetic, and your father could not make sums behave any better than he could make time behave. Four hundred marks, borrowed against the workshop, against the tools. When he died, the debt became yours. That is how things work here. Debts, like names, pass down." He let that sit for a moment. "It is also, I'm aware, a burden on a young man trying to earn his certificate."

"Is that why I've been summoned, Commander?" Dravenmoor asked quietly. "To discuss my father's accounts?"

"To discuss your future." Halewick leaned back. He let the candlelight do its work — soften him, gild him. "I'm going to offer you something, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide anything. First: the debt is forgiven. Tonight. I'll sign the paper before you leave if you like, or tomorrow morning in front of witnesses, whichever makes you sleep better. Second: I'll sponsor your master's examination. Full fees, my name at the bottom of the petition. You'd sit it in the spring. By summer you'd be Master Dravenmoor, holder of the workshop free and clear, with a reputation your father would be proud of."

The boy's face did something complicated. Want, first — naked, unguarded want, and Halewick noted it and filed it. Then suspicion arriving late, like a guest who had missed the address.

"In exchange for what?"

"There it is." Halewick smiled, and made the smile fond. "You're your father's son. Straight to the mechanism. Very well." He set down his glass. "There is a man named Pell. You know him. He has been bringing you letters."

"Commander—"

"Don't." The word was gentle. That was the trick of it; he made the interruption itself a kind of mercy. "Don't lie to me at my own table, boy. It insults us both, and I would prefer tonight to be honest, because honesty is what I'm offering you. Letters in a hand you recognize. Letters that make claims. I know they exist. I don't know what they say, precisely — but I can imagine, and my imagination has been keeping me up at night."

Dravenmoor's hands were flat on the table now, as if steadying it. "If you know the letters exist," he said carefully, "then why not simply take them? Search the observatory. You have the right — the charter grants—"

"The charter grants me the right to preserve order," Halewick said, "and I will use it if I must. But hear me, Dravenmoor, because this is the part I need you to understand." He paused, and chose his next words the way he had once chosen ground to fight on. "What you are carrying is not evidence. Whatever Pell has told you, whatever the letters claim — it is treason. A dead man's handwriting, forged or stolen, deployed against the lawful authority of the regiment. Do you understand what happens to a man found holding such a thing? Not to a soldier. Not to a gentleman. To an apprentice clockmaker with debts and no name worth protecting?"

The candle flames leaned in the draft. Neither man moved.

"I am not threatening you," Halewick said softly. "I am protecting you. That is what this dinner is. Because there are men in my garrison — good men, frightened men — who already know about the letters, who are asking me why the clockmaker's son walks free, and I cannot hold them back forever with promises of process. If those papers surface — in an inquiry, in a court, anywhere — everyone who touched them burns. Pell will burn. Marchmont, if she's touched them, will burn. And you, Emrys, will burn first and longest, and your father's name will burn with you, and there will be nothing left of the Dravenmoors but a ledger entry and a debt I'll have to forgive anyway."

He let the silence work. Then he reached into his coat — slowly, showing his hand empty of anything but paper — and laid a single folded sheet beside the boy's plate.

"My terms. Written plainly. Debt forgiven. Certificate sponsored. And this line here — read it — *the Commander undertakes that no proceeding shall be brought against the said Emrys Dravenmoor in respect of any matter arising.* Immunity, boy. In writing. Signed and sealed. No one else in this valley could give you that. No one else would."

Dravenmoor picked up the paper. His eyes moved across it twice. When he spoke, his voice was steady, and Halewick gave him credit for it. "And the letters?"

"Surrendered to me. Unread by anyone further. They'll go into the regimental archive, sealed, and be forgotten — I give you my word they will harm no one, because they will reach no one." Which was true, Halewick reflected, in the way that a thing burned reaches no one. "The past stays buried. Your future stands up and walks out of this room. That is the whole bargain."

"And Colonel Vane?" the boy said.

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Halewick did not move. He had learned, in forty years of command, that the first three seconds after a blow are the ones that decide everything. He drank his wine. He set the glass down without a sound.

"There is no Colonel Vane," he said. "There is a grave in the regimental cemetery with his name on it, and there is a grief this valley has carried for six years, and there are criminals who forge a hero's handwriting for their own purposes. If you mean to ask me whether Aldous Vane lives — then you have already answered questions I hadn't asked you yet, and I am sorry for you, boy, truly sorry, because that answer just doubled the price of everything."

He rose then, unhurried, and crossed to the sideboard, and came back with a second document — blank except for the heading, the seal already pressed into the wax, waiting only for terms and signature.

"But I will not double it," he said. "Because I remember Josiah's face when he brought me the clock he made for my study, the year you were born. Because your father repaired my mother's watch the week before she died and refused payment, and I have owed that kindness for thirty years. So here is what I'll do. One night. Take the paper home. Sleep on it if you can. Come to me at noon tomorrow — here, or send word, either serves. If you bring me the letters, we sign, and by autumn you are a master craftsman with a clean name and a whole life ahead of him. If you don't—" He stopped. Sighed. Let his shoulders come down from their command posture, deliberately, so the boy could see the weight on them. "If you don't, then tomorrow I do what I must to protect this regiment, and I will not be able to protect you from it. And I will spend the rest of my life regretting that this table couldn't hold you."

He saw the boy's throat move. Saw the flicker of the eyes toward the window, toward the dark valley below where the observatory light burned faint and high on its mountain like a lamp held by a drowning man.

"One night," Halewick repeated gently. "That is mercy, Emrys. Real mercy, the expensive kind. Don't mistake it for weakness, and don't mistake it for a trap."

He escorted him to the door himself. Shook his cold hand. Watched him walk down the gravel path toward the gate, shoulders hunched against nothing visible, and stood in the doorway until the dark took him.

* * *

Back inside, Halewick dismissed the servants, collected the untouched dessert course with his own hands and set it aside — waste offended him — and sat alone at the white-clothed table with both documents in front of him.

He was not troubled by what he had done. He wished the boy no harm; that was the honest truth of it, and he clung to it. Everything he had said was true, or true enough: the debt, the certificate, the immunity. If Emrys Dravenmoor surrendered the papers tomorrow, he would leave this house a free man and richer in prospect than his father had ever been. Halewick had ruined men before and slept well after, but he had never enjoyed it, and he counted that as character.

No. What troubled him was the arithmetic.

A boy does not climb above the snowline for letters he hasn't read. A boy does not ask, at a commander's own table, *And Colonel Vane?* unless someone has put flesh on the legend for him — shown him the man, or near enough. Which meant the ruin at the high pasture was occupied. Which meant the corpse walked. Which meant that for six years, while Halewick read the charter aloud every founding day in a voice that never shook, a man who knew what was written on the back of it had been alive within twenty miles, patient as frost, working.

And that meant the letters might not be forgeries at all.

Halewick poured the last of the wine and did not drink it, and found his hand, for the first time in eleven years of command, was not entirely steady. He thought of the charter in its cedar case beneath the hall — his charter, his certainty, sixty years of it — and of what he had never quite dared to examine too closely about the ink, the signatures, the convenient clauses.

*One night,* he had promised the boy.

He rang the bell. Sergeant Bray appeared, cap in hand, rain-freckled and reliable.

"Double the watch on the observatory road," Halewick said. "Quietly. And send two men you trust to the high pasture trail — not tonight, first light. If anyone comes down off that mountain before noon, I want to know before they reach the ford."

"And if the clockmaker comes to you at noon, sir, without the letters?"

Halewick looked at the blank document with its waiting seal, and then out the window, where far above the darkened valley a single light still burned at the top of the world.

"Then God help him," he said, "because by nightfall I'll have sent men up after him — and Bray? Send word to the cell. Our talkative friend. Move him somewhere I can visit."

Bray hesitated. "Visit him for what, Commander?"

Halewick smiled the smile that had made farmers weep with relief, and this time there was nothing underneath it at all.

"For a conversation about clocks," he said. "I find myself wanting to learn how they're stopped."
