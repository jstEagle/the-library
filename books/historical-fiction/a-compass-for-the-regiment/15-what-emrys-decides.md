## 15. What Emrys Decides

The observatory at midnight was a different country from the observatory at noon.

By day it belonged to Isolde and her instruments, to the scrubbed geometry of her charts, to the light that came down through the dome slit in a single honest blade. By night it belonged to the clock. Emrys sat on his stool before the great case with a single lamp turned low, and the machine breathed around him — the slow drop of the weights, the tick laid over tick like masonry, the faint metallic sweetness of oil warming in the escapement. He had been alone with mechanisms his whole life. He had never before felt that one was alone with *him*.

Halewick's offer sat in his breast pocket. He had not written it down; he didn't need to. *Your father's debts forgiven. A master's certificate under my seal. The letters, Dravenmoor, and no one need ever speak of treason.* Delivered that afternoon in the commander's own study, over sherry, with snow coming down past the windows like a curtain drawn for privacy.

A night to decide. Halewick had granted it the way a man grants a beggar permission to keep breathing until morning.

Emrys opened the clock case.

* * *

The letters were where he had hidden them, folded into the weight channel behind the lead, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as seeds. He did not take them out. He only looked at them, at the corner of Pell's careful wrapping showing in the lamplight, and let himself — for the last time, he promised himself, for this one hour only — consider the other road.

It was not a coward's road. That was the thing he kept having to admit. He turned it over in his mind the way he'd turn a broken wheel, looking for the flaw, and the flaw would not show itself. If he gave Halewick the letters, the charter stood, the valley stood, the regiment stood. His mother's grave stayed tended. The workshop's debts — his father's debts, four years of them, itemized in a ledger he knew by heart — dissolved like frost off glass. He would be certified within the month. Master Emrys Dravenmoor, by appointment of Commander Halewick, keeper of the mountain clock. A small life, made comfortable. Made *safe*.

And Vane would die in his ruined chapel, unmourned twice over. And the record of hours — every hour Isolde had charted against the falsified clock, every hour some farmer had been fined or flogged or dispossessed because the regiment's time was law and its law ran four minutes slow when it suited its masters — all of it would stay buried inside a machine everyone was too frightened to open.

His father had built that fear into the clock's reputation, maybe. Or fed it. Emrys still didn't know how much of the forgery his father had done with his hands and how much with his silence, and he suspected now that he never would know, that the ledger of a dead man's motives balances nothing and pays no one. But he knew what he had found in those effects. The practice strokes. The payment marks. The drafting tools worn to his father's grip.

And he knew what else he had found, folded small among them, in an officer's hand he had not recognized until this week: *When you can cut a gear that keeps true time, you will have learned honesty. The rest is arithmetic.* Signed, absurdly, warmly, *A.V.*

He had thought, as a boy, that the colonel was joking. He had thought, until three days ago, that the colonel was dead. Both mistakes had been comfortable ones.

Emrys took out his tools.

* * *

He worked without deciding to work. That was how he understood, afterward, that the decision had already been made — days ago, maybe years ago, maybe at nine years old in a sunlit workshop with a kind officer's hand over his small ones on the gear-cutting frame. The hands knew. They had simply been waiting for the rest of him.

The false slowness lived in the pendulum assembly. He had found it hiding the letters, that first terrible evening: a secondary regulating nut, machined beautifully, installed invisibly, capable of shifting the pendulum's effective length by a hair — enough to bleed four minutes a day, enough to do it so gradually that only someone comparing the clock against the stars would ever catch it. Someone had spent money on this. Someone had commissioned it from a craftsman good enough to make corruption elegant.

Emrys had checked the maker's mark weeks ago and then wished he hadn't. His father's punch. The same little anvil-and-star stamped into every wheel Emrys had ever cleaned.

He unscrewed the regulating nut and held it in his palm. It weighed almost nothing. Four minutes a day. Sixteen hundred days and more since the colonel's staged death — he had done the arithmetic against Isolde's charts, unable to stop himself — better than four full days of stolen hours, distributed across the valley like a tax no one had voted. Farmers measured their tithes by this clock. Soldiers measured their patrols by it. The gallows at the ford had been dropped by it, once, and Emrys had read the account in the regimental gazette: *the execution was conducted precisely per regulation.* Precisely, by a clock built to lie.

He set the nut on the workbench, next to the lamp, where it caught the light and threw back a small dull gleam, and he felt nothing cinematic at all. No thunder of resolve. Just the ordinary cold of the observatory, and the tick going on, and the understanding settling into him the way sediment settles — slowly, and then completely.

His father had made this. His name was half his father's name. Every certificate he would ever hang in a shop window would carry the same lineage as the punch-mark on this obscene little nut.

*Let it,* he thought. *Names aren't shields. They're debts.*

He replaced the standard nut from his own kit — the spare he'd carried up the mountain in case the original failed, back when he still believed failures were innocent — and seated it, and adjusted, and listened. The beat changed under his ear, so slightly that anyone but a clockmaker would have missed it. Anyone but a clockmaker, or an astronomer who had spent two years charting the difference.

He corrected the rate against the regulator watch, checked it, checked it again, closed the case, and stood there with his thumbs hooked in his apron strings like any tradesman finishing any job.

The clock lost nothing. For the first time in six years, the master clock of the valley told the truth, and told it second by second, and only three people in the world knew what had changed.

One of them was his enemy.

* * *

Pell was waiting in the instrument tunnel below the dome, exactly where Isolde said he would be, sitting on a crate with his coat collar up and his long cold hands wrapped around a candle stub. He rose when Emrys came down the ladder, and read him in one look, the way he seemed to read everything — quickly, hungrily, as if accuracy were a habit left over from a profession he wouldn't name.

"Well?" Pell said.

"It's done. The clock is true."

Pell let out a breath that was almost a laugh, almost a prayer, and sat back down on the crate as if his knees had filed notice. "Six years," he said. "Do you know what I would have given, six years ago, to hear someone say that sentence? I'd have given them the whole satchel." His mouth twisted. "Either satchel."

"He'll know by morning," Emrys said. "Halewick. He compares the town bells against the clock — he must, if he's been using the slowness. When the bells agree again, he'll know someone opened the case."

"Then we've bought the hours between midnight and dawn, and no more."

"No more." Emrys crouched so they were level, man to man, conspirator to apprentice, though neither word fit either of them anymore. "I need you to take a message to the chapel. Tonight, before first light. Can your legs still do it?"

"My legs are the only part of me Halewick hasn't priced yet." Pell was already pulling his boots on properly, lacing them with the speed of a man who had run from soldiers before and expected to again. "What's the message?"

Emrys had been composing it all evening, without admitting it, in the back of his skull where the best sentences assemble themselves while the front of the skull is busy being afraid. Now he said it aloud and found it had shrunk itself down to almost nothing, as true things do:

"Tell him the record will be honest. Tell him—" and here his voice did something he hadn't authorized, catching on a burr of feeling he'd have to examine later, "—tell him I'm keeping the promise. Tell him his friend's son cuts true gears."

Pell stood very still. Candlelight moved in his eyes.

"He asked for a true clock," Emrys said. "That's all he asked me for, up there in that ruin. Not rescue. Not revenge. A clock that doesn't lie. I thought it was a dying man's sentiment when he said it." He looked back up the ladder, toward the case above, toward the tick rising steady as a heartbeat through the floorboards. "It's the only request anyone has ever made of me that I fully understand."

"Message received," Pell said quietly. "Message will be delivered." He pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, and the tunnel went black except for the gray seep of snowlight from the vent slits. "Dravenmoor. One more thing."

"Yes?"

"The nut. The one you took out. Burn it, bury it, drop it down the well — I don't care. But whatever you do, don't keep it." A pause, and in the pause something old and tired moved in Pell's voice. "Men who win keep trophies. Men who survive keep witnesses, and there's a difference, and it's measured in whether you're alive to testify."

Emrys climbed back up into the clock room, and the little nut sat gleaming on the bench where he'd left it, small as a sin, exact as a confession.

He thought about keeping it. Of course he thought about keeping it. Proof, evidence, the physical fact of the fraud — the inquiry would want it, Isolde's charts plus the mechanism itself, the wound and the knife both. He picked it up. It was lighter than everything it had cost.

Then he crossed to the forge window in the corner — every clock room needed one, for soldering — raked the coals, and dropped it in. He watched the brass go soft, then bright, then shapeless, and he understood that he was watching something else too: the version of his life where he stayed safe, melting down to nothing, and he felt no grief about it at all.

The letters in the weight channel would be witness enough. The draft in the telescope tube would be testimony. And Isolde's charts —

Isolde's charts were already gone. She had mailed them two days ago, she'd told him last night in the dome, with the flat calm of a woman throwing a lit lantern through a window: to the Board of Inquiry at the capital, addressed in her precise astronomer's hand, with her name signed full and unashamed. When the inspector arrived — and he would arrive, roads or no roads, because the Board did not ignore signed star-charts contradicting regimental time — Halewick would learn that the observatory had turned against him not this midnight but nights ago.

Which meant the dawn was not just a dawn. It was a reckoning with soldiers in it.

Emrys banked the forge, trimmed the lamp, and took his stool beside the running clock, because someone should sit with it, tonight of all nights, the way a man sits up with the newly born or the newly dead. The tick went through him, even, unhurried, telling the truth at one beat per second, and he matched his breathing to it and waited for morning.

Far down the valley, a door would be opening soon. Far up the mountain, an old soldier would be hearing the words *the record will be honest* and knowing what they cost. And somewhere on the capital road, an inspector was already riding toward all of it, carrying a letter whose seal Emrys could picture without ever having seen it.

At half past three, boots sounded on the stair — too heavy for Isolde, too many for Pell.

Emrys stood up, put his hand flat against the clock case, and faced the door.
