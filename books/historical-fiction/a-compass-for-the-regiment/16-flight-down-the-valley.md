## 16. Flight Down the Valley

I had spent two years learning to lie with numbers, and I told myself I was good at it.

The star-charts in my cabinet drawers said one thing; the sky said another. Every night I logged the observatory's official time in the regimental ledger, neat columns in my careful hand, and every night I also kept a second set of records — the true hours, the ones the clock refused to tell because someone had taught it not to. Two years of watching Emrys Dravenmoor's clock lose four minutes a day and saying nothing, because a woman alone on a mountain learns quickly which truths are worth dying for and which are merely worth writing down.

Then, in a single midnight, the boy had gone and made all my lying obsolete.

I felt it before I confirmed it. I was at the meridian instrument just past two in the morning, taking a transit of Regulus for no reason except that it was mine to take, and the rhythm of the place was wrong. For six years the observatory had had a heartbeat with a murmur — that faint, patient drag in the master clock's escapement, four minutes stolen from every day, so constant I had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood. Now it was gone. The tick came clean and merciless, each second exactly a second, and the whole building felt like a man who has straightened his spine after decades of stooping.

He had done it. Of course he had done it. I had watched him weigh it for three days, that quiet apprentice with his father's hands, and I had known when he asked me for a comparison star at midnight — an odd request, a telling request — that he had chosen.

I hated him a little for it. Not because it was wrong. Because it meant the lying was over, and I would have to find out what I was when there was nothing left to hide behind.

* * *

Dawn did not come up the mountain. It came from below — a line of lanterns on the valley road, swinging in the particular urgent cadence of soldiers who have been woken early and told not to be gentle about it.

I counted them from the north gallery, where I take the sun's altitude each clear morning. Eleven men, moving fast, and behind them on the road a rider I recognized by his seat even at this distance: Sergeant Corven, Halewick's shadow, the man who signed the requisitions for the lamp oil that kept the lower observatory rooms lit during years when, officially, no one was stationed there. Corven knew those rooms. Corven knew what happened in them.

And Corven, if he had a brain in his head, knew what it meant when a clock that had lost four minutes a day for six years suddenly kept perfect time.

I found Emrys in the clock room, asleep sitting up against the case, his cheek resting on the oak like a child who had fallen asleep listening to a story. His tools were still laid out on the cloth beside him in their precise rows. He had not gone to bed. He had sat down for a moment after the final adjustment, I judged, some five hours ago, and simply stopped.

"Wake up," I said, and when he didn't move fast enough, I took his shoulder and shook it. "Dravenmoor. They're coming."

He came awake the way I imagine a struck bell does — all at once, already ringing. "What time—"

"Twenty minutes, perhaps less, if they push the climb." I was already pulling open the drawer where I kept the current charts, stripping them out, feeding them into the stove. He watched me burn two years of evidence and had the sense not to stop me. "The mailed copies went out Tuesday with the carrier. What's left here is only enough to hang us."

"The letters—" He was on his feet now, reaching for the clock case.

"Stay where they are." It was the one argument we'd already had and settled: the weight channel had survived a search once, and the clock itself was now beyond suspicion, because a man does not search the thing that is testifying for him. "It's you they want. The moment Corven puts his hand on that pendulum and finds it honest, he'll know the observatory has declared war, and he will want the person who declared it."

Emrys looked at the clock — really looked at it, the way he always did, as if it might say something back — and then at the window, where the first light was doing its slow gray work on the valley. "There's no road down that avoids them."

"There's no road down at all," I said. "There's a tunnel."

* * *

Every observatory is two buildings wearing one coat. There is the part visitors see — the dome, the transit room, the clock room, all light and brass and purpose — and beneath it, threaded through the rock like veins through marble, the instrument tunnels. They were cut thirty years ago so that mercury troughs could be carried in stable temperature from the cellar cisterns to the piers, so that masonry heat and lamplight and human breath would never warp a telescope's view of the sky. The garrison commander who funded them thought he was paying for convenience. What he actually paid for was a second map of the mountain, known to perhaps four living people, of whom I am one and Colonel Aldous Vane — though the world insists otherwise — was another.

I had never taken anyone through them but myself.

"Here." I knelt at the back of the coal store and pulled aside the false panel I'd pried loose my first winter, when I decided that a woman who lived alone above the snowline ought to know every way out of her own house. Cold air breathed up from below, smelling of wet stone and old oil. "Mind the third step. It isn't a step."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, no more — and I saw him look back at the clock, standing tall and honest in the middle of its room, keeping true time with no one to watch it keep it. Something crossed his face that I recognized because I had worn it myself for two years: the fear of leaving a thing unguarded.

"It will run," I said. "That's rather the point of what you did."

"Yes." He picked up his satchel. "Yes. All right."

I took the lamp and went first.

* * *

The tunnels are not dramatic. That is their virtue. Low ceilings, rough walls, iron brackets where the mercury carriers' lamps used to hang, and everywhere that deep mineral silence that makes your own footsteps sound like an intrusion. We moved by my count — forty-one paces to the junction, left at the bracket with the rusted hook, down seventeen steps that the guidebook of my memory insisted were thirteen.

Somewhere in that dark, walking with my free hand trailing the damp wall, I finally said the thing I had been carrying since midnight.

"I knew about the clock for two years, Dravenmoor. Before Pell ever came. Before you."

"I know," he said. "You told me."

"You don't know what I mean." My voice bounced strangely off stone; it sounded younger than I wanted it to. "I mean I sat at my instruments every night and recorded lies in an official ledger, and I told myself I was biding my time, gathering proof, being *prudent*. And then you — an apprentice, half my age, sent up here to fix a mechanism — you looked at the same evidence I had been drowning in for twenty-four months and within a week you were ready to burn your whole life down over it. So no, I don't need your forgiveness or your admiration. I needed to say aloud that I was a coward, so that I never have the comfort of pretending otherwise again."

The lamp lit his face from below. He considered it with that terrible seriousness of his, the seriousness that I suspect everyone mistakes for slowness until they learn better.

"You weren't a coward," he said. "You were alone. Those aren't the same thing. Being alone makes everything look like prudence." A pause, his boots finding the uneven floor. "Besides. Your charts are the reason any of this can matter. My word says the clock was slowed. Your word proves *when*, and *how long*, and *by whose order*. Vane's letters say what happened to him. But yours—" he glanced back into the dark behind us "—yours are the only records that connect the lie to the sky. Nobody can call the sky a liar."

I walked on for several paces without answering, because there was a hot, humiliating pressure behind my eyes and I would sooner have died in that tunnel than let him see it.

Two years of solitude, and it turned out I had been waiting for exactly one sentence.

* * *

We came up into the instrument shed at the mountain's northern shoulder, the little stone hut where the secondary telescope had lived before the new mounting made it redundant. From its narrow window the whole valley unrolled below us, gray-gold in the early light, and I saw immediately what I had feared seeing: the road.

Soldiers at the ford. Soldiers at the bridge below the terraces. And on the switchback path — the only path — three figures climbing with the unhurried confidence of men who know their quarry has nowhere to go.

"They've closed it properly," Emrys said quietly. "He's not sending a message this time. He's closing a ledger."

"He thinks he is." I set the lamp down and went to the corner where the packing crates stood under their shroud of canvas. "Help me lift this."

Beneath the crates, set into the shed's stone floor, was the old instrument well — a vertical shaft, lined with brick, that had once housed the telescope's counterweights and now housed darkness and a ladder. At its foot, the drainage gallery ran west, under the shoulder of the mountain, and emerged in the scree field above the mill stream, a mile and a half from anywhere anyone thought to guard.

But the shaft was not why I had brought us here.

From the crate I lifted the old telescope tube — four feet of brass, dismounted, its lens long since reassigned. I had spent the previous evening, while Emrys sat his vigil beside the clock, doing the work that was mine to do. Into the tube I had rolled the original draft: the charter's first working copy, the one with the corrections in the margins, the one whose very existence proved that the founding document of the regiment was not a founding document at all but a rehearsal. Wrapped in oilcloth, packed around with wool waste, capped at either end. To any searching eye, a broken telescope nobody had bothered to fix.

Emrys stared at it. Then at me.

"That's been ready since last night," he said slowly. "Before the lanterns. Before you woke me. You knew they'd come."

"I didn't *know*." I screwed the retaining ring down tight, more firmly than it needed. "I knew it was possible. There's a difference, and the difference is that one lets you sleep."

"And if they hadn't come?"

"Then I'd have smuggled a telescope tube down the mountain for nothing, and enjoyed the exercise." I slung the strap across my body, where it rode like a soldier's bandolier, absurd and necessary. "Listen to me, Dravenmoor, because I'll only say this once and I intend never to repeat myself. You cannot carry this. If they take you on the valley road with the draft on you, Halewick wins — not because he finds it, but because he doesn't need to. He burns it, and then he burns you, and the record shows an apprentice clockmaker who went mad with grief for his father. Everything ends."

"So it stays with you."

"It goes with neither of us." I checked the window again. The climbers had gained the second switchback. Twenty minutes, perhaps less. "The grain merchant, Aldous Rennick, passes the mill stream crossing on market days. Today is a market day. Pell arranged it before he was taken — a contingency, because Pell is the sort of man who arranges contingencies the way other men arrange flowers. Rennick carries flour to the capital. Flour goes everywhere, is searched by no one, and arrives regardless of who rules the valley." I met his eyes. "The testimony travels in a wagon of flour. You travel separately, on foot, with nothing but your name and your tools, because an apprentice walking to the low country to seek work is the most ordinary sight in the world. And I stay."

"No," he said.

"Yes," I said. "Because the astronomer who logs the observatory's hours must be at her instruments when the inquiry comes asking questions, and it must never occur to anyone that she was anywhere but at her post. My charts are in the capital already, but I am the charts' author, and authors are more convincing than paper. I am *useful* here, Dravenmoor. You, on this mountain, are dead."

* * *

We argued for eleven minutes. I know because the shed held no clock, and so I counted by the one I now trusted most, which is the habit of a lifetime, and by the time we finished, the climbers had reached the third switchback and there was no more time to count.

What ended the argument was not persuasion. It was the sound of fists on the observatory door, far off and muffled by rock, and Emrys going absolutely still, and then — I watched it happen — choosing. He took the tube from me with both hands, the way his nine-year-old self must once have taken a gear blank from a kind officer, and he said:

"When the inquiry reads your name in the record — and it will read your name, Isolde, I'll see to it — I want it to read as what you are. Not the woman who helped. The astronomer who kept the true time."

"Sentiment," I said, because my voice was unreliable.

"Purpose," he said. "There's a difference, and I've recently learned it."

Then he was at the shaft, and then he was in it, and then the ladder was ringing softly under his descending weight, and the darkness took him the way the sky takes the morning stars — not losing them, only taking them somewhere I could no longer see.

* * *

They broke my door politely, which was somehow worse than breaking it badly.

Sergeant Corven came into the clock room ahead of his men and stood a long moment before the master clock, and I watched him do the arithmetic. He was not a stupid man. He put his ear close to the case, the way you'd lean toward a patient, and whatever he heard there — the clean, unforgiving tick of honest seconds — made his jaw tighten.

"Lost time, this thing used to," he said, not turning around. "Six years I've known it to lose time. Had a use for it, losing time."

"Clocks drift," I said. "It's been serviced."

"It's been *betrayed*," Corven said, and turned, and gave me a smile with no warmth in it whatsoever. "Where's the clockmaker, ma'am?"

"Gone. Yesterday evening, down the valley road, before first light. He said something about his master's workshop owing him wages." I said it in the flat tone of a woman repeating gossip she found tiresome, and I saw him half-believe it, because it was exactly the sort of petty, human motive that makes a lie load-bearing.

They searched for two hours. They pulled up the clock room floorboards and went through my charts and shook out my linens and one young soldier held my best eyepiece up to the light as if treason might be written inside the glass. Through all of it I worked at my desk as though the fate of the regiment were a mild interruption to the reduction of Tuesday's observations, because terror conducted at conversational volume was apparently becoming my specialty too.

At noon they were gone, down the mountain, hunting an apprentice who was by then — if the ladder rang true and the gallery ran clear — a mile into the scree with a broken telescope on his back.

I climbed to the north gallery and looked at the valley. Somewhere down there, at the mill stream crossing, a grain merchant named Rennick would be loading his wagon for market, and a tube of brass would leave the valley inside a small mountain of flour, bound for the capital, for the inspectors, for the end of everything Halewick believed he owned.

Somewhere on the mountain's shoulder, a man officially six years dead sat in a ruined chapel, waiting to learn whether his promise had been kept.

And somewhere between those two points, walking alone with nothing but his name and his tools, was Emrys Dravenmoor, carrying the only burden that had ever truly been his: the duty of telling the time truly.

I went back down to my desk. There were observations to reduce, a ledger to keep, and an inquiry that would arrive within days — I had seen to that with my mailed charts, and I intended to be at my post, immaculate and unremarkable, when the inspector's knock came.

But before I sat, I allowed myself one indulgence. I opened the regimental ledger to the current day, took up my pen, and wrote the hour in my careful hand — and for the first time in two years, the number I wrote matched the number in the sky.

It was the smallest act of rebellion available to me, and it was also the largest.

Three days later, the knock came — and the man standing at my door was not the inspector I had summoned.
