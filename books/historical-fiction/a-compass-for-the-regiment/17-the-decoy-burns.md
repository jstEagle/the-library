## 17. The Decoy Burns

They took him at the mill bridge, three of them, and Pell let them.

That was the art of it — the whole architecture of the thing — and he held the architecture in his mind like a mason holds a plan while the wall comes down around him. He did not run when he might have run. He did not throw the satchel into the water, though the ford was at his back and the current quick enough to swallow leather whole. He fought, a little. Enough to make them believe they had won something. He put his shoulder into the tallest soldier and felt the man stagger, and then he let the second one take his legs out from under him on the wet planking, and the satchel went skidding across the boards into the boots of the third, who snatched it up like a man catching a baby thrown from a fire.

"Got it," the soldier said, and there was triumph in it, bright and stupid as a struck flint. "By God, we've got it."

*You've got nothing,* Pell thought, from the flat of his back, watching the sky go white above the bridge timbers. *You've got forty pages of my best handwriting and not one true word in them.*

But he said nothing. A captured courier who talks too much is either terrified or clever, and Halewick's men were paid to assume terror. Terror was cheaper. He gave them terror. He gave them a bleeding lip where he had split it himself against the plank — one sharp turn of the head while they were binding him, done so neatly that no one saw — and he gave them shaking hands, and he gave them the wide-eyed silence of a small man in over his head, which was the role he had been playing since the valley road, since before that, since the dusk he had walked into an observatory with cold hands and a dead man's letters and asked a clockmaker's apprentice to commit treason for him.

It had worked once. He prayed it would work twice.

They marched him down from the ford with his wrists bound behind him and the satchel swinging from the tall soldier's fist, and all the way he counted. It was what he did instead of praying, counting — steps, seconds, hours, the small arithmetic that kept the fear from pooling in the low places of him. Two days since the real testimony had gone east with Josiah Creel and his grain wagon, buried under a false floor beneath four hundredweight of barley, rolling toward the capital at the speed of a tired horse and a careful man. Which meant two days of lead already spent. Which meant that whatever happened to him now, the margin was three days, perhaps four if the weather held against them, and every hour he kept Halewick believing was an hour purchased with his own skin.

He had made a career, such as it was, of spending other people's hours carefully. It seemed fitting that he should end by spending his own.

* * *

Halewick received him in the long room above the barracks, the one with the regimental colours hung along the wall like trophies of a hunt, and Pell understood immediately that he had been brought here to be seen, not questioned — that the room itself was the first instrument of the interrogation. The commander sat behind a table that had been cleared of everything but a lamp, the satchel, and a single sheet of paper laid square before him, and he did not look up when Pell was pushed through the door. He looked at the paper. He let the silence do its work.

Pell stood in his soaked boots and dripped on the floorboards, and counted.

Forty seconds. Fifty. He knew this game too; he had played it from the other side of tables less grand. He let his knees tremble. That part required no craft at all.

At a full minute, Halewick lifted his head, and Pell got his first true look at him — not across a feast hall, not at parade distance, but close, in lamplight, man to man. He had expected a brute. What he found was worse: a handsome, grey-templed officer with kind creases at the corners of his eyes, the face of a man who would help you lift your cart out of a rut and then bill you for the axle. Certainty lived in that face the way damp lives in stone. You could not drive it out. You could only build around it.

"You are called Pell," Halewick said. Not a question. "No other name given at the ford, none in your papers, which were, I note, very nearly perfect. Nearly." He touched the satchel without opening it, one finger tracing the strap. "Perfect papers are more suspicious than none at all, did you know that? Only a guilty man polishes."

"I'm a clerk," Pell said, and made his voice crack in the middle of it. "I'm a clerk, sir, I swear it, I carry correspondence for—"

"For a dead man." Halewick opened the satchel at last, unhurried, and drew out the sheaf of pages Pell had written over six sleepless nights in a rented room above a tannery, copying Vane's testimony in a hand disguised as a shipping agent's, salting it with the dates and names and regimental numbers that made a lie load-bearing. He fanned them on the table like a card player showing his trick. "For Colonel Vane. Who has been dead six years, and whose ghost, it appears, has taken up forgery."

"They're shipping letters," Pell whispered. "I don't know any colonel. I swear on my mother I don't know—"

"Your mother," Halewick said pleasantly, "is a woman in Ashcombe who died eleven years ago, and whom you have not visited since, because I have had you followed since you came down off the mountain road, Mr. Pell, or whatever your name is, and I will tell you what I know, so that you can measure how much lying is worth your while."

And he did. He laid it out in that same pleasant voice, the voice of a man reading a laundry list: the observatory, the clockmaker's boy, the letters in Vane's hand, the chapel ruin above the snowline where a shepherd had reported smoke from a chimney that officially contained no chimney. He knew almost everything. He knew everything except the grain wagon, and Pell stood in the warm lamplight of the long room and listened to the shape of his own failure being described in detail, and kept his eyes wide and wet, and thought: *two days gone, three to buy.*

"The way I see it," Halewick went on, folding his hands, "you have one thing left to sell, and it is not courage. Courage is what men like me have, and men like you borrow. What you have is information. Where is the original? Where is the draft my predecessor's clerk copied all those years ago — the one with the corrections in old Dravenmoor's hand? Because the letters are sentiment, Mr. Pell. Letters move hearts. Hearts do not overturn charters. Paper does. The right piece of paper, in the right hands, and everything my family built in this valley comes apart at the seams." He leaned forward, and the kindness in his face did not change at all. "So. Where is it?"

"It's in the satchel," Pell said. "Everything's in the satchel. I was carrying it to the capital, that's all, I was paid—"

"Yes," Halewick agreed softly. "You were paid. And you will be paid again, and better, when you tell me who paid you the first time. Take some hours and think about it. Hours are the one commodity you have in abundance now."

*Hours,* Pell thought, as they marched him down to the cells. *Yes. That is precisely what they are.*

* * *

The cell was stone and straw and a slot of window too high to reach, and it smelled of wet lime and old iron, and Pell sat against the wall with his knees drawn up and began the accounting.

This was how he measured things. Not in courage — courage was a word other people used about you afterward, if it turned out well, and a word you never used about yourself while it was happening, because while it was happening it was only the absence of any better option. No. He measured in hours. He had learned it in the war, in the year he had served as Vane's dispatch rider, when survival had been a matter of dividing the night into watchable pieces: four hours to the river, three to the picket line, two until dawn. Fear is a flood, and hours are the sandbags. Stack them and stand behind them.

Two days gone already. Creel and his barley were somewhere past the second milestone, crawling east. Three days, maybe four, before the testimony reached hands that could not be bought — Isolde Marchmont had chosen the recipient herself, a surveyor-general with an old grudge and no regimental ties, and she was a woman who chose carefully. So: seventy-two hours, call it, and every one of them had to be earned.

How did a man earn an hour in a cell?

By being worth more unbroken than broken. By making Halewick wait. The commander could not simply hang him — not yet, not quietly — because a hanged courier tells no tales, and Halewick needed the tale. He needed the location of the original draft, and he needed it badly enough to feed Pell, and keep him breathing, and come down to the cell himself wearing that gentle face, day after day, peeling him layer by layer. Every hour Halewick believed there was still something to peel was an hour the grain wagon rolled east.

So Pell's task was simple, and impossible: lose convincingly. Break slowly. Yield, at the end of the third day, something plausible enough to satisfy — a hiding place that would cost Halewick another two days to search and find empty. The ruined chapel was no good; Halewick already had it. The observatory was no good either. But there was a dry-stone shepherd's hut on the western slope, abandoned these twenty years, half-swallowed by the hill — a place a desperate man might choose, and a place far enough from anywhere that digging it up would take a day at least. He would give them the hut on the third day. He would give it up weeping. He would make them believe the last of him had gone into it.

Three days. Sixty, seventy hours, each one a coin he would pay out one at a time and never get back.

He heard boots in the corridor that evening and knew it was Halewick before the lantern light reached the bars — knew it by the pace, unhurried, the walk of a man who owned the ground he crossed. The commander came alone, set the lantern on the floor between them, and sat down on a stool that someone had left there, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other through the iron like neighbours over a fence.

"Do you know what I admire about you?" Halewick said. "Truly. You committed to the fall at the bridge. Most couriers throw the bag in the river and leave us guessing. You let us take it. That is either stupidity or theatre, and your papers were too good for stupidity." He smiled, and it was the smile of a father at a recital. "So here is what happens now. Tomorrow I burn the letters. Publicly, on the drill square, so the regiment sees the treason consumed. And tomorrow night, and the next night, and the next, I will come here, and we will talk, and eventually you will tell me where the original is — because everyone tells me, in the end. Not because I am cruel. Because I am patient, and patience is the one weapon that never dulls." He rose, brushed off his coat, paused. "Your friend the clockmaker's boy is also in my custody. Did you know? Not in a cell. In my house. As a guest. He dines with my family tonight."

It was a lie. It had to be a lie. Pell turned his face to the wall and let his shoulders shake, and gave the commander exactly the sight he had come for, and inside the shaking he was doing arithmetic: Emrys was free or he was not, the wagon rolled or it did not, and none of it — not one hour of it — was in Pell's hands anymore. All he could govern now was the price of himself. He resolved, there in the dark, to be expensive.

* * *

They burned the decoy the next noon.

He heard it before he saw it — the crowd noise carrying over the cell block wall, the murmur of several hundred men arranged into formation — and then, when they moved him up to the barred window of the guard room to witness it, as the regulations required, he saw the square below: the regiment drawn up in ranks, the brazier already roaring, and Halewick on the steps holding the forty pages aloft in one gloved hand like a trophy fish.

"This," Halewick's voice carried across the square, pitched for the ranks and for the window both, "is what becomes of lies in this valley."

The pages went into the fire. Pell watched his own handwriting curl and blacken, six nights of labour going up in a column of sparks, the false names, the invented dates, the shipping-agent's hand he had practised until his fingers cramped — all of it consumed in ninety seconds before a thousand men who now believed the matter closed. He watched their faces, those he could see: relief, mostly. A settled thing. The regiment exhaling.

And he thought: *burn, then. Burn beautifully.* Every eye on the square was fixed on the fire, and not one man among them was looking east along the capital road, where a grain merchant named Creel was at that moment watering a tired horse and checking, for the hundredth time, the false floor beneath his barley.

Sacrifice, Pell had learned, was mostly a problem of audience. Done rightly, it looked exactly like defeat.

* * *

That night, and the next, Halewick came, just as promised, and Pell began the long performance of breaking.

It was the strangest work of his life — stranger even than forging a dead man's enemy's confidence, stranger than riding dispatches through the war. He had to build a collapse the way a watchmaker builds a movement, wheel by wheel, each piece meshing with the last. The first night he gave up nothing but exhaustion, and let Halewick see the exhaustion. The second night he gave up a name — a minor quartermaster in the capital who had once sold him information, a betrayal that cost Pell nothing because the man was already fled and Halewick would find an empty house and waste half a day confirming it. Half a day. Twelve hours. He watched the commander pocket the name and understood that he had just sold twelve hours of a dead quartermaster's silence for the price of appearing to bend.

The third night his body began to betray the performance. They were giving him little food and less water, and the cold of the stones came up through the straw into his bones, and his cough had started, deep and wet, the same cough that had killed his brother. He sat in the dark after Halewick left and did the honest accounting, the one he did not show anyone: how many hours remained in him, truly, if the performance failed and Halewick stopped playing the gentleman. His hands shook now whether he willed them to or not. His mind wandered mid-sentence. He caught himself, twice, forgetting which lies he had told — and a liar who forgets his lies is a man standing on river ice, hearing it talk.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow he would give them the shepherd's hut, and weep doing it, and it had better be enough, because there might not be a fourth night in him.

He pressed his forehead against the cold wall and counted what was left. Forty hours since the ford, perhaps fifty. The wagon should be past the toll bridge by now, past the last place Halewick's riders habitually patrolled. Another day and a half and it would be in the surveyor-general's office, and then the thing would be out of all their hands — his, Emrys's, Vane's, even Halewick's — and into the slow grinding machinery of the capital, where truth moved at the speed of ink and committees, but moved.

All he had to do was remain interesting for another day and a half.

He laughed — a small, cracked sound, there in the straw, the first laugh in three days — because he had spent his whole life being forgettable. Small man, cold hands, the courier nobody remembered five minutes after he'd left the room. And now his entire remaining value to the most powerful man in the valley depended on being unforgettable. On seeming to hold, behind his bloodshot eyes, the one secret that mattered.

Boots in the corridor. Unhurried. The lantern light coming.

Pell wiped his face, arranged his trembling, and began the count of the final hours.

The stool scraped into place outside the bars. Halewick sat, and leaned close, and his voice, when it came, was quieter than it had ever been — which was how Pell knew, with a cold drop through the middle of him, that something had changed.

"Now then," the commander said gently. "Before you offer me the shepherd's hut on the western slope — and do spare us both the trouble, my riders passed it yesterday — why don't you tell me instead what you know about the grain merchant."
