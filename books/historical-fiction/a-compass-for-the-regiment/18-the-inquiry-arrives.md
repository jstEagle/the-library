## 18. The Inquiry Arrives

The rain came down the valley like a rumor three days before the inspector did.

I noticed it first in the clock — of course I did; I notice everything in the clock now, the way a man who has confessed to himself notices everything in his own face. The humidity crept up the mountain and into the case, and the pendulum's period lengthened by a fraction my instruments could barely register but my nerves registered entirely. A machine does not lie about the weather. It was one more thing I loved about it, and one more thing that made me afraid.

Isolde had mailed her charts on the Tuesday. She had done it without ceremony, walking down to the valley post herself with the packet sewn inside her coat lining, because she said — with the flat practicality that had begun to feel like courage to me — that if she sent it through the observatory courier, it would pass through Halewick's hands first, and Halewick read other people's mail the way other men read scripture: daily, hungrily, looking for themselves in it.

"Two weeks by road," she'd said, sealing the last fold. "Less if the roads are dry."

The roads were not dry. The roads were rivers wearing a disguise. And so when the hoofbeats came up the switchback on the ninth morning — four horses, riding hard through drizzle, their riders muffled to the eyes — I told myself it was too soon for an answer from the capital, and then I told myself that hope is just fear wearing its good coat, and then I went down to meet them anyway, because there was no version of me left that could stand in a doorway pretending indifference.

* * *

Inspector Corvane was nothing like I expected, which I suppose is what everyone says and no one believes until it happens to them. I had imagined the capital sending someone grand: silk sash, brass insignia, a voice trained in courtrooms. What came off the lead horse instead was a narrow man of fifty-odd in a gray traveling coat gone dark at the shoulders with rain, with the posture of someone who had spent his life sitting slightly forward at desks, waiting for documents. He carried a leather dispatch case and nothing else visible. He looked at the observatory the way I look at a movement that has stopped: with professional disappointment, as though the world were full of machines that ought to be running better.

"Emrys Dravenmoor," he said. Not a question. "Master clockmaker." He pronounced *master* carefully, weighing it, and I felt the word land on me like a hand on a shoulder — steadying or arresting, not yet decided.

"Acting master," I said. "My certificate is pending."

"Everything here is pending, I'm told." He did not smile. "Inspector Corvane, Office of Records Inquiry. Your astronomer wrote us a very thorough letter."

"Did she?"

"She enclosed star-charts." He looked up at the cloud ceiling with faint distaste, as though the sky itself were an unreliable witness. "Nineteen months of observations cross-referenced against your observatory's official time-log. There are discrepancies. Rather a lot of them, all trending in one direction." He opened the dispatch case, took out a folded sheet, and held it where I could see the columns of figures without letting me take it. "Four minutes a day, Dravenmoor. Someone has been stealing time from this valley for years, and writing honest numbers over the theft."

Behind him, his three escorts dismounted and stood in the rain with the patience of men paid by the hour. One of them glanced back down the valley road, and I understood they had been sent partly as escort and partly as evidence that the capital could now reach this far.

"You'll want to see the clock," I said.

"I'll want to see everything," Corvane said. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

He spent two hours in the clock room and asked better questions than any soldier ever had.

He put his ear against the case the way Pell had once done. He asked me to stop the pendulum and start it again and timed the resumption on a pocket chronometer he produced from somewhere inside his coat — a beautiful thing, capital-made, accurate to a second a week, and I confess I envied it before I distrusted everything else about him. He asked when the drift began. He asked who had access. He asked whether the slowing could be accidental — worn pallets, a fouled weight channel — and watched my face while he asked it, and I gave him the truth because I had decided somewhere in the last weeks that I was finished handing out anything less, even to strangers, even when it cut me:

"It could have been accidental. It wasn't. Someone fitted a friction shoe inside the weight channel and adjusted it seasonally. I removed it in the autumn. The clock has kept true time since." I let him write that down. "And inspector — I know who some of the people are who did it. And why."

Corvane's pen stopped. For the first time he looked at me not as a document but as a man.

"Do you," he said.

"There are letters. An original draft. Testimony from Colonel Aldous Vane."

The name went into the room like cold air under a door. Even the escorts shifted. Six years dead and still able to change the temperature of a mountain room.

Corvane closed his notebook with great care, as though the pages might escape. "Colonel Vane is deceased."

"Colonel Vane is a fiction we tell ourselves so we don't have to look at what happened to him," I said. "I've met the man behind the fiction. He's ill, and hiding, and telling the truth, which is more than anyone in this regiment has managed in a generation."

I had not planned to say it. It came out whole, the way truth does when you've been carrying it too long — not delivered but dropped. Isolde, who had come in behind us and stood by the instrument wall with her arms folded, made a small sound that might have been approval or alarm.

Corvane regarded me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below us the valley smoked its morning fires, obedient to hours we had only recently made honest.

"You understand," he said at last, quietly, "that what you've just said, said aloud, in front of witnesses, is either the beginning of justice or the end of you. Possibly both. In my experience they usually travel together."

"I know," I said. "When do we begin?"

"We don't," said a voice from the doorway. "Because nothing begins today except an inquiry into treason, which I have the honor of preempting."

* * *

Commander Halewick filled a doorway the way weather fills a valley — not by force exactly, but by being everywhere at once, impossible to argue with.

He had come up from the garrison with six mounted men, which was twice courtesy and half an occupation, and he wore his dress coat though there was no ceremony scheduled, because Halewick understood that costume is argument. He crossed the clock room as if he owned the floorboards individually, shook Corvane's hand warmly, thanked him for coming such a long way in filthy weather, apologized for the state of the road, offered the use of the garrison's best rooms, and in the space of ninety seconds made the rest of us furniture.

"Commander," Corvane said, extracting his hand with the air of a man retrieving it from a trap. "You received my letter of intent?"

"I did, and I rejoice in it. Irregularities! Yes! I ordered this investigation myself, inspector, weeks ago, the moment the rumors reached me. A commander's first duty is to his own housecleaning." Halewick turned that smile on me, warm as a hearth. "Dravenmoor. You look tired. You work too hard. This clock will be the death of you."

It was so nearly true that I felt my face do something, and Halewick saw it, and his smile deepened by a degree no one but me could measure.

"But I regret," he went on, turning back to Corvane, drawing a folded oilcloth bundle from under his arm with the gravity of a man producing relics, "that your inquiry has already been overtaken by events. Three days ago my patrols intercepted a courier on the valley road — a known associate of the traitor Pell, who as you may know has been taken into custody following an armed flight toward the capital carrying stolen regimental property." He set the bundle on the instrument table and unfolded it with slow, ceremonial care.

The smell reached me before the sight did: char, wet ash, old smoke.

Inside the oilcloth lay the remains of a leather satchel, scorched and split, its contents burned past reading — blackened wads of paper fused together, edges curled like autumn leaves, one corner bearing a fragment of wax seal that might once have been anything. Pell's decoy. Pell's beautiful, empty, brilliant decoy, built to be caught, built to burn, built to buy three days. And it had worked, and now it was working for the other side.

"This," Halewick said, "is what your conspiracy actually looks like, inspector. Stolen records, burned by conspirators when pursuit closed in. The courier confessed —" his voice never changed, smooth as lacquer — "before his unfortunate collapse, to carrying forged testimony intended to discredit the founding charter and implicate loyal officers in forgery themselves. You see the elegance of it. Accuse the charter of being false so that no one examines the accusers."

"He confessed," Corvane said. "Before his collapse."

"Grief and guilt, inspector. The man is old. Interrogation is a mercy we extend reluctantly." Halewick's eyes touched mine for half a heartbeat, and in that half-heartbeat I heard Pell measuring his courage in hours in a cell below the garrison, spending it minute by minute to buy us time, and I understood that every word Halewick spoke was also a message to me: *this is what happens to couriers.*

"And the testimony he carried," Corvane said slowly, making notes, "was destroyed. So there is no original."

"The originals, presumably, remain with the conspirators," Halewick said pleasantly, "who will doubtless produce them at the moment of maximum theater. Conspirators always do. They cannot resist an audience." He spread his hands, magnanimous, wounded, certain. "Which is why I am grateful you're here, inspector. Search whatever you like. My garrison is open to you. The observatory is open to you. I have nothing to hide and everything to lose — my family has governed this valley under that charter for eighty years, and now a dead man's ghost and a clockmaker's apprentice propose to unwrite it with papers no one can examine." He shook his head sadly. "Burned papers. How convenient for them that fire destroys only what would have exonerated me."

It was magnificent. That was the horror of it. Standing there in his dress coat with the rain-wet ashes of Pell's decoy spread on the table like a trophy, Halewick had taken our one piece of misfortune and hammered it into armor. The decoy that proved nothing proved everything — proved conspiracy, proved flight, proved guilt — because proof was never about the object. It was about who stood next to it and how loudly they spoke.

Isolde stepped forward. "The star-charts—"

"—are a matter of great interest," Halewick said smoothly, without looking at her, "and will be examined in due course, along with the question of how an observatory funded by the regiment came to spend nineteen months recording falsified data without reporting it. Negligence, Miss Marchmont, has many shapes. Some of them look like loyalty to outsiders." He let that sit. Then, warmly, to Corvane: "You'll want to interview everyone. Of course. Begin anywhere. But I'd suggest beginning with Dravenmoor — he discovered the clock's fault, after all, or claims to. Remarkable timing, wouldn't you say? A failing clock, a hidden stranger, letters in a dead man's hand. It reads like something written to be found."

For a moment — one long, rain-ticked moment — I saw it as Corvane might see it: a story too neat, a hero too available, a clockmaker standing conveniently at the center of every irregularity in the valley. Hope and suspicion are twins; they arrive holding hands, and you cannot always tell which one is which.

But Corvane only wrote for a while, pen scratching, and then looked up.

"I'll begin," he said, "with the clock."

Halewick blinked. "The clock?"

"It's the only witness in this valley," Corvane said, closing his notebook, "that can't be promoted, punished, or burned. I want its complete maintenance record, every entry since construction, in the original hand. And I want it read aloud at the garrison, publicly, before the assembled officers — because documents die in private, Commander, and live in public. Surely a man proud of his housecleaning agrees."

Something moved behind Halewick's certainty — fast, buried, gone — the flicker of a man watching a stage being built he had not designed.

"Of course," he said. "Tomorrow at noon. Founding-day tradition, in fact — the charter is read aloud at noon. We'll add the clock's record to the ceremony. Two inheritances, side by side." His smile returned, full wattage. "Nothing hides better in tradition than the truth. Ask any family."

He gathered the burned satchel back into its oilcloth with the tenderness of a man folding a flag, bowed to Corvane, nodded graciously to Isolde, and paused beside me on his way out — close enough that only I heard him.

"Tomorrow at noon," he murmured, "your father's handwriting gets read to the whole regiment, one way or another. Decide overnight whose son you'd rather be remembered as."

Then the boots went down the stairs, the horses went down the switchback, and the rain took the sound of them.

* * *

We stood in the clock room a long time after, none of us speaking. Above us the mechanism ticked on, keeping perfect time now, honest to the second, and it seemed suddenly the most exposed thing on the mountain — a witness with a glass case and nowhere to hide.

Corvane broke the silence. "The maintenance record," he said. "Does it exist? In original hands?"

"Yes," I said. "My father's hand among them. Every year, signed."

"Then tomorrow," Corvane said, tucking his notebook away, "you'll be standing next to it. I suggest you decide tonight what you intend to say when it's read aloud." He picked up his hat, paused at the door, and added, almost gently: "In my experience, Dravenmoor, the moment always comes when the record must be read aloud by someone who knows what it means. Commanders read documents. Clockmakers understand them. Tomorrow noon, this valley finds out which of those it trusts."

The door closed behind him. Isolde and I looked at each other across the case of the clock, and between us, ticking, the true hour counted down the night.
