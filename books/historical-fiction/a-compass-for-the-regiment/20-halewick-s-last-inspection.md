## 20. Halewick's Last Inspection

Brigadier-General Corvus Halewick had been awake since four, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that he had spent the hours before dawn standing at his study window in full dress uniform, watching the mountain, where the observatory lamp burned steady and wrong.

He had known the lamp's rhythm for twenty years. It winked twice at the half-hour, once at the hour — a courtesy of the astronomers, a heartbeat for the valley. Last night it had burned flat and unwinking from midnight onward, and this morning no correction notice had come down the road. Isolde Marchmont had not sent her weekly transit report. The mail rider had carried nothing to the capital but one sealed packet addressed, in a woman's hand, to the Office of Records and Inquiry.

He had read that hand for two years without knowing what it meant. Now he knew.

Halewick poured his coffee, drank it black and scalding, and permitted himself precisely one minute of stillness. His father had taught him that. *A commander may feel anything for sixty seconds, Corvus. Then he feels the next thing that must be done.*

The clock on his mantel — a small regimental piece, its dial enamelled with the founding date — ticked with perfect, insolent accuracy. Four minutes had come back to the world overnight. He had noticed it at once, the way a man notices a floorboard that has finally given way beneath him.

So. The clockmaker had chosen.

* * *

He rode up himself. That surprised his adjutant, Lieutenant Sorrel, who had expected the order to be delegated — a detachment, a set of instructions, the general's will executed at arm's length as always. Instead Halewick took twelve men and the good horses and went up the valley road through thinning mist, sitting his grey mare with the straight, unhurried back that three generations of Halewicks had worn like a second spine.

He was not afraid. He wants that understood, even now, even here. Fear is for men whose authority is borrowed; his was inherited, which is a different metal entirely. The charter hung behind glass in the regimental hall, signed by the Founder and countersigned by the Crown's envoy, and it said plainly that within the valley's boundaries the regiment's commander held jurisdiction over life, property, record, and time itself. Time itself. That clause had always been his favorite. Whoever keeps the hours keeps the truth, his grandfather used to say, and whoever keeps the truth keeps everything else.

The trouble — and he admitted it now, riding upward, cold air in his teeth — was that someone had kept different hours than the ones he had ordered kept.

Four minutes a day. Two years of them, or more, siphoned off into darkness by whatever slow sabotage the old clockmaker's son had found in the mechanism. And if the clock could be made to lie, then the alibis built on those dark hours could be unmade. The ambush ledger. The supply diversions he had arranged to look like banditry. The night, six years ago, when a courier party was reported destroyed on the north pass and a colonel's death certificate was drafted before the bodies were cold — bodies that had never included a colonel at all.

Halewick had not killed Aldous Vane. He wanted that on the record too, wherever the record ended up. He had merely reported him dead, efficiently and in the proper format, because a living Vane was an indictment and a dead one was a monument. Monuments ask nothing of you. They stand where they are put.

The observatory emerged from the mist above him, white against grey, its dome catching the first real light. The lamp was dark now — daylight made it moot — but the building had a look he had never seen on it before. Occupied. Watched. As if the windows themselves had turned their faces toward the valley.

Lieutenant Sorrel drew alongside. "General, the inspector from the capital arrived last evening. Rode up with the mail. He's inside."

"I know he's inside," Halewick said. "That is why I am here."

* * *

The inspector's name was Quennel, and he had the gray, unhurried manner of a man who had spent thirty years being lied to by professionals. He stood in the instrument hall beside the master clock with his hands folded behind him, and he did not bow when Halewick entered, which told Halewick everything about how the morning would go.

"General." Quennel nodded at the clock. "Remarkable machine. Your clockmaker has it running to within a second a week, I'm told. The records I've seen suggest it hasn't managed that in years."

"The records," Halewick said pleasantly, "were falsified. By conspirators. You have my account of it — the intercepted courier, the satchel of forged testimony, burned rather than surrender it. Traitors rarely travel light without cause."

"I have your account," Quennel agreed. "I also have these."

Isolde Marchmont stepped out from behind the transit instrument, and behind her came Emrys Dravenmoor, carrying a flat wooden case the way a surgeon carries instruments. The astronomer laid her star-charts on the table between them, two years of them, each page ruled and annotated in her small exact hand: predicted occultation, observed occultation, discrepancy. Discrepancy. Discrepancy. A column of stolen minutes running down the margin like blood from a wound.

"My observations are independent of every record your office keeps," Isolde said. "The sky does not take orders from the valley. The clock was slowed deliberately. Someone gave that order, General, or benefited from it, and the discrepancies align — every time — with nights when regimental movements were later reported at hours the stars say were dark."

Halewick looked at the charts for a long moment. Then at Dravenmoor, who stood very still, younger than Halewick had expected, with his father's heavy hands and none of his father's stoop.

"You repaired it," Halewick said to him. "I offered you a fair price for leaving well enough alone."

"You offered me my father's debts and a certificate," Emrys said. His voice did not shake. "You bought the forgery, General. I'm only selling you the receipt."

And he opened the case.

Halewick had prepared for many things. He had prepared for the letters — he had known about the letters since Pell vanished into the river country. He had prepared for the draft, the original, the paper that showed his charter in its first dishonest skin, though he had hoped never to see it. What he had not prepared for was the voice of Aldous Vane coming off the page in Quennel's dry reading hands, sentence after patient sentence, describing an ambush that was staged, a death that was invented, a signature procured under a false promise of honor, and a man who had walked away from his own monument into the hills to wait for someone brave enough to carry the truth downhill.

Quennel read for a long time. The hall was silent except for the clock, ticking away at its new, unbearable honesty.

Then the general did the thing his father would have done, which was to reach for law.

* * *

"This is my jurisdiction," Halewick said. "By charter."

He crossed to the tall window and stood with his back to them, and when he turned again he had composed his face into the expression from the founding-day feast, the warm one, the certain one.

"Inspector, you operate under capital authority, and I respect that — within the capital's territory. But the charter places this valley, this mountain, and every soul on both, under regimental command. That is not my opinion. It is the founding law of this corps, sealed by the envoy of the Crown itself. Every inquiry conducted here proceeds through me or not at all. Those documents were removed from regimental custody by unauthorized persons. That makes them stolen property and their carriers criminals, and I am entitled — obliged — to recover them by whatever force the situation requires."

"Those papers," Quennel said mildly, "include the charter's own working draft, in which several clauses appear in a hand that matches no signatory but does match a workshop ledger we recovered this morning from a house in the lower town. Payment entries. Regimental payments. For 'script work.'" He paused. "Your law, General, appears to be written in forged ink."

"Then I'll have it authenticated properly—"

"It has been," Isolde said. "Three days ago. The inspector's office holds the analysis."

Something moved behind Halewick's eyes then, the first crack across the glaze. But a man does not inherit near-feudal power by folding at the first fracture, and so he did what commanders do when law abandons them: he reached for the men who had sworn to follow it.

"Sorrel." He did not raise his voice. He never needed to. "Take four men. Secure the documents. Detain anyone who resists custody under charter law — that means all of them, including the inspector, until the Crown can be persuaded to honor its own seal. Move."

It was, he would think afterward, a perfectly legal order according to everything he had been raised inside of. The charter said it. The charter had always said it. His grandfather had enforced less with fewer men and been thanked for it.

Lieutenant Sorrel looked at him. And did not move.

* * *

Later — much later, in the quiet of a stripped office, in the margins of a court-martial transcript — Halewick would replay that moment again and again, searching for the flaw in it. He never found one, because there wasn't one. That was what made it unbearable.

Sorrel was twenty-six. Halewick had promoted him personally, dined with him, lent him books, held his commission letter while the boy's mother wept at the ceremony. And Sorrel stood there in the instrument hall with his hand nowhere near his sword and said, in a voice that only shook at the very end:

"No, sir."

"Explain yourself, Lieutenant."

"I've heard the letters, sir." Sorrel's throat worked. "My father served under Colonel Vane. At the ford. He named me for — " He stopped, steadied. "We were told the colonel died a hero. We've had a monument built to it. Sir. If the colonel lived, then the regiment has spent six years saluting a lie, and the charter that gives me the right to arrest this inspector is part of the same lie. I can't secure documents under a law that the documents themselves prove false. It would be — " and here his voice did fail, just slightly — "it would be like arresting a man for telling you the compass is broken, while you're lost."

The silence that followed had weight. Halewick felt it settle on his shoulders like snow.

"Corporal Dane," he said. "You have the order."

Dane, fifteen years in the regiment, veteran of two border actions, shifted his weight and looked at the floor. "Beg pardon, General. I'd want it in writing. Under the charter. Which — " he glanced at the open case on the table, at the draft with its exposed clauses, and could not finish.

"Hayes."

Hayes didn't even answer. He simply stepped back, one pace, out of the line of any order that might come next, and the other eight men followed him by some silent arithmetic of inches, until Halewick stood alone in the center of his own jurisdiction with twelve soldiers arranged around it like an audience.

Twelve men. He had commanded hundreds. He had buried dozens and written letters home to their mothers in a hand so fine it looked printed, and every mother had written back thanking him, and not one of them had ever refused him anything.

"General Halewick," Inspector Quennel said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse, "the Crown's envoy's seal on your charter has been under dispute since the forgery analysis reached the capital. Until that dispute resolves, your jurisdiction is suspended pending formal inquiry. I'd take it as a personal courtesy if you would accompany us down the mountain without requiring anyone to make this difficult."

For a moment — just a moment — Corvus Halewick considered the other path. The saber was at his hip. The door was one. Some part of him, the grandfather part, measured distances and sightlines and odds out of pure habit, and found them poor, and reported the numbers like a faithful clerk: *no exit, no majority, no law left standing to stand on.*

He let his hand fall away from the hilt.

"You misunderstand what you're doing," he said quietly. "All of you. I did not build the lie. I inherited it, whole, the way one inherits a house with rot in the beams — and I kept it standing. That was my duty. My father's duty. My grandfather's. Somebody held the thing up. If it falls now, it falls on the whole valley, and you'll wish for the days when a man could keep four minutes dark and nobody thought to ask why."

Nobody answered him. That was the worst of it — nobody argued. Arguing would have meant his words still counted as reasons. These days they were only weather.

They took his saber first, then the sash. Sorrel did that himself, refusing help, his young face gray and wet. Halewick submitted to it the way he had submitted to inspections all his life: chin level, heels together, eyes forward. When they led him past the master clock he slowed — one step, half a step — and looked at it, really looked, at the brass pendulum swinging in its long case with the patience of something that had outlasted everyone who tried to own it.

"Four minutes," he said, to the clock or to himself. "All it ever cost to buy the valley was four minutes a day. Do you know what I could have done with honest time?"

"You could have told the truth," said Emrys Dravenmoor, from the shadows by the stair. "It was free."

Halewick looked at the boy — the apprentice, the forger's son, the keeper of the corrected hours — and for an instant something almost human crossed his face, something that might have been exhaustion or might have been envy, and might even, in a poorer light, have passed for understanding.

"Keep it running, then," he said. "See what it costs you."

They walked him out into the morning. The mist was burning off the valley below, and the terraces and roofs and smoke of the town lay spread out beneath the mountain like possessions laid out for appraisal, and not one thing down there belonged to him anymore. He did not look back. Halewicks did not look back.

But at the turn of the road, where the monument to Colonel Aldous Vane stood in its little circle of pines — the bronze figure, the sword point down, the inscription about sacrifice — Halewick stopped despite himself, because the escort stopped, and he read the stone one last time.

*HE DIED SO THAT THE REGIMENT MIGHT LIVE.*

"He didn't, though," Halewick said, softly, to no one who mattered. "That's the trouble with monuments. They stand where they're put."

And somewhere above him, on the mountain, in a ruined chapel past the snowline, a gaunt old man who had outlived his own legend woke coughing into a cold dawn, pressed a folded letter to his chest, and asked Pell — who had limped back up to him free and hollow-eyed, having bought three days with a burning satchel and his own capture —

"Pell. Tell me the boy came through."

"He came through, sir. The clock's honest. The inquiry has him."

Vane closed his eyes, and smiled the smallest smile there is, the kind that costs a dying man something, and said, "Then wind it. Wind it well."

And far downhill, in the capital, a clerk at the Office of Records dipped a pen and began the letter that would arrive too late — bearing, at last, the restoration of Colonel Aldous Vane's name.
