## 21. Vindication, Too Late

The word came on the third morning after the clock stopped, carried up the mountain by a boy in regimental grey who would not come inside.

"He's dead," the boy said, standing at the threshold with his cap crushed in both fists. "The colonel. Vane. Found in the chapel above the snowline."

Emrys had been holding a cleaning rag. He set it down on the workbench with great care, as if it were an instrument that might be damaged.

"When?"

"The night the inquiry began." The boy's eyes went to the master clock, standing true and silent in its case, and then quickly away, as though the mechanism might reproach him. "Pell was released this morning to identify the body. He's coming down now. They said — they said he was holding a letter."

Emrys knew which letter. There was only one letter it could have been. He had written its final line himself, six days ago, and sent it up the mountain in Pell's careful hands: *The record will be honest. Come down when you are able.* And Vane, who had waited six years in a ruined chapel for exactly those words, had read them, folded them against his chest, and died sometime in the dark while men in the valley below argued about his name.

"Thank you," Emrys heard himself say. "You can tell them I'll want the details."

The boy saluted — an odd gesture, half apology — and went back down through the pines.

Isolde emerged from behind the transit instrument, where she had been pretending to adjust its leveling screws. She did not say anything at first. That was one of the things Emrys had learned to value about her in these terrible weeks: she understood that silence could be a form of company rather than an absence of it.

"You should sit down," she said finally.

"I'm sitting."

"You're leaning on the bench like it owes you money."

He looked down and discovered she was right. His knuckles were white against the wood. He sat.

* * *

They brought the body down two days later, because the inquiry insisted — Halewick's counsel had demanded physical proof that Aldous Vane existed at all before they would entertain the question of what had been done to him. So they wrapped him in canvas and carried him down the switchback road he had climbed six years earlier with nothing but a stolen coat and a forged death, and laid him in the chapel of the regimental garrison, which had never once held a service for him while he lived.

Emrys went to see him. He told himself it was duty — the last apprentice task, the final winding of a stopped movement — but he knew it was something less orderly than that. It was the need to see the ending with his own eyes, the way a clockmaker must open a case to confirm what the hands have already told him.

Vane looked smaller than the man in the ruined chapel. Death had taken the fevered brightness from him and left something quieter, almost boyish, the face of an officer who had once been young and believed in things. His hands were folded on his breast, and between them, tucked with a formality that could only have been deliberate, was a single sheet of paper gone soft at the folds from handling.

The provost officer standing guard cleared his throat. "The inquiry has catalogued it. Evidence item forty-one. You may look, but not remove it."

"I know what it says," Emrys said.

"Then you know better than most." The officer hesitated, then added, gruffly, "I served under him. Before. At the ford. We were told he deserted. I believed it for six years." He said it like a man confessing to a debt he could not repay.

Emrys leaned close. Through the soft paper he could make out the shapes of the words he had written, and beneath them, in a hand grown shakier over six years of hiding but still unmistakably a soldier's, the words Vane had written back, the night the inquiry began, by whatever light the chapel afforded:

*Then let it run.*

Two hours of honest time. That was all he had asked for, and all he had lived to see. Emrys stood over the body of the regiment's greatest hero and felt no triumph whatsoever — only the enormous, patient weight of a debt that could never now be presented for payment.

* * *

The inquiry sat for eleven days in the garrison hall, and Emrys attended every session, because Isolde told him that witnesses who hide look guilty, and because his father's name was going to be spoken aloud in that room whether he was there to hear it or not.

It was spoken on the fourth day. The inspector from the capital — a dry, meticulous man named Corvasser who took notes with a pencil worn to a stub — read out the findings regarding the founding charter: the paper aged artificially, the ink compounded with iron salts unavailable in the valley before the charter's supposed date, and the script itself matched beyond dispute the practice sheets found among the effects of Josiah Dravenmoor, clockmaker, deceased eleven years.

"Josiah Dravenmoor," Corvasser read, "was paid the sum of four hundred crowns across three entries in the regimental ledger, under the heading of repairs to instruments, none of which appear in any inventory of instruments requiring repair."

Every eye in the hall turned toward Emrys. He did not lower his gaze. He had promised himself, alone with the clock at midnight, that he would not flinch from this part, and he kept the promise the way his father had not kept his.

"My father forged the charter," Emrys said, when Corvasser invited him to speak. "I didn't know until six weeks ago. But not knowing was my luxury, not my innocence. The signature is his. The crime is his. I claim neither, and I deny neither."

There was a silence in the hall that had a different quality than the other silences. Old Sergeant Bray, who had taught half the regiment to march, nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods at a weight that sits level.

Halewick's trial within the inquiry lasted another three days and was shorter in substance. The commander fought well, Emrys had to grant him that. He produced the burned remnants of Pell's decoy satchel and gave a performance of outraged loyalty that drew murmurs from the younger officers. He spoke of inheritance, of tradition, of the sacred trust passed down through commanders since the founding. He never once raised his voice. That had always been the most frightening thing about him.

But Isolde's star-charts did not raise their voices either, and they were more damning than any shout. Two years of recorded observations, each entry showing an observatory hour that disagreed with the sun by a widening margin — a margin that began, precisely, the week after certain meetings were held in the garrison whose participants preferred darkness. And then Emrys's own testimony about the mechanism in the weight channel: the filed pallets, the adjustable drag-shoe, the elegant, patient sabotage that had turned the master clock into a machine for manufacturing alibis.

On the ninth day, Halewick stopped fighting. Not because he was beaten, Emrys thought, watching him from the gallery, but because he had run out of audience. His officers had testified, one after another, in flat voices, that they had followed his orders regarding the search of the observatory under protest; that the charter's clause granting the commander authority over civilian records had always been read to them in private and never shown; that Colonel Vane's death notice had been signed by a surgeon who, it transpired, had left the regiment a month before the ambush at the ford.

On the eleventh day, Corvasser rose and read the finding. The charter of the regiment was declared void in its essential clauses. The powers of command reverted to the capital. All acts performed under the forged authorities were subject to review. And the name of Colonel Aldous Vane was restored to the rolls of the regiment, with full honors, effective from the date of his reported death.

Effective from the date of his reported death. Six years late. Emrys wrote the phrase down in the margin of his notebook and underlined it twice, because it seemed to him the most precise sentence anyone had uttered in that whole hall: justice, in this valley, ran slow, and the difference was measured in lives.

* * *

The honors came for Emrys on a bright, cold morning a week later, when the frost still silvered the observatory steps and the air had the clean bite of approaching winter.

They came in person — a delegation of three, led by none other than Corvasser himself, who had evidently decided that some things should not be entrusted to correspondence. With him came the regiment's acting commander, a colonel named Ashworth with kind eyes and a careful manner, and a clerk carrying a lacquered box.

They assembled in the observatory room, where the master clock stood running true, and Isolde hovered by her charts pretending she wasn't listening, and Emrys stood in his ink-stained apron feeling like a man summoned to repair a watch while three officials watched him do it.

Corvasser opened the box. Inside, on velvet, lay a medal Emrys did not recognize and a document bearing more seals than he could count at a glance.

"By order of the inquiry," Corvasser said, in the same dry voice he used for everything, "and confirmed by the Council at the capital: Emrys Dravenmoor is commended for service to the crown in exposing fraud against the state. He is granted the Freedom of the Regiment, a pension for life, and the thanks of the Council, to be entered in the permanent record." He paused, and something almost human crossed his face. "There is also talk of a commission. An official historian's post. The Council believes a man who can keep time honest might keep records honest too."

The room waited. The clock ticked — truly, evenly, each second the length every other second claimed to be. Emrys looked at it for a long moment before answering.

"May I ask a question first?" he said. "Who winds it?"

Corvasser blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The clock. When I'm gone — when I go back to my workshop, or take your commission, or accept your pension — who winds the master clock? Who checks it against the sun? Who reads the weights for tampering, the way someone should have done for twenty years?" He gestured at the case. "This machine lost four minutes a day for years and everyone called it weather. A boy could have caught it. No one looked, because looking was not in anyone's interest. Your medal doesn't fix that. Your commission doesn't fix that."

Ashworth shifted. "The observatory will be staffed—"

"It will be staffed by whoever the next commander finds convenient," Emrys said. "That's how it worked before. That's how it will work again, unless the record itself is protected from convenience."

He untied his apron, folded it, and laid it on the workbench. Then he turned to face them fully.

"I decline the medal, the pension, and the commission. Not from pride — I've earned very little pride in my life, and less lately. But I watched a man die in a chapel holding a letter that said *let it run*, and I think the least the regiment owes him is not a ceremony but a habit. So here is my price, and it costs you nothing: write into the regulation, where no commander can quietly amend it, that the master clock shall be inspected against solar time every month by an officer who does not report to the commander. Publish the readings. Post them where the soldiers can read them. And when I am dead and forgotten, some apprentice I've trained or someone I've never met will climb this mountain and wind this clock, and they will find the rule waiting for them, older than their commander, older than their memory of me. That is my honor. Give me that instead."

Silence. The clerk's pen had stopped moving.

Corvasser looked at Emrys for a long moment, and then — Emrys would remember this all his life — the dry little inspector smiled, briefly, like ice cracking.

"The Council offered you gold," he said, "and you've asked for a maintenance schedule." He snapped the box shut. "I think it's the better bargain. I'll draft it myself."

* * *

They buried Vane on the last day of autumn, in the regimental ground, with full honors and a headstone cut from valley granite: ALDOUS VANE, COLONEL, WHO KEPT FAITH. Six years of desertion erased by five words and a salute from two hundred men.

Pell stood beside Emrys at the graveside, thin and grey from his weeks in the cells, his decoy satchel replaced now by nothing at all — he had given away everything he owned in the service of the truth and owned, at the end, exactly what he'd started with.

"They'll want to make you a monument," Pell said, as the ranks marched past the grave. "Men like that always get monuments."

"No," Emrys said. "I got something better. A rule in a book."

Pell laughed — a rusty sound, as though the machinery of it needed oiling. "Your father would be proud. Or horrified. With your family it's hard to tell the difference."

Emrys smiled, but it faded as he looked up the mountain, to where the observatory dome caught the low sun. Somewhere up there, the clock was running, keeping honest time with no one to witness it. Tomorrow he would climb up and wind it. Next month, under the new regulation, an officer would come and check it against the sun, and write the reading in a public book, and the book would be boring and accurate forever, which was more than any monument could promise.

He stayed until the last of the mourners had gone, and then he walked back toward the workshop he had resented all his life for being small, and found, as he opened the door, a letter on the mat in a hand he did not recognize — a fine, formal hand, bearing the seal of the Council at the capital, and beneath the seal, in letters too deliberate to be anything but an invitation:

*The Council requests the attendance of Master Clockmaker Emrys Dravenmoor...*

Master Clockmaker. He turned the letter over twice before opening it, and outside the window the light was already changing, and somewhere above him the mountain waited with its one true clock.
