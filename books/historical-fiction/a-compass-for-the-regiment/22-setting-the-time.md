## 22. Setting the Time

The road up the mountain had been widened since the inquiry. Emrys noticed it first, before the pines, before the frost-bright meadows — the road itself, cut broader by order of the capital, so that wagons carrying instruments could reach the observatory without unloading and carrying each piece the last half mile on men's backs. A small thing. Most of what had changed was made of small things: a new lock on the observatory door with two keys instead of one, a printed schedule of windings posted in the entryway in official type, a brass plaque by the stair naming the clock *Regulator No. 1, Property of the Provincial Survey*, as though it had always belonged to everyone.

He stood a moment at the bottom of the stair with his case in his hand and let himself feel it: that he had come back, and that coming back was ordinary now. A year ago this climb had been the edge of his life, the place where the world's business happened to other people while he filed pinions in a workshop that smelled of oil and cold tea. Now the world's business had happened to him, and the strange discovery — the one he was still making, would probably keep making for years — was how much of it had been here all along, waiting inside the walls of rooms he had already walked through without reading them.

His father's tools were in the case. That had been his own decision, made slowly over the winter, and he still wasn't sure it was the right one. He could have left them in the shop drawer. He could have sold them. But a man who spent his days teaching apprentices to cut wheels had learned something about inheritance: you didn't get to choose which parts of it were yours. You only got to choose what you did with them.

He climbed.

* * *

Isolde Marchmont was waiting in the transit room, exactly where she'd said she would be, bent over her ephemeris with a pencil behind her ear and her coat still on, as if she'd come in from the meridian platform five minutes ago and might go out again at any moment. She looked up when he entered, and her face did the thing it always did — the quick assessment, the almost-smile held in reserve until she was certain of her facts — and then it softened into the real smile, the one she spent like currency because she knew its worth.

"You're late," she said.

"The coach was late."

"The coach is always late. You should have allowed for it. An astronomer allows for everything." She closed the book. "You've grown a beard."

"It grew itself. I didn't interfere."

"Master clockmaker," she said, trying the title aloud, weighing it. She had called him that twice before, once in a letter and once at Vane's memorial service, and both times she had watched him flinch slightly, and both times she had pretended not to notice. This time he didn't flinch, and she noticed that too. "It suits you better than it did."

"Everything suits me better than last winter did."

"No argument there." She took her coat off a peg and handed him the key — the second key, hers, the one that meant the winding was now a ceremony requiring two witnesses, per the Survey's regulations, drawn up in committee, signed in triplicate, immune to any single man's discretion. Emrys had read the regulations twice and laughed both times. They had been written in the language of bureaucracy, but they amounted to something older and simpler: no one watches the time alone. "He'd have hated it," Isolde said, following his thought as she so often did. "Vane. All those forms."

"He'd have loved what the forms are *for*."

"Yes," she admitted. "That too." She picked up the lantern. "Come and see your clock. It's been behaving, mostly, but it's developed an opinion about Tuesdays that I don't like."

* * *

The clock room smelled as it always had — machine oil, stone dust, the faint mineral breath of the mountain coming through the walls — but underneath it now there was something else, something Emrys could only call *attended*. For six years the great regulator had been slowed by a hidden hand, four minutes a day stolen from it so that hours marked in the dark could be disowned; for six years it had run dishonest and known it, if a machine can know anything, and Emrys had come to believe, in the year since he'd opened its case before the assembled regiment and stopped it dead at the hour of a living man's murder, that machines remember their treatment the way wood remembers weather. The clock had been honest for twelve months. It ran like something relieved.

He set down his case and unlocked the tall door of the case, and Isolde held the lantern high, and together they went through the ritual that was now written into the provincial regulations: check the escapement, check the suspension, read the barometer, compare the chronometer from the capital against the sidereal clock against the stars themselves, which waited outside in the cold with their opinions ready.

"Tuesdays," Isolde said, while he worked. "Four seconds slow by Wednesday morning, every week, then it recovers. I've checked the temperature record, the tension, the oil. Nothing."

Emrys put his ear to the case and listened. The beat came back to him, steady, patient, and somewhere in it — he wouldn't have heard it a year ago; a year ago he heard mechanisms whole, not in parts — somewhere in it was a hesitation so slight it might have been imagination. He straightened up and looked at the weights, and then at the wall beside the weights, where the mountain's masonry showed through the plaster, and he understood.

"It's not the clock," he said. "It's the building. Wednesday market day. The quarry blasting on the south face — they shifted the schedule in spring. The shock travels through the rock. The suspension feels it."

Isolde stared at him. Then she laughed, one short bark of genuine delight. "The *mountain* is interfering with my clock."

"The mountain has been here longer than either of us. We'll fit a compensation mount. It's a two-day job."

"A two-day job you talked me into letting you do, meaning you're staying the week."

"I hadn't got that far in the plan," Emrys said, "but yes."

She wrote it in the log in her precise hand — *irregularity traced to external shock; correction ordered* — and Emrys watched her write and thought about records. That was what the whole year had taught him, more than the workshop, more than the certificate, more even than the grief: that history was nothing but a very long logbook, and the only question that mattered about any entry was whether the hand that wrote it was telling the truth. His father had written false entries and known it. Vane had refused to sign false entries and died for it. Halewick had believed the logbook existed to serve whoever held the pen, and had gone to trial still arguing it, pale and immaculate, protesting to the end that he was the inheritor of a righteous lie — as if righteousness could be inherited at all, as if it were property rather than practice.

And Emrys himself — Emrys had spent twenty-two years believing he had no entries in the book at all. That was the lie he'd carried up the mountain the first time, heavier than any toolbox: that history happened elsewhere, in capitals, to colonels and commanders, while men like him merely kept the hours. He had learned otherwise in the worst possible classroom. History had been in his father's pen. In the marginal corrections on a forged charter. In the weight channel of a clock, wrapped in oilcloth, six years of letters from a man officially dead. History had been standing next to him at his bench since he was nine years old, handing him a gear blank and saying *if ever anyone asks, tell the time truly.*

He had told it truly. It had cost what it cost. His father's name was in the official report now, named honestly at last — first among the forgery's craftsmen, and also, three pages later, first among those whose evidence broke it. Both things were true. Emrys had insisted on both, and the inspector from the capital, a dry, careful man named Hollis, had looked at him over the documents and said, "You understand this dishonors him," and Emrys had said, "No, sir. It honors him *accurately*. He was both. So am I." Hollis had written it down. Emrys liked to think the sentence had survived into the final report. He had never checked. Some records you have to trust without reading.

"Where do you want to start?" Isolde asked. "The mount can wait for daylight."

"Now," Emrys said. "We do the winding now, and then the mount in the morning. Regulations."

"*Regulations*," she echoed, amused, but she took her position at the crank, and he took his, because the regulations required two, and because — though neither of them said it — the ceremony mattered more than the paper it was printed on. Winding the master clock alone had been Halewick's kind of power: one man, one key, one hand on the source of every hour in the valley. Two people turning the crank, checking each other's counts, initialing the same line — that was something else. That was the whole reform, the whole year, the entire meaning of everything they had done, compressed into a mechanism simple enough for a child to understand: *no one keeps the time alone.*

They wound it together. The weights rose, the drum turned, the great train below them gathered another week of falling ahead of it, and when it was done Isolde initialed the log and passed the pencil to Emrys, and he signed beneath her initials, *E. Dravenmoor, Master Clockmaker*, and looked at the words a moment longer than the task required.

* * *

Afterward they stood on the north platform, where the telescope lived under its tarpaulin and the valley fell away below them in the blue-dark, lamp-lit along the river road, one small town's worth of hours all hanging from the pendulum at their backs. Isolde pointed without pointing — the astronomer's habit, directing the eye by voice alone.

"There. The church clock. And the guildhall. And the regimental clock, new movement, they finally replaced it after the inquiry — did you hear? They commissioned it from a workshop in the capital." She paused, savoring. "Yours, apparently. Your apprentice told my assistant it's your design. Deadbeat escapement, mercury compensation. You've put a piece of yourself in every clock in that valley now."

"Not every one."

"Close enough." She pulled her coat tighter. Below them the town glimmered, and above them the stars did their ancient, unsentimental work. "Do you ever think about what he'd say? If he could see it?"

"Every day," Emrys said. "I hear him correcting my grammar."

"That sounds like him."

"He'd say we've made the clock honest but not the world, and that the second job takes longer, and that it falls to people who show up. He'd say it in fewer words. He was a soldier; he economized."

Isolde was quiet for a moment. Out on the meridian shed, the shutters ticked as the temperature fell. Then she said, not looking at him, in the flat careful voice she used for observations she'd verified three times before reporting:

"I've had a letter. From the survey office in the north. They're establishing a new observatory on the Fennmark coast — longitude work, the chronometer routes, the whole problem the capital's been failing at for a decade. They want someone to run the time service." She let that sit. "They asked me to name my own assistants."

Emrys looked at her. The valley light moved faintly on her face.

"You're asking me to leave my shop."

"I'm asking you to consider leaving your shop. There's a difference, and the difference is that I've considered it for both of us already and I'm prepared to argue." She turned to face him fully. "You repaired one dishonest clock, Emrys. There are more. There are always more. Every harbor, every mine office, every customs house with a commander who thinks the hours belong to him. Somebody has to build clocks nobody can quietly slow."

Below them the valley lay peaceful, trusting its hours to the pendulum at their backs. Emrys thought of his father's tools in the case at his feet — going with him, wherever he went, because inheritance traveled. He thought of a nine-year-old boy being taught to cut gears by a kind officer who was, even then, a dead man walking, and of the promise made in peacetime, and of how a promise, properly kept, doesn't close a debt but opens one, hand to hand, down the years.

He looked at Isolde, and found he was smiling.

"When would we leave?" he said.

She exhaled — he realized she had been holding the breath, this woman who allowed for everything. "Spring," she said. "When the coast road opens."

"Then I have all winter," Emrys said, "to teach my apprentice to keep the time truly. Come inside. It's nearly midnight, and this clock has an appointment with the truth."

They went in together, two keys, two witnesses, and Emrys Dravenmoor opened the case, took the minute hand gently in his fingers, and set the great clock to the true hour — and let it run.
