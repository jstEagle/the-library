## 2. Odile's Fingers

The typewriter had a stutter. It was an old Remington with a worn "e" that struck light, and Odile Marchand had learned its moods the way a sailor learns a tide — by failure, by repetition, by the small humiliations of a machine that would not be hurried. She sat at her corner of the workshop with her back to the sea-facing window, because Master Aldous had insisted that typists who faced the water produced sentimental work, and sentimental work, he said, was inaccurate work. So she faced the plaster wall, and the wall had a crack running through it shaped like a river delta, and she had spent four months of her life looking at that delta while her fingers did the work of a small, tireless engine.

Outside, the gulls were arguing over something in the harbor. Inside, the smell was ink and linseed oil and the particular cold-wax sweetness of copper plates, and beneath all of it the faint mineral breath of the sea that had gotten into the walls so long ago it no longer counted as an intrusion. It was the smell of the workshop, which was to say the smell of the only room in the town where Odile had ever felt entirely herself.

Cyrus Voss was at the far bench, inking the last plate. She could hear the small wet sound of the roller, the pause, the roller again. He had been at it since before dawn. She knew because she had arrived before dawn herself — she always did now, since Aldous died, since the work became a thing that had to be finished rather than merely made — and the workshop had been dark except for the single lamp over the inking bench, and Cyrus had been hunched beneath it like a man praying into his hands.

She had not yet told him. That was the thought that sat in her chest this morning, heavy as a stone in a pocket. Four months she had sat ten feet from him, passing him proofs and corrected galleys, watching his long fingers adjust the brass compass, listening to him talk about soundings and foreshortening and the way the coast lied about itself in winter. Four months, and he did not know what her fingers had actually been doing.

* * *

She had come to the workshop in the spring, answering a card pinned to the fish-market board: *Typist wanted. Neat hand essential. Discretion absolute.* She had expected labels. Chart titles, legend entries, the names of lighthouses — *Petrel Point, the Old Mole, Saint Aubin's Light* — set in small capitals for the engraver to trace. That was what she was paid for, and that was what she did, three days a week, in a neat hand that Aldous had inspected through his monocle and pronounced *adequate*, which from him was a parade.

The other work had begun in June.

She remembered the afternoon precisely, because it was the afternoon the light went strange — a greenish, submarine light that came before the summer storm — and Aldous had closed the shutters though it was barely four o'clock, and bolted them, and then stood with his back against them for a long moment as though expecting someone to try them.

"Miss Marchand," he said. "How is your memory."

She had thought it a strange question and said so.

"Your memory," he repeated. "Not your typing. Any typist can copy what is put in front of her. I am asking whether you can hold a thing in your head and let it leave no trace on the page."

"I can hold anything in my head," she said, and heard the stubbornness in her own voice and did not soften it. "It's the page I don't trust."

Something had moved in his face then — not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile would go, the way a coast chart shows the *shape* of a harbor without promising it is safe to enter. "Good," he said. "Because the page is exactly what I don't trust either."

He brought out a second ledger that day. Not the great coastal atlas with its plates and its careful soundings — that work lay open on the benches, half-finished, beautiful, a year from completion. This was a smaller book, bound in gray cardboard, the kind of ledger a grocer might use for accounts. And in it, in Aldous's own cramped hand, were names. Not lighthouses. Not points and shoals.

Names of families. Dates beside them. And in a column at the right, in a notation she did not at first understand, small figures that repeated — *4-11, 4-11, 7-3* — like coordinates.

"What is this?" she asked.

"This," Aldous said, "is the reason the atlas will be finished."

* * *

He explained it in pieces, over weeks, the way a careful man pours out something dangerous — a little at a time, testing the air between each measure. The army had begun compiling lists. She had seen the notices herself, the ones that used the phrase *administrative relocation* and gave no destination, the ones the harbor men read aloud to each other in voices that got quieter at the end. Families were being called in for *registration*, and some who registered did not come back to their houses, and their houses were sealed with lead seals, and the seals were stamped with a number, and the numbers went into ledgers, and the ledgers went into the colonel's office, and after that the families existed nowhere at all except in the memory of the people who had known them.

Aldous had friends in the registrar's office. Aldous had friends everywhere; that was what forty years of mapping a town did, it made you the man who knew where everything was, including the things people wanted hidden. He had copied the lists. And then he had done the thing that Odile did not fully understand until he put the atlas proof in front of her and said, *read me the margin*.

The margin of plate fourteen — the northern approaches, the channel soundings — carried the usual apparatus: depth figures, a compass rose, notes on the tidal streams. And threaded through the notes, in the abbreviations every chart used, were sequences of figures. *Dist. 4¼ cables. Brg. 112°. Lt. fl. 4-11.*

"It's a code," Odile said.

"It's a chart," Aldous said. "It is entirely a chart. Every figure in that margin is a true figure. The bearing is the true bearing to the light. The distance is the true distance. The flash characteristic is the true characteristic of the light. If a naval officer examined this plate, he would find it accurate to the last decimal, and he would pass it, because it is accurate." He tapped the margin with one blunt finger. "But *I* know that the fourth figure of every third sounding, read down the page, gives a date. And the bearing figures, taken in their sequence, index to a table — which exists only in my head and in yours, once I have taught it to you — and the table gives a name."

Odile had looked at the margin for a long time. The storm-light came through the shutter seams in thin green blades and lay across the paper like the bars of a cage.

"Whose name?" she asked, though she already knew.

"The Perrin family," Aldous said. "Who were taken in March. Who are, as of this morning, nowhere."

* * *

He taught her the table over six evenings, in the back room, with the shutters bolted, and she learned it the way she had learned the Remington's moods — by failure, by repetition, by the small humiliations of her own imperfect memory. The bearing figures indexed to a grid of syllables; the flash characteristics gave the order; the soundings, read in a pattern Aldous had invented and never written down, gave the dates. It was not unbreakable — she was honest enough with herself to know that a patient cryptographer with three plates and a year could worry it open — but it did not need to be unbreakable. It needed only to be invisible to a man who believed he was looking at a chart.

"You are the only typist," Aldous told her. "You are the only person besides myself who can produce these margins. That makes you the most dangerous person in this workshop, and I include myself, because I am an old man with a bad heart and everyone expects an old man to die of something. No one expects a typist."

"What do you expect me to do?" she had asked.

"I expect you to type," he said. "And to remember. And if the day comes when the plates are taken — and it will come, Miss Marchand, mark me, they will not let a coastal atlas of this accuracy leave this harbor in our hands — I expect you to be able to reproduce what was in these margins from your own head. The atlas can be reprinted anywhere in the world. The margins can be re-cut anywhere in the world. But only if the table survives. The table is you."

She had gone home that night along the harbor road with the wind pushing at her back, and she had felt, for the first time in her life, that she was not a small person in a small town. She was a table. She was a cipher that walked and bought bread and complained about the price of coal. She had stood at her window with her hands flat on the sill and felt the whole coast behind her — every light, every sounding, every name threaded invisibly through the numbers — and she had been afraid, and she had been, beneath the fear, something she did not yet have a word for.

* * *

Then Aldous died.

It was in October, and it was his heart, and it was — she would always believe this — partly the soldiers. They had come to the workshop twice that autumn, polite men in gray with clipboards, asking questions about the atlas, about its purpose, about who had commissioned it and where copies were intended to go. Aldous had answered them with the serene, infuriating literalism of an old craftsman: it was a coastal atlas; it was accurate; accuracy was its purpose; no one had commissioned it; the sea had. They left unsatisfied both times, and the second time Aldous had sat down heavily on his stool afterward and pressed his hand flat against his chest, and Odile had brought him his drops, and he had said, *they'll be back, and next time they won't ask*.

He was right. But he died eleven days before they came back, in the night, at his desk, with plate twenty-two — the final plate, the harbor itself — half-inked on the bench before him. Cyrus had found him in the morning.

She remembered the sound Cyrus made. Not a cry. A sound like a man setting down something heavier than he had realized he was carrying. She had been at her typewriter, already working — she worked now on the plates alone, keeping the table alive in her fingers, cutting the coded figures into the margins of the final sheets — and she had heard that sound from the back room and had known, before she saw, exactly what it meant.

They had buried him in the churchyard above the harbor, and the soldiers had not come to the funeral, and Odile had stood at the graveside with the table in her head and thought: *now I am the only one. Now if I die, the names die with me, and no one will ever know there was a code at all, and the atlas will be printed clean, and the town will be erased twice — once by the army, and once by the printer's silence.*

* * *

Which brought her to this morning, and to Cyrus, and to the stone in her chest.

He was at the inking bench, and the roller made its small wet sound, and she watched the back of his bent head and tried to calculate him the way she calculated a margin: what was the true figure here, what was the flash characteristic, what would he do when she told him. She had watched him for four months and she knew some things. He was careful. He grieved the way he worked — methodically, in layers, without display. He loved the master more than he knew how to say, which she could tell because he said nothing at all about him, only worked, only finished, only measured the world in what could be carried. He was, she suspected, exactly the kind of man who would do the right thing and be terrible at doing it quietly.

That was the danger. That was why she had waited.

But the waiting had a cost, and the cost was this: she was alone with the table, and alone was a word that had begun to feel like *dead*. If the soldiers came for her — and she had seen the registrar's clerk looking at her on the quay last week, that particular flat look of a man checking a face against a list — the code died with her, and Aldous's four months of her life died with it, and three hundred names stayed nowhere forever.

She could not carry it alone anymore. That was the plain arithmetic of it. And she had learned arithmetic from Aldous, and Aldous had taught her that arithmetic was a form of honesty.

"Cyrus," she said.

The roller stopped. He looked up, and in the gray morning light from the window his face was the face of a man surfacing from deep water — a moment of not knowing where he was, then the workshop assembling itself around him, then her.

"Miss Marchand."

"Odile," she said. "If we're going to do this, you should call me Odile."

"Do what?" he said. But he had set down the roller, and wiped his hands on the rag, and turned his whole body toward her, and she understood that he had been waiting too — that two people in a room with a secret develop a pressure between them the way a sealed flask develops pressure, invisibly, and that he had felt it for weeks without knowing what it was.

She rose from the Remington. Her legs were unsteady; she noted this with a typist's detachment, the way she noted a worn "e." She went to the flat file drawer where the finished plates were stored, and she drew out plate fourteen, the northern approaches, and she laid it on the bench between them, and she turned the lamp on it.

"Read me the margin," she said. "The soundings. Every third one, the fourth figure, down the page."

He bent over the plate. She watched his lips move slightly, the cartographer's habit of sounding figures aloud, and she watched the movement slow, and stop.

"Four," he said. "Seven. One. One." He looked up. "That's not a sequence of soundings. Soundings don't — " He stopped again. She could see the reordering happening behind his eyes, the trained mind refusing the trained pattern, reaching for the other thing the figures could be. "Four-seven-one-one. April, seventh, nineteen eleven. That's a — "

"Date," Odile said. "Now the bearings. In sequence. One-one-two, zero-nine-eight, one-two-one."

He read them. His face had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing on ice and listening to it speak. "Those aren't — the bearing to Petrel Point is one-one-two, that's true, but the next one, the light doesn't bear zero-nine-eight from the sounding station, it bears — "

"From a different point," Odile said. "From a point that doesn't exist on this chart. The bearings are true from the old signal station that burned down in 'ninety-eight. Aldous chose it because no living officer would think to check a bearing against a station that isn't there." She took a breath. "One-one-two is syllable *mar*. Zero-nine-eight is syllable *ceau*. One-two-one is syllable *laise*. Marceau. The Marceau family. Taken the fourth of November, last year. Five people."

The workshop was very quiet. The gulls had stopped. Even the sea, for the moment, seemed to be listening.

Cyrus straightened up slowly. He looked at the plate, and then at her, and she watched him arrive — watched him pass through confusion and disbelief and the brief hot anger of a man who has been kept outside something, and come out the other side into a cold, steady clarity that she recognized because she had felt it arrive in herself, in June, in the green storm-light.

"How many," he said.

"Three hundred and eleven names so far. In the margins of the finished plates, and in the plates not yet cut, and in here." She touched her temple. "The table is here. It has never been written down. Aldous said the day it was written down would be the day it was lost, so it lives in two heads only, and one of them is in the churchyard now."

"Three hundred and eleven," Cyrus said. He said it the way he said soundings — flatly, precisely, giving the figure its full weight. Then: "The army is going to seize this workshop. You know that. Everyone on the quay knows it; the troop ships were sighted off the point two nights ago. When they come, they will take every plate in this drawer, and they will take the atlas, and if they ever decode the margins — "

"They won't decode the margins," Odile said. "They'll burn them. That's the difference. A thing that is understood gets studied. A thing that is merely strange gets burned." She heard the bitterness in her voice and let it stand. "Aldous understood that. He built the code so that its discovery would be its destruction. But the names — the names have to survive somewhere they can be *read*. Not hidden. Read. In a book the world will actually open."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the workshop's easy professional shorthand, the voice of a man talking across a distance rather than across a bench.

"Why me? You could have carried this alone. You could have walked out of this town with it in your head and told no one. Telling me makes you — " he searched for it — "it makes you less safe. It makes the whole thing less safe. One more person who can be taken, and questioned, and — "

"Because I watched you finish his plate," Odile said. "The last one. The harbor plate. You stayed up three nights and you matched his hand so exactly that when I proofed it I wept, and I have not wept in front of anyone since I was nine years old. You didn't do it for glory. There's no glory in a harbor chart. You did it because the work mattered more than the worker, and that is the only religion Aldous ever had, and I needed to know, before I told anyone, whether anyone else in this town practiced it." She looked down at the plate, at the small true figures marching in their rows, each one carrying a family inside it like a seed inside a seed. "So. Now you know what my fingers have been doing all these months. Now you know that every chart you've inked since June has been carrying the dead in its margins. And you can tell me to leave, and take the atlas, and finish it clean, and I will walk out that door and never say a word. Or you can tell me to stay."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere out in the harbor a bell buoy rang, once, the way it did when the tide turned.

Cyrus looked at the plate for a long time. Then he did something she had not expected: he took the brass compass from his waistcoat pocket — Aldous's compass, the one she had seen him handle like a relic — and set it down on the bench beside the margin, so that it lay pointing, by chance or by some settled habit of the needle, toward the sea.

"Aldous told me once," he said, "that a map is a promise you make to strangers. That everyone who draws one is promising that the world is knowable, and that knowing it is worth something." His thumb moved along the compass's worn edge. "I thought he meant the coast. The soundings, the lights. I've been inking a promise for four months and I didn't know what I was promising." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Stay. Teach me the table. And then help me decide what we do, because if those troop ships are what I think they are, we don't have much time."

Odile let out a breath she felt she had been holding since October. It shook slightly on the way out. *Trust*, she thought, *is not typed in a single keystroke. It is typed the way the margins were built — figure by figure, true figure by true figure, until one day the whole page reads*.

"All right," she said. "Sit down. It will take six evenings, and you will fail at it, and I will correct you, and by the end you will be the second most dangerous person in this workshop."

The ghost of a smile crossed his face — the same ghost, she realized, that had crossed Aldous's, and she understood that it had not died with the old man but had simply changed hands, the way a trade changes hands, the way a light is transferred from one keeper to the next with the same characteristic, the same rhythm, the same flash.

She had just begun to recite the first syllable-grid when the door of the workshop opened without a knock, and the cold came in first, and then the gray coats, and the sound of boots on the floorboards — many boots, in step, in a rhythm that no craftsman in this town had ever used.

Odile's hand moved, without her deciding it, and closed the plate drawer.
