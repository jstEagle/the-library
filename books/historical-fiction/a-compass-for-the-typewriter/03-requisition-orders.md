## 3. Requisition Orders

Cyrus heard the boots before he saw them — that was the first wrongness of the day, the sound arriving out of order, like thunder before lightning. Boots in this town had always come singly or in pairs, arrhythmic, apologetic, the shuffling gait of fishermen and clerks and children late for school. These boots came in fours and eights and sixteens, struck to a count no one had taught them, and they came up Harbor Street from the direction of the customs house with the confidence of something that did not intend to stop.

He was at the workshop window with a rag in his hand, cleaning the last flecks of dried ink from the frame of plate forty-one, when the first soldier drove the nail.

The hammer blows carried all the way across the square. Three strikes, unhurried, and then the man stepped back to look at his work, and Cyrus saw what he had posted: a single sheet of paper, larger than any notice the town had ever needed, squared to the doorpost of the chandlery with the precision of a man who understood that documents were also maps, that paper could claim territory as surely as any line inked on vellum.

Cyrus's hands went still on the rag. The ink fleck stayed where it was, caught in the grain of the wood, a small black star that would never be wiped away now.

* * *

By noon there were eleven of them, one on every public surface the town possessed: the chandlery, the fish market gate, the ferry office, the churchyard wall, the post of the old signal tower where Aldous had once climbed, years ago, to sight a headland for the survey of the northern coast. Eleven sheets of heavy official stock, printed not written — printed, which meant the orders had been composed somewhere far away, in an office with other offices above it, by men who had never smelled this harbor and never would. The requisition of Saltmere had been typeset before anyone here had even heard it contemplated.

Cyrus stood before the notice on the chandlery wall and read it three times because the words refused to assemble into sense on the first two passes.

*BY ORDER OF THE IMPERIAL MILITARY ADMINISTRATION, the town of Saltmere and its harbor, wharves, dwellings, workshops, warehouses, places of worship, and appurtenant lands are hereby claimed for the purposes of the war effort. All residents are required to present themselves for registration within six (6) days at the customs house, bearing papers of identity and inventory of personal effects. Property deemed of military value will be retained. Property deemed otherwise will be forfeit. Persons failing to register will be subject to disposition.*

*Disposition.* The word sat at the end of the sentence like a stone at the bottom of a well. You could drop a pebble down past it forever and never hear it land.

Around him the square had gone quiet in a way Cyrus had never heard a town go quiet. Not the hush of evening, which has breath in it, movement, the settling of households — but the silence of people reading the same terrible arithmetic at the same time and arriving at the same sum. Six days. An old woman beside him read the notice aloud, slowly, her finger under each line as though the finger might steady the meaning, and when she reached *disposition* she stopped, and lowered her hand, and said to no one, "I was born in the house behind you."

A soldier at the corner watched them read. He was young, and he kept his rifle slung rather than shouldered, and he looked — Cyrus noted it with a strange, unwelcome pang — embarrassed. As though he too could read, and knew what the word meant, and wished the paper had been written in some language he could pretend not to know.

* * *

They came for the workshop at four o'clock.

Cyrus heard the boots again, the same metronome tread, and this time he was inside when they arrived, and Odile was at her desk with the typewriter, and between them, on the bench where Aldous had worked for thirty-one years, lay the final copper plate, still faintly warm from the polishing cloth, its coastline complete at last from the river mouth to the southern light.

"Stand away from the equipment," said the officer.

There were four soldiers and one officer, and the officer was gray the way winter water is gray, with a face that had been organized around a set of instructions and had never found cause to deviate. He wore the insignia of a captain. His eyes went around the workshop the way a creditor's eyes go around a debtor's house — measuring, valuing, dismissing.

"You are the apprentice," he said. It was not a question. "Voss."

"Yes."

"Where is Aldous?"

"Dead." The word came out of Cyrus flat and strange, a coin worn smooth from handling. He had said it perhaps ten times since the funeral and it had not yet begun to feel like a fact. "Eleven weeks. His heart."

The captain made a small mark on a clipboard. Somewhere in the empire's vast ledgers, Aldous Renner had just been reduced to a tick. Cyrus felt something twist in him at that — not grief exactly, grief had become a country he lived in now — but a kind of indignation on behalf of the dead man's whole enormous life, compressed into a stroke of a clerk's pen.

"The workshop is requisitioned under today's order," the captain said. "You have until the sixth day to remove personal effects. After that everything remaining is property of the administration." His gaze moved across the room and Cyrus tracked it the way one tracks weather, itemizing what it touched: the press. The type cases. The racks of plates, forty-one of them, each one a year of Aldous's patience given coastline. The drafting tables with their green lamps. The drawers of pens and burnishers and the little brass weights Aldous had used to hold down curling paper, each stamped with the maker's mark, each polished by thirty years of hands.

Every object in the room had weighed what it weighed yesterday. Now each one weighed something else besides. The press weighed the labor of hauling it. The plates weighed the atlas entire, weighed whether it would exist. The lamp weighed oil. The chairs weighed whether anyone would ever sit in them again. Home had become inventory, and inventory was a kind of violence — it made a man see his own life laid out in columns, assessed, depreciated, *forfeit*.

"The typist remains?" the captain asked, and his eyes found Odile, who had not turned from her machine.

"She does."

"She is on the employment register. She may finish contracted work through the sixth day. Nothing after." A pause, thin as onionskin. "What work remains?"

"Labels," Cyrus said. "Place names. Marginal notes." The lie was small and smooth and it slid out of him with a fluency that frightened him slightly; he had not known until that moment that he could lie to a uniformed man without his voice changing pitch.

The captain nodded, made another mark. Then he moved into the workshop proper, and the four soldiers moved with him, and for twenty minutes Cyrus stood at his own doorway watching strangers handle his master's tools.

It was worse than theft. Thieves take quickly and shamefacedly; these men took slowly and in order. One lifted each plate rack down and called out a count. Another opened the flat files and ran his thumb along the drafted coastlines with something almost like appreciation, and Cyrus wanted to strike the hand away — those lines were Aldous's sight-lines, Aldous's tide-tables corrected against thirty-one winters of observation, Aldous's patient argument with the sea itself — but he stood still, because standing still was the only move that kept everything.

At the back cabinet the captain paused. This was the drawer where the proofs were kept — working drafts, misprints, the failed attempts every map accrues like scar tissue. He pulled two folders out, glanced through them, and set them on the bench with a curt gesture. "These stay. The rest of the archive goes to the cartographic section tomorrow."

"Yes, Captain."

"You have skill," the captain said suddenly, turning. It was the first time he had addressed Cyrus directly rather than through the clipboard. His eyes were pale and level and entirely without warmth, but they were not without attention, and attention, Cyrus was learning, could be more frightening than cruelty. "The drafts I've seen from this shop are better than half of what our own section produces. That is a compliment. Take it as one."

"Thank you, Captain."

"Six days." He tucked the clipboard under his arm and left, and the boots went down the stairs in their terrible rhythm, and the cold came in behind them and stayed.

* * *

Odile did not speak until the boots had faded all the way to the harbor. Then she rose from the typewriter, crossed the room, and stood over the two folders the captain had ordered left behind.

"Why these?" she asked quietly. "Out of all of it — why leave these?"

Cyrus came and looked. The top folder held misprints he barely remembered: a smudged impression of the eastern cliffs, a sheet where the ink had ghosted double. But beneath it, in Aldous's own cramped hand on the folder's cover, was a label Cyrus had never noticed, or had noticed and never read properly: *Working margins — O.M.*

O.M. Odile Marchand.

"I don't know," he said. And then, because the day had already broken so many things that one more fracture hardly seemed to matter: "I think I do know. Don't I? There's something in the margins you've been typing that isn't labels."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Outside, a patrol went past the window, two soldiers this time, unhurried, their shadows crossing the glass like the hands of a clock. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and very even, the voice of someone who had rehearsed a confession for weeks and was only waiting for the world to provide the occasion.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight we move what can't be replaced. Tomorrow I'll show you. But Cyrus—" and here her evenness slipped, just once, like a plate shifting on a shelf, "—the ledger isn't in this building. It hasn't been in this building since before the captain ever came. If they'd found it today, we'd already be in the yard with our hands tied."

"The ledger," Cyrus repeated. He had seen her typing for months, hundreds of hours of it, grids of letters and numbers that he had assumed were Aldous's eccentric indexing system, some private shorthand for soundings or seamarks. *The ledger.* As if it were one thing, whole, singular, alive.

"Under the floorboards in my flat," Odile said. "Where I sleep on top of it every night." She almost smiled. It was not a happy expression. "Aldous insisted. He said the safest place for a dangerous document is directly beneath the body of the person willing to die for it."

* * *

They worked until dark, and then by lamplight, and the lamplight made the workshop into a place Cyrus did not recognize — every shadow doubled, every familiar silhouette altered, as though the room itself had begun the process of being erased ahead of schedule. They wrapped the forty-one plates in sailcloth and lowered them, one by one, into the old brine cistern beneath the floor, where the salt water would keep the copper bright and where no inventory clerk would think to look, because a cistern looks like a cistern. Cyrus did this work with his sleeves rolled and his hands in the cold brine, and with each plate he let down into the dark he felt the same thought pass through him, quiet and recurring as tide:

*Aldous began this atlas the year I was born. He measured every mile of this coast knowing it might someday be taken. What did he know that we are only learning now?*

When the last plate was submerged and the boards replaced and the sawdust scattered, Odile banked the stove and put on her coat. At the door she stopped.

"Six days," she said.

"Six days."

"They'll want the names registered. Everyone's name, everyone's family, everyone's trade — do you understand what a census is, Cyrus? It's a map of people. It's the atlas they're making of us." She pulled her collar up. In the lamplight her face was pale and absolutely resolute. "And a map can be used to find things. Or to make things disappear."

She went out into the blue dark, and Cyrus stood alone in the workshop that was no longer his, among the empty racks and the cold press and the small black star of ink dried forever into the window frame, and listened to the harbor breathing below.

Somewhere out in the streets, chalk marks were already appearing on doors — he had seen the first one that afternoon, a cipher in white on the baker's lintel, meaning nothing yet, meaning everything soon. Somewhere in a flat above the chandlery, beneath worn floorboards, three hundred names lay stacked in the dark, waiting. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, though no one in Saltmere could have said why they were certain of it, mail steamers still sailed on the morning tide.

Six days. Cyrus blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the room weighed almost nothing at all.
