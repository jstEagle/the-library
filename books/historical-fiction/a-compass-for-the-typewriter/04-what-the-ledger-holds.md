## 4. What the Ledger Holds

The floorboard came up easier than Odile had expected, as though the wood itself had been waiting years to be lifted.

Cyrus knelt beside her in the flat above the workshop, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, candlelight trembling along the blade of the pry bar he'd wedged beneath the second board from the window — *the one that creaks differently*, Aldous had told her once, in that offhand way he delivered everything important, as if it were a remark about weather. She had thought then that he meant the board was loose. She understood now that he had meant exactly this.

Beneath it lay an oilcloth packet tied with waxed string, and inside the oilcloth a book bound in plain gray boards with no title on its spine. It did not look like anything at all. That was the genius of it. A ledger that looked like nothing could sit under a floor for years while soldiers walked overhead, and the boards would keep their secret the way the sea keeps what sinks into it.

"Should we—" Cyrus began.

"Yes," she said, because her voice would fail if she waited.

They carried it down the back stairs to the workshop, where the shutters were drawn and the stove still held a little warmth from the morning, and they set it on the drafting table between the ink pots and the half-finished plate of the northern headland. The candle flame stood very straight in the dead air. Odile found herself unable to touch the cover. Her hands hovered over the gray boards like a woman warming herself at a fire she is afraid will go out.

"You open it," she said. "You were his hands. Let them be the first."

Cyrus's throat moved. He untied the waxed string with the same care he used when cleaning the brass compass, and he opened the ledger the way one opens a door into a room where someone has died.

* * *

It was not a beautiful book. That surprised her first — she had imagined, without knowing it, something illuminated, something worthy, gold leaf and careful capitals. But Master Aldous had been a practical man, and grief had made him efficient. The pages were ruled in faint blue lines. At the top of each entry, a name. Below the name, in Aldous's small, upright hand: a date. Below the date, a single line of description — trade, age, street — and then, in a column at the right margin, a code of letters and numerals that made no sense until Odile's eye caught the pattern and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

She knew that code. She had typed it herself, letter by letter, into the margins of forty-two atlas plates, believing until this moment that she was transcribing tide tables or soundings or some private shorthand of her master's cartography. *A-7, K-2, R-11.* She had thought it was coordinates. She had sat at the typewriter for eleven months with her fingers dancing over the keys and never once known that she was typing people.

"He didn't tell me," she said. Her voice sounded far away, as if someone else in another room were speaking. "He never told me what I was typing."

"He couldn't." Cyrus's hand rested on the page, not reading yet, just holding it flat. "If you'd known, and they'd taken you—"

"They would have gotten it out of me." She said it plainly because there was no other way to say it. "Yes. I know. I would have told them everything by morning. I'm not brave, Cyrus. I type well and I mind my own affairs. That's all I am."

"That's not all you are." He said it quietly, without emphasis, which somehow made it worse. Then he turned the first page toward the candle. "Look."

They read together, shoulder to shoulder, the way two children read a frightening book, each taking comfort from the other's nearness without acknowledging it.

*Vasseur, Émile. Fisherman, 51, Quai des Salines. Taken 14 March. Destination: unknown.*

*Bellamy, Marguerite. Seamstress, 34, Rue du Phare. Taken 14 March. Destination: unknown.*

*Vasseur, Luc. Son of the above. Age 9.*

Odile stopped breathing at the third entry. Nine years old. There was a boy of nine who had come into the workshop the previous autumn to watch Cyrus grind pigments, who had held up his ink-blackened fingers like trophies, and now here he was reduced to nine words in a dead man's hand, *son of the above*, as though he were an attachment, a footnote, a thing appended to a thing.

They turned pages. The entries accumulated. March became April became May, and the column of *destination: unknown* lengthened until Aldous had begun abbreviating it — *d.u.*, then finally just a dash, as if even the words had become too heavy to write out three hundred times.

Three hundred names. Odile counted them in batches of ten as they read, because counting was something her hands knew how to do, because arithmetic was a rope thrown to a drowning woman. Twenty. Fifty. Ninety. Some entries had notes in the margin — *wife escaped north? verify*, or *brother saw him loaded onto the transport, no papers issued* — small desperate investigations conducted by a mapmaker who had never been trained for any of this and had done it anyway, alone, for years, walking the town after every disappearance with his notebook, asking questions of neighbors who were too frightened to answer, writing down what he could prove and marking what he could not.

"He was building a case," Cyrus said at last. His voice was rough. "Like a lawyer. Like—no. Not like a lawyer. Like a witness. He was building testimony."

"And I helped him type it into the atlas," Odile said, "without knowing. All those nights. I thought my fingers were tired from labels." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I used to complain about the marginal codes. I told him they slowed me down. I asked him why we couldn't simply print the atlas without them and be done by spring."

"What did he say?"

"He said—" She closed her eyes. The memory arrived whole, merciless. *"Everything essential travels in the margins, mademoiselle. The coast is only what they let us see."*

* * *

The candle burned down past its first quarter before either of them spoke again. They had developed a rhythm without discussing it: Cyrus turned the pages; Odile read the names aloud, softly, all of them, even the ones whose spelling was uncertain, even the ones Aldous had marked with a question mark as though the name itself might dissolve. Reading aloud was her idea. She could not have defended it logically. But somewhere around the hundredth name she understood that silence was doing something terrible to the book — silence was letting it be paperwork, letting it be inventory — and voice was the only solvent she had. Spoken aloud, each name took up air again. Each name had weight and duration. Each name, for the four seconds it lasted in the workshop, existed.

*Cormier, Henrietta. Laundress, 62.*

*Duras, Paul. Ferryman's mate, 27.*

*Okonkwo, Adaeze. Trader, fabrics, 44, Harbor Road.*

The town unfolded through the names the way it unfolded through Aldous's maps — the fish market, the lighthouse street, the salt quays, the hill road — except that these streets were populated by absences. Odile had lived here nineteen years and had believed she knew everyone, or nearly; now she discovered she had known almost no one, that behind every shuttered window Aldous had catalogued a family being dismantled piece by piece, a father gone in March, a daughter gone in June, a grandmother gone in August, the household shrinking entry by entry like a coastline eroding under a storm tide.

Then she turned a page and the candle seemed to gutter, though afterward she could not swear that the flame had moved at all.

*Marchand, Sylvie. Midwife, 49, Rue des Trois Cyprès. Taken 3 June. Destination: unknown.*

Below it, in fresher ink, added later than most of the surrounding entries:

*Marchand, Odile. Daughter of the above. Typist, 19. Status: present. WATCH.*

Her own name. Her mother's name. And beneath her own name, in her master's careful hand, the word *watch* underlined twice.

The workshop went very quiet. Somewhere below the floor, the harbor water slapped against pilings, patient as a clock.

"She was a midwife," Odile said, because saying something true and small was the only way to keep from screaming. "Forty-nine babies in the parish register, Cyrus. I counted them once when I was a girl. Forty-nine. She kept a list of them in the back of her Bible with their birthweights written next to their names, because she said every life should begin with a number so it could grow out of it." Her voice was steady. That was the horrifying part. She could hear how steady it was, hear the bureaucratic calm settling over her own mouth, and she understood in that instant precisely how unbearable this book was — not because it was cruel, but because it was orderly. Grief with dates attached. Sorrow in alphabetical columns. Her mother, who had caught her at her first breath, rendered as a line item: *midwife, 49, taken, destination unknown.* The state had done its arithmetic and moved on.

Cyrus reached across the table and put his hand over hers where it lay on the page. His palm was warm and ink-stained and shaking slightly, and she realized he was not comforting her so much as anchoring himself, that they were both holding on to the table to keep from being swept off the edge of the map.

"I watched them take her," Odile said. "From the upstairs window. Three men in a grey motorcar, and she went down the steps with her bag — her medical bag, she brought her *bag*, as though someone in wherever they were going might need delivering — and she looked up at the window and she smiled at me. She smiled, Cyrus. She thought it would be temporary. Everyone thinks it will be temporary. That's the design of it. That's why they don't announce anything. You stand in your doorway watching the motorcar and you think, they'll sort it out, there's been a mistake, and meanwhile the car is already gone and your mother's smile is the last thing you have of her and you can't even hate anyone properly because no one ever told you it was happening."

She stopped. Her breath was coming fast and shallow. Cyrus's hand tightened over hers.

"Aldous knew," she whispered. "*Status: present. Watch.* He wrote my name in this book because he knew they'd come for me too, someday, and he wanted—"

"He wanted whoever found this to know you'd existed," Cyrus said. "Even if the town didn't."

That was the sentence that broke her. Not the ledger, not her mother's name, not the word *watch* — but this quiet young man stating her death certificate's purpose as calmly as he'd state a bearing, and meaning it as a kindness. Odile put her face in her free hand and wept, silently, thoroughly, the way she hadn't wept at the window in June because crying at the window felt like giving the grey motorcar something to take. Here, underground of the world, in a room smelling of ink and tallow, she wept for her mother and for forty-nine babies and for the nine-year-old boy with blackened fingers and for the whole ruled, numbered, dated town that had been disappearing one motorcar at a time while she typed labels for maps.

Cyrus did not tell her it would be all right. He was too honest for that, and she loved him a little for the honesty, in the sideways way one loves a person across the top of a shared catastrophe. He simply stayed, hand over hers, turning pages when she could not, until the weeping burned itself down to embers.

* * *

Later — much later; the candle was a thumb's width from drowning in its own wax — she wiped her eyes with her sleeve and looked at the ledger with new sight.

"Cyrus. How many copies of the atlas exist?"

"Two finished. The master copy and the presentation folio. Plus the plates — forty-two of them, zinc, boxed in the storeroom. Why?"

"Because the plates are going to be seized tomorrow with everything else. The requisition notice says *all property of cartographic value*. An atlas is cartographic value. A ledger is evidence." She laid her hand flat on the gray boards. "But this — this is just an old account book to anyone who doesn't know the code. Anyone who doesn't know what I spent eleven months typing into those margins."

Cyrus went very still. She watched the understanding arrive in his face the way dawn arrives on water — slowly, then everywhere at once.

"The atlas," he said. "The names are already in the atlas."

"Encoded, yes. In forty-two plates, distributed across thousands of miles of printed coastline. If the original burns, the names survive in the printing — but only if someone outside the country knows the code exists, and only if someone can decode it, and only if the atlas gets published abroad instead of confiscated with the rest." She heard her own voice quickening, taking on the cadence of her mother listing birthweights, one fact stacked on another until the stack could bear weight. "Aldous hid the proof inside the thing they'd never destroy. Maps are how governments brag, Cyrus. They'll reprint our coasts in every language. They'll carry the town's names into every capital on earth and never know they're carrying its people."

"But the code," Cyrus said. "The key. If it dies with us—"

"Then it dies with us. Which means we don't die with it." She stood. Her legs ached from kneeling; she barely felt it. On the wall behind the drafting table hung Aldous's framed chart of the harbor, the first map she'd ever seen him draw, and she looked at it now not as decoration but as cargo. "We have six days before the requisition takes full effect. The mail steamer sails on the morning tide after that. Someone has to get this book onto that ship, and someone has to make sure the world knows what the margins hold."

"It can't be both of us carrying it openly," Cyrus said. "The pier will be watched. It's already being watched."

"Then we'll find the man who knows how it isn't." She blew out the candle. In the sudden dark, the ledger lay between them like a sleeping animal, breathing its three hundred breaths. "My mother delivered half this town into the world, Cyrus. I am not going to let it be erased out of it. Whatever it costs."

In the darkness she heard him exhale, long and slow, the sound of a decision being made by two people at once.

At dawn, Cyrus went looking for Bram the smuggler — and found soldiers posting notices on the harbor gates.
