## 5. Three Checkpoints

Bram arrived the way weather arrived — without announcement, without apology, and with a certainty that made argument pointless.

Cyrus heard him on the stairs of the flat above the chandlery before he saw him: a heavy, unhurried tread, each step placed like a man who had learned long ago that hurrying drew eyes. Odile had already blown out two of their three candles. The ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose floorboard, and Cyrus stood over it as though his body alone might be argument enough against whatever came through that door.

Then the door opened without a knock, and there was Bram Vasseur in the doorway, filling it entirely — a barrel-chested man with a face like a dock piling, salt-cured and split by years of weather, one eyebrow permanently raised as though the world had been disappointing him since birth. He carried a sack that clinked faintly, and he smelled of tar, tobacco, and the particular cold of open water at night.

"You're the mapmaker's boy," Bram said. It was not a question. "Voss. And she's the typist." He looked past Cyrus to where Odile sat very still in the shadow by the window, hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral, which in a sense she was. "Aldous sent me word before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Before the end."

"Sent you word," Cyrus repeated. His voice came out thinner than he intended.

"That if anything happened to him, I'd be paid in fish." Bram set the sack down on the table between them, and from it drew a bottle of something amber, three mismatched cups, and a folded square of paper gone soft at the creases. "Which is how smugglers are paid for anything worth doing. Sit down, boy. You look like a candle about to go out."

"I don't drink."

"More for me, then." He poured one cup anyway, pushed it toward Odile — who took it, which surprised Cyrus — and unfolded the paper flat on the table, smoothing it twice with a palm broad enough to press laundry.

* * *

The paper was a chart. Not of the harbor — Cyrus knew the harbor better than he knew his own handwriting — but of the town itself, drawn in the quick shorthand of a man who thought in routes rather than coastlines. Three marks in red grease pencil, spaced along the mile and a half between Marta's street and the pier: the fish market gate, the customs house, the pier itself.

"Three checkpoints," Bram said, tapping each mark in turn with a blunt finger. "Between your door and that steamer. Everything else is walking. These three are where they can stop you, open what you carry, and end this story before it's told."

Cyrus pulled a chair close and leaned over the chart, and the cartographer in him woke despite everything, hungry for lines and distances the way a starving man is hungry for bread. "When does the steamer sail?"

"Morning tide. Six-forty. She's the *Corinne*, mail and passengers, neutral registry, and they'll not hold her because holding mail ships makes diplomatic noise, and noise is the one thing your captain out there hates more than smugglers." Bram took a pull from his cup. "But they'll search what boards her. They've done it every sailing since the requisition. Papers first, then cargo, then — lately — faces."

"Faces?"

"They've started carrying sketches. A man and a woman, young, seen leaving the workshop district after dark. The description could be half the town, but descriptions have a way of sharpening when someone's frightened." He glanced at Odile. "No offense meant, mademoiselle."

"None taken," Odile said quietly. Her cup sat untouched in her hands, warming them. "Go on."

"The fish market gate opens at four. Dawn catch comes in, ice goes out, crates move all morning — which means crates get waved through, because a soldier who inspects every fish crate will still be standing there at noon while the harbor rots behind him." Bram's finger moved to the second mark. "Customs house is the hard one. Proper officers, proper procedure, a man named Delacroix who takes pride in his work the way you take pride in yours, boy — which means he cannot be rushed and should not be insulted. Then the pier itself, last gate, gangplank, and a sergeant with a list of names and now, apparently, a list of faces."

"And between them?" Cyrus asked. "Distances."

Something flickered across Bram's heavy face — approval, maybe, or recognition. Smugglers and mapmakers, Cyrus thought, were both men who converted fear into arithmetic.

"Fish market to customs house: six hundred meters, call it eight minutes loaded, ten if you're being careful, fifteen if you're being stupid and running. Customs house to pier: nine hundred along the quay, twelve minutes, but add five for the crowd that gathers when the steamer's in, because crowds move at the speed of the slowest grandmother." He tapped the third mark. "Total, door to gangplank, forty minutes if nothing goes wrong. Plan for an hour. Plan for two. A plan that only works if everything goes right isn't a plan, it's a prayer."

Odile set down her cup. "And if we're stopped at any one of them?"

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him answer plainly, without the rough kindness he'd used on Cyrus. "If they find what you're carrying, you'll be arrested, the paper will be burned in front of you, and you'll likely be put on a train going the other way. That's the good version. I won't describe the other one, because you've got enough nightmares and I charge extra for mine."

* * *

They worked through the rest of the bottle and most of the night, and by the time the sky over the rooftops went from black to the grey of wet slate, Cyrus had covered the back of Bram's chart in his own annotations — timings written and rewritten, distances measured twice, tide tables copied from Aldous's own almanac in his small precise hand.

It steadied him, the arithmetic. That was the strange mercy of it. Fear, left alone, spread like ink in water, coloring everything, touching everything, until a man could not think of bread without thinking of hunger or of sleep without thinking of never waking. But measured fear — fear divided into segments, six hundred meters here, twelve minutes there, a crate of ice that must be packed at three-thirty so the ice would still be solid at four-fifteen — fear measured became merely a problem, and problems could be solved, and a man solving problems had no time to tremble.

"We carry it in the ice," Bram said. "Not under the ice, not beneath it — *in* it. Frozen into the block itself, wrapped tight in waxed canvas so the water can't reach it. A soldier opens a fish crate, he sees ice, he sees fish, he sees stink, and he closes the lid fast because his uniform has to last him the week. Paper frozen in ice is paper no man wants to touch."

"And if the ice melts?" Odile asked.

"It won't melt in an hour. It might sweat." Bram shrugged. "Everything sweats near the sea. But waxed canvas sheds water better than a duck, and I've brought the wax and the cloth, and I've brought this—" From the sack he produced a long fish-gutting knife, wicked and bright, and laid it beside the chart with a certain ceremony. "Because if it goes wrong at any checkpoint, you cut the canvas free and let the ledger sink. Weighted, it'll be at the bottom of the harbor before anyone reaches it. Better drowned than burned."

Cyrus stared at the knife. "You're telling us to destroy it ourselves."

"I'm telling you that a thing carried is a thing that can be taken, and a thing that can be taken should have a death planned for it that you choose rather than one chosen for you." Bram said it without drama, the way a man says *the tide comes in twice daily*. "Your master understood that. Why do you think he kept no copies?"

"He did keep copies," Odile said softly.

Both men turned. She had gone pale in the candlelight, and Cyrus watched her decide — watched the decision cross her face like cloud-shadow crossing a field — before she said, "That's mine to manage. But yes. There will be copies. If the original drowns, the names do not."

Bram grinned then, sudden and crooked, and for a moment he looked twenty years younger and considerably less trustworthy. "A typist with secrets. Aldous did pick his people well." He drained his cup and stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "Sleep. Both of you. Not because you'll get much, but because tired hands shake, and shaking hands at a checkpoint is a confession written before you speak."

He paused at the door, sack over his shoulder, and looked back at the chart lying on the table between the cups — three red marks, three gates, forty minutes of ordinary distance standing between them and the whole rest of their lives.

"One more thing," Bram said. "At the fish market, tomorrow, you'll be porters. Crates on shoulders, heads down, backs bent — porters don't look up, don't look around, don't have opinions. I'll get you the coats and the crates. What I can't get you is the walk. That part's yours." His eye found Cyrus. "Can you bend your back and keep your mouth shut for eight hundred meters?"

"I grew up carrying map plates down three flights of stairs without scratching them," Cyrus said. "I can carry fish."

"Good lad." Bram opened the door onto the cold blue dark. "Four o'clock. Don't be early — early looks eager, and eager looks guilty. Don't be late — late looks careless, and careless gets searched. Arrive exactly when a man arrives who does this every day of his life and wishes he were anywhere else."

The door closed. His footsteps descended, unhurried as ever, and faded into the sound of the harbor breathing below.

* * *

Odile did not sleep. Cyrus knew it because neither did he, and because he could hear, through the thin wall between their rooms, the arrhythmic ticking that was not a clock — it was her fingers, tapping against the bedframe, typing something over and over in the dark. He wondered what. He did not ask.

Near three, he gave up on his cot and came downstairs to find her already dressed, hair pinned severely under a porter's cap that made her look, heartbreakingly, like a boy of fourteen. The ledger lay unwrapped on the table, and beside it the waxed canvas, the knife, the ice tongs Bram had left, and a pot of glue and a brush.

"I've been thinking about the weight," she said, without looking up.

"The weight."

"Aldous always said a page weighs almost nothing. Half a gram. Less." She turned a page of the ledger with one finger, careful as a priest turning scripture. "Three hundred pages, then, a hundred fifty grams. Less than an apple. Less than a hammer. You could forget you were carrying it." She closed the book. "And yet I have been trying all night to imagine carrying it through those gates, and my arms ache already. Isn't that absurd? It weighs less than an apple and I can barely stand under it."

Cyrus sat down across from her. Outside, far off, a patrol's boots rang on cobbles in the dark, passed, and faded.

"It's not the paper," he said. "It's what's written on it."

"No." Odile shook her head slowly. "It's that we know what happens if it doesn't get through. Every gram of it is a person. My mother is in this book, Cyrus. Half a gram of her. When I lift it, I'm lifting her, and Marta, and the ferryman's sons, and every family on every page, and none of them weigh anything at all except that they lived." She looked up at him then, and her eyes in the candlelight were perfectly dry, which was somehow worse than tears. "So we pack it well. We pack it so well that no soldier on this coast can make it weigh nothing."

They worked in silence after that. Cyrus sealed the ledger in its waxed canvas, folding the cloth the way Aldous had taught him to fold charts — corners first, edges tight, no crease where water could find a seam. Odile melted the wax and painted the seams while it cooled, her brush steady, her breath held, painting each fold twice. Then they lowered the bundle into the center of the ice block Bram had delivered, packed fresh-crushed ice around it, froze the seams with snow-water and patience, and set the whole of it into a fish crate between two layers of herring, and the smell of it was so ferocious that Cyrus nearly laughed — a wild, cracked sound in the dark — because no living man would open that crate twice.

By the time they finished, the windows had gone the grey of old pewter, and somewhere below, the fish market was waking: carts rattling, gulls screaming their eternal grievance, men shouting prices into the cold. The world proceeding as if nothing were ending. As if everything were only beginning.

Cyrus lifted the crate to test its weight — thirty kilos, maybe thirty-five, awkward but bearable on a shoulder — and found Odile watching him.

"Six hundred meters to the customs house," she said. "Eight minutes loaded."

"Ten if we're careful."

"Fifteen if we're stupid." Almost, almost, she smiled. "I've memorized it too."

There was a knock below — Bram's signal, two knocks, a pause, one. Downstairs, the smuggler waited with two porter's coats that smelled of ten thousand voyages, and beyond him the town lay dark and occupied between them and the sea, and somewhere out on the water the *Corinne* rode at anchor with her mail bags stacked and her gangplank down, waiting for the tide, waiting for the dawn, waiting — though she did not know it — for three hundred names frozen in a block of ice.

Cyrus settled the crate against his shoulder, felt the cold of it bite through the wool, and reached for the door.

The fish market gate opened in twenty minutes.
