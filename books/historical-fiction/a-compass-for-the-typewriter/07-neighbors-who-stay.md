## 7. Neighbors Who Stay

The census men came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays had always been the day the harbor woke gently — nets mended, ledgers balanced, bread set out to cool in windowsills still warm from the night's ovens. Now the town learned to measure its days by the sound of boots on cobbles instead of gulls, and Tuesday was no different except that it was worse, because the boots came with clipboards.

Cyrus watched them from the workshop window he had once used for sighting landmarks. Three men in grey uniforms that did not quite match, carrying a folding table and a ledger of their own — an official one, bound in black leather, stamped with an eagle whose wings looked more like bars. They set up in the square beneath the plane tree where fishwives had argued prices for a hundred years, and they began calling names through a tin megaphone that turned every voice into something flattened and foreign.

"Household heads will present themselves for registration. Failure to register constitutes—"

He stopped listening. He knew what failure constituted. The notices pasted to walls all over town had made the grammar of disappearance very clear: *failure to register*, *failure to vacate*, *failure to comply*. As if the town were a schoolboy who had not done its sums.

Behind him, the workshop was already half-emptied. Captain Reiner's men had come two days ago with a requisition order and a inventory list, and they had carried away Aldous's printing press in pieces, his drawers of type sorted into crates marked with chalk numbers, his finished plates wrapped in oilcloth as if they were contraband — which, Cyrus thought bitterly, in a way they were. What remained was dust-shadows on the shelves where instruments had stood, and the smell of ink that would not leave the walls, and the floorboard under the drafting table beneath which the ledger lay wrapped in sailcloth, flat as a sleeping thing.

Odile was at the flat above the chandler's shop, typing. She had been typing since before dawn; he could hear the ghost of her rhythm even now, in memory, a sound like rain deciding where to fall. They had agreed she would stay there while he watched the square. It was safer. Everything now was measured in safer, and the word had grown teeth.

But it was not Odile he saw when he looked down at the square. It was Tomas Ferreol, the baker.

* * *

Tomas had flour worked so deep into the creases of his hands that no amount of lye ever fully cleaned it, and Cyrus had known him since boyhood, since the days when a copper coin bought a roll still crackling from the oven and Tomas would slip a second one in the bag if he thought your mother wasn't looking. He was a wide man gone soft at the edges, with eyebrows like grey hedgerows, and he stood now at the mouth of the queue forming before the census table with his cap in his hands — not removed in respect, Cyrus realized, but held ready, the way a man holds a hat when he intends to put it on and walk away.

The queue moved. The clerks wrote. A woman Cyrus didn't recognize — brought in from outside, her accent wrong, her eyes never resting on any face longer than a second — took down each household with mechanical strokes.

When Tomas reached the table, he did not give his cap to the clerk. He put it on.

"You are Tomas Ferreol?" the clerk asked. "Baker, Rue des Salines?"

"I am."

"You are listed for transport on the fifth convoy."

"I know what I'm listed for." Tomas's voice was mild, the voice he used for explaining to young apprentices why the dough had soured overnight. "I've baked bread in this town for forty-one years. My father baked here before me. I want you to write down that the bread was good."

The clerk's pen paused. "Sir?"

"The bread," Tomas said patiently, as though to a child slow at his letters. "Write it down. That the bread was good, and that people came from three villages over for it on feast days, and that when the fishing fleet was lost in 'ninety-eight, I baked for nothing until the widows could pay again. Put that in your book."

Cyrus saw the clerk exchange a glance with the officer beside him — Reiner's man, Vossig, a thin-lipped lieutenant who treated paperwork like a weapon he hadn't yet been issued ammunition for. "That is not information we require."

"Then require it." And there it was, the thing that made Cyrus's throat tighten: Tomas Ferreol, who had never in his life raised his voice above the temperature of a warm oven, standing straighter than Cyrus had ever seen him. "You're making a book about this town. Make it a true one."

They wrote him down as *obstinate, non-compliant*. But Cyrus, watching from above with the cold clarity of a man trained to record exactly what he sees, understood that Tomas had won something. The clerk had hesitated. For one moment, the pen had hovered over the official line, and behind the clerk's bored face something human had flickered — the recognition that a man's bread might matter more than a man's compliance.

Cyrus wrote it down. Not in any ledger that mattered yet, but in the pocket notebook he now carried everywhere, Aldous's habit become his own: *Ferreol, Tomas. Baker. Forty-one years. Bread good enough that people crossed villages for it.*

It was not much. It was a beginning.

* * *

Old Marta did not go to the square at all. Old Marta had never in her ninety years gone anywhere she did not choose to go, and she announced this fact to the entire street on Wednesday morning, standing in her doorway with her shawl pinned at the throat and her cane planted before her like a border post.

The census men came door to door that day, working the lower lanes, and Cyrus followed at a distance under the pretext of delivering a parcel, because he could not bear not to see. Marta Vasseur had been old when Cyrus's father was young; she remembered the town before the breakwater, before the lighthouse, before half the streets had their current names. Her doorway faced the harbor, and she had sat in it through storms and funerals and two wars that history books called small ones, and she sat in it now with her hands folded on the cane and her chin lifted at whatever came.

"Madame Vasseur." The clerk consulted his list. "You are scheduled for removal to—"

"I heard you the first time, boy. You shouted it through your little horn yesterday." Her voice was dry as August grass. "I am ninety-one years old. I was born in the room behind you. My mother died in it. My husband died in it, God rest his tedious soul. You may write down that I am not leaving it."

"Madame, I'm afraid the orders—"

"Are not mine." She smiled then, and it was not a gentle smile; it was the smile of someone watching weather approach and finding it unimpressive. "Here is what you will do. You will write my name correctly — Vasseur, with the double s, because your predecessor spelled it wrong in 1871 and my husband fought the tax office for six years over it. You will write that I kept the light burning in the harbor chapel for fifty years when the town couldn't afford a keeper. And you will write down my granddaughter's name too, Anouk Vasseur, deported last spring, age twenty-three, who played the fiddle and laughed too loud. Write her name even though she isn't here. Especially because she isn't here."

The clerk's pencil moved. Stopped. Moved again. Cyrus, thirty feet away with a parcel he no longer pretended to be delivering, saw the young man's ears go red.

"Anouk is... not on my list, madame."

"Then your list is wrong, and you should be ashamed of it." Marta leaned forward on her cane. "Listen to me, boy with the pencil. Books get burned. Lists get lost. But a name written truly, by a hand that meant it — that survives anything. So mean it. Do you understand me? Mean what you write, or don't bother writing."

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Then, glancing left and right, he added something to his page that was not on any form. Cyrus could not read it from where he stood. He only saw the young man's hand move carefully, twice, as though writing something worth writing.

Marta nodded once, satisfied, and shut her door — but not before her eyes found Cyrus across the lane, sharp as a gull spotting silver in the water. She inclined her head a fraction. *I know what you're carrying,* that nod said. *Good. Carry it properly.*

He walked home the long way, past the chapel, and added to his notebook: *Vasseur, Marta, with the double s. Keeper of lights. Granddaughter Anouk, fiddle, laughter.*

* * *

The ferryman came to the workshop himself, on Thursday evening, when the light was failing and the tide smelled stronger than usual of diesel and war.

Silas Renard had run the harbor ferry for thirty years, poling passengers across the inner basin before the bridge was built, then motoring them after, grumbling about engines the whole time. He was a leathery, bowlegged man who spat with precision and believed, publicly, in nothing but the water. He knocked on the workshop door with the flat of his hand, four beats, and when Cyrus opened it he pushed past without invitation, the way family does.

"They took my boat today," Silas said, without preamble. He stood in the middle of the ruined workshop, looking at the empty shelves. "Requisitioned. For patrol duty, they say. My boat, patrolling waters I've crossed ten thousand times, with some navy pup at the tiller who doesn't know the sandbar shifts east in autumn."

"I'm sorry, Silas."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." The ferryman turned, and in the dusk his face was hard to read, all weathered planes and shadow. "Your master — Aldous. He drew charts of this harbor finer than the admiralty ever managed. Everyone knew it. Everyone also knew he drew things the admiralty didn't ask for. Little marks. Notes." His eyes were suddenly very direct. "Names."

Cyrus's heart performed a slow, deliberate stumble. "I don't know what you mean."

"Cyrus Voss." Silas said his name the way he'd announce a shoal. "I've watched you sneak around this town for a week like a man with a stolen saint in his coat. I ferried your master to the islands eleven times in two years, always at night, always asking no questions, because Aldous Marchand saved my daughter's life in the fever winter and a man pays such debts however they're asked." He stepped closer. "I'm telling you this because in six days they march us out or ship us out, whichever suits their schedules, and I have decided I am not going. And I want to know if the thing you're hiding under that floorboard is going to remember us."

The silence stretched. Outside, a patrol whistled twice, far off, and somewhere a dog answered. Cyrus thought of Odile's warning — trust no one, tell no one, the fewer who carry it the farther it goes — and he thought of Silas Renard standing in the dark having just named the floorboard without being told, and he understood that the secret had already outgrown them both. Secrets, like tides, cannot be argued with. Only navigated.

"Yes," Cyrus said quietly. "It remembers. Every name. Every date. Where they went. What was taken. All of it."

Silas exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load carried a long way. "Good. Then listen. I can't leave — someone has to stay and watch the water, mark which nights the patrols run lazy and which nights they run keen. But when your night comes, and it's coming soon, boy, I can see it sitting on you like gulls on a rail — when your night comes, there's a stretch past the point where the lamp doesn't reach, eleven minutes' row if the chop is kind. Remember that number. Eleven minutes."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because somebody has to write down that the ferry ran true for thirty years." And here the old man's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across granite. "Because my wife is buried on the hill and my daughter is in the capital and neither of them will have a stone if these people have their way. Because old Marta stood in her doorway and shamed a clerk into writing her granddaughter's name, and I thought — well. If Marta can stand up at ninety-one, I can stand up at sixty-four. We stay, Cyrus. Some of us stay. Not because it changes anything." He looked around the stripped workshop, at the shadows where the press had been, at the floorboard, at the boy holding the dead mapmaker's craft in his two hands. "But because a town that nobody stays for is a town that lets itself be erased. Let them take the boats and the presses and the bread ovens. They can't take being witnessed. That's ours."

He left without saying goodbye, which was how ferrymen said goodbye — assuming, always, another crossing.

* * *

That night Cyrus climbed to the flat above the chandler's shop, and found Odile asleep at the typewriter.

Her fingers were ink-stained — onion-skin pages lay scattered around her like shed feathers — and her head had tipped forward onto her folded arms, her breath shallow with exhaustion. The candle had burned down to a coin of wax. Cyrus stood in the doorway a long time, unwilling to wake her, watching the rise and fall of her shoulders and thinking about doors and doorways and the people choosing to stand in them.

He had spent his life learning that maps were arguments about what mattered. A cartographer chose what to include, and every inclusion was a small act of love, and every omission a small erasure. The army had understood this better than he had, perhaps. They were not destroying the town first. They were destroying the *record* of the town first — the registers, the parish rolls, the shipping manifests — and the buildings would follow as naturally as shadow follows the object. Erase the names, and the stones were merely stones.

But Tomas stood in the queue and demanded his bread be written down. Marta stood in her doorway and dictated her own entry, correcting the spelling. Silas stood watch on the water and gave away his eleven minutes of darkness like a man endowing a church. None of them asked to be saved. That was the part that undid him. They asked only to be *written*.

He sat down carefully on the far side of the table, took out his notebook, and began transferring the day's entries into fair copy, matching Odile's code letter by letter, his pen scratching softly so as not to wake her. *Ferreol. Vasseur, with the double s. Anouk, fiddle, laughter. Renard, Silas, ferryman, thirty years true.* Around them the typed pages whispered in the draft from the window, hundreds of names riding on paper thin enough to see firelight through.

Odile stirred. Her eyes opened, unfocused, found him writing, and softened.

"What is it tonight?" she murmured.

"Neighbors," Cyrus said. "Staying behind. Standing in their doors so the faces get remembered." He turned the notebook toward her, and read aloud, quietly, the words he had written — bread good enough to cross villages for, lights kept for fifty years, a ferry run true, a fiddle and laughter — and as he read, Odile rose from her chair and came around the table and put her hand flat on the page, over the names, the way you'd steady a map against wind.

"Then we add a page," she said. "For the ones who stay."

Outside, far off toward the customs house, a whistle blew — twice, then again, sharper — and a lantern swung wild in the dark near the quay, and they both went still, listening, the ink drying between their fingers. Something was happening at the water. Something early.
