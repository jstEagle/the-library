## 10. A Night of Typing

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Odile unlocked the typewriter case, and she found herself hesitating over the latch the way one hesitates at the door of a sickroom — knowing what waits inside, knowing it must be faced anyway.

The room above the chandler's shop was small and smelled of tallow and salt. Cyrus had gone out an hour ago to speak with Bram about the customs house, and he would not return until well after midnight, which was precisely why she had waited for him to leave. This was work she needed to do alone. Not because she distrusted him — the strange, raw truth was that she had come to trust him faster than she had trusted anyone since her mother's name first appeared on a list — but because some griefs want no witnesses. Some griefs are easier to carry in company than to *perform* in it.

She set the original ledger on the table and opened it.

Three hundred names. She had counted them twice, once for Aldous in the autumn, when the encoding had begun and she still believed the margins were an eccentric old man's superstition, and once last week, alone, after the requisition notices went up and the word *disappear* stopped being a rumor and became a schedule. Three hundred names in Aldous's careful hand, each with its date, each with its destination rendered as a dash or a question mark or, in eleven cases, nothing at all — just the name and the date and a small, terrible blank where the future should have been.

She threaded a sheet of onion-skin into the platen.

Bram had procured the paper from a printer who owed him money, four quires of it, so thin that when Odile held a page to the candle flame she could see her own fingers' shadows through it, pink as something unborn. Thin enough to fold into nothing. Thin enough to sew into a coat lining with a length of thread and a curved needle borrowed from the sailmaker. Thin enough, if she did this right, that three hundred lives could pass across a sergeant's palm and feel like empty pockets.

She adjusted the ribbon — Bram had brought ink too, a bottle of it, and she had to dilute it with water so it wouldn't blot through and marry page to page. The diluted ink came out gray, ghost-colored, appropriate. Names written in the color of fog.

Her fingers found the keys.

*ABELARD, HENRIETTE. Taken 4 March. Destination unknown.*

The little bell at the end of the line rang, thin as an insect. She returned the carriage. The sound of it — that small bright *tink* — went through her like a needle, because she had heard it ten thousand times before under ordinary circumstances: labels for harbor charts, tide tables, the names of lighthouses. The same machine. The same bell. Only the words had changed.

*Abern, Tomas and Lise (child). Taken 11 April.*

She typed the child's name and thought of the ferryman's granddaughter, who used to chase gulls off the pier with her arms spread wide, shrieking with joy, and who had been gone since April along with her parents, and whose bed Odile had helped strip in May because no one else would do it. The mattress still held the shape of a small body. She had lain her own hand in that hollow and left it there far longer than was useful.

The candle guttered. She fed it a new one from the box without stopping, because stopping was dangerous. Stopping meant thinking about the second checkpoint tomorrow — no, today, now, it was past midnight — the customs house with its iron gates and its inspectors who opened crates with bayonets, the diversion Bram had promised to arrange with a confidence Odile did not believe in, the whole brittle architecture of their plan resting on a fish crate and a lie. If she thought about that, her hands would shake, and shaking hands struck keys badly, and badly struck keys made names illegible, and illegible names were a second disappearance. So she did not think about it. She typed.

*Adler, Marguerite. Taken 19 March. Destination unknown.*

Her mother.

She had known it was coming, of course. She had read the ledger through twice; she had encoded half these names herself into the atlas margins, letter by letter, number by number, hiding them in coordinates and soundings the way you bury seeds. There was no possible way she did not know her mother's name was in the book.

And yet when the keys rose up to meet her fingers and the letters assembled themselves — M, A, R, G, U, E, R, I, T, E — it was as if she were typing them for the first time in history. As if the name had never existed until this moment, in gray ink, on paper thinner than a moth's wing, and now existed forever.

Her hands stopped. The bell waited, unsounded, mid-line.

Her mother had been taken on the nineteenth of March, a Wednesday. Odile knew this because it was the day the bakery had no bread — the baker's wife had wept behind the counter — and because her mother had kissed her forehead that morning and said, *I have an errand in the next town, I'll be back by supper,* and Odile had believed her, because you believe your mother, that is the entire arrangement of childhood, and she had been twenty-two years old and still arranging her life around the certainty of her mother's return.

Supper had gone cold. Then the season went cold. Then everything.

Aldous had entered the date in his neat cartographer's numerals, and beneath *Destination unknown* he had drawn, very lightly, in pencil, a single horizontal line — the symbol for uncharted water. She had not noticed it before. Her eyes snagged on it now the way a hull snags on a reef. He had mapped her mother's vanishing. He had given it a cartographer's mercy: not *nothing*, but *unknown*. Not erased, but awaiting survey.

Odile pressed the carriage return and typed the date, and the bell rang, and she understood suddenly, completely, in a way she would spend the rest of her life trying to explain, that every keystroke was a resurrection. That is not poetry. It was mechanics. A name that exists only in memory exists in one place, and one place can burn. But a name typed onto onion skin and sewn into a coat lining exists in two places, and then three, and then wherever a neutral ship carries it, and then wherever a foreign press sets it in metal, and metal outlasts empires. Each strike of the key was a nail fastening a person to the world. Henriette Abelard, nailed fast. Tomas and Lise Abern, nailed fast. Marguerite Marchand, nailed fast, her daughter's bleeding fingers holding the hammer.

She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and kept working.

* * *

By two o'clock the first quire was finished — eighty-one names — and her fingers had begun to hurt.

It started as an ache in the first joint of her right index finger, the one that took the E, the most common letter, the letter that ended half the women's names. Then it deepened into something hotter. She looked down and saw, with distant surprise, a dark bead of blood where the cuticle had split against the steel guide of the type basket. Onion-skin demanded a light touch; a light touch demanded precision; precision demanded pressure; and pressure, repeated nine hundred times an hour, all night long, exacted its wage.

She wrapped the finger in a strip torn from her petticoat and kept going. By three o'clock two more fingers had joined the rebellion. By four she had both hands bandaged to the knuckle like a laundress, and the strips were spotting red, and the red spots were appearing on the margins of the pages in small irregular constellations, and she decided — with the cold, lucid stubbornness that arrives somewhere past the fourth hour of any true labor — that she would not retype them. Let the blood stand. Let whoever eventually decoded these pages understand that they were not transcribed but *paid for*.

The chandler's shop below them slept. Once, a dog barked itself hoarse near the fish market and fell silent mid-bark, and Odile's heart stopped with it, listening for boots. There were none. The patrols kept their own hours; Bram had told her the change came at three-fifteen, the night watch staggering home drunk on requisitioned brandy and the morning watch arriving resentful and cold. Three-fifteen was the seam in the soldier's day. She typed through it.

Somewhere in the fifth hour the typing changed nature.

She noticed it first as rhythm. The carriage return, the bell, the return — the motion had become metronomic, and her breathing had synchronized to it, and her thoughts had narrowed to the width of a single line. It was the state she sometimes reached copying tide tables, the state Aldous had called *the deep water*, where the hands know the work and the mind is free to wander elsewhere. And in that wandering she began, quite without deciding to, to speak to them. Not aloud. Something quieter and older than speech.

*Henriette Abelard.* I remember your blue shutters. I remember that you repainted them in October even though everyone said it was too late in the year for painting. *Tomas and Lise Abern.* Your daughter chased gulls. Her arms went wide like this — she demonstrated briefly to the empty room — and she laughed from her whole chest, a child's laugh, the kind you cannot counterfeit. *Perrin, Antoine, the pilot.* You taught Cyrus to read currents. He pretends he learned it from a book.

Name by name, the room filled. Not with ghosts — she was too practical for ghosts — but with presence. The candle flame seemed to lean toward the table like a listener. Outside, the harbor breathed in its sleep, the mooring lines creaking, the water slapping the pilings in the old patient rhythm, and Odile understood that the harbor remembered them too. That the stones her mother had walked were still walking-stones. What the army could take away was the census, the doorways, the mail route. What it could not take away was the fact of having happened. It could kill the witnesses. It could not unhappen the witnessed.

But it could try to make the world forget. And forgetting, unlike unhappening, was possible. Forgetting happened slowly, in archives and ledgers and the quiet replacement of one map by another, and it was against *that* — against forgetting, the slow bureaucratic tide — that she was typing tonight, one gray letter at a time, sewing shut the gap through which three hundred people might otherwise slip silently out of history.

So she typed faster. Her bandaged fingers left red crescents on the platen's ivory knobs. The bell rang and rang, a tiny churchbell tolling resurrections, and Odile Marchand sat upright in the small hours of the morning with her spine straight as a mast and performed the only sacrament she had ever truly believed in: she wrote the names down truly.

* * *

Dawn came the way it comes to harbors — not as light but as a slow lifting of gray, the darkness diluting until the window showed the masts and rigging of the fishing fleet standing in rows like a congregation. Odile's candle had drowned in its own wax sometime around five. She had not noticed; the dawn had simply replaced it.

Two hundred and ninety-nine names lay before her in six sewn signatures, the pages whisper-thin, the ink ghost-gray, flecked here and there with blood. Two hundred and ninety-nine, because she had saved one deliberately, and now she drew the last clean sheet toward her and threaded it into the machine with hands that shook — from exhaustion, she told herself, only exhaustion.

There was room at the back of the ledger. Aldous had ruled it for additions and left it blank, and last night, watching Bram argue logistics in the lamplight with his scarred hands moving like weather, she had known what the blank page was for.

She typed:

*BRAM. Family name withheld for his protection. Occupation: smuggler, and better than that. Taken: not yet. Destination: ours.*

Not a proper entry. Aldous would have corrected her grammar and her sentimentality both. She let it stand.

Then she folded. This was the surgeon's part, and she had rehearsed it on scrap until her folds were true. Each signature went inside the next, the whole bundle flattened to the thickness of a letter, and then she took up the sailmaker's curved needle and the waxed thread — fine enough, gray enough — and began to sew them into the lining of her coat, between the wool and the canvas, along the seam where a searching hand would find only tailoring. In and out, in and out, small invisible stitches, her pricked fingertips leaving tiny red commas on the wool. When it was done she put the coat on and buttoned it and stood, and felt nothing — no ridge, no weight, no tell. Three hundred lives distributed evenly across her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The original ledger she wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord. That would go with Cyrus, by water, past the breakwater. That was the plan they had agreed to and not discussed, because discussing it meant saying aloud that they had divided themselves between the copies and the original, between the boat and the gangplank, and that whichever of them the soldiers caught, the other would have to keep walking and not turn around.

She heard his step on the stair — she knew it now, the particular care of it, a man who had grown up reading ground — and she turned from the window as he came in, carrying bread and bad news in equal measure, if the set of his shoulders meant anything.

He stopped. His eyes went to the typewriter, the dead candles, the scattered strips of bloody bandage, the six empty quire-wrappers folded neatly beside them like shed skins. She watched him do the arithmetic — the hours, the names, the fingers — and watched something move across his face that was not pity. Pity she could have refused. This was closer to awe, and harder to bear.

"You finished," he said.

"I finished." Her voice was rough as rope. "All three hundred. Twice, counting the ones in my head."

She unpinned the oilcloth bundle and set the original on the table between them, and for a moment neither touched it. Dawn strengthened at the window. Down in the harbor, the mail steamer's smoke was a smudge on the horizon, early, waiting, indifferent as tide.

"There's something else," Cyrus said quietly, and she saw it in him then, whatever he had carried up the stairs behind the bread — "Bram didn't come back last night. And at the customs house this morning, they're opening crates."
