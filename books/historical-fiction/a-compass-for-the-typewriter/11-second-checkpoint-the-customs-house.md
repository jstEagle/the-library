## 11. Second Checkpoint: The Customs House

The customs house had been a granary once, Cyrus remembered, before the army came. He had mapped it as a boy — Master Aldous had sent him down to measure the harbor frontage when he was fourteen, and he had stood in the square with his chain and his notebook while grain carts rumbled past, and the building's long stone face had seemed to him then the most permanent thing in the world. Now its arched doors stood open under a new sign stenciled in black and white — ZOLLAMT / CUSTOMS INSPECTION — and two soldiers flanked each door with rifles slung across their chests, and the permanence of it felt like a lie told by masonry.

Dawn was still an hour off. The square before the customs house was already crowded, though: fish porters with their barrows, farmers driving carts heaped with cabbages and early strawberries, families with carpetbags who were not leaving so much as being processed, their faces gray in the light of the electric lamps the army had strung along the quay. Bram moved through all of it like a man walking through his own kitchen.

"Keep your eyes forward," the smuggler said quietly, without turning his head. He carried the crate himself — the heavy one, packed with ice and herring packed around the oilcloth bundle, because Bram had argued that guards opened crates looking for paper and never looked twice at something that smelled properly of fish. "You're brothers. You've done this run forty times. You're bored. Boredom is the whole performance. If you look frightened, they'll find something to be frightened of."

Cyrus nodded and did not trust his voice. Odile walked on his other side, her hands buried in the sleeves of her borrowed coat, and he could feel her trembling through the air between them, or imagined he could, which amounted to the same thing.

The line shuffled forward. A soldier with a ledger of his own — everyone keeps ledgers, Cyrus thought bitterly; the whole world had become a ledger — ticked off barrow after barrow, waving some through and directing others into the covered inspection bay where the doors of the old granary gaped like a mouth. From inside came sounds that Cyrus tried not to translate: wood cracking, a man's raised voice, the wet slap of fish thrown from crates onto stone.

Bram stopped at the table set up before the doors. An inspector sat behind it, a thick-necked sergeant with a stamp and a list, and beside him stood a second man — younger, in a better coat, with the collar tabs of an officer and the tired eyes of someone who had been awake since midnight. This one held a sheaf of papers and read them slowly, as if each were a letter from home.

"Papers," said the sergeant.

Bram produced them — bills of lading, a consignment note for the Herring Guild, a stamped chit that must have cost him half of whatever he was being paid, if indeed he was being paid at all. Cyrus had asked him once, on the walk down from the fish market gate, why he was doing this. Bram had shrugged. *I grew up three streets from Marta,* he had said. *That's the entire contract.* And then he had grinned and said the rest was professional habit: nobody smuggles anything for free, not even freedom.

The sergeant thumbed through the papers. Behind him, the officer in the good coat looked up from his own reading.

"Herr Voss."

The name went through Cyrus like cold water finding a seam in a wall. He kept his face blank, which took everything he had. "Sir?"

"You're Voss the elder's boy. The apprentice." The officer held up one of the papers he'd been reading, and Cyrus saw with a distant, ringing clarity that it was a draft chart — one of Aldous's working sheets, taken from the workshop when it was seized, its margins dense with soundings and tide tables in Aldous's crabbed hand, and beneath them, in Cyrus's neater script, the fair-copy labels. *Pointe des Trois Frères. Anse Saint-Aubin. La Chenaie.* His handwriting, ten feet from a customs officer, pinned to the morning like a moth.

"I know this hand," the officer said. "It's good work. Better than good." He looked at Cyrus over the top of the sheet, and his expression was difficult to read — appraisal, certainly, but underneath it something almost like hunger. "Captain Reiner has been asking after whoever drew these."

*Reiner.* The name meant the requisition, the sealed workshop, the inventory lists nailed to the door of every public building in town. Cyrus made his face into the shape of a young man flattered and slightly stupid. "That would be my master, sir. Aldous. He died in the spring."

"So I heard." The officer set the sheet down, and for a moment Cyrus thought that was the end of it — but the officer's gaze stayed on him the way a compass needle stays on north, and Cyrus understood that he had just stepped into a current he could not yet see the far bank of.

The sergeant cleared his throat and pointed at the crate. "Open it."

Bram opened it. He did it well — slowly, grumbling about the ice melting, levering the lid with the practiced annoyance of a man who has done this forty times — and the smell rolled out over the table, salt and brine and the iron reek of fresh-killed herring, layered thick over the oilcloth bundle in the center. The sergeant prodded the fish with his baton. The officer watched. Cyrus counted his own heartbeats and got to eleven before the sergeant straightened.

"Close it," he said, and reached for the stamp.

And that was when the diversion went wrong.

It began, as Bram had promised it would begin, at the far side of the square — a barrow overturning, a shout, fish sliding silver across the cobblestones, a cart horse shying. It should have drawn two soldiers, maybe three, for the minute or two that Bram needed to get his crate waved through and his porters moving. That was the arithmetic. Bram had sketched it on the back of a cigarette packet the night before with the same precision he used for tide charts: *one overturned barrow buys you ninety seconds.*

But the horse bolted. It came through the crowd at a dead gallop, cart jouncing, a woman screaming somewhere behind it, and instead of two soldiers the whole doorway emptied — the sergeant vaulting the table, the officer shouting orders, rifles swinging, and in the chaos a corporal near the inspection bay bellowed the worst sentence in the language:

"Nobody moves! Close the doors! Search everything!"

Bram did not run. Cyrus, pressed against the customs house wall with Odile's wrist locked in his grip, watched the smuggler take stock in a single glance — the sealed doors, the ring of soldiers, the crate sitting alone on the table with its melting ice — and Cyrus saw him decide. It happened in his shoulders first. They settled, the way a man settles into a chair he knows he will occupy for a long time.

Then Bram knocked over the crate himself.

He did it clumsily, loudly, cursing in three languages, and the oilcloth bundle slid out among the spilled herring on the stones — and Cyrus's heart stopped until he saw what Bram's hands did next, quick as a card sharp's: the bundle vanished up Bram's sleeve in the same motion that sent him sprawling after it, on his knees in the fish and the meltwater, hands splayed, exactly where four soldiers wanted him.

"Thief!" Bram roared at no one. "My consignment! You've ruined my consignment!"

They took him for causing a disturbance. That was the charge, at first — drunk and disorderly, a porter making trouble at dawn. Two soldiers hauled him up by the arms and he let them, sagging between them like any belligerent fisherman, his sleeve hanging loose, the bundle gone to wherever his coat's lining hid it. And all the while, as they dragged him toward the inspection bay, Bram talked — loud, profane, magnificent — filling every ear in the square with the story of a drunk porter, so that no one would have room for any other story.

Odile's fingers dug into Cyrus's arm hard enough to bruise. "The crate," she breathed. "The crate is still—"

"I know."

The crate sat on the table, lid half-seated, ice weeping out of its seams, herring scattered across the sergeant's paperwork. And the sergeant, returning red-faced from the middle of the square, planted himself before it with the affronted dignity of a man whose morning had been ruined, and said, to the world at large:

"Every crate gets opened. Every single one. Starting with this one."

There were perhaps thirty seconds in which Cyrus could have done nothing, and he spent them understanding that he was going to watch Odile die, or watch the ledger die, or both, and that the whole enterprise — Aldous's atlas, the names, Marta's doorway, Bram's ninety seconds — had come down to a wooden box on a table and a sergeant with a pry bar.

Then the seconds closed, and Cyrus stepped forward.

"Sir," he said. "Please. Don't open that one flat."

The sergeant turned. Whatever he saw — a fish porter, pale, hat in hand — did not impress him. "Get back in line."

"The ice," Cyrus said, and heard his own voice do something remarkable: steady itself, find the register of expertise, the voice Aldous had trained into him for presenting charts to harbor boards and naval men. "You'll want to stand it on end before you pry it, sir. The packing's layered — herring above, ice below, the way the guild does it for the morning trains. Opened flat, the melt drains through the whole catch and you lose the lot. Spoilage in six hours. The Guildmaster will want the weight docked from somebody's pay, and it won't be mine." He paused, precisely long enough. "Though I expect the army doesn't concern itself with the guild's weights anymore."

It was a risk. Flattery with a barb in it — but he had watched the officer in the good coat handle Aldous's chart, and he was gambling that men who confiscated maps also requisitioned accounts, and that a sergeant drowning in paperwork would seize on any excuse not to add spoilage claims to his morning.

The sergeant's jaw worked. "Layered," he said. "Ice below."

"Yes, sir. Stand it up and pull the bottom board — it's the loose one, the guild marks them — and everything comes out in one piece. Fish stays sellable, ice stays countable." He lowered his voice, confiding, apologetic. "Truth is, sir, most of your inspectors open them flat and it costs the requisition office a fortune. Nobody ever believes the porters."

Something shifted in the sergeant's face — the small, fatal softening of a man offered a chance to be better-informed than his superiors. He turned the crate on end. He found the loose board, because Bram had built the crate and Bram marked every board, and pried it, and the packed ice came out in a solid brick with herring frozen into its upper layer, and beneath it — Cyrus did not breathe — beneath it there was only more ice, and sawdust, and the false bottom Bram had built sitting empty as a promise, the real bundle having traveled up a sleeve into a coat that was even now disappearing into the inspection bay along with its wearer.

"Happy, sir?" Cyrus asked, hating the eagerness in his own voice, hating himself for producing it.

The sergeant grunted. He took the stamp off the table and brought it down on Bram's bill of lading with unnecessary force. "Next."

Cyrus collected the papers. His hands, he noticed distantly, were perfectly steady, which struck him as a kind of betrayal — they ought to have been shaking, they ought to announce what had just happened — but Aldous had trained those hands too, and they did their work while the rest of him reeled. He and Odile walked through the arch of the old granary doors, past the second pair of soldiers, out into the gray light beyond, and neither of them spoke until the customs house was a hundred yards behind them and the fog had folded itself around their shoulders.

Then Odile stopped walking.

"He took it," she said. Her voice was very small. "He took the bundle off the table in front of all of them. He—" She turned and looked back through the fog toward the customs house, where the doors had closed again and the sounds of inspection resumed. "Cyrus. They have Bram."

"They don't have the ledger."

"That isn't the same thing." She said it gently, which made it worse. "You know that isn't the same thing."

He did know. He stood in the fog with the stamped papers crushed in his fist and knew it, and the knowledge had a taste like copper. Bram had grown up three streets from Marta; that had been the entire contract. And now Bram was in a stone room with the doors shut, paying for a debt he had never owed, buying with his body the ninety seconds that arithmetic required, and Cyrus had walked past the doors on legs that worked, with papers that were stamped, because a sergeant had wanted to feel clever about ice.

"We go back," Cyrus said. "Tonight, or—"

"No." Odile's hand found his. Hers were cold and did not tremble now; the shaking had burned out of her somewhere between the table and the archway, replaced by something harder. "Not tonight. Not with the sketch of my face going up on walls. We finish the route. That's what he bought, Cyrus — he didn't buy us a rescue, he bought us the pier. If we waste it going back for him, then he's paid for nothing."

She was right. He hated that she was right, and he hated the cold competence of her rightness, and he loved her for it, in the sideways unspoken way they had agreed to love each other — in logistics, in route plans, in the division of danger.

They walked on toward the third checkpoint, toward the pier, and the fog thinned ahead of them to show the masts of the mail steamer standing against the brightening east like pins in a chart.

Behind them, faint under the cry of the gulls, came a sound from the customs yard. It might have been a crate being broken open. It might not have been.

Cyrus did not let himself decide which, because he was afraid of what deciding would cost him, and he had miles left to carry, and the tide would not wait.

But that night, waiting out the hours in the sail loft above the chandlery, he took Aldous's pencil from his breast pocket and wrote a single line in the margin of his own working copy of the atlas, small enough to hide in a sounding, true enough to survive any erasure:

*Bram, who grew up three streets from Marta.*

And across the harbor, in the lit windows of the requisitioned workshop, a lamp burned late, and a man in a good coat was reading charts by it — reading them, Cyrus would learn soon enough, the way other men read letters addressed to them personally.
