## 12. Bram's Silence

The rooftop above the chandler's shop smelled of tar and old rope, and Odile lay flat against the warm slates with her cheek pressed to the shingles as though the roof itself might hide her if she only believed in it hard enough. Below, the customs yard lay open like a stage no one had asked to perform on. The morning fog was burning off in slow rags, and through the thinning of it she could see everything: the crates stacked in their rows, the soldiers moving between them with the unhurried confidence of men who owned whatever they touched, and Bram, standing alone in the center of it all with his hands bound in front of him.

She had wanted to look away. She had told herself she would look away.

She did not look away.

Cyrus lay beside her, close enough that she could feel the tremor running through him, a fine continuous shaking like a wire under strain. He had not spoken since they climbed up here — since they fled the customs house with the ledger still sewn into Cyrus's coat lining, since they heard behind them the shout and the scuffle and the terrible sound of Bram's voice calling out something cheerful and obscene to the guards who took his arms. Now Cyrus's eyes were fixed on the yard below with an expression Odile could not bear to name, because naming it would mean admitting that the man down there was being beaten for them, that every blow landed on Bram's body was a payment made on their behalf, and that they were lying flat on a roof doing nothing, nothing, nothing.

"He'll hold," Cyrus whispered at last. It was not a statement. It was a prayer he was trying to pass off as cartography — a man who trusted measurements, trying to measure a thing that could not be measured.

Odile said nothing. She watched.

* * *

They had known Bram for eleven days. That was all. Eleven days since he had come to the workshop's back door with salt crusted in his beard and said, without preamble, "Aldous owed me a chart of the northern shoals, and I mean to collect from whoever finished it." He had looked at Cyrus, then at Odile, then at the atlas plates drying on the bench, and something in his weathered face had shifted — some calculation completing itself behind his eyes — and he had said, "Ah. So that's what the old man died of. Knowing too much."

Eleven days. In eleven days he had sketched them the route through three checkpoints on the back of a fish invoice, taught Cyrus which tide would carry a small boat past the breakwater, argued with Odile about whether a woman carrying Red Cross papers should walk with her chin up or her chin down (chin down, he insisted; chin up reads as defiance; chin down reads as grief, and grief is invisible), and laughed at his own jokes in a voice like gravel rolling downhill.

Eleven days, and now he stood in a customs yard with blood coming from his mouth, and Odile realized with a horror that felt almost physical that she did not know his full name. *Bram.* Just Bram. A smuggler's name, half a name, a name you'd shout across a harbor. And she thought: this is what we have reduced him to. This is what we have taken. A man who gave us everything and whose whole name we never thought to ask.

Below, an officer — not Reiner, someone smaller, a man with a clipboard and the posture of a clerk promoted past his competence — was speaking to Bram. They were too far away to hear words. But they could see the shape of the exchange: the officer gesturing at the crates, at the opened one with its spilled cargo of sardine tins, at the space where two fish porters with a crate of ice had been standing twenty minutes ago. And Bram, swaying slightly on his feet, answering with a shrug so elaborate, so insolent, so perfectly calibrated to waste everyone's time, that Odile felt an absurd, inappropriate laugh rise in her throat and die there as a sob.

The first blow came without ceremony. A guard stepped in from Bram's blind side and struck him across the back of the knees with a baton, and Bram went down onto the cobbles with a sound Odile felt in her own spine. Then he got up. Slowly, grinning — she could see the grin even from here, even through the blood — and spat red onto the stones, and said something that made the guards' shoulders stiffen with anger.

"Stop watching," Cyrus breathed beside her. His voice had gone thin. "Odile. Stop—"

"No." The word came out of her harder than she intended, flat and final, like a stamp on a document. "He doesn't get to be unseen. That's the whole point of everything we're doing. Nobody gets to be unseen."

Cyrus turned his head and looked at her, and in his face she saw the same recognition she had felt the night she showed him the coded margins: that they were two people who had been strangers bound by a dead man's purpose, and were becoming something else entirely, bound now by living ones.

They watched together.

* * *

The interrogation had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm was the worst part. Question. Blow. Question. Blow. The officer with the clipboard would lean in, ask something, step back; the guard would swing; Bram would fall or stagger or laugh; and then the officer would lean in again, patient as a metronome, certain that repetition would do what violence alone could not.

And through all of it — through all of it — Bram did not look up.

That was the thing Odile could not stop seeing. The rooftop where they hid was directly above the yard's north wall. If Bram lifted his eyes — if he glanced toward the chandler's roofline even once, even casually, even with the blank upward drift of a beaten man looking anywhere but at his tormentors — the guards would follow his gaze. She understood this. He understood this. Every second he kept his face aimed at the cobbles, at the officer's boots, at the middle distance of his own suffering, he was holding shut a door with his body.

Once — once — the officer grabbed Bram's jaw and wrenched his head upward, forcing him to face the north wall, and Odile's heart stopped against her ribs. For a moment Bram's eyes swept across the rooftops, dragged there against his will, and she pressed herself so flat against the slates that the tar bit into her cheek, and she thought: *he will see me, he will see us, and they will see him see.*

But Bram's gaze passed over the chandler's roof the way a man's gaze passes over weather. Nothing. No flicker. No hitch. He looked at the sky above their hiding place as if it contained nothing worth noticing, and then he let his head drop forward again, heavy, deliberate, a man closing a ledger.

"He's doing it on purpose," Cyrus whispered. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "Every second. He's choosing it. He knows exactly where we are and he's choosing, over and over, not to look."

"I know."

"How can he—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "How does a man do that?"

And Odile, who had spent months typing names into margins, who had learned that courage often looks like clerical work, found she knew the answer. "By deciding beforehand," she said quietly. "You decide before it starts. Afterward it's too late to decide anything. Aldous decided years ago. Marta decided when she stayed. Bram decided the moment he drew us that map on the back of the fish invoice." She swallowed. "It's not bravery, Cyrus. It's bookkeeping. You make the entry early, and then you don't go back on it."

Even as she said it, she knew it was a lie of comfort. It was bravery. It was exactly bravery, the kind that has no audience and wants none, and calling it bookkeeping was just her way of keeping her hands steady enough to keep watching.

* * *

The sun climbed. The fog burned off entirely, and the yard turned bright and pitiless, every detail sharpened: the blood on the cobbles, dark as spilled ink; the officer's clipboard, its pages fluttering; the other porters and fishermen being held along the far wall, forced to witness, their faces carefully empty in the way of people memorizing what they must later describe.

One of those faces belonged to the young guard from the fish market gate. Odile recognized him instantly — the boy barely older than Cyrus who had waved them through, who had seen her shaking hands and said nothing. He stood among the witnesses now, and his face was not empty at all. His face was wrecked. He watched Bram take each blow with his lips pressed white, and twice he shifted his weight as if to step forward, and twice the sergeant's glance pinned him where he stood.

*There,* Odile thought. *There is your mercy, walking around in an enemy's uniform, learning today what his mercy costs.* She felt no triumph in it. Only a bleak, complicated sorrow, the sorrow of watching a boy discover that kindness in wartime is not a small thing given freely but a debt that compounds.

The beating stopped sometime after mid-morning. Not because Bram broke — he did not break; Odile watched for it the way you watch a candle flame in wind, waiting for it to gutter, and it did not gutter — but because the officer with the clipboard ran out of questions that produced nothing, and men like him cannot abide labor without yield. Bram was hauled upright one final time, and this time he did not stand so much as hang between two guards, his legs done, his face a mask of swelling and blood, and still — still — his eyes stayed down, stayed away, stayed shut to the north wall and the two people lying on it who owed him everything.

The officer said something final. Bram answered. Odile could not hear the words, but she saw the shape of them, saw the officer's neck redden, saw the guards half-drag, half-carry Bram toward the customs house door, and just before they took him through it — just before the darkness of the doorway swallowed him — Bram turned his head.

Not toward the roof. Toward the harbor. Toward the water, where the mail steamer lay at anchor beyond the breakwater, smoke already threading from her funnel, preparing for the morning tide tomorrow. He looked at the ship for one long second. Then he smiled — Odile saw it, bloodied and crooked and absolutely unmistakable — and let himself be taken inside.

"He knows," Cyrus said hoarsely. "Somehow he knows we're still going. He looked at the ship and he—"

"He's telling us to sail." Odile's voice came out strange to her own ears, level and cold and very far away. "He's telling us not to be fools about it. Tide waits for no smuggler."

* * *

They did not move for a long time after the yard emptied. The soldiers returned to their crates; the fishermen were released and shuffled off; the cobbles were rinsed by a bored private with a bucket, the blood diluting to pink and then to nothing, as though the morning had been an ordinary morning. Odile lay on the hot slates and felt the full weight of her helplessness settle over her like the fog had settled earlier — total, suffocating, without edges.

She had spent her life believing that words were power. She typed; therefore she mattered. She encoded names; therefore she fought. But lying on this roof, she understood that there were powers words did not touch. There was no sentence she could type that would unbind Bram's hands. No margin code clever enough to reach through the customs house walls. All her art, all her precision, all her bleeding fingers over onion-skin pages — and for the man below, in this hour, she had been able to offer nothing but witness. To lie flat and watch and remember.

And yet.

And yet she thought of Aldous, dead in a doorway with his plates scattered around him. She thought of Marta, standing in her door so her face would be remembered. She thought of the baker and the ferryman who refused to leave. She thought of three hundred names in a ledger, people who had vanished without witnesses until a typist and an apprentice decided to be their witnesses. Witness was not nothing. Witness was the entire currency of the world they were trying to build on the far side of this war. Bram had bought their passage with his body, and the least, the very least, the absolute minimum she could pay him back was to see it, all of it, and carry the sight the way she carried the names.

"Cyrus." Her voice was steadier now. Something had hardened in her while she lay there, something forged rather than found. "Give me your pen."

He fumbled in his coat — careful, always careful, even now, of the lining where the original ledger lay sewn flat against his chest — and produced the pen and, after a moment's thought, the scrap of packing paper he'd used to sketch tide tables. She took both, and braced the paper against the slope of the roof, and wrote at the top of the page, in her clearest hand, the heading for a new page of the ledger:

*Bram — smuggler — who gave his silence for our passage.*

"That's not his whole name," Cyrus said softly.

"Then we'll find it. Someone in this town knows it. The harbormaster, the chandler downstairs, someone." She looked at the words she'd written, and they seemed to her suddenly the most important words she had ever set down, more important than the three hundred, because these three hundred had been recorded by Aldous, and this one was hers — hers and Cyrus's, the first entry in the ledger of their own making, the first name they chose rather than inherited. "His name goes first. Before all the others. When this is printed, when it's read abroad, when schoolchildren someday read what happened to this harbor — his name comes first, and it comes whole."

Cyrus nodded. He took the compass from his pocket — Aldous's brass compass, *For whoever finishes it* — and held it loosely in his palm, and Odile saw that his hands had stopped trembling.

"The tide turns at dawn," he said. "We sail with it. Both of us. He looked at that ship, Odile. He spent every blow making sure we'd be on it. If we hesitate now, if we get caught grieving instead of moving, then he bled for nothing, and I won't—" His voice caught. "I won't have that on my charts."

"Then we move." She folded the paper with his name-heading into the smallest possible square and tucked it inside her blouse, over her heart, where the onion-skin copies had been sewn into her coat lining until yesterday, where all the paper lives that matters. "Tonight we prepare. Tomorrow we walk through that pier checkpoint and we do not look back, because he didn't look up, and we owe him the same discipline."

They began to crawl back from the roofline, toward the trapdoor, toward the dim stairwell and the long afternoon of preparation. At the edge of the slates Odile paused and looked down one last time at the customs house, at its blank door behind which Bram hung between his guards, silent, unbroken, unnamed.

*Hold on,* she thought at him, with all the force a typist can put behind a thought, as if words could travel. *Hold on, and live, and when this is over I will come back and ask you your whole name, and write it down properly, and you can yell at me for spelling it wrong.*

Then she followed Cyrus down into the dark, where the tide tables waited, and the plan, and the hours ticking toward dawn — and somewhere below them, faint through the floorboards, the chandler's radio murmured the shipping news: *mail steamer departs on the morning tide, all passengers to present papers at the pier by six o'clock.*
