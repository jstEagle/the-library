## 14. Marta's Doorway

The morning of the fourth day came in grey and salt, the kind of light that made everything look already forgotten. Odile stood at the window of Marta's back room — they had moved there after the requisition notices went up, because Marta's house sat at the crooked end of Rope Lane where the census men grew tired of walking and counted twice as fast — and she watched the soldiers chalk numbers on doors.

She had not slept. The onion-skin pages were finished — all three hundred names typed twice over, sewn into the linings of two coats that hung now by the door like patient ghosts — but her fingers still remembered the rhythm of the keys, still ached with it, and her mind kept returning to the ledger open on the table behind her, to the column she had read eleven times now without wanting to. *Marchand, Éloise.* Her mother's name, written in Aldous's careful hand, followed by a date and the phrase he used for all of them: *destination unrecorded.*

Downstairs, Marta was making bread.

Odile could hear it — the dull knock of knuckles on dough, the scrape of the bowl, the small grunts the old woman made when she bent to the oven mouth. It was absurd, the ordinariness of it. Six days, the notice said. This was day four. The town was being emptied around them like a drawer being cleared out, cart by cart, family by family, and Marta Vasseur, seventy-one years old, who had buried a husband and two sons and one whole generation of nieces and nephews, was making bread as though there were anyone left to eat it.

"There's flour enough for two more loaves," Marta called up the stairs, as if Odile had asked. "After that we'll be eating the wallpaper."

Odile came down. The kitchen was warm and dim and smelled of yeast and coal smoke, and for a moment — only a moment — she let herself stand in it the way you stand in a place you are saying goodbye to, memorizing the chipped blue bowl, the copper rod hanging by the hearth, the crack in the window glass mended with resin and years.

"You didn't sleep," Marta said, not looking up.

"Neither did you."

"I'm too old to waste time sleeping." Marta cut the dough into rounds with hands that trembled only slightly, and her eyes, when she lifted them, were quick and dark under the white dusting of flour. "Did you finish?"

Odile hesitated. They had never said aloud, in this house, what was sewn into the coats. Marta knew anyway. Marta had always known everything that happened in this town before anyone thought to tell her; she had known about Aldous's ledger before Cyrus did, Odile suspected, and had known about the army's lists before the army finished writing them.

"Yes," Odile said.

"Good." Marta slid the first loaf into the oven and closed the iron door with her forearm bare despite the cold. "Then sit down and eat something. You can't carry a thing on an empty stomach. Not a thing in the world."

* * *

They ate bread and dripping at the little table by the window, and Marta talked the way old women talk when they have decided to give away their last coins freely — small stories, tossed out like crumbs. How Aldous Renard had come to her door twenty-two years ago with a compass in his fist and mud on his boots, asking to sketch her husband's ferry from the water. How he'd sat at this very table and eaten three slices of her plum cake and said, *Madame, your kitchen is the exact center of this coastline; I've measured it.* How she'd laughed at him then, and how, last winter, when his cough got bad, he'd come again and sat in the same chair and hadn't laughed at all.

"He gave me letters," Marta said suddenly. "That last visit. A bundle of them, tied in oilcloth. He said, *If I die, keep these until someone comes who is typing something they shouldn't be.*" She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly. "I told him he was a dramatic old fool. He said, *Yes, but keep them anyway.*"

Odile's heart turned over. "Where are they?"

Marta didn't answer. Instead she cocked her head, listening, and Odile heard it too: boots on the cobbles at the top of Rope Lane. Not two pairs. Six. Eight. The measured, unhurried tread of men doing a job they expected to finish by noon.

"The census," Odile whispered, and reached for the coats.

"Sit still." Marta's voice was flat and calm as slate. "They come to every door. We've been passed over twice because I'm old and this house leans. But they're working down the lane today, aren't they." It wasn't a question. She rose, untied her apron, folded it over the chairback, and did something Odile would remember for the rest of her life: she took the fresh loaf from the cooling rack, wrapped it in cloth, and set it on the table between them like a gift laid on an altar.

"Marta—"

"Hush now. Listen to me, girl, because I've only got a few sentences left in me worth hearing." She took Odile's face in both floury hands, and her grip was stronger than her age. "Aldous's letters are under the third floorboard from the hearth, the loose one. Oilcloth bundle, tied with green string. You take them. Don't read them here — read them where no one can hear paper turn. He wrote down things he shouldn't have known, dates and orders, things that happened before there was any war to excuse them. Understand? Before. He kept every circular, every memorandum that fool of a captain threw in the stove carelessly. Proof inside proof. The names say *they lived.* The letters say *they planned it.* One without the other is grief. Both together—" her jaw tightened— "both together is an indictment."

The boots stopped four doors down.

Odile's eyes stung. "Come with us. There's room in the plan — Bram knows the fish carts, we could hide you in—"

"In a crate of ice?" For the first time all morning, Marta smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile, unafraid. "Child, I've stood in my own doorway through two funerals and a flood. I'll stand in it for this. Someone has to be here when they count the houses. Someone has to have a face. You can erase a name off a list easy enough — ink gives up its word without a fight. But a face, standing in a doorway, looking at you while you write down *vacant*—" she shook her head. "That stays in a young man's memory whether he wants it or not. That's my work now. Mine and the ferryman's and the baker's. Let us do it."

"But they'll take you."

"Of course they'll take me." She said it the way you'd say *of course it rains.*

The knock came. Three blows, official, indifferent.

* * *

What happened next happened slowly and very quickly at once, the way catastrophes do. Marta crossed the kitchen, knelt with a grunt at the hearth, pried up the third board with a thumbnail worn to a hook, and pressed the oilcloth bundle into Odile's hands — it was heavier than she expected, dense with folded pages, tied in green string gone soft with age. Then Marta straightened her collar, wet her thumb and smoothed Odile's eyebrow where the flour had whitened it, and looked at her the way you look at someone you are entrusting with the only valuable thing you have left.

"Third coat hook," she whispered. "Take yours and go out the wash yard, over the wall into the tannery lot, and don't stop until the harbor. And Odile Marchand—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, and her voice dropped so low it was nearly inside Odile's own chest— "when you get wherever you're going, and you write the names down truly, like you promised — put mine at the bottom. Not the top. Bottom. I was never important, but I kept my door open, and that ought to count for something in the record."

Then she opened the door.

Two soldiers and a clerk with a ledger of his own — Odile registered this with a bitter, spinning clarity, *everyone keeps ledgers, everyone thinks their book is the true one* — filled the doorway. Marta stood square in the frame, small and upright, arms folded, filling it as completely as a monument fills a plaza.

"Number forty-one, Rope Lane," the clerk read. "Occupant?"

"Present," said Marta.

The clerk looked up. Whatever he saw in her face made him pause — Odile, flattened against the passage wall with the bundle burning under her coat, could see only slivers: the clerk's cap, Marta's shoulder, the grey morning beyond. "You're listed for relocation. Today. Collect essentials."

"I have collected them," Marta said. "I've been collecting them for seventy-one years. Ask your questions and be done."

Behind the soldiers, doors along the lane were opening. The baker came out in his flour-whitened apron and stood in his doorway. Old Joris the ferryman appeared at the corner, cap in hand, and planted himself like a fencepost. Odile understood, with a pang that felt like a rib cracking, that they had arranged this among themselves — that this was the work Marta had described, and they had come to do it together, standing, visible, *counted*, so that whatever the clerk's pen wrote next, it would have to write past their faces first.

She should have run then. The wash yard, the wall, the tannery lot — Marta's instructions, precise as a map. But her feet would not move, and some treacherous part of her wanted to see it, wanted to be the one who witnessed, because that was the whole terrible economy they were all caught in now: someone had to watch, or none of it had ever happened.

The clerk asked his questions. Name. Age. Family. Marta answered each one clearly, loudly enough to carry down the lane, spelling her own name letter by letter as though instructing a schoolboy, and Odile realized she was doing it deliberately — giving him her name in full, in syllables, forcing it into his mouth and his book. *V-A-S-S-E-U-R.* "Write it properly," Odile heard her say. "There have been Vasseurs on this coast since before your empire had a flag."

One of the soldiers shifted, uneasy. The young one — the same boy from the fish market gate? Perhaps. Perhaps every young soldier looked the same in the grey light, barely older than Cyrus, holding his rifle like something he'd been handed by mistake.

And then, in the narrow wedge of visible world, Odile saw Marta do the smallest thing. While the clerk bent to write — head down, pen scratching — Marta turned her head a fraction toward the passage, toward the dark where Odile stood, and lifted her chin once. A nod. A dismissal. *Go.* The gesture of a woman shooing a child out of a kitchen before the grown talk starts, or sending her out into the world because the staying has become more dangerous than the leaving.

*Marta,* Odile thought, *Marta, wait—*

But there was nothing to wait for. There was no version of this where she stepped out of the passage and changed anything except the number of people taken. The arithmetic was merciless and Cyrus had taught it to her without meaning to: distances, timings, weights. If she stepped out, the bundle was found, the letters burned, the names lost, and Marta taken anyway — plus one. The ledger said so itself, in Aldous's level hand, entry after entry: the ones who stepped forward were recorded, and the ones who ran were erased, and neither survived. Except — her hands closed on the oilcloth through her coat — except whoever carried.

So she carried. She went backward down the passage on legs that shook, out through the wash yard where the laundry lines hung empty and swaying, up and over the wall with the green-string bundle grinding against her ribs, and she was halfway across the tannery lot before the sound reached her: the clerk's voice, raised now, and Marta's, higher, and then the sound Odile knew she would hear for the rest of her life, the specific sound of an old woman being taken hold of by soldiers and refusing, audibly, to hurry.

She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury of people whose cargo weighed nothing. She put her head down and ran, and the tears came sideways across her vision with the speed of it, tearing loose into the wind, and what she felt was not only grief. Grief she knew how to carry; grief had been her companion since she read her mother's name in the ledger. This was grief's hotter sibling, the one that clenched the fists: *fury*. They had done this in daylight, in front of doors, with pens. With *pens*. The same instrument she had used all those nights to resurrect the dead, and they used theirs to unmake the living, and both pens were just pens, and the difference was only ever the hand that held them.

At the wall's far corner she finally stopped, gasping, and looked back once — allowed herself the single look — and saw the lane emptying, the soldiers marching Marta and the baker and Joris away between them, unhurried, and Marta walking in the middle with her spine straight as a mast, not stumbling, not weeping, going toward the carts like a woman keeping an appointment.

Odile pressed her forehead against the cold brick and let the fury burn through her until what remained was fuel.

* * *

Cyrus found her an hour later in the loft above the cooperage, where Bram had said no one would look before noon. She had the bundle open on the floor between her knees and the green string coiled beside it like something severed, and she was reading Aldous's letters by the grey light from the skylight, page after oiled page, and she was crying without any sound at all, which frightened him — he said later that her silence was worse than sobbing.

He knelt. "What is it? What did she—"

"They took her." Odile's voice came out sanded flat. "In the doorway. Standing up. She made me go first." She pushed the letters toward him across the boards. "Look. Look what he kept."

Cyrus read. She watched his face change as he read — the way a coastline changes on a chart as the soundings deepen, everything shifting from safe water to danger marked in close-set lines. The letters were Aldous's, in his drafting-room hand, and interleaved with them were copies — carbon copies, cunningly thin — of military circulars dated *before* the war. Directives for the administrative clearing of coastal populations. Schedules. Allocations of transport. A list of harbors, with this town's name third from the top, and a date beside it eighteen months past. Orders predating the war that supposedly required them. Proof that none of it was necessity. All of it was intention.

"My God," Cyrus said quietly. "He wasn't just recording it. He was building a case."

"A case needs somewhere to be tried." Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, once. "We get this on the steamer. Both. The copies and the original. Names and orders. What happened, and that it was decided in advance by men who signed their initials." She gathered the pages, squared them with a violence of neatness, rewrapped the oilcloth. "She told me to put her name at the bottom of the list. At the *bottom*. Because she was never important." Her voice cracked on the last word and she forced it straight again. "When we make the new page — Bram's name at the head, like I swore — Marta goes beneath him. And the baker. And Joris. Every one of them who stood in a door."

Cyrus was silent a moment. Outside, a gull cried over the harbor where the steamer waited, one tide away.

"Then we'd better make sure there's a list to write them on," he said. "Tide turns at dawn."

Odile tied the green string in a knot that would not slip, and held the bundle against her chest, evidence within evidence, grief braided with purpose, and looked at the man who would carry it through the water while she carried the copies through the gate — and neither of them yet knowing which of them the morning would spare.
