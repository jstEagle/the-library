## 15. Splitting the Cargo

The tide table said four hours and eleven minutes. Cyrus had it written on his wrist in pencil, copied from the almanac in Aldous's drawer, and he kept looking at it as though the numbers might rearrange themselves into something kinder. Four hours to divide everything they owned — which was not much: a ledger, a coat with sewn seams, a brass compass, a leaking dinghy, and whatever it was that had grown between two people who had never found a word for it.

Bram's map of escape lay open on Odile's kitchen table, weighted at the corners with a teacup, a spool of thread, a candle stub, and the compass itself. Bram had drawn it in the smuggler's shorthand he used for contraband routes: an X for the fish market gate, a hatched square for the customs house, a bold stroke for the pier where the mail steamer would take on passengers before dawn. Beyond the breakwater, in water too shallow for patrol boats but deep enough for a pilot cutter, he had drawn a small circle and labeled it, in his blunt carpenter's hand, *the meeting place*. Bram was in the customs yard now, in a cell or a chair or worse, and the circle waited for one of them all the same.

"We should say it plainly," Odile said.

Cyrus looked up from the tide table. She stood at the window with her back to him, watching the harbor where the steamer's mast lights had begun to show through the last of the night's dark. She had not slept. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, which she held straight by effort rather than habit, and in her hands, which hung still at her sides — hands that were never still, that lived on keys and needles and the hem of things, hanging quiet only when they had run out of work to hide behind.

"Say what plainly?" he asked, though he knew.

"Which of us takes which risk." She turned. The candle caught the ink stains on her fingers, the ones that never washed out entirely, blue-black crescents under every nail. "Because we are each of us going to argue for the worse half of it, and I'd rather we did the arithmetic first."

So they did the arithmetic. It was what Aldous would have done; Cyrus felt the old man's presence at his shoulder like a draft from the workshop door. Distances first. From this room to the pier checkpoint: nine hundred meters, mostly straight streets, one corner where a sentry stood beneath a gas lamp. From the room down to the slipway where the dinghy sat among a dozen other small boats: three hundred meters, all of it downhill, all of it exposed to the water where patrol lamps swept at intervals Bram had timed once and Cyrus had memorized. Times next. A woman walking with papers could pass the pier checkpoint in under a minute if nothing went wrong. A man rowing a quarter mile against chop, with a lamp sweeping the water every six minutes, needed at least twenty, and the steamer would not wait past the turn of tide.

Then the weights. This was the part Cyrus made himself say aloud, because it was the part that mattered.

"The original is the proof," he said. "Typewritten pages can be called forgery. Copies can be called rumor. The ledger in Aldous's own hand, with his notations, his dates, his seal in the cover board — that cannot be argued with. Whoever carries it has to survive, because if it's lost there is no second one." He put his flat hand on the table, on the space beside the compass where the ledger itself lay wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards below their feet. "But whoever carries it will be searched less carefully than anyone walking a checkpoint. No one opens crates on the water. They only sink them."

"And the copies," Odile said, "are already sewn into my coat lining. Twelve pages at the back, twelve at each front panel, onion-skin against wool. If they search me, they find them only if they cut the coat apart, and a Red Cross clerk's coat gets inspected, not destroyed." Her voice was level. Too level. He had heard her read names aloud from that voice, three hundred of them, the night the candle burned to a puddle. "But the pier checkpoint has a sergeant with a sketch, Cyrus. My face is nearly on it. Nearly. That is the worst kind of nearly."

"There's no sketch on the water."

"There's a lamp, a rifle, and cold water, and you can't swim in a coat full of paper any better than I can talk my way past a wanted poster."

They stood on either side of the table with the dead man's compass between them, and the argument went the way arguments go between people who have already decided: slowly, and then all at once, and then in reverse.

"I take the boat," Cyrus said.

"You take the boat," Odile agreed, and then heard herself, and stopped. "No. That isn't — Cyrus, the ledger is the whole point. If the ledger survives and we don't, the town still exists. If we survive and the ledger doesn't—" She pressed her palms flat to the table. "I typed those names. Every one. I know them in my fingers the way you know coastlines in yours. Let mine be the fingers that carry them out."

"That's why it must be me." He hadn't meant to say it so hard. The words came out with edges. "You've carried them since Aldous was alive. You typed your mother's name into a margin knowing what it meant. You've paid more than your share." He made himself lower his voice. Made it gentle, because the truth underneath was not gentle and he didn't want it to sound like an accusation. "Odile. One of us has to be the one whose loss the other can't afford. And I'm telling you it can't be you."

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, far off, a patrol engine coughed and settled into its idle drone along the quay.

"And I'm telling you," she said finally, "that if you row out there and the lamp finds you and I'm standing on that gangplank with a forged Red Cross card in my pocket, I will walk onto that ship knowing you died for paper. And if I walk through that checkpoint and you're the one who drowns, then every name I saved will have been bought with the one name I—" She stopped. The sentence had gone somewhere she hadn't authorized it to go.

Cyrus held very still, the way he held still over a plate when the ink was wet.

"The one name you what?" he asked. Not pressing. Just holding the door open.

Odile looked at the compass instead of at him. Its brass lid caught the candlelight and threw a small warm coin of it onto the ceiling. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — the levelness gone, something rawer under it.

"I planned this whole morning around keeping you alive," she said. "Did you know that? While I was sewing pages into this coat at three in the morning, I was sewing them in a pattern that puts the thickest layer over my left side, because if anything comes at me it will come from the checkpoint side, which will be on my right as I queue, unless the queue doubles back, in which case — I worked it out, Cyrus. I spent the hours you slept working out how to stand, which way to hold my elbow, how to arrange my scarf so the bulk wouldn't show. All of it so that I would walk off that pier and you would be somewhere behind me, wet and cold and swearing at the oars, and alive." She laughed, short and unsteady. "And here you are, planning the exact mirror of it. We are two people building the same wall from opposite sides."

"Then let's build it properly," Cyrus said. "Together. Which means neither of us gets to choose the brave option alone."

* * *

They split the cargo on the kitchen floor, kneeling across from each other like children over a game.

The ledger came up from under the floorboards wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord. Cyrus unwrapped it only halfway, enough to check the cord's knots, and ran his thumb along the spine where Aldous's blind-stamped mark sat in the leather — the little anchor-and-star that appeared on every plate the master ever drew. Three hundred names inside, in a hand steadier than any Cyrus could produce, recording a town that by nightfall would officially have never existed.

He rewrapped it. Then he took the oilskin sack he had prepared and packed the ledger into it the way Aldous had taught him to pack plates: snug against the frame of the thing, no room to shift, weight distributed low. In this case the frame was a bread tin, borrowed from Marta's empty kitchen two doors down, its bottom lined with cork. The tin would float for a minute or two even if the sack went into the water. A minute or two was not rescue. But it was not nothing, and Aldous had believed in measuring in what can be carried, and Cyrus had learned at his knee that *not nothing* was often the whole difference between a coastline and a blank page.

Into the sack's outer pocket he put the compass. Then he took it out. Then he put it back, and stood looking at it.

"For whoever finishes it," he said aloud.

"It's yours," Odile said from the doorway where she was doing something unnecessary to her sleeve. "He gave it to you. I saw him do it."

"He engraved it before I ever apprenticed here. Before you ever typed a label. He engraved it and then he waited eleven years to find out who he'd made it for." Cyrus closed his fist around the brass. It was warm from his hand already; it always warmed fast, like something living. "I keep thinking about that. Eleven years. He wasn't waiting to see who deserved it. He was making sure whoever got it would understand what it cost."

"Then take it on the water," Odile said. "Where understanding matters."

He nodded and did not trust his voice after that, so he busied his hands.

Her coat came next. She put it on and turned her back to him and he went over the seams one last time, feeling through the wool for the ridges of the onion-skin packets, counting them by touch the way he counted soundings. Twelve at the left front. Twelve at the right. Twelve at the center back, where a searcher's hands naturally flattened cloth and might, if careless, feel the wrong kind of thickness. He had argued for leaving the back panels empty and lost the argument; she'd said the back was the least likely to be patted twice, and she had typed the ledger so she knew which names could afford thinner margins. He had not understood her reasoning until she explained it, and then he understood it better than she did, and told her so, and she had said *don't flatter the coat, check the coat.*

He checked the coat. The stitches were small and even and invisible unless you knew. He had watched her make them by candlelight with bleeding fingers and had not once been allowed to help.

"Papers," he said.

She handed them over: her forged Red Cross clerk's card, the matron's letter of escort that the actual Red Cross office had supplied two days ago for a woman matching no description at all, and the third-class steamer ticket. Cyrus checked them the way he checked a chart against the coast — date, spelling, the stamp that was supposed to be smudged being smudged in exactly the believable way. Everything was correct. Everything would pass, if faces passed.

"The sketch is almost you," he said again. He couldn't help it. Saying it was like touching a bruise.

"Almost," she said. "The sketch gives me brown eyes."

"Your eyes are brown."

"In lamplight they're gray, and the sergeant will have a lamp." She took the papers back and slid them into the inner pocket against her body. "I've been practicing looking bored. Bored people don't get looked at. I intend to be the most bored woman on that pier."

He believed her. That was the terrible thing; he believed her completely, and belief didn't keep people safe, it only made their absence afterward harder to bear.

* * *

They came down to the slipway in the last dark, moving through alleys that smelled of kelp and coal smoke, past shuttered houses where nobody who had stayed was sleeping either. At the head of the stone steps Cyrus stopped and took her hand — took it without deciding to, the way a hand goes out to steady itself — and they stood a moment looking at the harbor. The mail steamer rode at her mooring with her ports lit, small and ordinary and impossibly far away beyond the gray line of the breakwater. Somewhere along the quay a patrol lamp swung its slow arc, painting the water silver and letting it go dark again.

"Say the plan back to me," Odile said.

"I row from the slipway north of the fish market within ten minutes of now. I keep inside the shadow of the mole until the breakwater, then cross the gap on the dark half of the lamp cycle — six minutes between sweeps, five if the wind drops. I meet the pilot cutter at Bram's circle. I give the signal — two flashes, a pause, one flash. The cutter takes me and the tin to the steamer's far side, and we go up the sea ladder like stowaways, which we technically are." He paused. "You queue at the pier gate when the matron arrives at half past four. Papers first, then the crate inspection lane, then the gangplank. You are bored. You do not look at the sergeant longer than at anyone else. You board, you find the saloon, you sit near the rail facing land, and you watch the water."

"And if the cutter isn't there?"

"Then I row alongside and climb, same ladder, less dignity."

"And if the lamp—"

"Odile."

"No," she said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she let it. "You don't get to stop my questions with my own name. If the lamp finds you, I want to know what you want me to do, because I will be standing on that gangplank and I need to know, Cyrus, I need to know whether you want me to come back for you—"

"No." He said it fast and flat, before the word could grow any alternatives inside itself. "If the lamp finds me, you get on that ship. That's the plan. That was always the plan. Every version of the plan we didn't say out loud had that one line in common."

The wind moved along the water and pushed a strand of hair across her face and she didn't fix it, and he didn't either, though his hand started to.

"Here is what I want instead," she said. "Since we're saying things plainly."

"Say it."

"I want you to come back. Not the ledger. Not the names. Not the proof. You." Her chin came up, the way it did when she was about to type something difficult. "Aldous gave the atlas to the world and it killed him and it was worth it, fine, I'll sign my name under that. But I have buried one man I loved already this year and I am telling you now, on this stair, in the cold, before either of us can pretend later it was never said — the town needs its names and I need you, and if you trade the second for the first I will spend whatever's left of my life making the atlas famous and being furious about it. Do you understand the terms?"

Cyrus stood very still. The harbor light shifted; the lamp swept past and left them in the dark again, and somehow that made it possible.

"I understand the terms," he said. "Counteroffer."

"Don't you dare negotiate with me—"

"I intend to live," he said. "Not for the ledger. For the person who taught me that a margin can hold a family. I row well. I've been rowing this coast since I was nine. The lamp has a six-minute cycle and I have a compass engraved by a man who anticipated everything, including his own death, and specifically so that I wouldn't have one." His thumb moved over the brass in his pocket. "I am going to come up that sea ladder wet and swearing, exactly as you described, and you are going to be at the rail pretending to be bored, and you are going to fail at pretending, and I am going to see your face before I see anything else in this world. Those are my terms."

For a moment she didn't answer, and the sea said all the things neither of them could — slapping the stones, patient, indifferent, keeping its own count of every promise ever made on this shore. Then Odile reached up and fixed the strand of her own hair, deliberately, as if completing some formality, and kissed him once, quickly, on the mouth, like sealing an envelope.

"Notarized," she said, unsteady.

"Notarized," he agreed.

Then there was nothing left to plan and no time left to be afraid in private, so they were afraid together for one more breath, hands clasped on the rail between them, and let go.

* * *

The dinghy came off the stones easier than he feared. The leak was the slow kind, a seep at the sternpost that the bilge block could outrun if he bailed every quarter hour. Cyrus pulled north along the mole's shadow with the bread tin wedged between his feet and the oilskin sack lashed under the forward thwart, and the town slid away behind him window by window — Marta's doorway, dark now, standing open to the street even so; the bakery's cold chimney; the workshop roofline where he had spent seven years learning how much world a man could carry.

At the gap in the breakwater the current took the bow and shoved, and he corrected, and the first sweep of the patrol lamp came around the point ahead of schedule because the wind had dropped, just as Odile said it might. Five minutes now, maybe less.

Cyrus shipped the oars and let the dinghy lie quiet on the black water, close under the stone, and put his hand in his pocket and closed it around the compass. *For whoever finishes it.* Ahead, beyond the breakwater, the steamer's riding lights burned small and steady, and past them, fainter, the pilot cutter's single lantern at Bram's circle, exactly where a smuggler who was currently being interrogated in a customs yard had promised it would be.

The lamp swept toward him. Cyrus counted under his breath, watching the light crawl across the water toward the little boat with its bread tin and its three hundred names, and began to pray in the only language he had left — distances, timings, the arithmetic of survival.

Four minutes. Three. The light touched the breakwater's end, ten meters short of the boat, and swept on.

Cyrus bent his back to the oars and pulled for the gap.

Behind him, inland, thin over the water, he thought he heard the pier clock begin to strike the half hour — half past four, the matron arriving, Odile stepping into the queue with her coat full of resurrected names — and there was nothing to do about any of it now but row, and trust, and not look back.
