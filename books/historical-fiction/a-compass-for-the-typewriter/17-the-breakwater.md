## 17. The Breakwater

The dinghy was called the *Speranza*, which was a joke, or a prayer, depending on how you looked at her — and Cyrus Voss had never looked at anything in his life the way he looked at that boat now, crouched at the waterline beneath the collapsed pilings of the old net-drying shed, watching her take on water through a seam that Bram's caulking had only half mended.

"She'll float," Bram had said that afternoon, before the customs house took him. "She won't float *well*. There's a difference, and you'll learn it personally."

Cyrus had laughed then. It seemed impossible now that he had ever laughed.

The tide would turn at 4:40. He knew this not from the almanac but from Odile, who had written it on his palm in pencil two nights ago as they bent over the split of their cargo, her finger tracing the numbers into his skin as though she could type them there permanently. *Tide turns 4:40. Ship stands off the breakwater at first light. You have one window and it is narrow.* Her handwriting had been steady. Her hand had not been. He had felt the tremor travel through her fingers into his palm like a small animal trying to get warm.

The ledger sat inside an oilskin packet lashed against his chest, beneath his shirt, against the place where his heart did its foolish, frightened work. Three hundred names. He had carried heavier things in his life — a crate of copper plates down the workshop stairs, Aldous himself when the fever finally dropped him, dead weight in every sense — but nothing had ever pressed against him like this. Paper should be light. Everyone believed paper was light. Cyrus now knew that paper was the heaviest substance on earth, because paper held people, and people were heavier than stone.

He checked the compass for the hundredth time. No light — he dared no light — so he held its face close to his eyes and let the last of the moon do the work. The needle swung, wavered, settled. East by northeast, out past the breakwater, to where the mail steamer *Corinthian* rode her anchor lights like a constellation someone had pinned low over the water. The brass case was warm from his pocket. The engraving caught what little light there was: *For whoever finishes it.*

Aldous had known. That was the thing Cyrus kept circling back to in the dark hours, the thought he could neither land nor shake off. The master had engraved those words years ago — before the war, before the requisition orders, before the ledger, before any of it — and Cyrus had read them a thousand times without once understanding them. A compass made for an apprentice who didn't yet exist. A tool waiting for its hands. And now here he was, crouched under a ruined shed with the sea slapping the pilings above his head, holding a dead man's instrument toward a living ship, and understanding — finally, stupidly, completely — why Aldous had measured everything the way he had.

Not in miles. Not in fathoms. Aldous had measured the world in what can be carried.

* * *

He launched at half past three, pushing off from the pilings with the oars shipped flat so they wouldn't knock, and the cold came up through the hull boards immediately, soaking the seat of his trousers, finding the gap between collar and neck as though the sea had been waiting all night for exactly this moment to introduce itself properly.

The chop was worse than Bram had promised. A groundswell out of the northeast lifted the little boat and dropped her again with a laziness that was somehow more frightening than violence — she wasn't being broken, she was being *handled*, turned over in enormous indifferent fingers. Cyrus got both oars working and pulled east-northeast, counting strokes the way Aldous had taught him to count paces when surveying: *one, two, three, four*, each number a small brick laid in the wall between himself and panic.

The harbor behind him was dark in the wrong way. Towns that are merely sleeping still breathe — lamplight in a window, a dog, a cart somewhere, the sound of a door. This town held its breath. Six days ago it had been full of lives; now it was an inventory, and inventories don't breathe. He could see the customs house roofline, black against blacker sky. Somewhere behind that wall Bram was sitting in a cell, or standing, if they'd let him stand. Cyrus pulled harder and tried not to think about the sounds he'd heard drifting up from the yard yesterday, and failed, and pulled harder anyway.

A quarter mile out, the leak announced its opinion of the voyage. Water was coming through the seam faster now, gathering around his ankles in a shallow cold pool that rose with each swell. He bailed with the tin cup wedged under the thwart — scoop, throw, scoop, throw, four strokes of the oars, scoop, throw — and understood that this was the arithmetic now. Every stroke forward cost water taken in; every cup thrown bought four strokes more. Distance purchased at the rate of a leaking hull. Aldous would have drawn it as a curve on graph paper, the exchange rate of progress against ruin, and labeled it something dry and precise. Cyrus just kept rowing.

The lamp found him at twenty minutes out.

It came off the patrol boat without warning — no engine note he'd heard, no running light until the instant there was one — a great white blade of light that swept across the water and caught the *Speranza* broadside and then, mercifully, swept on. Cyrus froze with the oars mid-stroke, blades dripping, and the boat drifted, and the beam swung back toward him along the surface like a searchlight reading a page.

He did what Aldous had drilled into every apprentice in the first week, back when it was only a lesson about instruments and not about survival: *when you cannot control the instrument, become part of the landscape.* He shipped the oars. He lay down flat in the bilge, face against the wet boards, the oilskin packet digging into his ribs, and he let the boat look like what she was supposed to look like — a derelict, a lost skiff, a piece of wreckage riding the swell. The water sloshed around his chin. His hands, gripping the gunwales below the waterline, shook so badly he could feel the vibration traveling through the whole hull, and he thought absurdly: *she's leaking and I'm shaking, we're making the same confession.*

The beam crossed him again. Held.

Held — one second, two, five — white light through his closed eyelids, turning the darkness red, and Cyrus lay in three inches of seawater with three hundred names against his chest and did the only navigation left to him. He steered his own breath. In for four. Hold for two. Out for six. The way Odile breathed when the typing went wrong and she had to pull a page and start again; he had watched her do it a dozen times across the workshop without knowing he was memorizing it, the way you memorize coastlines without meaning to, until suddenly the chart is in your hand and you cannot say when you drew it.

The beam moved on.

He lay there another full minute after the light died, letting the swell rock him, listening to his own heart bang against the oilskin like a fist on a locked door. Then he sat up, took the compass out, opened it against his chest, and read the needle in the shelter of his own body. East by northeast. Still true. Everything else in the world had proved negotiable — home, safety, the names on doors, the law, mercy — but the needle swung the same as it had swung for Aldous, and the same as it would swing tomorrow, and there was a ferocious comfort in that. Some things did not answer to orders.

He rowed.

* * *

The breakwater rose out of the sea ahead of him like the spine of some drowned animal — granite blocks stacked in two long arms, green with weed below the waterline, white with bird filth above, the channel between them marked by a buoy that clanked in the swell with a rhythm like slow, idiot applause. Beyond it, the *Corinthian* lay at her anchor station, a dark mass wearing three lights, and Cyrus's chest tightened with something that was not quite hope and not quite relief and was mostly terror wearing their clothes. She was real. She was there. She was leaving on the morning tide whether he reached her or not, and the sea between was doing its best to make sure he didn't.

The leak had grown ambitions. Water stood above his ankles now, and the *Speranza* rode lower, sluggishly, answering the oars the way a tired horse answers a whip — technically, resentfully. He bailed and rowed and bailed and rowed, and set his mind to work the way Aldous had taught him to set it when a survey went bad: *give the fear a job.* So he gave it the names.

He recited them silently as he pulled. Marta Brenner, doorway of the corner house on Salt Lane. Tomas the ferryman, who had refused the census men with such courtesy they'd needed three visits. The baker Oskar and his wife and their four children, whose names he knew because he'd bought bread from them every Sunday of his life and had never once, until this week, thought to write any of them down. Name after name after name, three hundred of them, and he found that the strange grace of it was this: they weighed exactly as much as he needed them to weigh. When his arms burned, the names were heavy enough to keep him honest about why. When his courage thinned, they were light enough to carry. That was the trick of it. That was the whole secret Aldous had built the atlas around and never said aloud, because saying it aloud would have sounded too simple: you measure the world in what can be carried, and what can be carried is names, and names weigh nothing, and names weigh everything, and the difference depends entirely on who is doing the lifting.

The channel mouth swallowed him.

Inside the breakwater the chop eased all at once, the granite arms breaking the groundswell, and the *Speranza* steadied beneath him as if grateful. The buoy's clanking fell astern. Ahead, maybe four hundred yards, the steamer's hull rose sheer and black, and he could see movement on her deck — lanterns, shapes, the ordinary industry of a ship preparing to sail. Ordinary. He wanted to laugh at how beautiful ordinary things became when you had rowed through a patrol boat's searchlight to reach them. A man coiling rope. Steam rising from a galley vent. A bell struck twice somewhere deep in her body.

He raised one arm and waved his cap in slow circles, the signal Bram had described — three passes, pause, three passes — and watched, and waited, and the leak continued its patient work around his knees.

Nothing happened. The shapes moved. The rope got coiled.

He waved again, three passes, pause, three passes, arms aching, and this time a lantern appeared at the rail and swung once — acknowledgment — and began to descend. A boarding ladder, unlit, going down the ship's flank toward the water. Toward him. Toward the names.

That was when he heard the engine.

It came from behind the breakwater's eastern arm, low and throttled-down, the particular unhurried thrum of a patrol boat on a routine circuit, and Cyrus's whole body went cold in a way the seawater hadn't managed. They were coming around the point. In ninety seconds, perhaps less, they would enter the channel, and their lamp would find a leaking dinghy and a man in a rowboat four hundred yards off a departing steamer at dawn, and no landscape in the world would be big enough for him to hide inside.

There was no decision to make, which was the strangest part. He had imagined, all these weeks, that the terrible moments would arrive with choices attached — that he would stand at some crossroads and choose bravely. But the crossroads had all been behind him, small and unremarkable at the time: a yes to Odile in the workshop, a nod to Bram at the fish market, a hand under a floorboard. Now there was only the doing. He dug the oars in and pulled for the ladder with everything the *Speranza* had left, which was not much — she wallowed, shipping water, the seam groaning — and he counted strokes and named names and rowed as if the rowing were a prayer that required no belief, only effort.

The patrol boat's bow slid around the breakwater arm, a black shape against grey, and its lamp bloomed.

Light leapt across the channel — and found the steamer's hull first, climbing her flank in a blaze, and the sailors at the ladder sawed the lines and hauled it up and vanished behind the rail with a speed that told Cyrus everything about how many times they had done this before. The beam swung down toward the water. Toward him. Fifty yards to the ladder. Forty. He stopped rowing, shipped the oars, and grabbed for the ladder's lowest rung as the light hit the empty spot where his boat had been a heartbeat earlier.

The *Speranza*, unburdened, unguided, and thoroughly finished, rolled gently onto her side and began to sink with what Cyrus could only describe as dignity.

"Hands," said a voice above him, and hands there were — hard, salt-cracked, impersonal, hauling him up the rungs while the patrol boat's lamp probed the channel below, sweeping back and forth across a patch of disturbed water, a floating cap, and nothing else. Cyrus came over the rail in a tangle of arms, collapsed onto wet deck planking, and lay there gasping like landed fish while the ship's engines woke beneath him with a shudder he felt through his whole chest.

A sailor crouched beside him. Older, grey-bearded, smelling of tar. "Packet," the man said quietly, not asking, and Cyrus understood — Odile had arranged it all, of course she had, Odile and her thin pages and her bleeding fingers — and he drew the oilskin bundle from his shirt and put it into the stranger's hands with the care of a man setting down something that has been carried a very long way.

The sailor weighed it in one palm, nodded once, and stowed it inside his coat.

Below and astern, through the rail, Cyrus watched the patrol boat circle the sinking dinghy, its lamp playing over the swells, its crew leaning out to look at nothing. Then the *Corinthian*'s anchor chain began to come home, link by rattling link, and the great ship leaned into the turn toward open water, and the harbour — the seized workshops, the posted notices, the doorways where Marta and Tomas and Oskar had stood to be remembered — began, block by block, to shrink.

He got to his feet at the stern rail because he could not stay lying down, because some part of him refused to meet the horizon horizontally. The town fell away behind them. The patrol boat grew small. The sun was coming up behind the hills in a smear of iron and rose, lighting the water gold all the way to the breakwater, and Cyrus Voss, mapmaker's apprentice, soaked to the waist and shaking and alive, gripped the rail and searched the shrinking quay for one figure, one coat, one silhouette among the few early shapes moving along the pier.

The gangway at the pier was crowded. Red Cross uniforms. Clerks. And somewhere in that crowd, walking toward the third checkpoint with three hundred names sewn into the lining of her coat, was Odile.
