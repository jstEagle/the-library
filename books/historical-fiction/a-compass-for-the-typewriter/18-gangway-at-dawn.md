## 18. Gangway at Dawn

*Odile*

The queue at the gangplank moved like something wounded — slowly, in shuffles and small collapses, each person advancing only when the person ahead had been pronounced acceptable. Odile stood in it with her Red Cross satchel pressed to her hip, the onion-skin pages sewn into the lining of her coat whispering against her ribs with every breath, three hundred names riding just above her heart.

The tide was turning. She could feel it in the pier's timbers, a long muscular shudder beneath her feet, as if the harbor itself were trying to decide whether to let go of them or hold on forever. Above, gulls wheeled through a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the mail steamer *Aurelia* sat low in the water, her stacks breathing pale smoke, her rails already crowded with faces that looked down at the quay the way people look into wells.

Odile counted her own breathing. In for four. Out for four. The sergeant at the foot of the gangplank was checking papers against a clipboard, and beside him, propped on an easel where everyone could see it, stood a charcoal sketch of a woman's face.

It was almost hers.

That was the terrible thing about it — not that it was accurate but that it was nearly so. Someone had described her badly. The nose was too narrow, the mouth too generous, the hair pinned in a style she had never worn. And yet the eyes were right. Whoever had drawn those eyes had seen her, had stood close enough to be seen back, and Odile could not stop herself from cataloguing who it might have been: the clerk at the requisition office, the woman who sold candles, the guard at the fish market gate with his young, kind, unreadable face.

She had done this before, she reminded herself. She had walked past soldiers every day for two years, typing their orders with fingers that never trembled until the work was safe inside the margins. Trembling now would be a betrayal of every name in her coat.

But her hands shook anyway. She folded them into the fabric of her skirt and pretended to adjust her glove.

"You there." The sergeant beckoned. "Papers."

She produced them — Bram's forger friend had been worth every coin, and the Red Cross matron's countersignature sat in the corner like a small blue benediction. The sergeant took the folder, looked at her face, looked at the sketch, looked back at her face. Behind him the line waited; behind her, somewhere out on the gray water, Cyrus was rowing toward the breakwater in a boat that leaked, and she could not think about that, she absolutely could not —

"Eyes forward," the sergeant said, though she hadn't moved. He held the sketch up beside her head as if comparing two samples of cloth. A wind came off the sea and fluttered the paper. For one absurd moment Odile wanted to laugh at the whole apparatus of it — the easel, the clipboard, the charcoal eyes that saw everything and understood nothing.

"Matron," the sergeant called without lowering his gaze. "Your clerk?"

The Red Cross matron came forward at her own pace, unhurried, a stout woman in a veil whose starched wings caught the dawn light like sails. She took Odile's papers, glanced at them, and handed them back with a briskness that had the sound of authority.

"My clerk," she said. "With me since spring. She types a fair hand and faints at the sight of blood, which in my experience is the mark of a useful woman." She turned to Odile, and her voice dropped to something only the two of them could hear, dry as old paper and twice as knowing. "Stand straight, girl. Your coat is heavier than you are. I've carried such coats myself, in another war, in another color. Walk up that plank as if your mother is watching from the rail, because one day you'll want to tell her you did."

Odile's throat closed. All she could manage was a nod, but the matron received it as if it were a speech.

The sergeant looked at the sketch a moment longer. Then he lowered it, waved his hand at the gangplank, and turned to the next person in line, already forgetting her, already bored, a man doing his job in the cold while the tide pulled at his boots.

Odile climbed.

The plank swayed under her feet. Halfway up she made the mistake of looking down at the water, at the gray chop rolling between the pier pilings, and thought of Cyrus out there beyond the breakwater, small and alone and carrying everything original, everything irreplaceable, in a canvas wrap lashed to his chest. If the patrol boat found him — if the lamp swept across the water at the wrong moment —

Don't think it. Type it instead, the way she typed everything: one letter, then the next, then the next. One step, then the next.

Her feet touched steel deck. A steward asked for her ticket. Somewhere behind her, the matron was arguing cheerfully with a customs officer about crates of bandages. The *Aurelia*'s engines turned over beneath Odile's shoes, a vibration like a second heartbeat, and she went to the rail and gripped it with both hands and stared down at the harbor that had eaten her mother and was now, God willing, about to give her back her life.

The water lay empty all the way to the breakwater.

*Cyrus*

The dinghy leaked the way old men talk — steadily, without malice, refusing to be argued with. Cyrus bailed with the tin cup wedged between his knees and rowed with everything his shoulders had left, and the breakwater came toward him in slow, agonizing increments, stone by stone by stone.

The canvas wrap rode against his chest, strapped tight the way Bram had shown him, and inside it the ledger lay in its oilcloth, three hundred names wrapped around brass plates and Aldous's compass, the whole weight of the town balanced against the pull of the sea. Every time the dinghy shipped water, Cyrus's first thought — before the cold, before the fear — was *the ledger*, and he would shift his body between the wrap and the incoming wave like a man shielding a candle.

He had stopped being afraid somewhere behind the fish market, or rather the fear had changed state, solidified into something he could use. This was what Aldous must have known at the end, Cyrus thought, hauling on the oars while the patrol boat's searchlight swung somewhere behind him like a slow white moon. That terror was only information. That a coastline was nothing but a decision about distances, and courage nothing but arithmetic done honestly. Two hundred strokes to the steamer's anchorage. Bail every tenth stroke. Keep the wrap high. Do the sums and trust them.

The compass sat in his pocket, engraved in Aldous's cramped hand: *For whoever finishes it.* Cyrus had carried it for months as a keepsake, a relic, a dead man's warm brass. Only now, pulling against the chop with his palms torn raw, did he understand it was none of those things. It was an instruction. *Finish it.* Not the atlas — the atlas had been finished in a drafty workshop by lamplight weeks ago. Finish the other thing, the thing underneath the atlas, the ledger of names, the map of who had lived. That was the true survey, and it had no northern edge, and it could only be completed by carrying it out of the country on a leaking boat at dawn.

The searchlight found the water.

He heard it before he saw it — engine noise, low and purposeful, and then the beam came sliding across the harbor mouth like a great pale hand feeling for him in the dark. Cyrus froze mid-stroke, oars lifted, and the light crawled past his bow, past his gunwale, close enough that he could see the dust of spray glittering in it, and moved on toward the rocks of the breakwater, lingered there a moment, probing the shadows where a smaller boat might hide.

Then it swept back.

Cyrus rowed. There was no more arithmetic after that, no counting, only the animal knowledge that the light was widening behind him and the steamer's black hull was growing ahead of him, and he bent his back and pulled until the oarlocks screamed and his vision went red at the edges. The dinghy's seams wept constantly now; the water in the bilge sloshed at his shins. He bailed without rhythm, bailed and rowed and rowed and bailed, and the patrol boat's engine note deepened as it turned, as it committed itself, as it came.

A voice rolled across the water from the steamer's deck — a sailor, leaning far over the rail, arm extended downward. "Here! Here, lad, jump!"

Cyrus measured the distance the way his master had taught him to measure everything, in one clean glance: eight feet of black, churning water between the dinghy's bow and the ship's side, and below it the hull rising green and barnacled, and above it twenty hands reaching down into the dark.

He took the ledger strap in his teeth, knotted his fist in the painter, and jumped.

The cold closed over him like a door. Then hands — many hands, rough and certain — had his collar, his wrists, his armpits, and the world became a blur of hauled rope and scraped steel and someone shouting for blankets, and then he was lying on the *Aurelia*'s deck, coughing seawater onto her clean planks, the canvas wrap still strapped to his chest, still dry beneath the oilcloth, still heavy, blessedly heavy, with names.

Behind him, through the rail stanchions, he watched the patrol boat slide alongside the anchorage, its searchlight playing impotently over the steamer's flank. A loudhailer crackled. A voice demanded boarding rights, inspection, the return of a fugitive. The captain's voice came down from the bridge, unhurried, citing mails, treaties, tide tables, the sovereignty of the postal service, and Cyrus lay on his back on the deck of a neutral ship and laughed until the laughing turned into something else entirely and a Norwegian steward put a blanket around his shoulders and pretended not to notice.

*Odile*

From the rail she saw it happen — the splash, the scramble of arms, the small dark figure hauled dripping up the ship's side like something the sea had decided, at the last moment, to return. Her knees went. The matron's grip arrived under her elbow, iron beneath the wool.

"Yours?" the matron asked.

"Mine," Odile said, and found she was crying and laughing in the same breath.

They brought Cyrus along the deck wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tar and someone else's voyage, hair plastered to his forehead, lips blue, clutching the canvas wrap the way drowning men clutch driftwood. When his eyes found hers, he didn't smile — he was too far past smiling — but he nodded once, the cartographer's nod, the nod that says *surveyed, confirmed, charted true,* and Odile gripped his frozen hands through the blanket and held on.

"He wouldn't let go of that bag," the first mate said, coming up behind them. "We had to pry one finger loose to get a rope on him. What's in it, gold?"

"Names," Odile said.

The mate looked at her, waiting for the joke to land. It didn't land. It never would again, she realized; she would spend the rest of her life saying that word to people who expected it to be a joke.

The ship's whistle sounded, long and low, a sound that seemed to come up through the deck from the earth's own bones. Below, on the quay, lanterns were moving — a cluster of them, hurrying, military in their precision, and Odile felt Cyrus go rigid under her hand.

"It's him," Cyrus said quietly. "Reiner."

*Cyrus*

Captain Reiner came down the quay the way he had come into the workshop weeks ago, coat buttoned to the throat, hands clasped behind him, a man arriving at the scene of his own signature. Even at this distance, even through the rigging and the morning haze, Cyrus knew the set of those shoulders, the deliberate unhurried pace of an officer who has learned that haste reads as weakness. The lanterns parted for him. The patrol boat's crew stood at attention along the pier, and Reiner ignored them entirely, his face tilted up toward the steamer, searching the rail.

Searching, Cyrus understood, for him.

There was time, in the way there is always time at such moments — the ship still warping off the pier, the gap of dark water widening inch by inch, the engines not yet fully committed. Time for a signalman to relay a message. Time for an officer of the occupying army to raise his hand, to point, to speak a name, and set in motion whatever machinery existed for men pulled off neutral steamers in international waters. Time, in short, for Reiner to do his duty, cleanly and correctly, the way he did everything.

Cyrus did not look away. That felt important — that whatever happened, it should happen witnessed, face to face, mapmaker to officer, the way Aldous would have wanted it. He stepped to the rail. The blanket slipped from his shoulders. Below, Reiner's upward gaze traveled along the deck, past the matron, past the crowd of emigrants and refugees and mail clerks, and found him.

They looked at each other across thirty feet of cold air and a widening seam of sea.

Cyrus saw the recognition arrive — saw it move through the captain's face like light moving across a landscape at dawn, touching feature after feature: the man at the rail, the apprentice, the boy whose drafting hand he had praised, whose future he had offered to secure with one signature, whose record could have shown he was never here at all. He saw Reiner's jaw tighten. He saw the captain's right hand stir behind his back, half-forming the gesture — the pointing, the calling, the order —

And he saw, in the last instant before it happened, the exact moment the hand went still.

Reiner stood on the quay with the lanterns burning around him and the patrol crew awaiting his word, and he looked up at Cyrus Voss for a long, long moment. His face gave away almost nothing. But Cyrus had spent his life reading surfaces — coastlines, contours, the subtle grammar of where land yields to water — and he read this: not indifference, not defeat, but a man standing at his own checkpoint, weighing his own cargo, deciding what could be carried and what must be let go. Perhaps he saw Aldous in the boy at the rail. Perhaps he saw the ledger in the canvas wrap and understood that some documents, once they exist, burn the hand that tries to hold them. Perhaps he was simply tired, the way Cyrus had glimpsed him tired once, late at night in the confiscated workshop, sitting alone among the dead master's maps like a man visiting a grave.

Perhaps mercy does not need a reason. That was the whole lesson of this year, Cyrus thought. The young guard at the fish market. The matron with her coat-knowing whisper. Bram's silence in the customs yard. Marta's doorway. A chain of small mercies, each one improbable, each one enough — and now the last link forged by the enemy himself, the one man with every reason to refuse.

Reiner turned away.

He did it slowly, deliberately, so there could be no mistaking it — turned his back on the rail, on the ship, on Cyrus, and spoke a single sentence to the patrol lieutenant that ended the matter, and walked back up the quay with his hands clasped behind him, a straight-backed man resuming his duties, leaving behind him on the pier the smallest possible space where a fugitive had almost been.

The *Aurelia*'s engines surged. The gap of water widened from inches to feet to fathoms. The quay began to slide backward, taking its lanterns and its soldiers and its requisition notices with it, and the harbor opened out ahead of them, gray and vast, and the breakwater passed to port like a threshold crossed.

Cyrus stood at the rail until the town was gone.

*Odile*

The passengers gathered on the aft deck as the sun came up — a strange congregation of emigrants and clerks and seamen, drawn by rumor, by the sight of the soaked young man with the canvas bag, by something in the air that told them history was standing nearby and they should witness it. The matron stood at the front, arms folded, veil snapping in the wind. The Norwegian steward brought coffee nobody drank.

Odile unwrapped the ledger on the deckhouse roof, weighted its corners with Cyrus's brass compass and two coffee cups, and opened it to the first page. The wind tried to take it immediately, hungry for the paper, and Cyrus anchored the opposite corner with his hands, and together they held the book open against the sea air.

"I can't read all of them," she said to Cyrus, quietly. "Three hundred. My voice won't last."

"It will," he said. "Read."

So she read. She read the names into the wind the way they had been typed into the margins — clearly, completely, giving each one its full measure of letters. Some were family names she knew from shopfronts and school registers; some she knew from graves; one she reached on the second page, and her voice broke on it, and Cyrus's hand came over hers on the page, steady as a ruling line, and she finished her mother's name and kept going.

The passengers listened. Some wept for strangers. An old man removed his hat. Near the end of the third page, when her voice had gone raw, Cyrus leaned close and added a name of his own in a clear young voice — *Bram Sundvik, smuggler, who kept silent* — and Odile marked it in the margin with the stub of pencil she had carried since the workshop, opening a new page beneath it, a page that would grow.

The *Aurelia* ran south with the morning behind them. Odile read the last name as the harbor's smoke sank below the horizon, and closed the book, and the wind took a strand of her hair and the applause, when it came, was quiet, the way applause is at funerals and launches — the sound of people who understand they have seen something off on its voyage.

Cyrus touched the compass where it lay weighting the page. "He wrote 'for whoever finishes it,'" he said. "I used to think he meant the atlas."

"And now?"

"Now I think the atlas was just the paper it came wrapped in." He looked south, toward whatever waited — exile, printers' shops, foreign ink, the long work of making the erased visible. "Odile. Three hundred names went out with the tide this morning. But Bram said something once, when he was sketching us the route. He said ledgers have a habit of coming home."

Odile followed his gaze to the empty southern water, and then to the typewriter case standing at her feet, and felt — for the first time since the soldiers came, since the floorboards, since the candlelit pages — not dread, not grief, but the beginning of intention, sharp as a fresh ribbon in a carriage return.

"Then we'd better find out who's still alive to receive them," she said.
