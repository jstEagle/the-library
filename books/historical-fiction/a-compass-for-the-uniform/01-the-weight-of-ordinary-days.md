## 1. The Weight of Ordinary Days

There is a particular hour in Greyhaven when the fog has not yet decided whether to lift or settle, and the whole town seems to hold its breath inside it. Ivo Brightwater loved that hour—or believed they loved it, which amounts to the same thing after nine years—and it was in that hour that the Meridian Trading Company's counting-house came most fully alive. Not with people; people were a later invention. With sound: the creak of the great building settling on its pilings, the tick and groan of tide-wrack against the seawall, the soft percussion of ink drying on paper.

Ivo arrived before the lamps were trimmed, as always. The key was under the loose brick by the rain barrel, because the Meridian Company trusted its clerks exactly as much as it had to and no more, and Ivo had long ago stopped taking this personally. Personal was a luxury. The company paid twelve shillings a month, docked two for lodging in the loft above the ledgers, and asked in return for a life so unremarkable that even God might overlook it.

That was the arrangement. That was the philosophy. *Keep your head down. Copy your figures. Want nothing.*

It sounds like surrender when said aloud. Ivo never said it aloud.

* * *

The ledgers themselves were beautiful, if you were the sort of person who could find beauty in columns. Ivo was. Nine years of them stood along the north wall in ranks—oak boards, vellum spines, brass corners gone green with salt air—and every morning Ivo unlocked the case, chose the day's volume, and began.

The work was simple and total, like prayer. Cargo in, cargo out. Tons of Greyhaven salt cod bound for the southern ports. Bales of wool. Barrels of pitch and tar from the yards upriver. Pearl shell, sometimes, packed in straw—the pale nacreous plates the oyster divers brought up from the beds beyond the harbor chain, sold south to be cut into combs and buttons and inlay. Ivo's father had been an oyster man once, before the debt, before the indenture papers, before the company bought a family the way it bought anything else: cheaply, permanently, without looking twice.

Ivo wrote figures in a hand so precise it looked printed. Not elegant—elegance drew the eye—but exact, machine-true, the handwriting of someone determined to leave no mark that could be traced back to a person. There were four clerks in the counting-house, but Ivo's pages were the ones the auditors never flagged. There is a kind of praise that feels exactly like erasure, and Ivo had learned to live on it.

"Morning, Brightwater." Tam Wicker, the junior clerk, ducking in out of the fog with his collar up and his usual air of a man perpetually late to something. "Harbor chain's down for the spring tides. Big ship coming in Thursday—company colors, big as a cathedral."

"The *Meridian Star*," Ivo said, not looking up.

"You'd know."

"I copy the manifests." Which was true, and also a wall, and Tam knew better than to knock on it. He went to his desk and began noisily sharpening a quill he did not need to sharpen, which was his way of talking without talking, and for a while there was only the sound of nibs and breathing and the sea working patiently at the pilings below.

* * *

At mid-morning the harbormaster sent for a clerk. This happened perhaps once a month—a manifest query, a signature verification, some small administrative tooth-pull. The summons came by boy, who delivered it with the contempt boys reserve for people whose names they've never bothered learning.

The harbormaster's office sat above the customs shed at the head of the quay, all polished oak and imported glass, and Harbormaster Dunmore occupied it the way a barnacle occupies a hull: thoroughly, and with no intention of ever leaving. He was a broad, pink man with rings on fingers too thick for them, and he did not look up when Ivo entered. He let the silence run first. That was his method. Silence sorted visitors into the important and the unimportant faster than any question could.

Ivo waited. Being waited over was a skill, like penmanship.

"The Corvane shipment," Dunmore said at last, still reading whatever held his attention. "Outbound, pearl shell, forty crates. Your hand on the tally?"

"Yes, harbormaster."

"Count's wrong."

Ivo's stomach performed its small practiced drop. "I counted twice, sir. Forty crates, three hundred weight each, logged against the divers' guild receipts—"

"I know what's logged." Now he looked up, and his face had the flat pleasantness of a man enjoying himself in a way he considered legitimate. "Corvane says thirty-eight crates loaded. Corvane counts what Corvane likes. So either your tally's wrong or your eyes are. Which is it, clerk?"

Neither. Both crates had been diverted at the quay by men who did not sign their names, and everyone in that room—including, Ivo suspected, the harbormaster—knew it perfectly well. The discrepancy would resolve itself somewhere above Ivo's pay grade, into some director's private account, and the only question was which invisible person would absorb the error.

"I'll recount against the quay book," Ivo said quietly.

"Do." Dunmore returned to his papers. Then, almost as an afterthought, with the door already half-open behind Ivo: "You're Vane Brightwater's girl, aren't you? The oyster man who drowned himself in debt."

"My father," Ivo said. Something old and cold turned over in their chest.

"Mm. He wanted things, that man. Boats, land, a name." Dunmore smiled without warmth, examining Ivo the way one examines a slightly damaged barrel—one that will still sell, at a discount. "See it didn't take. Wanting things. It's a bad trade in a port town. Best thing about you is nobody knows your face."

He waved dismissal. Ivo walked back down the quay stairs with their spine straight and their hands steady through nine years of discipline, and only when they reached the fog-thick stretch past the net-drying racks did they allow themselves to stop, once, and press a palm flat against cold wet stone until the shaking stopped.

Nobody knows your face. As though it were a compliment. As though being unwitnessed were the same as being safe.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way all afternoons passed. Figures. Nibs. The fog lifting just enough to show grey water and grey sky and the masts of ships Ivo would never sail on, standing in their moorings like a congregation that had forgotten why it gathered. Tam left at five. The senior clerks left at six. By seven the counting-house belonged to Ivo alone, and the lamp threw their shadow enormous across the ledger wall—a giant made entirely of hunched shoulders.

They worked late, correcting the Corvane tally, entering the phantom crates in the margin in the tiny notation reserved for discrepancies that must exist on paper and nowhere else: *2 cr. adj., quay variance.* Their own handwriting, testifying neatly against itself. Somewhere in these nine years of volumes, Ivo thought—not for the first time, but tonight with an unfamiliar sting—there were hundreds of such margins. Hundreds of small honest lies copied faithfully by an honest hand. If the ledgers ever burned, the truth of the Meridian Company would burn with them, and the paper would remain innocent forever.

That was the trick of it. That was what Dunmore understood and what Ivo had spent nine years refusing to understand: invisibility was not neutrality. The unseen hand still moved the pen.

Ivo blew out the lamp.

Sleep came slowly in the loft, on the narrow cot between the eaves, beneath the smell of paper and tar and the distant mineral breath of the sea. And sleep, when it finally took them, gave them back their father—standing waist-deep among the oyster beds at low tide, holding up a shell that caught the dawn light, saying something Ivo couldn't hear over the water.

* * *

They woke in the blue hour before the fog decided, as always, and dressed, and took the key from under the brick, as always. But the tide was out further than usual, far out, exposing the mudflats and the oyster beds in their ragged miles, and something—habit, or the ghost of the dream—turned Ivo's feet toward the seawall path instead of the counting-house door.

The flats at dawn were another country. Channels of silver water threading black mud, gulls arguing over the beds, the whole estuary smelling of iron and iodine and rot. Ivo had walked this shore as a child, beside a father who read the water the way other men read scripture.

That was how they saw it: a shape among the beds, half-sunk in the channel silt, dark against the paling east.

Not driftwood. Driftwood doesn't curl around something. Doesn't clutch.

Ivo scrambled down the seawall ladder, boots sucking into mud, heart doing something loud and unprofessional, and reached the shape in time to understand that it was a woman—young, salt-white lips, clothes heavy with seawater—and that her arms were locked around a packet wrapped in oilskin with the desperation of a drowning woman holding the last solid thing in a liquid world.

She was breathing. Barely. Her eyes flickered open, found Ivo's face, found nothing in it to recognize, and closed again.

Ivo looked at the oilskin packet. Looked down the empty flats, at the grey water, at the town still asleep behind its fog. Every trained instinct said *fetch the watch, fetch the harbormaster, be a good clerk.*

Instead—before any decision could properly be made—Ivo hooked their arms under hers and began dragging her out of the mud, toward the counting-house, toward the loft where nobody would think to look, wanting, for the first time in nine years, something they were not supposed to want.

Her grip on the packet never loosened, even unconscious. Whatever she carried, she had chosen it over breathing.

Ivo meant to find out why.
