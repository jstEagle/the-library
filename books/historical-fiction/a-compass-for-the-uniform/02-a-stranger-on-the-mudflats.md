## 2. A Stranger on the Mudflats

The tide went out before the sun came up, as it always did, and Greyhaven's mudflats lay exposed like something the sea had grown tired of holding. I walked the low road at that gray hour because it was the only hour that belonged to no one—not to the Meridian Company, not to the harbormaster, not to the gulls who screamed ownership at everything. For half a turn of the glass, the flats were mine, and I could pretend that wanting nothing was the same as having something.

Nine years I had walked this way. Nine years of copying figures into ledgers with a hand so steady it looked printed, of eating my noon bread in the counting-house stairwell where no one thought to look for me, of answering *good morning* with a nod instead of words because words invited conversation and conversation invited notice. My father had been an oyster-man before the company bought the beds out from under him, and his father before him, and I had learned early what happened to people who stood in doorways with their names in their mouths. They got doors closed on them. Better to be the draft through the keyhole. Better to be weather.

The humiliation at the quay yesterday was still lodged in me like a fishbone. Harbormaster Quill had needed someone small to shout at—a barge had come in short forty casks of salt—and he had chosen me out of a dozen clerks, not because I'd erred but because I would not err back. He had called me *you there*, which is what you call furniture, and made me stand with my ledger open while he spat his fury into my face, and when he was done he said, *carry on,* the way you'd say it to a mule. And I had carried on. That was the whole of it. That was the shape of my life: carry on, carry on, until carrying on looked from a distance like peace.

I want to be clear that I mistook it for peace. I truly did.

* * *

The light was just turning from ash to pewter when I saw her.

At first I took her for driftwood—Greyhaven's flats give up strange things after spring storms: whale ribs, figureheads, once an entire ship's boat with its name burned off. But driftwood does not lie at an angle like that, one arm flung upstream, fingers curled as if catching something the water owed her. She was face-down among the oyster beds my grandfather had worked, half in the shallow pool left by the retreating tide, her dark hair spread across the mud in ropes studded with broken shell.

I should have gone for help. Help was the sensible thing, the invisible thing—fetch the watch, fetch the harbormaster, become a bystander in my own morning. Instead I found myself already off the seawall, boots sinking into the cold suck of the mud, and I remember thinking, with a strange clarity: *this is how it happens. This is how history gets its hooks into ordinary people. It lies still in the shallows and waits for them to walk close.*

She was breathing. Barely—a wet rattle, shallow as a trapped bird's. Her lips were gray-blue and her skin was river-cold, and when I turned her over I saw she was young, younger than me perhaps, with a salt-scoured face and a cut above her brow that had crusted over with silt. And clutching her chest, both arms wrapped around it like a mother around an infant, was an oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord and knotted twice.

I tried to take it from her. I tell myself now that I only wanted to ease her breathing, loosen whatever she'd strapped herself to, but some honest part of me wanted to know, and I have learned since that curiosity was always the crack in me, hairline-thin, waiting for weight.

Her hands did not loosen. Unconscious, drowning-deep in whatever black sleep the sea had poured into her, she would not let go. I pried at one finger and it resisted like iron; I spoke to her—I said, *let go, I'm not going to take it*—and her eyes opened, or half-opened, one sliver of dark iris finding me with an accuracy that frightened me. She looked at me the way you look at a thief. Then her eyes rolled back and she was gone again, and her grip never changed.

So I carried them both. The woman and the packet, up the mud, over the seawall, along the low road while the gulls announced us to the whole waking port. She weighed less than she should have, as though the sea had already taken its toll of her, and the packet rode against her chest the entire way, warm from her body where everything else about her was cold.

* * *

There is an art to being unseen, and I am its reluctant master. I brought her into the counting-house through the coal chute at the rear, the one the boys use when they shirk, and I carried her up the ladder to the loft above the record room where obsolete ledgers go to die. No one climbs there. The stairs are judged unsafe, which in Meridian arithmetic means the company has decided not to spend money on them, and so the loft has become a country all its own, ruled by dust and paper-moths and nine years of my stolen noons. I knew its every board. I laid her on the old sail canvas behind the stacks of defunct manifests, built a screen of ledger-bales so that anyone glancing up the ladder would see only archive, hauled water and the dregs of a spirit-lamp's alcohol, and did what little one can do for the half-drowned: stripped the wet wool from her, rubbed warmth into her hands, set her on her side so the sea could leave her the way it came.

Then I sat back on my heels in the paper-smelling gloom and looked at the packet, still locked in her arms, and admitted to myself that I was not going to leave without seeing it.

I am not a brave man. Please understand that. Everything I did that morning I did in a state of quiet terror, the same terror I feel when Quill shouts, the same terror that has kept me upright and unnoticed and alive for nine years. But terror and curiosity share a border, and I crossed it the way you cross any border: one small step, telling yourself it hardly counts.

Her fingers, in deep sleep, had slackened by a finger's width. Enough. I worked the packet free—it took longer than it should have, my hands shaking, the knots waxed against exactly this—and unwound the cord, and folded back the oilskin, and the smell of it rose up at me: tar and brine and under both, faintly, lavender, as if the letters had been packed near soap once, in another life.

Seven letters. Salt-stained, water-marked, but written in a hand I knew.

That is the sentence I have been circling all this while and cannot make gentler: *a hand I knew.* Nine years of copying correspondence teaches you handwriting the way a priest knows Latin. I had inked copies of half the company's outgoing letters; I knew Director Pell's cramped stinginess, knew Corvane's bold slashing capitals, knew the harbor pilots' scrawls and the factors' schoolroom rounds. And I knew—every clerk in Greyhaven knew—the hand that had signed the founding charters that hung framed in the entry hall, the hand on the commemorative plate we polished each Founder's Day, the strong, sloped, elegant script with its distinctive crossed sevens, sevens crossed like little sails.

Aldous Vane. Captain Aldous Vane, founder of the Meridian Trading Company, lost at sea eleven years ago with the flagship *Persephone* and all hands, mourned in an annual sermon, embalmed in marble and gilt lettering above the exchange.

Dead men do not write letters. Dead men especially do not write seven of them, dated across three years *after* their drowning, and dead men do not write in their own unmistakable hand with its sevens crossed like sails, and yet here they were against my thumbs, real paper, real iron-gall ink gone brown with age, addressed in that impossible script to names I did not recognize—and the first line of the topmost letter, the one I let my eyes fall upon because looking away had stopped being possible, read:

*I write to you from a death the Company arranged for me, and I write quickly, because they believe I am at the bottom of the sea and I intend to keep that belief alive long enough to finish what I know.*

I put the letters down. I actually set them down on the sail canvas and moved my hands away from them, as though they were hot iron, as though contact were the danger. My heart was doing something unhelpful against my ribs. The loft was very quiet; below me I could hear the first clerks arriving, chairs scraping, Quill's voice rising in its usual morning complaint, the whole familiar machinery of the ordinary day grinding into motion, utterly unaware that directly above it lay a drowned woman and a dead man's testimony.

I thought: *put it back. Knot the cord. Never speak of this. When she wakes, tell her nothing, direct her to the mission house, return to your ledgers. You are nobody. Nobody is safe. Safety is the thing you traded everything else for, and the trade was fair.*

And then I thought of the oyster beds. My grandfather's beds, bought for pennies by Vane's company, worked afterward by men who drowned in debt if not in water. I thought of the sailors' widows who still lined up at the company window each quarter-day for their pensions, and how the pension clerk—I had copied those disbursement lists—always found a reason to reduce them. I thought of Quill saying *you there,* and of nine years of *you there,* stretching ahead of me into decades, into the grave.

The letters sat on the canvas, patient as tide.

Behind the ledger-bales, the woman made a sound—the first since the flats, a raw scrape of a breath, almost a word. I moved to her, and her hand came up out of sleep and caught my wrist with startling strength, and her eyes opened all the way this time, black and burning, fixed on mine.

"The letters," she said, her voice scraped down to the grain. "Where are the letters."

"Here," I said. "Safe. I only looked—"

"If you looked," she said, "then you know whose hand that is."

Below us, somewhere in the counting-house, a bell rang the half-hour, bright and indifferent, and the day went on being an ordinary day for everyone but the two of us in the dust and the dark.

"They say he's dead," I whispered. "They say he drowned."

She smiled then, and there was nothing of comfort in it—only grief, and fury, and something being sharpened behind her eyes like a blade on a whetstone.

"So does everyone," she said. "That's the only reason he's still alive."
