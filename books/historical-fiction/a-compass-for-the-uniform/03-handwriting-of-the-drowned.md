## 3. Handwriting of the Drowned

I woke to the smell of tallow and salt, and for a long moment I did not know which sea had claimed me.

The loft above the counting-house was low-beamed, close, breathing dust. Through a gap in the floorboards I could see lamplight rising from below, gold as yolk, and I could hear the small industrious sounds of a clerk at his work: pen-scratch, page-turn, the sigh of a man who has been awake too long and will not admit it. My body felt like something salvaged rather than inhabited. Every joint ached with the memory of cold water. When I moved my arm, it screamed in a voice that was not mine.

The oilskin packet was still against my ribs.

I had slept with my hand over it the way a child sleeps clutching a knife after the wolves have come. It was damp through, but sealed, and when my fingers found its edges I felt my heart slow by degrees, like a ship coming off the storm into calmer water. Still there. Still whole. Nine days in the open sea and three nights running the coast, and it was still there.

I lay back and let myself remember how I had come to be in this stranger's loft, because remembering was a discipline now. Forget one thing and you might forget everything; forget everything and Vane's last words died twice—once on the water and once in me.

* * *

His name was Aldous Vane, and for eleven years he had been the Meridian Company's flagship captain, and for eleven years he had been dying by inches in plain sight of everyone who called him sir.

I came aboard the *Meridian Star* at fifteen, a navigator's apprentice with ink-stained fingers and no family anyone would claim. The sea does not ask about your bloodline. That is what I loved about it first, before I learned that ships have their own aristocracies, their own ledgers, their own quiet drownings. Vane taught me the stars. He taught me that a fix taken honestly is worth more than a fix taken conveniently, and he said it lightly, the way men say things they know will cost them later, so that you laugh and only understand years afterward that he was writing you a letter too.

He was a tall man going grey at the temples, careful in his dress, careless with his laughter. The directors loved him because he brought in cargo like a man pulling coins from a conjurer's hat. They trusted him the way powerful men trust useful ones—which is to say, not at all—and Vane knew it, and smiled anyway.

It was in the third year that I noticed him keeping a second log.

Not a falsified one—he was not that kind of man, whatever the company would say of him now. A true one. Hidden. He copied every manifest twice: once as the purser gave it to him, once as the hold actually showed. And the two never matched. Crates entered as dye-stuffs left as muskets. Tons of grain became tons of gunpowder somewhere between paper and plank. And the profits—the enormous, unlogged, un-taxable profits—flowed into accounts I was never shown, though I saw Vane's face when he tallied them, and I saw what grief looks like on a man doing arithmetic.

"They've done it before," he told me once, in the chartroom, with the lamp turned low. We had just lost two sailors to a "mishandled" cargo inspection ashore—men arrested for smuggling they'd never touched, tried fast, transported faster. Their names had been struck from the muster before their families finished weeping. "Corvane and his circle. Every few years, when the audits come close, they need someone to hang it on. Sailors are convenient. Sailors don't have advocates. Sailors—" and here he stopped himself, because I was a sailor, and because there are some truths you cannot hand a person gently.

"You're building a case," I said.

"I'm building a lifeboat," he said. "For whoever's left."

* * *

They came for him in the autumn of the ninth year. Not with chains—they were too clever for that—but with weather. A storm out of season, a route altered by "urgent dispatch," a flagship sent into waters every pilot in Greyhaven knew were rotten that time of year. The *Meridian Star* broke apart on the Serpent's Teeth with all hands.

All hands but mine.

I will not write down here, even in my own head, what those four days in the open boat were. Some cargoes you carry without opening them. But I will say this: Vane did not die in the wreck. He had known it was coming the way an old dog knows a beating is coming—had seen the shape of it in the manifests, in the dispatches he was ordered to carry and forbidden to read, in Corvane's smile at the farewell dinner, which had been warm the way a trap is warm before it springs. So he put his true log, and eleven years of letters, into an oilskin packet, and he put the packet in my hands on the last calm night, and he said:

"If I go down, you go down separately. You swim away from the wreckage. Never toward it." He held my face in both hands then, this man who had taught me the stars, and his own were wet, whether from spray or otherwise I chose then not to know. "They'll burn everything, Sefa. The moment an audit ship's keel touches Greyhaven harbor, they'll burn nine years of records and call it damp. These letters name them. Every director. Every forged signature. Every sailor they hanged with paperwork. Get them to the magistrate aboard the audit vessel—not the harbor magistrate, he's bought, the one at sea—and then get yourself somewhere they can't reach, and live a life big enough to make this worth it."

Then the storm took the ship, and the sea, which owes nothing to anyone, took everyone else, and I alone washed up on the oyster beds of Greyhaven like a piece of the wreckage itself.

* * *

Now I sat up in the loft, slowly, testing my body like a rope of doubtful age, and looked down through the floorboards at the man who had pulled me from the mud.

Ivo Brightwater. I had heard the harbormaster shout the name at them on the docks yesterday—or the day before; my sense of time was soaked and warped. I had watched, half-conscious, slung under an arm, as this clerk stood very straight and very silent while being dressed down like a child, and I had thought: *there is a person who has made invisibility into a cathedral.* There was something in the way they absorbed the humiliation—not flinching, not fighting, just folding it away somewhere deep—that frightened me more than anger would have. Anger can be aimed. This was armor. Armor means someone decided, long ago, that the world was not safe enough to be felt in.

And yet. They had carried me here. They had hidden me in the counting-house loft of the very company whose crimes I carried proof of—a hiding place either of genius or of madness—and they had not gone to the directors, though the reward for turning in Vane's "surviving accomplice" would likely buy a clerk their freedom papers ten times over.

Or perhaps they simply hadn't realized yet what I was worth.

That was the question I had to answer before I could decide anything else. Ally or liability. I turned it over in my mind the way I'd turn a doubtful coin, checking its weight, listening for the ring of false metal. Ivo Brightwater had spent nine years copying figures for the Meridian Company. Nine years of falsified manifests passing through those careful hands. Either they had never looked—which made them harmless—or they had looked and never seen, which made them something rarer: a witness who didn't yet know they were one.

There was a third possibility, the one I liked least. That they had seen, and chosen not to see, because seeing would cost them their small safe life. I had met that kind before. The sea is full of people who watched.

* * *

In the end, curiosity decided it—or perhaps loneliness, which wears curiosity's coat.

I came down the ladder in the grey hour before dawn, moving carefully, and Ivo startled so badly they knocked an inkwell sideways, caught it, and stared at me standing in the lamplight with the packet held against my chest like a shield or a wound.

"You should be lying down," they said. Their voice was exactly what I expected: level, apologetic, a voice built for not disturbing anyone.

"I've been lying down for two days." I set the packet on the desk between us. I did not push it toward them. I wanted them to look at it where it lay, at the distance I was keeping. "Do you know what this is?"

"An oilskin packet."

"It's a grave," I said. "Nine graves, if I count the sailors your company hanged to cover the first theft. Eleven years of graves if I count everyone who ever asked the wrong question aboard a Meridian ship. Captain Aldous Vane wrote what's inside these letters with his own hand, and he gave them to me the night your directors murdered him."

Ivo went very still. It was the stillness of prey, I thought, and then I corrected myself: it was the stillness of someone recalculating everything.

"Vane is dead," they said quietly. "Lost with all hands. There's a memorial plaque in the company chapel."

"There is. Your Director Corvane paid for it personally. Gilded letters." I laughed, and the sound came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and I hated it. "He gilded the name of the man he drowned. That's what power does, clerk—it pays for the headstone and calls it grief."

"The letters," Ivo said. "You said letters. Plural. From a dead man. Written when?"

"Over eleven years. He kept a second log, a true one, and he wrote to himself as if he were writing to a court that would one day exist. Names. Dates. Signatures copied from documents he was ordered to carry. The directors framed him for the embezzlement that was theirs—no. Worse." I leaned across the desk, and I watched Ivo not lean back, and some small part of me marked that. "They didn't frame him. Framing implies effort. They simply needed a corpse with a captain's rank on it, and when the sea wouldn't give them one quickly enough, they arranged the weather. Then they pinned nine years of stolen cargo onto dead sailors' names, and now the audit ship is coming, and Director Corvane means to burn every record in this building before her anchor drops. Everything you have copied in nine years. Everything I nearly drowned carrying. Ash, and then a clean story."

For a long moment there was no sound but the lamp's small combustion and, far below, the harbor beginning to wake—gulls, hawsers creaking, the tide turning with that soft colossal patience the sea brings to everything, even murder.

Then Ivo said, in a voice I would think about for a long time afterward, "The manifests. The ones I copy. The dye-stuffs that weigh wrong."

So they had weighed them.

"You knew," I said.

"I knew the figures didn't reconcile." They were looking at their own hands now, at the ink stains, as though the hands belonged to someone else who had been living their life. "I told myself the discrepancy was error. Rounding. Sloth upstream. I am very good at telling myself things." They looked up, and there was something new in their face—not courage, not yet, but the raw material of it, ore not yet smelted. "Show me the handwriting. If it's really Vane's, the company seal appears on the dispatch copies he countersigned. I'd know it. I've copied it ten thousand times."

That surprised me—that a clerk would want verification before belief—but it shouldn't have. Belief is cheap for people who have nothing to lose. Ivo had nine years of survival invested in what was true.

I opened the packet and laid the first letter on the desk between us, and I watched them read the name *Aldous Vane* in that strong, sloped, unmistakable hand, and I watched something break and reform behind their eyes.

When they finally spoke, their voice was barely above the sound of the lamp burning.

"When does the audit ship arrive?"

"Three days. Perhaps less. The morning tide after next, at the latest."

Ivo looked at the letter, then at me, and I understood that I was watching a person stand at the edge of their entire former life and feel the ground already gone beneath them.

"Then we have until the morning tide," they said, "to get these past Corvane's own checkpoint—and I know exactly how much he trusts his clerks to carry sealed dispatches aboard."

I stared at them. "You're offering to help me."

"No," Ivo Brightwater said, and for the first time since I'd washed up half-drowned in their city, they looked directly at me without apology. "I'm offering to help *us*. Because whatever's in those letters, my handwriting is in the margins of it too. I just hadn't read my own confession until tonight."

Outside, the tide began to turn, dragging the dark water of Greyhaven toward the sea, and somewhere beyond the harbor chain, a ship I could not yet see was already sailing toward everything we were about to risk.
