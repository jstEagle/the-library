## 7. Fire in the Customs Shed

The customs shed smelled of pitch and old salt, of tar barrels stacked like fat black soldiers along its western wall, and beneath all of it the sweet rot of fish that had gone to waste in a hundred wet seasons. Sefa stood in the queue of porters and cart-drivers waiting for inspection, and she let the smell teach her what her eyes had already measured: four barrels of tar, a brazier burning low by the door, a lamp hung from a beam that swayed with every gust off the water.

Fire, then, if fire became necessary. She had known since the gatehouse. She had carried the knowledge the way she'd once carried lead weights in her pocket on the *Meridian Star*, touching it now and again to be sure it was still there.

*Don't need it,* she told herself. *Won't need it.*

Ivo was somewhere behind her in the yard, playing their part — the invisible clerk with a delivery docket and a stooped shoulder, hauling the crate of pearl shell that held everything that mattered in this world. Sefa had argued against that arrangement at first light. Put the packet with the clerk, she'd said, because no customs man alive looks twice at a Meridian clerk; they're furniture, they're ballast, they're nothing. But Ivo had shaken their head with that stubborn quiet of theirs and said, *You're a stranger with salt still in your lungs. If they stop anyone, it'll be you.* And they were right, which Sefa found she resented almost as much as she was grateful for.

So Ivo carried the shell, and Sefa carried nothing but her own face — which was the problem. Her face was known here. Three years sailing out of Greyhaven aboard the company's flagship meant three years of walking these quays, buying oranges from the fruit-wife, drinking bad rum with the harbor watch. Dead men's faces were remembered precisely because everyone expected them never to come back.

She kept her hood up. She kept her eyes down. She did what she'd watched Ivo do for two days now, this study in vanishing, and discovered it was harder than it looked. Invisibility was a craft. It had joints and seams, a grammar of small refusals.

*Next.*

The customs officer was a heavy man with a waxed mustache and rings on four fingers, and he worked through the crates ahead of her with the bored violence of a man prying open other people's property for wages. The queue shuffled forward. Beyond the shed, past the customs fence, Sefa could see the quay where the *Providence's Grace* — Corvane's ship, the one meant to carry the burned records and the clean story out on the morning tide — lay loading under lanterns. The audit vessel waited beyond the harbor chain, a gray suggestion in the dark, patient as winter.

Somewhere on that audit ship sat a magistrate who believed Captain Aldous Vane was dead. Somewhere in the crate six feet behind Sefa sat letters written in the hand of a living man, naming the directors who had made him so on paper.

*Next.*

Her turn. She stepped up to the table and set down the manifest slip Ivo had forged — good forgery, honest-looking wear, the clerk's initials smudged just enough to suggest haste — along with the small crate she carried: empty pearl shell, packing weight, nothing. The lie wrapped around the truth like an oyster around grit.

"Name."

"Shell-runner for Brannick & Sons." A name she'd borrowed from a real house, small enough not to keep records, big enough to be believed.

The officer turned the crate over. Tipped it. Shells rattled across the table like knucklebones. He glanced at them with a fowler's eye — and Sefa saw the exact moment his eye caught on something. Not the shells. Her hands. A sailor's hands, rope-scarred across the palm, the kind of hands a shell-runner's ledger-keeper wouldn't have.

He looked up slowly. "Hood off. House rule."

There was no such rule. They both knew there was no such rule. Behind her the queue went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet when they smell blood or trouble.

She lifted the hood.

She watched his mustache twitch, watched recognition move through his face like ink through water. *Sefa Anwar, deckhand, Meridian Star, lost with all hands.* His mouth opened to say her name aloud — to make her real, to make her hunted —

And the world went orange.

Not her fire. That was her first mad thought — *I didn't light it yet, so whose is it?* — before she understood: it was hers after all, just lit by another hand. Because ten feet away, at the tar stores, a lantern had swung from its hook in the wind off the water, and swung again, and fallen. She would learn later that a dockhand named Pell, drunk as dawn, had knocked it with his shoulder and stumbled off swearing. But in the moment there was only this: flame kissing the seam of a tar barrel, the barrel blooming black smoke, and every customs man in the shed turning toward the light the way flowers turn toward the sun.

The officer's attention shattered. Sefa moved before thought could catch her.

She swept the shells back into the crate — didn't bother, half of them scattered — shoved past the table, and grabbed Ivo's sleeve as she came even with them in the chaos. Ivo didn't flinch. Nine years of ledgers had built a person who did not startle; they simply turned, took in the fire, the running men, the bucket line forming with the tragic slowness of officialdom everywhere, and adjusted course to match her stride.

"Not yet," Sefa breathed.

"I know." Ivo's voice was flat calm, infuriatingly calm. "Walk. Don't run. Running is memorable."

They walked. Behind them the customs shed vomited smoke, and officers shouted contradictory orders, and someone rang a bell that wasn't a fire bell but got answered anyway. The tar caught properly then, with a sound like a long exhale, and heat rolled across the yard in a wave Sefa felt against the back of her neck. The crate of pearl shell passed within arm's reach of three different men of the customs service, and not one pair of eyes touched it. Fire was the best diversion in the world because fire was honest. Everyone believed in it.

They were forty feet from the quay, close enough to see the gangway lamps of the *Grace*, when the shout went up behind them.

"You! Shell-runner! Stop!"

Sefa's heart performed a maneuver she hadn't known hearts could do. She knew that voice. The mustached officer, soot-streaked now, hands still smelling of tar, standing in the middle of the yard with her forged manifest crushed in his fist and her name — her true name, dragged up from three years down — already climbing his tongue.

"Sefa Anwar," he roared, and the whole quay heard it. "Lost crew of the *Meridian Star*! Seize her!"

*Run,* said every animal part of her, the part that had climbed rigging in storms and swum the cold mile under the harbor chain. And she ran — three strides, four — before Ivo's grip closed on her wrist like a shackle made of resolve.

"No," Ivo said. "Look. They're not looking at us."

They weren't. The officer was shouting, yes, pointing — but pointing past them, at the fire, at the smoke boiling over the customs shed where the company's most recent falsified manifests were filed, and the watchmen who should have been converging on a resurrected dead woman were instead converging on ten thousand pounds of burning evidence, because Director Corvane's orders about the records had been very clear and very recent, and fear of a director outranked pursuit of a ghost. Two men broke into a run toward the shed. One hesitated, torn between duties, and chose the fire. Only a single young watchman — barely shaving, lantern shaking in his hand — started toward Sefa and Ivo across the cobbles.

So Sefa did the bravest and stupidest thing she had done in a life well stocked with both.

"Ivo," she said, "take the shell. Take it up the gangway. Find your moment."

"And you—"

"Someone has to be memorable."

She kissed them — quickly, hard, on the mouth, tasting salt and ink and terror — and stepped out from behind the safe gray shadow of Ivo Brightwater for perhaps the first time since the wreck, and let herself be seen.

She walked straight at the boy with the lantern. She pulled back her hood. She let him see her face in full, the firelight gilding it, and she watched his eyes go wide as portholes as the impossible assembled itself in front of him.

"I'm Sefa Anwar," she said, loud enough to carry, loud enough for the yard, for the burning shed, for the ship at anchor beyond the chain. "Late of the *Meridian Star*, all hands reported lost, and I'm telling any magistrate who'll listen that we were never lost at sea — we were written off. Ask Director Corvane why."

The boy raised his whistle. The whistle shrieked. Boots thundered, and Sefa turned and ran at last, properly ran, hood flying back, taking the long way — away from the quay, away from Ivo, drawing the chase after her like a kite string.

And here was the thing she discovered in the running, in the cold wind and the hot blood and the shouts behind her multiplying like starlings: she was laughing. Terror and exhilaration had gotten tangled somewhere in her chest and become a third thing, fierce and bright, and she finally had a name for it. For nine days since the wreck she had been fighting for the dead — for Vane's letters, for drowned crewmates, for justice that tasted of ash. Justice was worth having. But justice was a debt paid backward. This — the running, the plan, the ink-smudged hand that had gripped hers, the morning tide coming — this was fighting forward. This was fighting for a future worth being alive inside.

She cut down the alley behind the chandlery, the one that dead-ended if you didn't know about the drain grate, and she knew about the drain grate. Behind her, boots and whistles and Corvane's name being shouted in fury. Ahead of her, somewhere, a clerk carrying a crate of pearl shell toward a gangway, carrying her future in packing straw.

She dropped through the grate into the dark, heart slamming, grinning like a lunatic at nothing.

Then, from the quay above, muffled by stone, she heard Ivo's voice — raised, formal, unmistakably the voice of a trusted Meridian clerk — saying words that stopped her blood:

"Director Corvane, sir. Yes. I have it. The packet is right here."
