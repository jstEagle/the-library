## 9. The Morning Tide

*Ivo*

The tide came in grey and gold at once, the way it did on mornings that did not know what they were yet. I stood at the foot of the quay with Director Corvane's seal warm in my breast pocket—freedom papers, he had called them, as though a man could be printed into existence—and I let him believe I was afraid.

I was afraid. That was the trick of it. Fear and resolve weigh almost nothing apart from each other, but carried together they are nearly unbearable, and I had learned in nine years of copying other men's figures how to carry weight without letting it show in my face.

"The packet," Corvane said. He was not a large man; he had never needed to be. His voice did the work of his fists. "You will hand it to me before the gangway, and you will board as my clerk, and by noon you will be a free man with a purse heavy enough to forget Greyhaven entirely."

Beyond him, the *Meridian's Pride* lay against the quay like a sleeping animal, her hull black with tar, her rails crowded with crates of pearl shell. Somewhere among those crates—no. Not anymore. Last night Sefa and I had moved the letters one final time, from shell to something no customs man would dare open.

"Before the gangway," I said. "Yes, Director."

The lie sat easily in my mouth. That frightened me more than anything else. I had spent nine years being honest in small ways precisely because I was dishonest in none; now I discovered that treachery, too, is a craft, and that I was better at it than I had ever been at invisibility.

Corvane studied me with pale eyes the color of harbor ice. "You understand what happens to clerks who disappoint me."

"I've seen the ledgers," I said. "I understand everything."

He smiled at that, believing it was surrender. It was, in its way. Just not to him.

* * *

*Sefa*

They gave me a porter's coat two sizes too large and a handcart loaded with sealed dispatches for the magistrate's ship, and I pushed it through the waking streets of Greyhaven while the gulls screamed their opinions overhead. The oilskin packet rode inside the third dispatch case from the left, sewn into the canvas lining by lamplight, beneath a magistrate's wax stamp that Ivo had forged so well it made me want to kiss them and arrest them in the same breath.

*You're certain,* I had asked, needle in hand, three hours before dawn.

*Certain as arithmetic,* Ivo had said. *A clerk is furniture, Sefa. Nobody searches furniture. They only search people trying not to be furniture.*

I had laughed then—a raw, cracked sound—and Ivo had looked up at me across the candle with an expression I would spend the rest of my life learning the name of.

Now the quay opened before me: the crowd of stevedores, the smell of pitch and brine and pearl shell drying in the sun, and Corvane's men in their grey coats, moving along the line of cargo like weevils through flour. One of them glanced at my cart. I kept my eyes down, my shoulders rounded, my step the plodding, apologetic step of a man paid too little to care. Nine years of watching Ivo taught me how to be nobody. It is a strange inheritance, to learn someone's armor by watching them wear it.

At the gangway, a guard put out a hand. "Dispatches?"

"For the magistrate aboard the audit vessel," I said, in Ivo's borrowed flatness. "Company seal."

He turned the top case over, saw the wax, saw the boredom in my face, and waved me up. Behind me I heard a whistle—two notes, low—the signal Ivo and I had agreed on, meaning *the cart is through.* Meaning also, if I let myself hear it honestly, *you are not alone.*

I climbed toward the deck of the ship that would either save us or hang us, and I did not look back, because looking back is how people drown.

* * *

*Ivo*

Sefa passed me in the crowd without a flicker of recognition, which was its own kind of miracle, and then there was nothing between me and Corvane but the packet—or the appearance of it, wrapped in oilskin exactly as the real one had been, weighted with oyster shell and a dead man's handwriting copied out in my own careful clerk's script. A forgery of a forgery. There was a poetry in it I suspected Vane himself would have appreciated.

"You have it," Corvane said. Not a question.

"I have it." I held it out. He took it, weighed it in his palm, and for one long breath the whole morning hung on the accuracy of my forgery and the depth of his greed.

He tucked it inside his coat. "Board," he said. "You sail with me. If the magistrate asks questions, you will answer them the way a loyal clerk answers."

"Aye, Director."

The gangway planks rang under my boots. Halfway up, I heard shouting behind me—not alarm, not yet, just the ordinary violence of the loading quay, a crate dropped, a man cursed—and I understood that Sefa had begun whatever she meant to do, and that my part now was simply to walk, unhurried, into the belly of the thing.

On deck, the first mate directed me to the clerk's alcove beside the great cabin, where the magistrate's dispatches were already stacked in their locked chest, waiting for the short crossing to the audit vessel anchored beyond the harbor chain. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Nine years of steady hands, and here they were at last doing something worth the steadiness.

I unlocked the dispatch chest with the mate's key, borrowed an hour ago and returned before he missed it, and lifted out the third case from the left. Inside, sewn into the canvas, the oilskin packet waited like a heartbeat.

I set it atop the stack of true dispatches, in plain sight, sealed with the magistrate's own wax, addressed in the magistrate's own cipher—which is to say, addressed to no one who would question it. Then I closed the chest, locked it, and sat down at the little desk to wait, as clerks wait, invisibly, for history to arrive.

* * *

*Sefa*

It went wrong the way things go wrong: quickly, and all at once.

I had made it forty yards from the quay when a hand closed on my collar and spun me around, and there was Brannick—Corvane's chief man, the one with the scarred knuckles and the memory like a ledger—staring at my face under the porter's cap.

"You," he said. "The mudflat woman."

There was no time for cleverness. The tar stores were behind me, the company's own supply shed, barrels stacked to the eaves, and I had spent the walk rehearsing this the way Ivo rehearsed figures. I pulled the lantern from the cart's hook and threw it.

Glass burst. Flame went up the barrel staves like a rumor going through a town. The heat shoved me backward and the shout went up—*fire, fire in the tar stores!*—and the quay dissolved into running men and rolling barrels and the bell in the gatehouse beginning to clang its stupid, beautiful head off.

Brannick still had my arm. In the orange light his face was all shadows and decision. "Where's the packet?" he shouted over the roar.

"Burned," I lied, "with everything else—" and drove my heel down on his instep, and twisted, and ran.

They chased me along the waterline, three of them, then four, boots hammering the boards. Ahead, the *Meridian's Pride* strained at her lines, and at her rail a small figure in clerk's drab stood watching me come, and I thought: *this is the choice, then. This is the whole of it.* I could dive into the harbor and swim for the mudflats and live, probably, maybe—or I could run at the gangway where Corvane's men were already gathering, because Ivo was on that ship, and some things matter more than surviving them.

I had spent my life choosing survival. It had gotten me half-drowned on oyster beds with a dead man's letters clutched to my chest.

I ran at the gangway.

The first man grabbed for me and got a fistful of coat. The second got my elbow in his teeth. The third raised a belaying pin, and I saw it start to fall—

—and it didn't land. The gate sergeant from the checkpoint, Pell, red-faced and breathing like a man sprinting from his own conscience, caught the swing and wrestled the man back, roaring about fire regulations and harbor law and anything else his mouth could find. It bought me three seconds. Three seconds is a long time, if you spend them well.

I came up the gangway on pure refusal, and hands hauled me over the rail, and I found myself face to face with Ivo Brightwater, who took my wrist and said, very quietly, in the voice of a person reading figures aloud:

"Anchor's lifting."

* * *

*Ivo*

Below us, Greyhaven burned in one corner and raged in all the others. Corvane stood at the quarterdeck rail screaming orders that the wind took apart, and I watched him reach into his coat for the packet—for *my* packet, full of shells and lies—and I allowed myself, for the first time in nine years, to smile openly where anyone could see.

The *Meridian's Pride* slid from the quay. The gap between hull and stone widened by inches, then feet, then the width of a man's courage. On the dockside, Corvane's men hurled grappling hooks and curses in equal measure; Pell and the gate sergeant cut the first rope with a hatchet, laughing like boys, and I understood suddenly that I had never been invisible at all—that all these years, people had been watching me the way you watch a lamp in a window, glad of it, counting on it, waiting for it to mean something.

The dispatch chest stood open at my feet. Sefa knelt beside it and drew out the oilskin packet, and together—we, plural, a word I was only just learning to bear—we broke the magistrate's seal in front of half the crew.

"What is this?" the first mate demanded, striding up.

"Letters," Sefa said, holding them high so the wind could read them too. "In the hand of Captain Aldous Vane. Naming the directors who framed him and the sailors they hanged for it. Bound for the magistrate aboard the audit vessel—as sealed company dispatches, delivered by your trusted clerk."

Every eye turned to me. I had imagined this moment all night and in every imagining I trembled. In fact I felt calm as tide-tables.

"I can confirm the chain of custody," I said. "I copied the original manifests myself. The falsifications are in our own ledgers, cross-referenced by date and vessel. The magistrate will want both sets of documents. I have them memorized regardless."

Somewhere beyond the harbor chain, a gun spoke—one round, high, the universal grammar of *heave to and prepare to be boarded*—and the audit vessel's sails came alive against the morning.

Sefa's hand found mine at the rail, salt-cracked and steady, and neither of us looked away from the widening water.

"Hold your course," she said.

And the *Meridian's Pride*, carrying the truth in her hold like ballast, ran out to meet it.
