## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Names

The fog came off the river before dawn and did not leave, and by the time Tamsin Sandoval had boiled the morning's first kettle for bandages, the windows of the sailmaker's workshop had gone the color of skimmed milk. She could not see the harbor. She could hear it—the groan of hulls against pilings, the cough of a tug somewhere out in the white, and beneath it all, faint but constant, the ice on the river muttering to itself like an old man who had forgotten how to sleep.

She carried the kettle past the long bench where canvas had been cut for sails in another life, past the racks of wooden blocks and the smell of tar and hemp that no amount of carbolic could ever quite drown, and knelt beside the cot in the corner where Private Aldous Fen lay with a wound in his thigh that the army had decided he did not have.

"Water's hot," she said. "This will sting."

"It stung when they shot me," Fen said. "I expect I can manage a wash."

He was twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, with a farm boy's broad hands and a mustache he was growing in defiance of regulations because there was no one left to enforce them. He had come to them eleven days ago in the hold of a coal barge, feverish, half-delirious, calling for a sergeant who was almost certainly dead. The field hospital had turned him away—not from cruelty, but from arithmetic: too many beds, too few of them for a man whose papers said *deserted, 14 October*, though Tamsin had seen the shrapnel hole in his tunic and knew desertion when she saw it, and this was not it.

"You'll want to bite down on something," she said, and gave him the leather strap.

She cleaned the wound the way Sister Okafor at the base hospital had taught her—quickly, thoroughly, without apology—and Fen bore it the way they all bore things now, jaw tight, eyes fixed on some point above her head where perhaps the ceiling beams arranged themselves into home. When she packed it with iodoform gauze his breath came out through his teeth in a long whistle, and then he said, "Thank you, miss," as if she'd handed him tea.

"Don't thank me yet. You've got fever coming back. I can smell it."

"I don't smell anything but tar."

"That's the fever," she said. "It makes everything smell like the last place you were happy." She stood, pressed the back of her hand to his forehead out of habit rather than need—he flinched from her cold fingers—and went to fetch the quinine.

* * *

The ledger lived under the third floorboard from the wall, the one with the knot in its grain, beneath the shelf where old Mr. Calloway the sailmaker had kept his waxed twine until the war took his trade and left him a building and nothing to put in it. Tamsin waited until Fen's breathing deepened into the shallow rhythm of exhausted sleep, then checked the street through a gap in the curtain—fog, a lamplighter making his rounds early because the dark came so soon now, nobody else—and only then did she kneel and lift the board.

The ledger itself was nothing. A stationer's book, cloth-bound, bought for sixpence from the shop on Cordwainer Lane by a woman buying ribbon and asking for nothing else. Its cover had once been green; the fog and the damp had aged it to the color of pond water. Inside were forty-one names.

Forty-one men whom the war had mislaid.

She sat on the floor with her back against the shelf, the book open across her knees, and read the last entry to herself the way a priest might reread his office. *Corporal Edmund Voss, 2nd Battalion, wounded at—* she never wrote places, only dates—*wounded, 3 November. Left arm amputated below elbow. Alive. Sent north on the *Merry Alice*, 19 December, to his aunt in Harrowgate.* The entries were short because brevity was safety. A page of names meant nothing to anyone who found it. It was the finding she intended never to permit.

She dipped her pen. She had made rules for herself in the beginning, back when the book held three names instead of forty-one, and she kept them the way other people kept faith: absolutely, without comfort.

No unit numbers that could be traced. No ranks above corporal—officers had their own machinery, however broken, and the machinery sometimes worked for them. Dates of wounding, not of birth. And always, always, the final line: what had become of them. *Alive. Sent to—* or *Died, 22 January. Buried in the naval cemetery, plot unmarked, stone to follow if God is good and the war ends.* Because that was the whole of it, the entire reason she bent over this book at five in the morning with her hands smelling of carbolic and someone else's blood: the army wrote men out when it suited it, and a man written out could not be mourned, could not be claimed, could not be found by a mother with a photograph and a train ticket and four years of hope. A man written out was worse than dead. Dead men had graves.

Aldous Fen had no grave and no record. He had a hole in his leg and a mother in Millbrook who had received, by now, either nothing at all or a printed card saying her son was absent without leave, which was a kind of death sentence delivered by clerk.

Tamsin wrote:

*Aldous Fen. Wounded, 9 February. Thigh, shrapnel. Fever returning. Alive.*

Then she sat with the pen still poised, because there was one more line, and the line was the dangerous one. Under each entry, in a hand smaller than the rest—a hand she had taught herself, cramped and narrow, nothing like the confident roundness of her ordinary writing—she noted how the man had come to her. Not names. Never names. But routes. *Barge.* *Fish cart.* *Walked.* It was the map of a smuggling operation, and she knew it, and she had drawn it anyway, because someday—if there was a someday, if the ice broke and spring came and the war staggered toward whatever passed for an ending—someone official would have to be shown. Someone would have to count these men properly, give them their beds and their boards and their discharges and their mothers' arms. And that someone would ask how. And she would answer.

She wrote *coal barge* under Fen's name, closed the book, and returned it beneath the floorboard, pressing the board down with both palms until it seated with a sound no louder than a heartbeat.

* * *

Mr. Calloway arrived at seven, stamping fog off his boots in the doorway, carrying a paper sack of bread that he set down on the bench without ceremony, because ceremony had died in this room sometime during the second winter.

"Fen still breathing?"

"For now. Fever's climbing."

Calloway grunted. He was seventy if he was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and a face that gave away nothing except to those who knew where to look, and Tamsin had learned where to look within a week of arriving. He crossed to Fen's cot, looked down at him for a long moment—the boy slept through it, sunk past dreaming—and said, "My son would've liked him."

"He would've," Tamsin said, because Calloway's son had gone down with a destroyer in '15 and the old man permitted himself exactly one sentence about it per day, and it was kinder to let him spend it here than save it up.

"The *Merry Alice* docks Thursday," Calloway said, meaning the boat, meaning the next movement south, meaning the next set of arrangements that existed nowhere on paper and therefore could not hang anyone. "If the fever breaks."

"If the fever breaks," she agreed.

He nodded, satisfied with the terms of the world as negotiated between himself and it, and went to his bench to work at a repair job for a fishing smack—work he did badly on purpose, slowly, taking twice the hours the job needed, because a sailmaker with work was a sailmaker with a reason to have strangers' boots by his door.

Tamsin washed her hands, tied on her apron, and began the day's accounting: bandages to boil, the good stove to keep fed without smoking the chimney—for smoke drew eyes, and eyes were the enemy now more than any officer—the stores to count, the two men upstairs in the loft to feed and keep quiet. The workshop held six souls besides herself and Calloway, counting Fen. Six coats hung on the pegs by the door because six men needed to look, at a glance, like one sailmaker's household. One bed visible in the loft, six pairs of boots under it, a fiction maintained with the diligence other people spent on prayer.

It had started small. That was the thing she could never make anyone understand, the thing she suspected she would die without successfully explaining: it had started with one man, a single night, a decision so small she hadn't recognized it as a decision at all. A corporal carried in half-conscious by a fisherman who wouldn't say where he'd found him, a bed made up on the floor, a week of fever, and then the question she had asked herself standing over his sleeping body with her ledger not yet imagined: *who will tell anyone this man exists?* And the answer, which had arrived whole and terrible: *no one, unless I do.*

So she had. That was all. That was everything. She had chosen it the way you choose to bail a leaking boat—not because you believe you'll reach shore, but because the alternative is to watch the water rise and call that fate.

She was folding boiled bandages when the knock came.

* * *

Three knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried—the knock of a man with business, not trouble. Trouble knocked fast and many times. She wiped her hands, glanced at Calloway, who gave the smallest possible shake of his head: *not expected*. Upstairs, she could feel the loft going silent the way it had learned to, three men becoming furniture.

She opened the door on a thin man in a coat too good for the district, holding a leather satchel and a board with a clipboard, spectacles fogged immediately in the warm air of the doorway. Behind him the fog stood in the street like a wall.

"Morning," he said. "Elias Wren. Census. District twelve—I'm doing the harbor front today." He produced a card, official-looking, creased from a pocket. "May I ask you some questions? Shouldn't take five minutes."

"We're just a workshop," Tamsin said. "One sailmaker, one nurse. There's hardly anyone to count."

"Then it'll be quick." He smiled. It was a tired smile, the smile of a man who had been knocking on doors since eight o'clock and would go on knocking until dark, and she felt herself relaxing against her own judgment, which was precisely, she understood later, how he worked.

She stepped aside. He entered, stamped his feet, looked around with an assessor's eye—she watched him take in the bench, the stove, the boots—and began to write before he asked anything, which unsettled her more than questions would have.

"Name of head of household?"

"Calloway. Josiah Calloway. Sailmaker."

"And you?"

"Tamsin Sandoval. Nurse. I lodge here and tend—" she let the pause stretch a half-second— "the old man's rheumatism."

"Mm." He wrote. "How many sleep in the building?"

"One bed," she said. "Mine's in the loft. Mr. Calloway has his rooms behind the shop."

His pen paused. He looked up, not at her—at the pegs by the door, at the coats, and then down, briefly, at the boots beneath them. Six coats. Six pairs of boots. One bed. She saw the sum arrive behind his eyes with the clean inevitability of tide.

"Big household for two people," he said mildly. "Lots of outerwear."

"Sailmakers get coats left as payment," Calloway said from his bench, without turning around, in the voice of a man discussing tar. "Half the waterfront owes somebody something."

"Of course." Wren wrote again. Then, conversationally, terribly: "And the loft? Census regulation says I note sleeping arrangements. Loft access—ladder or stair?"

"Ladder," Tamsin said. "Steep. You'd want to leave your satchel down here."

"I imagine I would." He closed his notebook partway, keeping his thumb in it like a man marking scripture. At the door he stopped, and the fog behind him seemed to lean in with him, and he said, in a different tone entirely—one with no clipboard in it, one that sounded almost human—"Mrs. Sandoval. Miss. Forgive me. The harbor district is the hardest to count. Half these buildings hold more people than they admit and fewer than they should. I'm not the police. Whatever number I write tonight, it goes into a ledger in a government office, and ledgers are where people get lost." He settled his spectacles. "I'd hate to lose anyone who didn't deserve losing."

He tipped his hat and went out into the white, and the door closed, and Tamsin stood with her hand flat against it, feeling her own pulse in her palm.

Calloway spoke without looking up from his canvas. "Well?"

"He counted the boots."

"They're just boots."

"Yes," she said. "That's what worries me."

She went back to the floorboard that night after the lamps were out, lifted it, and added a line to the inside cover in her smallest hand—not a name, but a date, and beside it: *census, district twelve. Watched.* Then she sat in the dark with the book against her chest, listening to the river ice groan, and understood with a clarity that felt almost like relief that the quiet years of her work were finished. Whatever came next would come quickly, and she would meet it with forty-one names in a sixpenny book, and she would not run, because running meant the names stayed lost, and she had promised every man in that book—though most of them never knew it—that she would be the one who remembered.

Above her, through the floorboards, the loft creaked once. Someone turning in sleep. Someone alive, and uncounted, and hers to keep.

She put the book away and blew out the candle.
