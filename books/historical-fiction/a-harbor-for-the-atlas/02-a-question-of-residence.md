## 2. A Question of Residence

The census man came on a Tuesday, when the fog had settled into the harbor like a guest who intended to stay the winter.

Tamsin heard him before she saw him—boots on the cobbles, measured and unhurried, the walk of a man who counted things for a living. She was at the workbench with a sail needle in her hand, mending a jib that belonged to no one, and she did not look up. Looking up was a confession. She had learned that much in three years: the eyes give away what the mouth has been drilled to keep.

"Good morning." His voice carried through the workshop's open door, polite and slightly nasal, the accent of somewhere inland where rivers were small and nobody drowned. "I'm looking for the sailmaker's premises."

"You've found them." She set the needle down in its groove of wax and rose, wiping her palms on her apron—a gesture she'd practiced until it looked like habit instead of theater. "Though if you've come about canvas, we're weeks behind like everyone else."

"Not canvas." He stepped inside, bringing the fog with him, a gray dampness that clung to his coat shoulders. He was younger than she expected—thirty perhaps, maybe thirty-two—with spectacles that had fogged at the rims and a leather satchel worn shiny at the corners from years of carrying it. He produced a document folder, official buff paper, and a pencil already sharpened to a stub. "Elias Wren. Census of the harbor district. I'm required to enumerate every dwelling between the breakwater and the tannery road."

"Then you'll be busy." Tamsin kept her voice flat, pleasant, bored. Boredom was armor too. "Half these buildings are empty. The other half are falling down."

"And this one?"

"Occupied." She said it lightly, as though the word were simple.

His pencil moved. *Sailmaker's workshop, occupied.* Then he looked up, and behind the fogged spectacles his eyes were the color of the river in March—pale, patient, and moving under the surface toward something. "By whom?"

"The owner. Mr. Calloway. He keeps rooms above the shop." This was true, or true enough. Old Calloway had signed papers once, taken a fee, asked no questions, and gone to live with his sister in the valley, leaving Tamsin the keys and the rent book and his name to use like a borrowed coat.

"Just him?"

"Him and me. I do his sewing." She let a smile flicker—the tired smile of a workingwoman interrupted—and turned toward the bench as though the interview were over. "You can put down two. One sailmaker, absent. One seamstress, present. I'd be happy to sign whatever you need."

He didn't move. That was the first wrong thing. Census men, in her experience—and she had watched them work the waterfront twice before—wanted signatures and speed. They wanted to finish the district by dark and get to the public house. They did not stand in doorways letting the cold in, reading a room.

But Elias Wren was reading the room.

She felt his attention travel the way a draft travels—along the floor, up the walls, across the ceiling beams hung with drying canvas. She knew precisely what he would find, because she had spent three years arranging what he would find, and she knew the arrangement was not perfect because perfection is a luxury and war taxes everything, including lies.

One bed, visible through the curtain of the back alcove. Hers. Iron frame, army blanket, one pillow dented to the shape of her head.

Six coats on the pegs by the stove. She had meant to thin them out—had meant to for a month—but coats are difficult things to explain away, and each of them belonged to a man who might need it again when the fever broke or the wound closed, and so they hung there, six of them, like six men standing very still.

And boots. Boots by the door, boots under the bench, boots tucked beneath the cot where Private Hollis slept through his fever in the loft she called a storage space. Too many boots. She had counted them herself last night by candlelight—eleven pairs—and had thought, with the peculiar clarity that comes at three in the morning, *a woman alone does not own eleven pairs of boots*, and then had fallen asleep anyway because exhaustion forgives everything except arithmetic.

"Mind the stove," she said. "It smokes when the wind's off the river."

"I'll be brief." But he wasn't moving toward the door. He was moving toward the pegs. "Cold spring for canvas work," he remarked, touching the sleeve of the nearest coat. A greatcoat. Army issue. The buttons had been cut off and replaced with horn, but the wool was the wool, gray-green and government-milled, and no horn button in the world could make it civilian.

"Calloway's son was in the territorials," she said. The lie came out smooth and warm and ready, because she had built it months ago against exactly this moment, brick by brick, in the hours when she couldn't sleep. "Killed last autumn. His father can't bring himself to clear the pegs."

Wren's hand came off the coat slowly. Something crossed his face—not suspicion exactly; something quieter and worse. Recognition. The look of a man who has heard a story that fits too well, the way a lid fits a box.

"I'm sorry," he said, and she believed that he meant it, which unsettled her more than suspicion would have. Suspicion she could fight. Pity got under the door.

"It was some time ago." She picked up the census form herself, holding it out like a shield. "If you'll write the names, I'll sign. Two residents. You have your count, Mr. Wren, and I have a hem due by noon."

He took the form. But he didn't write. Instead he stood there, pencil poised, and asked the question that cracked her armor—not with force, but with precision:

"How many take their meals here?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, in the loft, a floorboard shifted under a sleeping man's weight, and Tamsin's heart performed a maneuver she had trained it never to perform—it stopped.

"That's an odd question for a census," she said. Her voice held. Her voice always held; it was the rest of her she worried about. "Meals aren't enumerated. Beds are."

"No," he agreed. "They're not." He wrote *two* in the residents' column, in a small, exact hand. Then, without appearing to decide to, he added something else—a note, a mark, a number—in the margin, and closed the folder over it. "There's a kettle on, isn't there? I smelled it from the lane."

"You have a sharp nose for tea."

"For arithmetic," he said mildly. "A kettle that size boils for more than two."

* * *

She gave him tea because refusing would have cost more than pouring.

He sat at the workbench table while she filled the chipped brown pot, and he talked—about the census, about the fog delaying the ferry, about the price of coal—and all the while his eyes went on their quiet inventory. She watched him watch the room and understood that she was watching a man count boots without counting boots. Eleven pairs. Six coats. One bed. A kettle sized for a mess.

Under the loft, Private Hollis coughed.

It was a small sound—a single dry syllable of a cough, half-swallowed, the cough of a man who had been drilled for three days to hold his lungs silent—and to anyone else it would have been nothing, the house settling, a gull on the roof. But Wren's pencil hand paused above his cup, and Tamsin saw him file it away next to the coats and the boots and the margin-note, another entry in whatever private ledger he kept behind his spectacles.

"My cat," Tamsin said.

"You keep a cat in a sailmaker's loft?"

"She keeps herself. She earns her keep in rats." She smiled again, and hated how easily the smile came now, how fluent lying had become, the way handwriting deteriorates and then stabilizes into something efficient and ugly. "Though she's been scarce since the frost."

"Mm." He drank his tea. He asked her name for the form—Tamsin Sandoval, seamstress, age thirty-one, born in the county—and wrote it in that small exact hand, and then he asked, folding the folder shut, as casually as a man asking the time: "How long have you worked for Mr. Calloway?"

"Two years."

"And before that?"

"A convalescent hospital. Up the coast."

"Ah." He nodded, satisfied, and she realized her error a full second after making it, the way you realize a stitch has dropped only when the seam splits. A hospital nurse. A woman who had spent the war learning to feed men, wash men, bandage men, lift men—living alone above a workshop with one bed and eleven pairs of boots. Every fact she offered him fit the picture better than any lie could have. She had handed him the truth in pieces and each piece was a nail.

"Well." He stood, buttoning his coat, tucking the folder under his arm. At the door he paused, and the fog stood behind him like a congregation. "Thank you for the tea, Miss Sandoval. I may have to come back—there's a discrepancy in my district totals. The vicar's register and my count don't agree, and I'm required to reconcile them before submitting." He adjusted his spectacles. "You understand. Numbers that don't add up trouble me. It's the whole difficulty of my profession."

"Numbers rarely behave," Tamsin said.

"No." He looked past her shoulder, just once, at the pegs with their six silent coats—at the largest of them, broad-shouldered, tall, belonging to a sergeant who was at that moment asleep ten feet above his own empty sleeves. "They don't. Good day."

* * *

She waited until his footsteps faded into the fog, until the cobbles gave back silence, until she had counted to three hundred with her hand pressed flat against the door. Then she climbed the ladder to the loft.

Hollis was awake, propped on one elbow, gray-faced, listening the way the wounded learn to listen—with their whole bodies.

"He's gone?" he whispered.

"For now." She knelt and put the back of her hand to his forehead. The fever was down; the wound in his side was knitting; by week's end he could go to the fishing boat and the fog route north, and one pair of boots would leave this house forever. "How much did you hear?"

"All of it." His young face worked. "The cat. Christ, miss, I'm sorry—I couldn't—"

"You held it. It was nothing." She said it firmly enough that he almost believed her. "Listen to me. When the boat comes Friday, you go, and you don't write, and you don't send word, and you don't tell anyone where you healed, ever. Not your mother. Not your sweetheart. No one. Because the men who wrote you down as a deserter will shoot you for the truth, and the only thing keeping you alive is that you were never here."

"Yes, miss." A pause. "Was he army? The census man?"

"No. Worse." She climbed down and stood in the middle of the workshop, turning slowly, seeing it as Wren had seen it—the coats, the boots, the bed, the kettle, the margin-note folded away in official buff paper. "Army men want deserters. That man wants *numbers*. And numbers—" she looked at the ledger's hiding place beneath the third floorboard by the hearth, where forty-one names slept in her cramped, careful hand, "—numbers get audited."

That night she did not sleep. She took the ledger out and read it by candle-shielded light, running her thumb down the columns of men the reports had erased, and for the first time since she had begun it she saw the pages as someone else might see them: evidence. Not a memorial. Evidence. Forty-one counts of a crime the army had committed and she had documented, and she had hidden that documentation four feet from a hearth in a building a man with pale, patient eyes had already decided did not add up.

Near dawn she took the six coats off the pegs and sewed civilian buttons onto every one of them, and packed two pairs of boots into a sack for the rag-and-bone man, and told herself the room was cleaner now, safer now.

But she caught her reflection in the black window glass, candlelit, hollow-eyed, and what she saw there frightened her more than Sergeant Quill or the military police or the whole machinery of the war office combined. She saw a woman who had believed herself invisible for three years and had just discovered the belief was a fiction—one polite man in fogged spectacles had seen through it in eleven minutes, without raising his voice, armed with nothing but a pencil and a talent for noticing.

He would come back. He had told her he would come back—*discrepancy in my district totals*—and he had said it the way careful men say important things: lightly.

Tamsin banked the fire, slid the ledger home beneath the floorboard, and lay down on her one bed in her one visible room, and did not close her eyes.

Outside, the fog thickened over the harbor until the ships disappeared entirely, and somewhere in it, she was certain, a man was walking with a satchel, reconciling his numbers, deciding what to do about a kettle that boiled for more than two.
