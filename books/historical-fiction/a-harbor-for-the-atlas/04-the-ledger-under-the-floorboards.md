## 4. The Ledger Under the Floorboards

Captain Merrick arrived on a Tuesday in the third week of February, carried up the workshop stairs on a stretcher by two orderlies who would not meet anyone's eyes. They set him down in the corner bed—the one with the good frame, salvaged from a wrecked hotel—and left without signing anything, which told Tamsin everything about how he had come to be there.

"He's nobody," the older orderly said at the door, pulling his cap down. "Officially. You never saw him."

"Nobody bleeds like that," Tamsin said.

"Then you never saw it." And he was gone, boots ringing on the stairs, out into the fog that had sat over the harbor for six days like something drowned and unwilling to rise.

She turned to the man on the stretcher. He was perhaps forty, gray at the temples, with the particular pallor of a man who has lost blood faster than his body can argue about it. A shell fragment had opened his left side below the ribs—not deep enough to kill, deep enough to matter. His uniform was an officer's, but someone had stripped the insignia from it with more haste than care; she could see the ghost of darker wool where the pips had been.

Tamsin stood over him for a long moment, doing the arithmetic she always did. One more mouth. One more body to hide when the inspectors came. One more name.

Or no name at all, if he was lucky.

* * *

He woke properly on the second day, and the first thing he did was look at the ceiling beams and count them.

"Twelve," he said. His voice was dry as old rope. "This is not a hospital."

"This is a sailmaker's workshop," Tamsin said, coming to the bedside with the basin. "It hasn't been a sailmaker's workshop since 1914, and it isn't a hospital now. Drink."

He drank. She watched his eyes move while he did it—officers' eyes were trained to move, to inventory, to assess—and she saw him take in the rows of canvas hung from the rafters like ship's ghosts, the long trestle table scarred with wax and tar, the stove with its kettle, the six cots arranged along the north wall under the high salt-fogged windows. Four of the cots were empty. Two were occupied by men who had turned their faces to the wall with the practiced smoothness of men who had done it before.

"You have other patients," Merrick said.

"I have lodgers."

"Lodgers who don't turn around."

"People here mind their own business. It's the harbor way."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Sister Sandoval."

"You know my name."

"The orderlies talk. They talk about a nurse down by the water who asks no questions and files no reports and keeps men alive that the army has already finished with." He shifted, winced, and went pale under the stubble. "I'm told you're very good."

"I'm told you're nobody," Tamsin said. "So we should get along well."

This time the smile came all the way, briefly, and then the pain took it away, and she gave him the morphine and let him sleep, and stood afterward at the window watching the fog eat the masts along the quay, and felt the first cold thread of what she would later recognize as fear.

Because Captain Merrick was not a deserter. He was not a shell-shocked private gone missing from his line. He was an officer, convalescing off the books, which meant someone above him wanted him invisible too—which meant he knew things, or was suspected of knowing things, or had displeased someone with reach. Men like that brought weather with them. And her workshop had thin walls and thinner legal standing, and beneath the third floorboard from the stove, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a ledger with forty-one names in it that could hang every person in this room.

* * *

The trouble began on the fifth day, when Merrick started walking.

He walked the way officers walked—shortly, purposefully, as though the room owed him a destination. Dr. Fenwick had said three weeks before he'd bear weight; Merrick managed it in eight days, gripping the trestle table, jaw set, sweat standing on his forehead, and Tamsin let him because she had learned long ago that forbidding a soldier anything only made it a mission.

But he walked everywhere. That was the problem. He walked to the window and counted boats. He walked to the door and studied the lock. He walked past the other cots and paused, and Private Aldous—who had no feet worth mentioning and a discharge that existed nowhere but in Tamsin's ledger—kept his face to the wall so steadily that Tamsin feared he would grow into the posture.

And on the fifth day of his walking, he stopped beside the stove, where the floorboards ran oldest and loosest, and looked down.

"What's under there?" he asked.

Tamsin, boiling bandages at the copper, did not miss a beat. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

"Rats," she said. "We poison them. The boards come up for the bait trays."

Merrick looked at her. In the light from the window his eyes were a flat harbor-gray, patient, the eyes of a man accustomed to being lied to by professionals and enjoying the practice of catching them at it.

"Rats," he repeated.

"Harbor rats. Big as cats. The sailmaker used to say they'd learned to read."

"And the bait trays are kept under the floor."

"Where else? On the table?" She lifted the bandages out with the wooden tongs, steam rising between them, and laid them across the drying rack with hands that did not shake. "Captain, you've been on morphine for five days. If I were hiding something under my floor, do you think I'd tell you the truth about it?"

"No," he said agreeably. "I don't. That's rather the point."

He let it go. He limped back to his cot and sat, breathing carefully through the pain, and Tamsin wrung out bandages and thought: *he doesn't believe me, and I lied to him without even paying for it, the lie came out of me like water.*

That night she lay in her narrow room above the workshop and listened to the building settle and the ice groan out in the channel—a long, low complaint, like a giant turning in its sleep—and she made herself look honestly at what she had become.

She had started the ledger as an act of witness. That was the story she told herself, and mostly it was true. The names had begun accumulating in the autumn of '15, when a boy named Wilfred Crane died in her ward of gas gangrene and the casualty list that reached his mother in Leeds said *missing, presumed deserted.* She had written Wilfred Crane's name in a school exercise book because the official paper had refused to hold it, and once she had started she could not stop. Every man the machinery of war spat out unrecorded—dead without papers, mad without diagnosis, wounded and shipped home under another man's number, vanished into the administrative fog that hung over this port thicker than any weather—she wrote them down. Date, name, unit if known, wound, fate. Forty-one entries now. Forty-one small resurrections in ink.

But somewhere in two years of keeping the book alive, keeping the men alive, keeping herself alive, she had become fluent in lying. To census men and provosts and doctors and priests. To the families who wrote asking after sons she had buried in the pauper's field behind St. Aubin's, whose letters she answered in a careful hand with careful half-truths because the whole truth would bring investigators down on forty living men. She lied the way she boiled bandages—routinely, thoroughly, without thinking—and today she had heard herself tell a decorated captain about rat bait with a steady voice and warm eyes, and she had hated it, and she had done it anyway, because the alternative was the ledger under the floorboard and everything it held.

*The names are safe,* she told herself. *That's what matters. The rest is scaffolding.*

But she remembered her mother, a chapel woman with a spine like a mast, saying: *a lie is a debt, Tamsin. It always comes due, and always with interest.* And lying under the floor of her own conscience was a ledger of a different kind, and it was filling too.

* * *

On the ninth day, Merrick found the edge of it.

Not the ledger itself—she had moved it by then, after the rat-bait conversation, wedged it inside the false bottom of her instrument case, which no one searched because no one wanted to touch a nurse's instruments—but the fact of it, the shape of it, the way a drowning man finds the edge of a current before he understands the sea.

He found it because of Aldous.

Aldous had fever that evening—not serious, but enough to loosen him—and when Tamsin changed his dressings he talked, the way fevered men talk, half-dreaming. He talked about Ypres and the mud and his mother's kitchen, and then he said, clear as a bell: "Will you write it down, Sister? What happened to me? So somebody knows?"

"I write everything down," Tamsin said gently. "Sleep now."

Across the room, Merrick had gone very still.

She felt it the way you feel a change in wind. She finished the dressing, bathed Aldous's forehead, dosed him, and only then turned, and found the captain watching her with an expression she could not file anywhere—not suspicion exactly, not pity. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a language he'd almost forgotten.

"You keep records," he said quietly.

"I keep notes. For continuity of care. Any nurse does."

"Continuity of care." He tested the phrase like a coin bitten for gold. "For men who officially aren't here."

"For patients," Tamsin said. "Whoever sends them. However they arrive." She began gathering the basin, the cloths, the kidney dish, arranging her hands in work because work was the best armor she owned. "You should sleep, Captain. Fever's going round."

"Sister." He waited until she looked at him. "I signed casualty returns for eleven months. I know what an omission looks like. I know what it costs to make one, and what it looks like on the face of the person who has to carry it." His voice was low, pitched under the sound of the stove, under Aldous's breathing, under the groan of the river ice. "Whatever you're carrying—it's heavy. I can see the way you stand."

"The way I stand is from lifting soldiers," Tamsin said. "Good night, Captain."

She put out the lamp by his cot and crossed the dark room to the stairs, and her heart was going like a hammer, and behind her she heard him settle back against his pillow and say, softly, to the ceiling or to her or to no one:

"I won't ask again. But if you ever need a signature—I'm told I'm nobody. Nobody's very good at those."

* * *

She did not sleep. At two in the morning she went down into the cold workshop with a candle, knelt at the instrument case, and took out the ledger, and sat with it on her knees at the trestle table like a woman at a deathbed vigil.

Forty-one names. She knew them all—not just the spellings but the weights of them, the way each entry had arrived: carried, dragged, wept, dictated by a dying mouth. She turned pages. The candle flame leaned in the draft from under the door.

And she understood, sitting there in the dark with the ice complaining out in the channel, that the ledger had stopped being only a witness. It had become a liability with a heartbeat. Every name in it pointed at her. Every lie she told to protect it was another strand in a net that would hold her as surely as it held the men. And now there was an officer in the next room—an officer with eleven months of casualty returns behind his eyes—who had seen the net and named its weight aloud.

She should move the book again. She should burn it. She had thought about burning it maybe a hundred times, usually at three in the morning, usually after Quill's predecessors—there had been others, minor functionaries, sniffing along the waterfront—had come and gone. Burning it would be safe. Burning it would also be the second death of every man in it, and she had promised them, wordlessly, page by page, that there would not be a second death.

Outside, the river spoke again—that long grinding exhalation, louder this winter than any before, the old fishermen saying the ice was rotten early, saying breakup would come fierce this year. Tamsin pressed her palm flat against the open page, over the most recent name, and felt the paper cold as the floorboards.

Footsteps on the stair.

She closed the ledger in one motion, slid it beneath the trestle table's canvas apron, and had the candle shielded and her face composed before the door swung open and Captain Merrick stood in it, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his side, sweat shining at his temple—he had torn something, or nearly, getting down the stairs alone at two in the morning like a man walking in his sleep.

"Sister," he said. "I need to tell you something. And I think—" his gaze went, deliberate and unmistakable, to the canvas apron of the table, and back to her face, "—I think you're going to want to write it down."
