## 5. Ice on the River

Elias Wren had corrected worse numbers than a harbor district's. He had once spent eleven days reconciling the parish rolls of a mining town where half the men had gone down a shaft and the other half had gone west, and no two documents agreed on who remained. Numbers, in his experience, lied for the same reasons people did: fear, shame, and the desire to be left alone.

But he could not stop thinking about six coats on one hook.

It had been nine days since his visit to the sailmaker's workshop, nine days of ordinary rounds—the chandler's widow, the rope-walk tenements, the pilot families whose houses leaned against the seawall like tired drunks—and still the workshop sat in his mind like a stone in a boot. One bed registered. Six coats. Fourteen pairs of boots by the door, counted in the three seconds before the nurse had stepped into the doorway and made counting impossible. He had written *one resident, T. Sandoval, nurse* in his ledger with a hand that had felt, for the first time in fourteen years of census work, like it was committing a forgery.

The regulations said a discrepancy of that size warranted a report to the district military liaison. The war had made every count a matter of security; unregistered persons were deserters, or worse—foreign agents, saboteurs, men who moved through port cities like water through a cracked hull. Elias knew the procedure. He had recited it to himself on the walk home that night, and again the next morning, and each time the words had dissolved on his tongue like river ice in April.

Because there was another thing he could not stop thinking about, and it was not boots.

It was her hands.

---

He told himself the pretext was legitimate. The Bureau of Enumeration did allow corrections; a census taken under wartime conditions was expected to require them. He wrote out the correction slip himself, in his careful clerk's hand: *Re-survey required — dwelling classification uncertain — Workshop, old sailmaker's, Quay Lane.* He stamped it with the office stamp, which was technically permitted and technically unnecessary, and he chose a Tuesday afternoon because Tuesdays were when the military patrols crossed to the east side of the harbor, and he did not examine too closely how he knew this.

The fog came off the river in long gray ropes. It was the kind of afternoon where the harbor ceased to exist as a place and became instead a collection of sounds: hawsers creaking, a bell buoy tolling somewhere unseen, the groan of the ice field downstream where the river narrowed and the winter's plates ground against each other like teeth. Spring was weeks away yet, but the ice had begun its arguments early this year. Everyone said so. Everyone said it like a warning.

Quay Lane smelled of tar and cold salt. The workshop stood at its end, low-shouldered under the warehouse wall, its windows fogged from within.

Fogged from within.

Elias stopped in the lane and looked at those windows for a long moment. A building housing one nurse and no patients did not steam its glass on a February afternoon. A building with a stove going hot enough to breathe through the seams—

He went up the three steps and knocked, and while he waited he heard it clearly: the sound of water at a boil. And beneath it, fainter, muffled as if someone were trying to hold it underwater—a man's voice, saying something that ended in a moan.

The door opened. Tamsin Sandoval stood in it with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and steam rising off her forearms, and for one bare instant, before she arranged her face into polite blankness, Elias saw what was underneath: a woman caught, weighing in a single heartbeat whether the man on her step was a problem to be managed or an end to be endured.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "Census."

"Correction survey," he said, and held up the slip as though the paper itself proved his innocence. "Dwelling classification. It takes five minutes."

"It took you forty last time."

"The stove is going," he said. "I'll be faster warm."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like the ghost of a decision she decided not to make. She stepped back. "Five minutes."

---

Inside, the workshop had been transformed in a way that was less a disguise than a confession nobody had asked for. The trestle tables were pushed together and draped with canvas. A second bed—no, a third, its frame visible behind a hanging sailcloth—stood along the north wall. The air was thick with carbolic and wet wool and the particular sweetish smell Elias had learned to know at nineteen years old and had never since been able to separate from grief: the smell of wounds kept too long from proper care.

And over the stove, a great dented copper boiler, and in it bandages—strips of boiled linen being lifted out with wooden tongs by a young man in a hospital shirt with no buttons, a young man whose left arm ended just below the elbow and whose face was gray with pain and fever.

Elias looked at him. The young man looked at Elias. And the whole arithmetic of the harbor district rearranged itself in the space between their two glances.

"You have patients," Elias said quietly.

"I have a residence," Tamsin said, "with one occupant. That's what your form says."

"That's what my form says," Elias agreed. He set his satchel down on a stool, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weight he intends not to pick up again soon. "Sergeant Aldous Vey, Third Battalion, listed missing at—" He stopped. He hadn't meant to say the name aloud. It had simply come up out of him like groundwater, because he knew the boy's face now, didn't he—he'd seen it in the casualty photographs the harbor office kept pinned to corkboard, faces of the missing that families paid a penny apiece to have displayed, hoping some sailor might recognize a son.

The young man with one arm had gone very still. "You know my name," he said. His voice cracked on it. "They told my mother I was dead. They sent her the telegram in November."

"They sent a lot of telegrams in November," Tamsin said. Her voice was level, but Elias heard the strain in it, the sound of a dam holding. "Mr. Wren. Your five minutes."

"Yes," Elias said. "My five minutes."

He should take out the correction form. He should ask her about occupancy, about heating fuel consumed versus registered residents, about any of the twenty questions that would let him do his duty and walk away clean. The regulations were in his breast pocket, literally printed there in his field manual. All he had to do was be the man the government had hired, and Sergeant Vey would go into a report, and a patrol would come, and the boy would be processed—court-martialed or shipped home or vanished into the same paperwork that had already declared him dead once, whichever way the army's mood ran that week.

Elias thought about his brother.

Thomas Wren, Second Battalion, reported killed in action, October 1915. Except that three months later a chaplain's letter had arrived, apologizing—an administrative error, the name transposed, the wrong Wren. Thomas was not confirmed dead. Thomas was not confirmed anything. His name had appeared on the casualty list and then been struck from it, and in the space between those two lines their mother had buried a son and dug him up again and never known which grave to stand at. She had died the following spring with the question still open, and Elias had inherited it along with Thomas's watch, which he carried in his coat pocket and which had stopped at 4:17 and which he had never repaired, because repairing it would feel like closing something.

A name erased was not a small thing. He knew that better than most clerks. A name erased was a mother standing forever at the wrong grave.

"I'm not here for the count," Elias said.

Tamsin's chin came up slightly. Waiting.

"I'm here because I've spent nine days trying to make your numbers lie to me convincingly," he said, "and they won't. Six coats. Fourteen boots. One bed. And now a sergeant the army killed in November, boiling bandages in your kitchen." He paused. "I lost a brother to an omission. Not a bullet—to an omission. His name went on a list and then it came off, and my family spent eight months not knowing whether we were mourning or waiting." He looked at the boy, at Vey, who was watching him with the desperate attention of a drowning man watching a rope. "So I'm not going to write a report. But I am going to ask you a question, and I'd like you not to lie to me, because I think we're both tired of lying."

Tamsin said nothing. The boiler hissed. Somewhere outside, the river ice gave a long groaning complaint, the sound carrying through the fog like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"What ledger?" Elias asked.

Her breath went out of her slowly. Not surprise—he realized. Recognition. As if she had been waiting nine days too, listening for these exact footsteps on her stairs, and had dreaded them, and had dreaded more the possibility that they would never come.

"How do you know there's a ledger?"

"Because you're a woman who counts," he said. "I saw it in your eyes when I was counting your coats. You weren't afraid of me. You were afraid I'd find the record and not understand what it was for."

For a long moment she studied him, and Elias had the strange sensation of being read himself—of being entered into someone else's book, weighed, and found either worth the ink or not. The fire ticked in the stove. Vey shifted on his stool and winced and said nothing.

Then Tamsin turned, crossed to the trestle table, and did not go beneath the canvas apron at all. Instead she pulled open a drawer beneath the bench, lifted out a false bottom made of sailcloth stretched over a frame, and brought up a book.

It was nothing remarkable to look at. A school exercise book, the kind sold for pennies, its cover soft with handling, its spine re-stitched twice with waxed thread. She held it against her chest the way a woman holds a child in a crowd—protected, and aware of the crowd.

"Names," she said. "Men who came through here, or died here, or were sent on and whose papers were never filed. Men the reports call deserters, or don't call anything at all. Their regiments. Their wounds. Their next of kin, when they remembered to give them. The date they left us, one way or the other." She looked at him over the top of the book. "Forty-one names. Forty-one mothers, if they're lucky enough to have mothers still living, who've been told nothing, or told lies."

"And you wrote them down," Elias said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "In a penny exercise book."

"In a penny exercise book," she agreed. "Because official paper gets burned, Mr. Wren. Official paper gets *filed*. Nobody burns a schoolbook full of a madwoman's handwriting."

Elias held out his hand.

She didn't move. "Why?"

"Because I keep records for a living," he said. "And in fourteen years I have learned exactly one thing about them: a name that exists nowhere doesn't exist at all. Not to the army. Not to the pension board. Not to God, if God keeps files." His hand stayed out, steady, though his heart was going hard against his ribs. "Your brother sergeant there has a mother in November who thinks she's grieving and a mother today who doesn't know she can stop. Somebody put that difference in writing. Let me help you keep it written."

The ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a giant door opening somewhere under the world. In the fog beyond the windows, the harbor bell tolled twice and went silent.

Tamsin looked at his hand. Then at his face. Whatever she found there, she must have found it sufficient, because she placed the ledger in his palm—and Elias felt its true weight arrive, the way you feel the weight of a sleeping child handed to you by a stranger who trusts you absolutely and has no right to.

He opened it. The pages were dense, ruled lines filled edge to edge with a small, fierce, upright hand. Names. Dates. Regimental numbers recovered from dog tags and memory and the labels sewn into shirts. Beside one entry, in different ink: *died 14 Jan., 3 a.m. Asked for his sister Ruth. Tell her he wasn't alone.*

Elias read that line twice. Then he closed the book, carefully, the way one closes a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

"Three battalions are represented here," he said. "Possibly four. This isn't a harbor problem. This is a system."

"I know what it is."

"No," he said, "I don't think you do. I think you know what it costs. Those aren't the same thing." He tucked the ledger under his arm and met her eyes, and saw the alarm flare there and understood it—he had just picked up her whole world and she had no idea whether he would set it down again. He set it on the table between them, flat, and put both his palms on either side of it. "Yours. I'm not taking it anywhere. But I want to copy names, cross-reference muster rolls, find out how many more there are. I want to know if my brother's name is in this book, Mrs.—"

"Sandoval. Miss." Something moved through her expression, quickly, like a fish under ice. "Thomas Wren? Second Battalion?"

The workshop went very quiet. Even the boil of the copper seemed to lower itself.

"He was at the aid station at Poperinghe," Tamsin said softly. "Autumn of '15. He came through with a chest wound. He was evacuated south on the barge train." She hesitated, and Elias watched her decide, watched her choose truth over mercy the way he had just asked her to. "That's all I know. He was alive when he left us. After that he's only a name on a manifest I never saw."

Alive when he left. Eighteen months of not knowing, compressed into four words, and Elias found that his legs wanted to inform him of something and he refused to let them. He gripped the edge of the table.

"Then we look at manifests," he said, when he could speak. "Barge trains. Evacuation routes. Everything that got written down and then mislaid." He looked up at her. "I can get into the harbor office after hours. I've done it before—for overtime, for corrections. No one watches a census clerk."

"Why would you risk that?"

Elias looked at the ledger. At Vey, asleep now, finally, his cheek against the table's wood, his single arm curled around himself like a question mark. At the copper boiler, breathing steam into the fog-gray light.

"Because somebody in an office wrote my brother off a list," he said, "and I have spent two years telling myself it was a clerical error. And now I'm standing in a room that suggests it was a Tuesday." He straightened. "Miss Sandoval. I'd like to see the rest of the book. And then I'd like to start finding out who these men are supposed to be."

Outside, the river ice spoke again—longer, louder, a cracking that rolled up the lane and set the windowpanes shivering in their frames. Tamsin glanced toward the sound, and Elias saw her face change: not fear, exactly. Reckoning. As if the river had said something she had been refusing to hear.

"When that goes," she said quietly, "the harbor wakes up. Patrols double. The navy moves everything that floats." She turned back to him, and her voice was steady and cold and clear as the water that would come. "We should assume whatever we're going to do, we have until the ice breaks."
