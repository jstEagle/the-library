## 6. Six Names from the Marne

He came back on a Tuesday, when the fog lay so thick against the windows that the harbor might have been invented rather than real.

I had known he would return—I had counted the days the way one counts stitches in a seam, without wanting to but unable to stop—and still, when his knock came at the workshop door, my hands went cold around the kettle. Elias Wren did not knock like a census man. Census men knocked like weather, indifferent and expected. He knocked twice, paused, then once more, as though asking permission of the door itself.

I set the kettle down and looked at the room behind me. Six men slept in it, though officially there was only one bed and no occupants at all: Private Aldous with his bandaged hip, the two brothers from the transport who hadn't spoken since Arras, young Pell shivering through a fever that had nothing to do with his wound, and the two I thought of as the quiet ones because their files—if they had ever had files—had been burned before they were born into paper. The stove ticked. The sailcloth hanging from the rafters moved in the draft like slow ghosts. Somewhere under the floorboards beneath the third worktable, wrapped in oilcloth, lay forty-one names in my own careful hand.

Forty-one. And now perhaps a forty-second reason to be afraid, standing on the other side of the door with his hat in his hands.

I opened it before he could knock again, so that he would not wake the sleeping men.

"Mr. Wren."

"Mrs.—Miss—Sandoval." He had corrected himself the first time too, and gone red about it, and was going red about it now. Fog had beaded on his coat and in his eyebrows; he looked like a man who had walked a long way through something and wasn't sure what he'd walked through. "I've come about the count. There were—irregularities. In my submission. I'm obliged to correct them before the district office closes the rolls."

"Then you'd better come in and find them," I said, and stepped aside, and hated myself for the invitation even as I made it, because the alternative was letting him stand in the fog where any passing patrol might see him and wonder what a census taker wanted with a sailmaker's workshop at half past seven in the morning.

* * *

I did not take him past the beds. That was the first thing. I steered him along the wall by the rope locker, where the shadow is deepest, and poured him tea at the small table by the window where I do the mending accounts. He sat with his cup held in both hands and did not drink it, and I understood that he had not come for arithmetic.

"You wrote six coats," he said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"On my form. One resident. But there were six coats on the pegs, and—" He stopped himself. Looked at the tea. "I've been doing this work three years, Miss Sandoval. Harbor districts, boarding houses, the sailors' homes. You learn to read a room by its coats. Six coats means six men or five men and a very generous landlady." His voice stayed level, apologetic almost, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "There are no six men registered within a quarter mile of this building."

The stove ticked. Behind the sailcloth, someone turned in his sleep and groaned—a small sound, quickly stilled, but Wren's eyes went to the hanging canvas the way a compass needle goes north.

"The coats belong to the owner's family," I said. "The Harrow brothers keep their sailing things here."

"There is no Harrow family in the harbor registry."

"Then the registry is incomplete."

"Yes," he said. "I've begun to think it is. Incompletely. Extensively." He set the cup down and turned it once, a full rotation, watching it as if the answer might be printed on its bottom. When he looked up, his eyes were not the eyes of a man investigating anything. They were the eyes of a man drowning politely. "My brother's name was Thomas Wren. Second Battalion. He went missing in September of 1914, near the Marne, and when I wrote to ask after him I received a form letter stating that no soldier by that name appeared on the casualty lists. Not *wounded*. Not *missing*. No soldier by that name. As though the army had misplaced an entire human being and then denied ever owning him."

I did not move. I am good at not moving; it is the nurse's oldest skill, the stillness that says *nothing here is wrong, go back to sleep.*

"My mother died not knowing," he said. "She died writing letters to a son who had never, officially, existed. And last month I stood in your doorway and counted six coats, and I thought—what if the omission isn't an error? What if it's a policy?" His jaw worked. "I didn't come to correct the count. I lied on the doorstep. I'm sorry. I came because I can't sleep, and because you boil bandages at four in the morning, and because the smoke from your chimney smells like carbolic, and because I have nowhere else in this world to put the question."

The fog pressed white against the window glass. Under the floorboards, forty-one names lay in the dark like seeds.

If I sent him away, I thought, he would go to someone else. Men who cannot sleep do not stay quiet; they write letters, they make inquiries, they become Sergeant Quills. If I sent him away, the question would leave my hands and become everyone else's problem and eventually our ruin. If I let him stay—

If I let him stay, then for the first time in two years I would not be alone with the ledger, and God help me, I wanted that the way a drowning woman wants a plank: unreasonably, gratefully, without examining whose hand was extended.

"Come with me," I said.

* * *

I led him to the back of the workshop, past the sail loft, to the third worktable, and told him to kneel. He knelt without question, which told me something about him that I filed away. I pried up the board with my thumbnail—the nail head I use as a handle—and drew out the oilcloth packet and set it on the table between us like a thing just dug from a grave.

"Three pages," I said. "Not more. You understand that. You see three pages, and then we discuss what you've seen, and whether you see any more depends entirely on the discussion."

"That's fair," he said hoarsely.

"It isn't fair. It's all I have."

I unwrapped the oilcloth. The ledger inside is an ordinary school exercise book, the kind boys use for sums, bought in lots of twelve from the stationer on Cordage Lane so that no single purchase would be remembered. Its cover is soft with handling. I opened it to the pages I had already chosen—not the first pages, which are too raw, too full of the year I learned how bad it could get—and laid them flat.

"Names," I said. "Rank, unit if I know it, wound if I saw it, date they came to me, date they left and how. These are men the army recorded as dead, or deserted, or never enlisted. Every one of them breathed. Every one of them has a mother or a brother or a wife who got a form letter." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I use for triage. "Six names from the Marne are on these pages. You said the Marne. So look."

He looked.

I watched him read the way I have watched men read telegrams—first the refusal to understand, then the understanding arriving anyway, unstoppable, like water finding a seam. His finger came up and traveled down the second column and stopped.

"Corporal Edric Vane," he read aloud. "Second Battalion. Wound: shell fragment, left shoulder, partial deafness. Admitted October 1914. Left June 1915, transferred inland, recovered." His voice cracked on *recovered*, of all words. He looked up at me. "Second Battalion. That's—that's Thomas's battalion. That's his *company*, Vane would have been—did he know him? Did he say anything—"

"Sit down, Mr. Wren."

He sat. I poured him tea I knew he wouldn't drink and pulled the stool beside him because the man's hands were shaking and I am a nurse before I am anything, it happens before thinking.

"Vane came to me in October of '14," I said, "with his shoulder packed with field dressing and his ears ringing so badly he couldn't hear his own name. I asked every man who comes through me the same questions: full name, next of kin, anyone he served beside who might be looking for him. He gave me a list of eleven names from his company. Eleven." I reached across and turned the page toward the margin, where my smaller handwriting runs perpendicular to the entries, crowded in like weeds between rows of wheat. "I wrote them down. I always write them down. Because if a name appears on two different men's lists, that tells me the name is real, whatever the army says."

Wren's finger found it before I pointed. Third from the top, in ink gone brown: *T. Wren, Pte., reported KIA 8 Sept., no confirmation, Vane says he saw him alive near the river, morning of the retreat.*

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere outside, far down the harbor, ice shifted in the channel with a sound like a long sigh.

"He saw him," Elias said. His voice had gone strange, thin, the voice of a man speaking from the bottom of a well. "Vane saw him alive. After the date of death. After—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Do you understand what this is? This is my brother, alive on the eighth of September, written in a stranger's notebook. Do you understand that I have spent seven years being told I imagined having a brother?"

"Yes," I said. "That's why the ledger exists."

* * *

We sat a long while after that, and neither of us spoke, and it was not an uncomfortable silence, which surprised me. Grief shared out loud has a way of settling, like sediment in river water. He drank his tea finally, cold. Above us the sailcloth stirred. One of the brothers coughed in his sleep and was silent again.

Then Elias straightened, and wiped his face with the flat of his hand the way men do, briskly, as though the face had misbehaved, and became businesslike, and I recognized the transformation because I perform it myself twenty times a day.

"I'll help you," he said. "Whatever this is—keeping it, adding to it, hiding it—I'm in. You should know I'm not brave. I'm not useful in a fight. I know forms, though. I know registries, filing systems, which offices answer and which offices swallow. Three years of counting this city's beds and boots. I know where records live and how they die."

"They don't die," I said. "They're killed. There's a difference, and the difference has a signature on it."

"Then we find the signature." He turned back to the ledger and his hand hovered over the open pages without touching them, the way one hovers over a relic. Then he closed it gently and pushed it back toward me across the table, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—steadier now, harder, the grief compressed into fuel. "But hear me, Miss Sandoval. Hear this before you trust me with anything more. This book cannot stay under a floorboard. It cannot stay in this building, or in your keeping alone, or in any single place where a match or a magistrate could end it. It must go somewhere it can't be burned."

"Nothing can't be burned."

"A copy can't be burned all at once. An original in a strongroom, a copy in other hands, the knowledge of it spread so that killing the book kills nothing but paper." He leaned forward. "You've built something true in a city full of lies, and true things that exist in only one place are called *secrets*, and secrets are exactly the kind of thing this war eats for breakfast. You need an ending for it. A destination. A place where these names become *record* instead of *risk*."

I looked at him and felt something move in me that I had not permitted myself to feel in two years, and I resented it immediately, the way one resents a draft from a window one had sealed carefully shut.

Hope. He was offering me hope, and hope is an intruder. Hope walks into the house you have kept dark and lights a lamp in every room and says *now you have something to lose*, and it is right, and you hate it, and you cannot send it away.

"I haven't planned an ending," I said. My voice came out smaller than I meant it to. "Endings are for stories that are allowed to finish. I planned a system. Systems don't end. They continue until they're discovered, and then they burn."

"That was a good plan for surviving," he said. "It's a terrible plan for meaning anything. Forty-one names in oilcloth under a floorboard survive you by exactly as long as you do, and no longer. Is that what they're worth? One woman's lifetime of holding her breath?"

"They're worth their lives," I said, sharper than I intended, gesturing at the sailcloth, at the sleeping men behind it. "Every name in that book is a man breathing above a floorboard right now because somebody decided he didn't exist loudly enough. Don't tell me about meaning, Mr. Wren. Meaning doesn't change dressings."

"No," he said, and took the rebuke without flinching, which I was beginning to understand was his way. "But meaning gets dressings changed after you're arrested. Or shot. Or simply tired. You cannot be the only vessel, Miss Sandoval. Vessels break. Books copied are books that float."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor in ragged sheets, and the light coming through the window had turned the color of pewter, and far out in the channel the ice groaned—a deep, structural complaint, the river testing its own seams, the way it does for weeks before breakup. Everyone in the port pretends not to hear it. Everyone in the port hears it.

"An inquiry," I said slowly, because the thought was arriving even as I spoke it, dangerous and whole. "A board. A naval inquiry, a court, a commission—anything official, anything with minutes taken and witnesses sworn and a record that becomes *theirs*, not mine, so that destroying my copy destroys nothing. That's what you mean. A place where the names enter the world's accounting and can never be subtracted again."

"That's what I mean."

"It would require someone to receive it. Someone senior enough to matter and honest enough not to bury it." I laughed, briefly, without much humor in it. "Do you know many of those, Mr. Wren? In your three years of counting beds?"

"I know clerks," he said. "Clerks run everything. The admirals only sign." He was almost smiling now, and the smile transformed him—he looked suddenly much younger and much more determined, a man who had located his grief and converted it to bearings. "Give me a month. Give me the three pages' worth of trust and nothing more yet. Let me find the door these names can walk through. And meanwhile—" he nodded at the floorboard, still gaping dark beside the table "—put that back where it lives, and I never saw it, and if anyone asks, I came to correct six coats down to one."

"And if they don't stop asking?"

"Then we'll have larger problems than coats," he said, "and better reasons to hurry."

I wrapped the ledger while he watched—not suspiciously, but the way one watches a reliquary being closed—and slid it back into the earth-smelling dark beneath the boards, and pressed the plank home with my thumb. When I straightened, he was standing, hat in hand, and for a moment we faced each other across the worktable with the whole hidden weight of the morning between us, and I understood that something had happened in this room that I had not authorized and could not undo: I had acquired a witness. Worse—a confederate. Worst of all—an ally who believed in endings.

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn all the way around.

"Vane's list," he said. "The eleven names. May I—someday—see the rest of that page? Not today."

"Someday," I said. "When the book has somewhere to go."

He nodded once and went out into the thinning fog, and I stood listening to his footsteps diminish along the quay until the harbor sounds swallowed them.

Behind me, from beyond the sailcloth, Private Aldous's voice came, low and dry, from a man I had believed asleep:

"That the census fellow again?"

"That was nobody," I said.

"Mm," said Aldous. "Nobody's been here twice this week."

And out in the channel, the ice groaned again—longer this time, deeper, a sound with intent in it—as though the river had heard everything and was only waiting for spring to say its part.
