## 7. The Naval Inquiry

The Records Office on Custom House Row smelled of cold iron and old paper, and Elias Wren had come to think of it as a kind of church—except that in this church the dead were filed alphabetically and no one prayed.

He arrived before eight each morning now, though his rounds did not begin until nine. He had learned that the first hour belonged to whoever wanted it: the clerks were still warming their inkwells, the supervisor was still at his breakfast, and the long drawers of the harbor registry stood open like drawers in a surgeon's cabinet, waiting to be examined. For three nights running he had stayed past closing with a lamp and a legitimate excuse—the census corrections he was obliged to file—and for three nights he had pulled records he had no official reason to touch.

Tonight there would be no such luxury. Tonight there was a meeting.

He had heard it first as rumor, two days earlier, from the clerk named Hobbes—a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs who handled correspondence between the civil authorities and the naval station upriver. Hobbes had mentioned it the way men mention weather, half to himself, sorting letters into trays: *Board of Inquiry convenes at breakup. They always wait for breakup. Can't get witnesses or admirals across the ice before then.*

Elias had kept his head down over his forms. But the words had lodged in him like a splinter, working deeper with every hour. *At breakup.* The river ice, which had lain solid since December, thick enough that carters crossed it with loads of coal, groaning some nights in the dark like something enormous turning over in its sleep—that ice would not hold forever. March was coming. And when it went, everything changed.

This morning he came early not for the registry but for Hobbes himself, and found him already at his desk, blowing on his tea because the radiator in the corner gave more noise than heat.

"Mr. Wren." Hobbes glanced up, unsurprised. The census man had been haunting the office long enough that his presence no longer registered as unusual. That was the beauty of paperwork, Elias had learned: a man who handled paper became invisible among it. "You're keen this morning."

"I wanted to ask you something," Elias said, keeping his voice level, "about the naval board. The one convening at breakup."

Hobbes's eyebrows rose a fraction. He set down his tea. In the three weeks Elias had known him, he had never once seen the man surprised by anything except the price of tobacco; this small movement therefore carried the weight of an alarm bell.

"What about it?"

"When exactly. What it covers. Who sits on it."

Hobbes looked at him for a long moment, and Elias watched him decide—not whether to answer, but how much. Clerks lived their whole lives in that country between what they knew and what they were permitted to say, and they navigated it with a skill no officer ever acquired.

"It's a standing inquiry," Hobbes said at last, lowering his voice although the office was empty except for the two of them and a boy polishing the brass rail downstairs. "Casualty accounting. They sit whenever there's a discrepancy too large to bury quietly. Missing vessels, missing manifests, missing—" he paused, choosing, "—*men*. Names that appear in one ledger and vanish from another. It doesn't happen often. When it does, they want it handled before the shipping season starts and civilians start asking questions on the piers."

"And it convenes when the ice breaks."

"Always. The senior officers winter inland. The moment the channel's open, the steamers come up from the capital, and the board sits in the customs hall for however long it takes. A week. Two." Hobbes picked up his tea again, and Elias understood the audience was ending. "Why does a census man care?"

Elias had prepared an answer on the walk over—something bland about cross-referencing residence records against casualty notifications for families who claimed men still listed as serving. He delivered it now, flat and bureaucratic, and watched Hobbes receive it with the expression of a man accepting counterfeit coin because refusing it would be more trouble than it was worth.

"Mm," said Hobbes. "Well. If you want the dates, they'll be posted when the admiralty wires them. Usually ten days' notice, sometimes less if the thaw is early." He turned back to his letters, then paused, quill suspended. "Mr. Wren. A piece of advice, freely given, worth what you paid for it. Discrepancies that reach a board of inquiry have usually already been through three pairs of hands whose job was to make them disappear. Whatever you've found in my registry—" he did not look up, "—ask yourself who else has found it, and what they did about it, before you go writing it down anywhere official."

The radiator clanked. Somewhere below, the boy finished with the brass rail and let himself out. Elias stood a moment longer, then thanked him and left.

Outside, the morning was white and windless, the kind of cold that made the masts along the waterfront stand perfectly still, like a forest of dead trees. Down on the river the ice stretched bank to bank, gray-white, scarred with cart tracks, and out in the middle a seal hole smoked faintly where the current ran beneath. It looked eternal. It was not. Every year without fail it went—first a lead of black water off the point, then the cracking, the booming, the whole surface shuddering like a struck drum—and every year the port transformed overnight from a place of silence to a place of horns and hawsers and men shouting numbers.

A deadline. He had one now, whether Tamsin knew it yet or not.

* * *

She met him at the workshop at midday, in the interval when the sailmaker next door took his dinner and the street emptied of even the gulls. Elias came the back way, through the net-yard, and found her boiling bandages in the copper again—the same as the first time he'd truly seen her, though now the sight landed differently. Now he knew what lay under the floorboards. Now he knew six of the names by heart.

"You look like a man carrying news," she said, not turning from the copper. Steam had beaded on her forehead. Her hands moved with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand things while thinking of something else entirely.

"There's a naval board of inquiry," Elias said. "It convenes when the ice breaks. Casualty accounting—missing names, vanished records. Exactly what your ledger is."

That got her to turn. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, and he saw her mind working behind her eyes the way he'd seen it work before: not quickly, but thoroughly, like a lockmaker feeling the shape of a new key.

"A board that looks for omitted names."

"A board that exists so the navy can say it looked for them." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to soften it. "Hobbes says they sit a week, maybe two, in the customs hall. Then the season opens, the ships come, and everyone goes back to pretending. After that—" he gestured vaguely at the walls, the loft above where two of the hidden men slept through the daylight hours, "—after that, this place has no cover at all. Right now the harbor is half-empty, half-frozen, nobody counting. When the steamers come, the counting begins. Quill will have warrants inside a month."

Tamsin sat down on the workbench beside the copper, which was not a thing she did often; Elias had learned to read her postures the way sailors read weather, and sitting meant the ground had shifted under her.

"How long," she said, "before the ice breaks?"

"Early thaws have come in the second week of March. Late ones, April. The old-timers say the river's been groaning heavy this year—deep frost, fast current underneath. Could go any time after the first hard rain." He paused. "We should assume the earliest date. We should assume we have four weeks."

"Four weeks." She said it the way a doctor says a prognosis. Not despairing. Measuring.

"The board is our chance, Tamsin. It's the only door that opens on its own. If we bring the ledger to them—file it formally, make it part of the record before they convene—then it can't be burned quietly. Burning evidence that's already entered into an inquiry is a different crime than burning a nurse's private notebook. Quill can raid a workshop. He can't un-ring a bell rung in front of three admirals and a court reporter."

"And if they're not the men we hope they are?" She looked at him steadily. "If the board convenes precisely to bury the discrepancies—Hobbes said it himself. Three pairs of hands whose job was to make them disappear."

"Then we'll know that too," Elias said. "And we'll know it in a room with witnesses, instead of here, alone, when the soldiers come." He heard how it sounded and made himself finish. "I'm not promising the board delivers justice. I'm promising that it *receives*. Once those names are read into a naval record—in front of the admiralty, in front of the press if we can manage it—they exist outside your floorboards and outside my desk. They become facts. Facts are harder to kill than people."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. The copper muttered. Above them a floorboard creaked—Corliss, moving to the window on his crutch, checking the street out of a habit the war had installed in him permanently—and both of them glanced upward and waited until the sound settled.

"Four weeks," she said again. "To copy the ledger cleanly. To find the clerk who'll actually enter it instead of losing it. To keep Quill from tracing the deserter he's hunting back to this door." She shook her head, but it wasn't refusal; Elias had learned that too—it was her way of weighing. "You understand what you're proposing. The moment we move toward that board, we stop being careful people. Everything we've done to stay invisible—your false corrections, my quiet admissions, the fishing boats in the fog—all of it becomes a trail leading straight here. There's no version of this where we hand them the ledger and remain untouched."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you have a position, Mr. Wren. A pensionable, respectable, dull position, and a brother whose grave—" she stopped herself, and he was grateful and stung in equal measure, "—whose memory you've spent three years keeping decently. You could stop today. File your census, close your drawers, and no one would ever know you looked."

"My brother's name is in your ledger's neighborhood, Tamsin. Sixth Battalion, listed as transferred, then listed nowhere." His voice stayed low and even, mostly. "I spent three years writing letters to ministries and receiving replies written by machines wearing men's signatures. You are the first person I've met who writes the names down because no one else will. If you think I can walk away from that and go back to counting chimneys—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I lost him once to a list. I won't lose him twice to my own caution."

She held his gaze, and something passed between them that neither of them named—something older than alliance, almost like recognition across a border. Then she nodded, once, businesslike, and stood, and the moment folded itself away into the work the way all their moments did.

"All right," she said. "Four weeks. Then we do it properly. First: a clean copy, in a hand that isn't mine, on good paper, witnessed. Second: a clerk. Not Hobbes—he's too watched—but someone in the customs house with access to the board's intake and a conscience or a grudge. Do you know anyone?"

"I'm going to find out," Elias said. "There's a junior registrar named Pell, ex-navy, invalided ashore with a ruined ear. He hates the admiralty with the pure hatred of a man they used up and discarded. If anyone will carry paper into that hall and make sure it's read aloud rather than shelved, it's him."

"Pell." She committed it to memory the way she committed everything—completely, instantly, as though her mind were another ledger. "And third: Merrick."

Elias frowned. "Merrick is a risk."

"Merrick is a *witness*," Tamsin said. "A captain who signed the transfer orders for at least four of these men, convalescing ten feet above the proof. If the board hears only me—a woman, a nurse, no rank—they'll smile and thank me and lose the ledger between sessions. If they hear an officer confirm the orders existed, the arithmetic changes." She met his objection before he raised it. "Yes, he might turn us in. Yes, he might collapse into army loyalty and denounce the whole thing as forgery. But Elias—we are past the point where the safest road and the right road are the same road. Four weeks means we spend our risks now, deliberately, instead of hoarding them until someone spends them for us."

Above them, Corliss coughed—the particular cough that meant *someone's coming*, drilled into all of them by now—and Tamsin moved toward the stairs with the bandages, and just like that the war council dissolved back into the daily labor of keeping men alive invisibly.

But at the foot of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at him.

"Elias. Thank you for finding the clock. I'd begun to think we had forever, and nobody has forever." Her mouth curved, tired and real. "Not even the ice."

Then she climbed, and Elias stood alone in the steam-smelling workshop with the plan taking shape around him like scaffolding: copy, clerk, witness, board. Copy, clerk, witness, board. Four weeks.

* * *

That night he did not go to the registry. He went to find Pell.

The junior registrar lodges over a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, in a room Elias reached by an outside stair that complained at every step. Pell answered the knock already dressed despite the hour—a thin man with a boxer's flattened ear and the restless eyes of someone who no longer trusted rooms he couldn't see all of. He recognized Elias from the customs house, offered tea he clearly couldn't afford, and listened.

Elias told him very little. That was the discipline he'd learned from watching Tamsin: give a man the shape of the thing before its weight. So he spoke of discrepancies—casualty lists that shed names between the field and the ministry, transfer orders that ended in nothing, a harbor full of men the paperwork had swallowed—and he watched Pell's face change from courtesy, to attention, to something harder and hungrier.

"I processed forty-one casualty notifications in November," Pell said finally, staring at his teacup. "Forty-one. I counted them twice because the number felt wrong against the tonnage reports—ships coming home light, crews short, and the ministry lists showing barely a dozen dead or discharged. I wrote a memorandum. It was acknowledged." He laughed, short and bitter. "*Acknowledged.* You know what acknowledgment means in that building? It means your paper has been placed in a folder with other papers, and the folder has been placed in a room, and the room will burn down mysteriously sometime before the war ends."

"The board of inquiry sits at breakup," Elias said. "Customs hall. Public record, court reporter, admirals on the bench. I need paper entered into that proceeding—formally, on the record, read aloud. Not filed. *Entered.* Can you do it?"

Pell was silent for a long moment. Outside, far off, the river spoke—one long, deep groan rolling up from the ice like the sound of a ship changing its mind—and both men turned their heads toward the window as if it had called their names.

"That's early," Pell said quietly. "Groaning like that in February. The current must be high under there." He looked back at Elias, and whatever he saw in Elias's face decided him, or perhaps it was the river, or perhaps it was simply that a man can only acknowledge so many folders before he needs to put one *down* somewhere that counts. "Yes. I can do it. Intake for the board comes through the deputy registrar's office, and the deputy drinks his lunch every day from noon till three. Paper handed directly to me during those hours goes into the board's book with my initials and no one else's. It's not clever, what I'd be doing. It's just—refusing to be a hallway." He set down the cup. "What is this paper, Wren? I'll do it either way. But I'd like to know what I'm hanging myself with."

"A list of names," Elias said. "Men the war wrote off as deserters, dead, or never born—who are none of those things. Kept by a nurse who has spent two years recording what the ministries erased. Forty-three names so far. There may be more before we're done."

Pell closed his eyes briefly, and Elias understood that somewhere in that ruined-ear man's past were names of his own—men from a deck or a gun crew who had gone from a muster roll to a memory without passing through any page in between.

"Forty-three," Pell repeated. He opened his eyes, and they were dry and steady. "Bring them to me before the ice moves. After that, the hall fills with admirals' aides and provost marshals, and nothing gets in that isn't stamped by someone whose stamp we can't trust."

Walking home along the frozen quay an hour later, collar up, breath smoking, Elias heard the river again—louder now, or perhaps he was only listening better. A deep percussive boom rolled out of the darkness beyond the breakwater, followed by a crack like a rifle shot, then a long descending groan, the sound of a thousand tons of winter admitting it would not last.

The old fishermen said you could hear the breakup coming for weeks before it came, if you knew how to listen—the ice talking in its sleep, rehearsing.

Elias stood at the rail and listened until his feet went numb, and what he heard was a clock. Four weeks. Perhaps less. Copy the ledger. Wake Merrick. Carry the names to Pell before the channel opened and the world came flooding back in—inspectors, warrants, steamers, Quill.

He turned up his collar and walked on, and behind him the river boomed again, patient and enormous, keeping time.
