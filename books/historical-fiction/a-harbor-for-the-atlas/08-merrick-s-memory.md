## 8. Merrick's Memory

He slept badly. Tamsin could tell by the sound of him—the creak of the cot frame under a man turning and turning again like meat on a spit, the ragged catch of breath that wasn't quite a groan because officers did not groan, even in their sleep. Especially in their sleep.

She had moved Captain Merrick to the corner behind the canvas screen three nights ago, when the fever finally broke and it became clear he would live. That had been, she understood now, a mistake of arithmetic. A man who lived was a man who remembered.

The workshop at four in the morning was her favorite hour and her most dangerous one. The stove had burned down to a red heart in its black belly; frost ferned the inside of the north windows where the river's breath crept through every seam; and along the far wall, six men slept stacked like cordwood in bunks Aldous the sailmaker had built from packing crates. Six men who were not on any muster roll in the Empire. Six names in her ledger, sewn into oilcloth, under the third floorboard by the tar kettle.

And now a seventh presence: a captain of the line, convalescing ten feet from them, separated by a sheet of duck canvas and whatever lies she could keep stitched together.

She fed the stove and sat down with the ledger open on her knees—not to write, only to look. It was a habit she'd developed without noticing, the way a woman who keeps coins in a tin will rattle the tin just to hear them. *Private Alfred Dunn, 2nd Battalion, listed dead at Ypres, alive as of November.* *Corporal Ivo Marsh, listed deserted, carried off the field with a head wound he could not report.* The ink was brown where she'd mixed too much iron gall. Her own handwriting looked younger on the early pages. Frightened people write small, she'd learned. She used to write very small indeed.

Behind the screen, Merrick said her name.

Not loudly. Not the way fevered men called for mothers or for God. He said it the way a man says a word he has been testing in his mouth all night, weighing it, finding it heavier than expected.

"Miss Sandoval."

She closed the ledger. "You should be sleeping, Captain."

"So should half my battalion," he said, and swung his legs off the cot. She heard him sit up—the effort cost him something; there was a long exhale—and then the canvas shifted and he was looking at her across the dim room, barefoot, shirtless, the dressing on his shoulder catching what little light the stove gave. His wound was a shell fragment high in the muscle, ugly but clean now, and he wore the scarred flesh without embarrassment. Men who had been opened up once tended to lose their modesty along with everything else.

"You're writing at four in the morning," he said. "Or you're not writing. You were looking."

"Inventory," she said. "Bandages, laudanum, carbolic. We run short if I don't count in the dark."

"It's a small book for an inventory."

The room seemed to hold its breath around her. One of the sleepers turned over; a bunk frame complained. Out on the river, the ice made its low grinding note, the sound she had come to think of as the harbor clearing its throat.

"Captain," she said, "you've been given morphine for eleven days. You'll find your memory is full of things that didn't happen and empty of things that did. That's normal. Sleep."

"That's just it." He stood—unsteadily, one hand flat against the wall, but he stood. "I remember Ypres. I remember the road out, the mud to the axles, the guns. And then I remember—" His jaw worked. "I remember a room. A green-shaded lamp. Papers. My signature, over and over. Twenty-one times. I counted the signatures in my head while you were changing the dressing yesterday, Miss Sandoval, and I could see my own hand making them, and I cannot for the life of me remember what the papers said."

Tamsin put the ledger beneath the folds of her apron before she was aware she'd decided to. Her heart was going hard enough that she feared he could hear it across the room.

"Field returns," she offered. "Requisitions. Officers sign their lives away in triplicate."

"Yes." He came closer, favoring the left leg where the shrapnel had bruised the bone. "Except that I signed those twenty-one papers after we'd already left the field. And except that Corporal—I can almost see his face—Corporal somebody, from the platoon I lost on the ridge, is standing at the edge of the lamplight in this memory, and he is *looking at me*, and no requisition ever made a man look like that."

*Marsh,* she thought. *Ivo Marsh. He was on the ridge.*

"I think," Merrick said quietly, "that you know."

* *

She made tea because her hands needed employment and because a cup between two people is a kind of truce. They sat on either side of the stove, and she gave him the weak stuff and kept the strong pot near herself, an old nurse's economy.

"Tell me what you do remember," she said. "Not the room. Before the room."

He stared into the cup. Steam climbed past his face.

"The retreat from the ridge. Third day of the bombardment. We'd been ordered to hold a position that could not be held—that much I knew then, that much every man knew—and we held it anyway because we were told relief was coming. Relief did not come. By nightfall I had perhaps sixty men left of two hundred, and a runner brought me a written order to withdraw the *wounded only* and leave the rest to cover the withdrawal." He said it evenly, like a man reading a train timetable, which was how Tamsin knew how much it was costing him. "I refused. Verbally. I told the staff major that I would not split my company into the saved and the sacrificed, that we would come out together or not at all. I was informed that my objection was noted."

"And then?"

"And then I woke up in that room with the green lamp, three days later, at a casualty clearing station I don't remember traveling to, with my shoulder full of German steel and my signature drying on twenty-one pieces of paper." He looked up at her, and his eyes in the stove light were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a hole in the ice, looking down. "Twenty-one names, Miss Sandoval. I have been trying to make the arithmetic work all night. If I signed for twenty-one men, they were men being *entered* somewhere—or men being taken *out*. And I am afraid, in a way I have not been afraid since the ridge, that I know which."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, in the loft, Aldous shifted in his sleep, and the old beams spoke their creaking language.

"Why come to me?" Tamsin asked. "Why not your own doctors? Why not write to the battalion?"

"Because I wrote to the battalion." He set the cup down with great care, as though it might go off. "Three days ago, while you were at the market. I asked the adjutant for the current strength return of C Company. The reply came this morning." He reached into the trousers folded on his cot and produced a single sheet, creased into eighths. He did not hand it to her. "Do you know what a form B.103 is?"

"Casualty notification," she said. Her voice came out flat.

"A man's active service record. Birth, enlistment, wounds, discharge—or death. This is mine." He smoothed it on his knee. "It's correct down to the shell fragment. But here—" his finger stopped at the bottom of the page, "—here is a marginal entry, initialled by someone at the base, dated the day after my signing. *Records amended per directive. Certain entries consolidated.* Consolidated." He laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "I spent six years learning to read orders, Miss Sandoval. I know a euphemism the way you know a fever chart. Someone took twenty-one men out of existence and filed it under housekeeping."

Tamsin's hands were steady around her cup. She had learned steadiness the way other women learned piano—hours of practice, mostly unwilling. But underneath the porcelain calm she was doing rapid, cold arithmetic of her own: *How much does he suspect? Has he seen the ledger? Did he hear me reading names aloud the night I copied pages? Is he a danger walking, or a drowning man reaching for the same rope I'm holding?*

"Captain," she said, "what do you think happened to those twenty-one men?"

"I think some of them died on the ridge and were never reported, because reporting them meant reporting the order that abandoned them." His voice dropped. "I think some of them survived and were written off as missing or worse—deserted—to save the staff the trouble of explaining why a holding action became a rout. And I think—" he hesitated, and the hesitation was the worst thing he'd said yet, "—I think I may have signed the paper that made it legal. Or made it *paper*, which in the army is the same thing."

*He doesn't know about the ledger.* The certainty settled in her like a stone dropping through dark water. He suspected the shape of the crime because he could feel the hole where his memory had been scooped out—but he didn't know she kept a record. He didn't know that twenty-one was a rounding error beside the forty-six names under her floorboards.

She could tell him. She could stand up, fetch the oilcloth packet, and lay it on his blanket, and let a captain of the line see exactly what his signature had sown.

Or she could lie to him again, smoothly, kindly, the way she'd lied to him about the sounds from the loft, about the extra bowls, about the second cot that appeared and vanished. She was good at lying to officers. It was the one skill the war had perfected in her, and she hated it the way a hunchback hates his shadow.

"Merrick," she said—his surname alone, rank stripped off, which made him flinch slightly, which she intended—"when you remember those papers, does remembering feel like waking up? Or like being buried alive?"

He considered her for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face and made him look older than his thirty-odd years, made the scar tissue on his shoulder shine dully.

"Both," he said finally. "That's the honest answer. Both at once."

* *

The others woke at seven, and the workshop performed its daily transformation from morgue-quiet to something almost like a household. Private Dunn, whose hands still shook, hauled water because hauling water was the one chore that calmed him. Corporal Voss argued amiably with young Pell about the correct way to patch a sail, neither of them knowing anything about sails and both enjoying the argument. The fog outside thickened and lifted and thickened again, and the harbor bells rang their blind counting.

Merrick watched all of it from behind his screen, and Tamsin watched him watching.

That was the thing about a convalescent officer: he had nothing to do but observe, and observation was his trade. She saw him cataloguing. Six coats on six pegs where the census schedule said one lodger. Boots drying by the stove in sizes that told stories. The way Dunn flinched when boots rang on the cobbles outside. The way everyone went quiet—not frightened quiet, *practiced* quiet—whenever the harbor police boat whistled at the pier.

At noon she changed his dressing, and he held still beautifully, the way good soldiers do, and said, without preamble:

"There are more than twenty-one, aren't there?"

Her hands did not stop moving. Tweezers, carbolic, the slow peel of linen from healed granulation. "Hold still."

"I've heard you at night. Reading. You read like a priest at a bedside—names, dates, units. I counted at least a dozen before I stopped counting, because stopping felt like the decent thing to do." He looked at the ceiling beam. "I'm not asking you to show me. I understand why you wouldn't. God knows I haven't earned—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm telling you that I remember the ridge now. All of it. Including the part where Major Hessler told me the wounded would be 'administratively reconciled' and that my objections were 'noted for the record.' And I'm telling you that when I'm well enough to walk to the base without leaning on your wall, I intend to ask Hessler what reconciliation means. So if there are men in this building whose continued breathing depends on nobody asking questions—"

"There are," Tamsin said.

"—then I should know whether my questions will get them killed."

She tied off the bandage, sat back, and looked at him properly for the first time since the fever broke. This was the moment she'd been circling for three days, the fork in the road she couldn't walk around any longer. Everything in her training said: *protect the patients, control information, trust no uniform.* Every instinct honed through two years of erased men said this man was a loaded gun left in the kitchen—harmless until it wasn't, and never harmless for long.

But she had also watched him, these three days. Watched him give Dunn his rum ration untouched. Watched him learn the men's first names and use them. Watched him lie awake not in fear of his wound but in grief for men whose names he was only beginning to recover.

Dread and tenderness, tangled together like wire. There was no separating them. There never was.

"If you ask your questions," she said slowly, "men will come here. Men with notebooks and warrants and no interest in reconciliation. That is certain."

"I assumed so."

"And the men in this room cannot run. Two of them can barely walk. One of them is nineteen and cries in his sleep and would confess to burning London if anyone shouted at him."

"I assumed that too." He met her eyes. "So I won't ask. Not yet. Not until you tell me it won't drown them—or until you tell me there's a better place to ask than a staff office." He paused, and something almost wry crossed his ruined face. "You run this harbor like a divisional headquarters, Miss Sandoval. I've been in enough of both to recognize the difference between a woman hiding something and a woman defending a position. You're defending a position. I'd like to know what it's worth to you."

*Everything,* she thought. *It's worth everything, which is why you frighten me, and why I haven't thrown you out, and why God help me I'm going to answer you.*

"What it's worth," she said, "is that every man under this roof exists somewhere besides a staff officer's convenience. That's all. That's the whole position. Hold it or abandon it, Captain, but decide soon—because the river ice is starting to talk, and when the river opens, this district gets busy, and busy harbors have ears."

Something moved behind his eyes at that—recognition, maybe, or resolve assembling itself. He lay back against his pillow, exhausted by the morning's excavation, and just before sleep took him he said, slurred and quiet:

"Twenty-one signatures. When I get them all back—every name—I'll sign them again. Properly. On the right side of the paper this time."

Tamsin stood a moment in the stove light, listening to his breathing lengthen toward sleep, feeling the terrible vertigo of having decided something she couldn't take back. He was not a gun in the kitchen. He was something more dangerous and possibly more valuable: a man with a conscience and rank, waking up.

She knelt, eased up the third floorboard by the tar kettle, and drew the oilcloth packet into her lap. Not to add a name. To count.

Forty-six. And upstairs, snoring gently, a forty-seventh waiting to be asked for.

She was re-seating the board when the knock came at the street door—heavy, official, twice—and Aldous, coming down the ladder, mouthed the words she'd been dreading for a week:

*Harbor police.*
