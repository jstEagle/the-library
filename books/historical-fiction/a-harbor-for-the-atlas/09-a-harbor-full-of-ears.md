## 9. A Harbor Full of Ears

The fog came in off the river before dawn, the way it always did in late February, rolling up from the water like something alive and hungry, swallowing the cranes, the coal heaps, the masts of the fishing fleet until the harbor was nothing but sound—halyards clinking somewhere unseen, the groan of ice shifting in the channel, a bell buoy tolling for no one.

Elias Wren had been at his desk in the census office since six, correcting entries by lamplight, when the knock came. Not the usual knock of a clerk arriving with a stack of forms, but a hard, deliberate rap-rap-rap that seemed to want an answer rather than a greeting.

He opened the door to a man he hadn't seen before, though he'd have known him anywhere as what he was. Sergeant Quill of the military police was not tall, but he occupied space the way a nail occupies wood—driven in, immovable, with a head that caught the light. His greatcoat was army-issue but better brushed than any soldier's on the waterfront, his boots polished to a dull shine that fog had already begun to film over. He carried himself like a man who had never once been lost, even in a city he'd never seen.

"Mr. Wren." He said the name as if confirming it against a list inside his own skull. "Census taker. District Fourteen, the harbor wards."

"That's me."

"Quill." He didn't offer a hand. "I'm looking for a man. Deserted from the Third Transport Company eleven days ago. Private Aldous Fenwick. Five foot ten, brown hair, a scar through the left eyebrow. Last seen attempting to book passage north."

Elias felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath him, the way it did when a ship's wake reached even this far inland. *Fenwick.* He knew the name. He knew it because three nights ago Tamsin Sandoval had read it aloud to him from a page of her ledger, her finger steady under the ink while his heart was anything but: *Fenwick, Aldous. Left eye ruined at Ypres. Discharged without papers. Sent home to no one.*

He was lying in the loft above the sailmaker's workshop right now, probably asleep, his bandaged face turned toward the wall.

"Deserters come through here," Elias said, and heard his own voice do something remarkable: remain level. "Half the country's army passes through this port one way or another. What does that have to do with the census?"

"Nothing. Everything." Quill stepped past him into the office without being asked, and Elias understood that the question of whether he would be invited had never been open. The sergeant surveyed the room—the shelves of ledgers, the map of the harbor district pinned above the desk with its colored pins marking every registered dwelling—and stopped before the map the way a man stops before a mirror.

"You've been making corrections," Quill said.

"I make corrections constantly. People move. People lie. People die and their landlords keep collecting rent on them for months." He kept his tone bored, clerical, a man discussing weather. "It's the whole job."

"Mm." Quill leaned closer to the map. His gloved finger rose and touched a pin near the waterline, south of the fish market, where the old sailmaker's workshop sat between a chandlery and a net-drying shed. "This one. You corrected it twice."

"The count was wrong twice."

"A single dwelling. One bed registered. And yet your second correction notes—" he tilted his head, reading from memory, which meant he had memorized it, which meant he had been sitting with Elias's forms longer than any auditor should—" '*occupants appear to exceed stated accommodation; recommend re-inspection.*' Your words, Mr. Wren."

"My words," Elias agreed. "And my recommendation stands. Re-inspection. I've simply been busy."

"Busy." Quill turned from the map, and his eyes were pale and patient and entirely without warmth, the eyes of a man who found other people's lies interesting mainly as craftsmanship. "Where are you busy just now?"

"Everywhere. It's a harbor district. Two thousand souls and half of them registered to addresses that are boats."

A smile crossed Quill's face without disturbing anything above his cheekbones. "Then you won't mind walking with me. South along the waterfront. I'm told the fog makes strangers bold—deserters travel in it, deal in it. A man who knows every dwelling in the ward could save me a day's work."

There it was. The trap dressed as a courtesy. If Elias refused, he marked himself. If he came along, he would be walking the sergeant down a street that ended at her door.

*You could say no,* he told himself. *You could let him search alone. Whatever he finds, you never pointed him there.*

But he thought of Tamsin's face in the lamplight, three nights ago, when she'd shown him those three pages. Not afraid—that was the thing he couldn't shake. She had been *resolute*, the way she was resolute about everything, boiling bandages, dosing morphine, writing names in a ledger hidden under floorboards as if ink were a sacrament. She had chosen this burden knowing its weight. And he had stood in her workshop last week among men who officially did not exist and promised—hadn't he?—that the ledger would go somewhere it couldn't be burned.

If Quill walked into that workshop alone, there would be no ledger to deliver anywhere. There would be a cell, a tribunal, and names erased a second time—this time permanently, with the state's own stamp.

"I'll walk with you," Elias said, reaching for his coat. "Though I warn you, in this fog everything looks like everything else."

"That suits me fine," Quill said. "I'm not looking with my eyes."

* * *

They went south along the quay, and Elias talked.

He talked the way he imagined a river talks—continuously, unremarkably, carrying things along whether they wanted to go or not. He gave Quill the district in layers: which boarding houses took soldiers' money and asked no questions, which publican watered his gin and hated the army enough to hide a fugitive out of spite, which warehouse foreman had been falsifying manifests since before the war and would swear falsely on any subject whatsoever for a fee. All of it true. That was the craft of it—he fed the sergeant a meal of truths, each one seasoned toward a different table, so that by the time they passed the chandlery with its barrels of tar stacked outside, Quill's attention was fixed two streets east of where it needed to be.

"Fenwick wouldn't come here," Elias said, nodding back over his shoulder at the stretch of waterfront they were leaving behind. "Too exposed. The workshops all share walls, and the sailmakers and riggers are navy-adjacent—they'd report a stranger for the reward alone. If I were a one-eyed man running north, I'd try the tenements past the gasworks. Foreign quarter. No one speaks to the authorities in the foreign quarter, including each other."

Quill stopped walking.

For a long moment the only sound was the bell buoy, far out, and beneath it a deeper sound Elias had been hearing all week and trying not to name: the ice on the river, groaning. Not cracking—not yet—but groaning the way a ship groans in a swell, a vast structural complaint, the sound of something that has held all winter beginning to reconsider.

"You're helpful," Quill said at last. "Suspiciously helpful."

"I'm a census taker. Being helpful is the entire profession."

"No." Quill resumed walking, east now, toward the gasworks, and Elias allowed himself one breath. "Helpful is a profession. You're something else. You correct your own forms after filing them. You know which publicans hate the army. You look at a map of your district the way a quartermaster looks at stores." He glanced sideways. "I've arrested men like you. They always talk too well."

Elias laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, because it was either that or confess. "Sergeant, if I were hiding deserters, would I walk you past their door?"

"You might. If the door weren't theirs anymore." Quill's voice dropped, conversational, almost gentle. "Here's what I know, Mr. Wren. Fenwick boarded a fishing boat out of this harbor four days ago. Bought his way north with a wound badge he stole off a dead officer. The boat was seen putting back in yesterday morning, empty of passengers, and the fisherman who runs her has suddenly developed a stammer whenever anyone mentions the army." He paused at a corner where the fog stood in the street like a wall. "So Fenwick may be gone. But whatever that fisherman was carrying besides Fenwick is still here. Boats don't run one passenger at a time. Not in wartime. Not at night."

Elias kept his face still through sheer force of will, and inside his skull a single sentence rang like a struck bell: *She moved them. She's moving them now.*

Two patients. The fishing boat. Through the fog. This morning—of all mornings—while he walked the sergeant along the quay like a decoy duck who believed himself the hunter.

"And where," Quill said softly, "does this fisherman berth?"

* * *

Later, Elias could never reconstruct exactly how he did it. He only knew that he stood at that foggy corner and heard himself say, "Berth? Half these boats don't berth, they beach. But if you mean the fishing fleet, it's strung along the north quay past the customs shed—and you'll want the harbormaster's register anyway, because half of them are licensed to wives and dead men." And then he walked Quill north, away from the south quay, talking about licenses and widows and the customs clerk who took bribes, and every step was a step bought with a coin he could feel leaving him forever.

Because the truth was that the fisherman's boat—the *Kittiwake*, Tom Cade's boat, the one that had carried Fenwick north and slipped back through the fog at dawn—was moored at the south quay. Fifty yards from the sailmaker's workshop. Elias had seen her there Sunday, her nets drying, her hull patched with fresh pitch.

He was sending Quill north on a lie built out of true parts, and the lie would hold for an hour, perhaps two, and then some harbormaster's clerk would mention the *Kittiwake* and the whole architecture would come down with Elias standing inside it.

He did it anyway.

They walked the north quay in the fog, and Quill questioned a boy mending lobster pots who knew nothing, and a customs officer who knew less, and Elias narrated the whole time, pointing out boats, naming owners, inventing nothing because invention was unnecessary—the truth of the fishing fleet was confusing enough to swallow a sergeant whole. And all the while, somewhere south of them, beyond the wall of fog, he pictured it: Tamsin in the gray half-light, moving two men who could barely stand down a ladder of tarred rope to a boat that smelled of fish and blood, Tom Cade swearing softly at the ice floes coming downriver, the engine turning over muffled, wrapped in sacking, and then the fog taking them—all of it happening blind, in silence, while he kept the wolf on the wrong side of the wood.

"Mr. Wren." Quill had stopped at the end of the north quay. The fog thinned here, and the ice field was visible far out—a white plain broken by leads of black water, groaning, shifting, the whole river in slow motion toward spring. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to consider that I already know most answers and am only interested in the shape of the last one."

Elias looked at the ice. It was easier than looking at Quill.

"When the inquiry convenes—when the river opens and the board sits—there will be men who testify and men who are testified about. Which are you?"

The question hung in the fog between them. It was, Elias realized, the first honest thing the sergeant had said all morning, and it deserved—demanded—an honest answer, and honesty at this moment would kill everything.

"I'm a census taker," Elias said. "I count people. That's all I've ever done. But I'll tell you what I've learned doing it." He turned and met the pale eyes at last. "In this district there are forty-one men registered as dead whose landlords swear they paid rent in January. There are nine women listed as widows whose husbands send letters postmarked from towns that don't exist. The war has made a fiction of every record in this harbor, Sergeant. When the board convenes, someone should testify to that. Maybe that's all I am. Someone who counts."

Quill studied him for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, muffled by fog and distance, a boat engine coughed, caught, and faded southward—and Elias's heart went with it, riding that sound across the black water like a man watching his own life depart on a tide he could no longer call back.

"Counting," Quill said finally. "Yes. I imagine that's how it starts." He buttoned his coat. "Thank you for the tour, Mr. Wren. I'll find my own way back. I expect we'll speak again—when the river opens."

He walked away into the fog, and Elias stood at the rail of the north quay and let his hands shake now that there was no one to see. The ice groaned again, louder, a long tearing complaint from the middle of the river, and he thought: *it's early this year. The ice is breaking early. Everything is moving faster than we planned.*

* * *

He found her at the workshop in the early afternoon, when the fog had burned down to a gray gauze and the south quay lay open to the sky like something flayed. She was in the doorway, arms crossed, watching the water, and she didn't turn when the door creaked—she knew his tread by now, the way you know the tread of anyone you've waited for.

"Well?" she said.

"Gone. Both of them. Cade took them out through the eastern channel before the fog lifted, past the ice field, and put them ashore at the pilot station. From there it's the coastal train and then nothing anyone can prove." He came to stand beside her. Below, the *Kittiwake* rode at her mooring, innocent as Sunday, her decks scrubbed of everything but salt. "Quill's spending today in the foreign quarter, chasing a fisherman who doesn't exist through streets where nobody talks. I bought us a day. Maybe two."

Tamsin was quiet. Then: "He came to the office first. To you. Why?"

"Because he's good." Elias said it plainly; she deserved plainness. "He'd already read my corrections. He'd already talked to Cade. He knows there's a boat and knows it ran more than one passenger. He doesn't know about the workshop yet—or he doesn't know he knows. There's a difference, and we're living inside it."

She nodded slowly, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd watched her do a hundred times—lives against risks, ink against blood—and arrive, as always, at the same answer.

"Then we move faster," she said. "Merrick can travel within the week, maybe sooner. The rest—" She stopped. Looked at the water. "How many days until the river opens, do you think?"

Elias looked out at the ice field, white to the horizon, groaning its long complaint. "Fewer than we thought. It's breaking early. Listen."

They listened. Beneath the lap of water against pilings, beneath the cry of a gull working the fish market refuse, there it was: the deep, grinding voice of the river, the sound of a season ending ahead of schedule, of a deadline shortening while they stood there.

"Tamsin." He made himself say it. "When Quill left, he asked me whether I'd be a man who testifies or a man who's testified about. He knows. Not everything—but the shape of it. And when the ice goes out, he'll be standing at that board with a warrant and a theory, and everything I did this morning will be evidence." He exhaled. "There's no walking back from today. I sent an investigator chasing fog on purpose. I lied to the army for you. For the names."

She turned and looked at him then, fully, and in her face was neither gratitude nor fear but something steadier—the look of a woman confirming a calculation she'd already made.

"I know," she said. "I watched you do it before you ever told me. You walked him north with your hands in your pockets and your voice like a ledger line." Almost, nearly, a smile. "You're a terrible liar, Elias Wren. Your ears go red."

"They're red now?"

"Scarlet."

He laughed, and the laugh cracked something open in his chest, relief and terror in equal measure, and for a moment they stood together in the doorway of the workshop like any two people in any harbor town watching the ice, except that between them lay a floorboard, and beneath the floorboard a book of names, and beneath the names a war.

Inside, Merrick's voice called weakly from the loft—asking for water, asking the hour—and Tamsin moved to answer it, because that was what she did, that was all she had ever done. But at the foot of the ladder she paused and looked back at him.

"Elias. The next time Quill comes—and he will come—we won't have a boat ready. We won't have a fog. So whatever we're going to do with that ledger, we decide tonight." Her jaw set, the way it did over a wound too deep to close gently. "Because the river isn't waiting for us. And neither is he."

Above the harbor, far out on the white plain of the ice, something shifted, settled, and groaned again—longer this time, and lower, like a door opening at the bottom of the world.
