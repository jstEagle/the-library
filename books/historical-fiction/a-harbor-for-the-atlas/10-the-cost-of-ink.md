## 10. The Cost of Ink

The workshop after midnight was a different country. By day it smelled of canvas and pitch and carbolic; by night, when the stove burned low and the harbor fog pressed its gray face to every pane of glass, it smelled only of time—old rope, older wood, the salt of a hundred years of sails hanging in the rafters like memory itself. Tamsin had come to know that smell the way sailors knew weather. It meant she was alone with her work, and her work was never what anyone thought it was.

She cleared the sailmaker's bench near the back wall, the one with the cracked leather apron still draped over its edge as if old Halloran might return from the dead and take up his awl. From beneath the third floorboard—the loose one, the one she could pry with a thumbnail if she knew where to press—she lifted the ledger.

It had grown fat since winter began. What had been a slim notebook, bought for sixpence at the chandler's on Pier Road, was now a swollen thing bound in twine, pages interleaved with whatever she could salvage: the backs of dispensary forms, butcher's paper, the flyleaf of a hymnal no one had claimed. Forty-one names now. She counted them the way other women counted spoons or buttons—compulsively, in idle moments, in the dark before sleep. Forty-one men whom the army had crossed out, misfiled, declared dead who were breathing, or declared never-born who had bled on her table.

Tonight there would be forty-two copies of each. Or rather: one copy of all forty-two, made in her own hand, in ink she would have to mix herself because the chandler's shop had stopped stocking any color but black and she refused to write these names in mourning colors alone.

She set two sheets of paper on the bench—one original, one copy—and began.

The trouble started at the third name.

*Private Alfred Dunmore, Royal Fusiliers, age twenty-three. Left behind at the dressing station at—*

She had always copied in silence. Silence was the professional habit; silence kept the mind on the hand, the hand on the letters, and let the rest of her stand apart like a nurse watching an operation from the gallery. But tonight the workshop was so still, and the fog so thick against the windows, and somewhere out on the river the ice was talking to itself in long groaning syllables, and she heard her own voice say the name before she knew she meant to.

"Alfred Dunmore."

It came out barely above a whisper. She kept writing.

"Alfred Dunmore. Royal Fusiliers. Twenty-three years old. He lied about his eyesight to get in, and he told me so on the table at Passchendaele, laughing through the morphine, saying he'd read the eye chart by memorizing what the man ahead of him said."

Her pen had stopped. Ink gathered at the nib in a small black bead, trembling.

"He couldn't see past the third line without them. He carried a rifle for eleven months." She wrote the rest of the entry. "He is alive. He is upstairs. He snores."

She blotted it, moved to the next line, and found she could not do it silently anymore.

* * *

Elias Wren came down the stairs at half past one because the sound of her voice had woken him—not the words, which were indistinct, but the cadence, rising and falling like a liturgy, like a woman at a wake telling the life of the deceased one last time so the neighbors will have it right.

He stood on the bottom step in his coat, not wanting to break whatever spell held the room. The single lamp threw her shadow enormous against the sail-loft wall, arms moving, head bent, and around her on the bench the ledger lay open like something dissected for study. She looked, he thought, like a scribe in a painting his mother had owned—a saint recording martyrs, though the saint in the painting had looked serene and Tamsin Sandoval looked like a woman being flayed slowly and refusing to cry out.

"Corporal Thomas Ives," she was saying. "East Surreys. Nineteen. Reported dead at Monchy, April the ninth. He was not dead. He walked eleven miles on a foot that later came off, and when they processed his kit they marked him killed, and his mother received a letter, and she believed it, because mothers believe." A pause. The scratch of the pen. "His mother lives in Rye. Someone should tell her. Someone will tell her, if this reaches where it's going."

"Tamsin."

She didn't startle. That was the war in her; sudden voices slid off her like rain off oilcloth. She turned her head just enough to see him and went back to the page.

"You should be sleeping."

"So should you."

"I'm not the one who has to be able to lift men tomorrow."

"No." She dipped the pen. "You're the one who has to remember what I'm about to forget, if forgetting ever comes kindly. Sit down, Elias. If you're going to be awake, then witness this. That's the job tonight. Witnessing."

He pulled Halloran's stool to the far side of the bench and sat, and did not speak again for a long time.

* * *

She read them all. Not quickly—there was nothing quick in it—but steadily, in the flat clear voice she used for reporting casualties, which was the steadiest voice she owned, and under it, audible only to someone sitting as close as Elias sat, ran another voice entirely, the one that broke on certain names.

"Lance-Sergeant Peter Okafor—" and here the pen hesitated, because Peter Okafor had died on her table in November, quietly, holding her wrist with a strength that frightened her, and the army had listed him as a deserter three weeks before he bled out, and she had written him into the ledger anyway, in the space reserved for the truth. "Lance-Sergeant Peter Okafor. Died the fourteenth of November. He was not a deserter. He was a man who went back for his corporal, and the going-back took too long, and the paperwork punished him for it." Her voice did not crack. It simply went very quiet, the way a room goes quiet. "I could not save him. Write that down somewhere too. She could not save him."

Elias watched her hands. They were steady. He understood suddenly that the steadiness was the most expensive thing in the room—that every unshaking letter cost her something she would not be able to itemize until the war was over, if it ever ended, if there were ever an accounting grand enough to hold what she'd spent.

"Private William Ashcombe," she said. "Grenadier Guards. Missing since the Somme, July 1916. Found in a farmhouse cellar by a Belgian farmer, both legs broken, half-starved, and unable to convince anyone of his own name because he'd lost his speech in the shelling. They wrote him missing presumed dead. His wife remarried on the strength of it." Pen scratch. Pause. "His speech came back in February. First thing he said was her name. Second thing he said was that he hoped the fellow was decent to her, because she deserved decent." She looked up for the first time in an hour. "Do you understand what I'm copying, Elias? Do you understand what's actually in this book? It isn't names. It's everything the names were carrying."

"Yes," he said. And then, because it was true and because the hour made truth cheap: "My brother's name carried a mother, a dog named Bantam, and a terrible singing voice. That's what was in it. Nobody official ever asked what was in it. They just closed the box."

She put down the pen.

For a while neither of them spoke, and the stove ticked, and outside the ice groaned again—nearer now, or maybe only louder in the silence, the great white plain of the river shifting on its bed like a sleeper turning toward morning.

"There are nine in here I couldn't save," she said finally. "Nine out of forty-one. Do you know how that arithmetic works on me at night? One man in five. I lie awake doing it over and over, trying to make the fraction smaller, trying to find the move I didn't make, the tourniquet tied ten seconds late. Nine men. And the whole time the army was writing off hundreds, thousands, entire battalions of them with a rubber stamp and a clerk's headache, and I sit here grieving my nine like they were the world's whole loss."

"They were your whole loss," Elias said. "That's allowed."

"It's not efficient."

"Nothing about grief is efficient." He reached across the bench—not for her hand, not quite; he laid his fingers on the edge of the page she'd just finished, as if steadying the paper, as if the paper were the thing that needed steadying. "That's rather the point of it. If it were efficient it would be paperwork, and we have quite enough of that already."

Something in her chest, wound tight since Passchendaele, wound tighter since Quill's boots first rang on the cobbles, loosened by one degree. One degree. She picked up the pen again.

* * *

They worked until the fog thinned toward gray. She read; he transcribed the second copy in his census-taker's hand, which was beautiful in the way official hands were beautiful, each letter identical to its sisters, and she teased him once—only once—that his handwriting looked like it had been court-martialed into obedience, and he laughed, and the laugh startled them both, because neither of them had laughed over this bench before and neither quite knew what to do with it afterward.

At the thirty-first name, hers failed her.

"Private James—James—" She stopped. Put down the pen. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "James Ferris. I've written him four times and I can't—I can't remember his face tonight. I know his wound. I know his sister's address in Cardiff. I've got the whole man filed except his face, and it's gone, Elias, it's gone and I need it, because if I lose the faces then I'm just doing what they do, aren't I? Turning them into entries. Turning them into ink."

Elias came around the bench. He did not touch her. He stood close enough that she could feel the warmth of his coat and said, quietly:

"Tell me his wound instead. Start there."

"Left thigh, fragment, above the knee."

"Then he was tall," Elias said. "Or kneeling. Fragment trajectories run upward on standing men. Tell me his sister."

"Margaret. She sent socks. Terrible socks, all lumps."

"Then she loved him badly and thoroughly, which is the usual way of sisters." He was quiet a moment. "And he was in your care for how long?"

"Six days."

"Six days of a bad-joke sister and lumpy socks. You don't need the face, Tamsin. You have the shape of him. That's what the ledger keeps—the shape. Faces go. Shapes stay. That's why we're copying it twice."

She looked up at him then, really looked, lamp-yellow and exhausted and something else, something that had been arriving for weeks the way spring arrived under the ice—imperceptibly, then all at once. Neither of them named it. Naming it would have been like opening a door in a hull, and there was no time for water in the boat, and both of them knew exactly how much water a hull could take.

Instead she said, "Hold the lamp closer. We've ten more names."

And he did. He stood beside her the rest of the night, holding the light steady while she wrote, and when her hand cramped near dawn he took the pen and finished the last three entries himself, reading each aloud first so she could correct him, and his voice saying the names in this room, by this light, made them somehow doubly real—as if between the two of them they constituted enough witness to hold forty-two lives against the weight of everything official in England.

When it was done, they had two ledgers where there had been one. She wrapped the original in oilcloth and returned it beneath the floorboard. The copy she held in both hands for a moment, feeling its small warm weight, its absurd fragility—paper and ink against the machinery of an empire.

"Now what?" she asked. "Where does it go?"

"That," Elias said slowly, "depends on a question I haven't dared ask yet." He straightened, and she saw the exhaustion in his face rearrange itself around something new. "Tamsin—who runs inquiries in this port? When a ship sinks, when cargo vanishes, when the harbor itself lies? Who has the authority to make the navy answer for its own paper?"

She frowned. "There's a board. The naval inquiry—it convenes at the Custom House whenever there's a shipping loss to account for. Why—"

"Because my corrections file went to the registry office yesterday," Elias said, "and the clerk who stamped it mentioned—offhandedly, the way clerks mention things that will ruin us—that a board is being seated for the *Meridian* losses. Convening when the ice breaks." He met her eyes. "Tamsin. The river is our clock now. Everything depends on when the ice goes out."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, through the fog, through the gray beginning of morning, the river answered—long, low, and unmistakably nearer than it had been the night before.
