## 11. Quill Closes In

The Bureau of Statistics kept its files in the basement of the Customs House, three floors below the harbor's business and a full flight below the water table, so that in wet springs the drawers of the lower cabinets had to be propped open with bricks to keep the paper from blooming gray mold. Elias Wren had worked among those drawers for six years and had come to think of the basement as the true brain of the city—everything above it, the offices and the counters and the aldermen's committee rooms, was only the face the brain wore when it went out in public.

He was pulling the correction slips from his February rounds when he heard the boots on the stairs.

That in itself was not remarkable. Clerks came down all day, and clerks wore ordinary shoes. These were army boots, hobnailed, and they came down without hurry, each step placed as though the man descending wanted the basement to know he was arriving well before he did. Elias kept his head over the drawer. His hands, he noticed with a strange clinical detachment, had gone cold at the fingertips, and he made himself keep sorting slips—*Wren, corrections, District Four, waterfront*—as if the small orderliness of the task could hold the world in place.

"You'd be Wren." The man stopped at the end of the row. He was shorter than his voice suggested, built like a capstan, with a sergeant's crown on his sleeve and a face that had been reddened by weather that wasn't this weather. "Quill. Military police."

Elias closed the drawer with both hands, carefully, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child. "Sergeant. This section is civilian records. If you're after enlistment papers, you'll want the third floor."

"I'm not after papers." Quill came down the row unhurried and set himself against the cabinet opposite, arms folded, so that Elias would have to look at him sideways or turn his back, and either choice said something. "I'm after the man who made them lie."

The lamp above them buzzed. Somewhere in the stacks a pipe dripped into a bucket with the patience of a metronome.

"I don't—" Elias began.

"District Four. Waterfront census, February round." Quill produced from his tunic a folded sheet—not a copy; Elias recognized the cheap foolscap of the Bureau stock, recognized even the smudge of his own thumb in the corner where the ink hadn't dried. "Original household returns say the sailmaker's workshop on Corder's Slip houses one person. Your corrected sheet says four. Then there's an addendum in your hand—I've had it read twice—that amends the count again to *one resident and three persons in temporary care*, which is a very careful way of writing it, Mr. Wren. Very careful. Almost as though you knew someone might come asking what the difference was."

*Three.* Tamsin had moved two out through the fog before Quill ever reached the slip; Elias had thought that number buried. But the paper didn't know what had happened after it was written. Paper never did. That was the whole reason he had fallen in love with it as a boy, and the whole reason it was killing him now.

"A correction is not a lie," Elias said. "It's the opposite of one. I counted wrong the first time. I went back."

"Six times," Quill said. He unfolded the sheet and smoothed it against the cabinet with one flat hand. "You visited that address six times between the ninth of February and the first of March. No other property in your district got more than two visits. The Bureau logs every call-out, Mr. Wren. Did you think we wouldn't?" He tilted his head. "Though of course you're not military. You don't think the way we think. You think in columns and sums. So let me put it in your language." He tapped the sheet. "One bed. Six coats on six hooks—and I've stood in that workshop, I'll tell you how it smelled: carbolic, boiled linen, and the particular sweetness of wounds going bad. There are men in there who do not exist on any muster roll I can find. And there is one woman who officially resides alone in a building that stinks of surgery."

The drip filled the bucket. Elias found he was gripping the edge of the open drawer hard enough to feel the brass rail bite his palm, and he made himself let go.

"What is it you want from me, Sergeant?"

"Ah." Quill smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was friendly. It was the smile of a man who had already won something and was enjoying the interval before saying so. "Now that's the right question. Not *what do you know*. You know what you know; denying it would insult us both and waste my afternoon. The question is what I want. That's better." He pushed off the cabinet and stood straight. "Walk with me. I hate negotiating in basements. Feels like being buried alive with witnesses."

* * *

They walked. Not along the quay—the quay had too many eyes—but up through the streets behind the Customs House, where the warehouses gave onto cobbled lanes and the fog sat thick between the gables like wool carded and left out. Quill talked about the weather first, then about the harbor, then about the war, in the easy way of a man establishing that he could be reasonable. He pointed out the naval hospital as they passed it. He mentioned, in passing, that the ice on the river had begun to talk in the nights, and that when the ice broke, the inquiry board would convene aboard the receiving ship, and that boards like that preferred clean narratives to complicated ones.

Then, under the arch of the old grain exchange, out of the wind, he stopped and laid it out plainly.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "I have a deserter's trail that ends at Corder's Slip. I have a census clerk who falsified a return—or corrected one, depending whose story survives. And I have a woman in that workshop who is harboring men the army has written off. Some of them are deserters. Some of them are worse than deserters—they're *inconvenient*, which around this harbor is the graver crime. The board will want her name in front of it when the river opens. Arrested, tried, made an example of. Harbor discipline. You understand—there are ships' companies in this port who'd scatter like sparrows if they thought a wounded man could vanish into a loft and be nobody."

"And if she resists arrest," Elias said quietly, "she can't. She has men in her care who can't walk."

"Yes," Quill said. "I know. That's rather the point." He looked out at the fog for a moment, and for just that moment something honest crossed his face—weariness, maybe, or the memory of some other fog, another port, another job. Then it was gone. "So here is what I'm offering, Mr. Wren, and I'll offer it once. Immunity. Yours. Full and formal—a signed statement that your corrections were made in good faith pursuant to Bureau procedure, that any discrepancy arose from clerical error at the property level, and that no evidence implicates you in concealment. Your record comes out of this whiter than it went in. In exchange, you testify. You give the board your account of that workshop: the numbers, the dates, the movements. Names if you have them. You give me the ledger she keeps—we both know she keeps one, a woman like that always keeps one—and you give me her."

The words went into Elias like splinters, one at a time, each finding a different place to lodge. He became aware that he was standing very still, the stillness of a man listening for a floorboard to hold.

"Why me," he said. "You could take the workshop tomorrow."

"With warrants? Yes. And find what? By the time my men cross the slip in daylight, whatever's in that loft is on a fishing boat or under a coffin lid. She's faster than I am, Mr. Wren, because she's been doing this longer than I've known about it, and I don't like operations I can't see the shape of. What I need is inside the shape." He paused. "Also—and I'll say this because it may weigh with you—there's a man on my list. Harbored. Convalescent. An officer. Captain Merrick. Whatever else that woman is guilty of, she is holding an officer of the Crown in an unregistered sickroom while he should be reporting his own situation through channels. Think about what that looks like on a tribunal. Think about whether she's protecting him or keeping him."

"She's keeping him alive," Elias said. It came out before judgment could catch it.

Quill studied him. "There it is," he said softly. "That's the voice I wanted to hear. Not the clerk's voice. The believer's. You've been in that workshop. You've seen her work. You admire her." He said it without mockery, which somehow made it unbearable. "Good. Admiration will make your testimony convincing. Nothing reads truer than a man describing something he wishes he didn't have to describe."

* * *

Elias walked home the long way, along the top of the embankment where the fog thinned and the river showed itself in gray slabs below. The ice had begun to change—he had heard it for a week now, the deep irregular groaning at night, like a large animal turning in its sleep—and by day the seams between the floes had widened into black channels that breathed cold. Spring was coming the way artillery comes: not suddenly, but inevitably, announced long in advance by everything shifting and cracking under strain.

His brother's name had shifted and cracked the same way, four years ago. Thomas Wren, private, second battalion, listed in the first casualty telegram as *wounded, missing*. Then, three weeks later, simply missing. Then not even missing—struck from the supplementary roll in a revision so quiet that their mother wrote to the War Office three times before someone admitted, in a fourth reply, on thin official paper, that the earlier lists had contained errors and the corrected roll superseded them. Corrected. Superseded. Words that meant *we have removed him*. His mother died still setting a place at the table. Elias had gone through his own grief and come out the far side into something harder, which was the suspicion that grief itself was a kind of clerical error—that somewhere in a basement like his there was a drawer with the truth in it, misfiled by men who found truth inconvenient.

And then a woman in a sailmaker's workshop had opened a drawer for him and shown him pages of names in her small fierce hand, and every one of them had been a man the rolls had swallowed, and he had understood that she was doing for strangers what no one had done for Thomas.

*I'll give you immunity,* Quill had said, *if you give me her.*

Immunity. He turned the word over the way he'd turn a suspect coin. It had a bright face and a dull one. On the bright face: safety, continued employment, the ability to go on pulling correction slips in the basement brain of the city until he was old and the mold got the files. On the dull face: the knowledge that he would have purchased it with the only currency he possessed, which was the truth, spent against the only person who had ever shown him what the truth was for.

Because that was the thing Quill hadn't understood, sitting in the fog-smug certainty of his offer. Quill thought loyalty was a fixed asset—you had yours to the Crown, and Elias must have his to the woman, and the whole game was flipping one to the other. But Elias's loyalty had never been to Tamsin Sandoval as a person, not only. It was to the ledger. To the idea that a name written down was a name that existed, and that existence could be defended with ink, and that a clerk—one clerk, in a basement, with a steady hand—could stand between the war and the men it wanted to erase. Tamsin had taught him that by example, one entry at a time. To testify against her would be to unlearn it. It would be to strike Thomas's name from the roll a second time, with his own hand, in his own fair clerk's script, and no revision would ever supersede that.

But oh, the other road. The other road led to the tribunal steps with her, to charges that would attach to him like burrs, to a cell perhaps, to his mother's table finally and permanently bare. The other road asked him to be brave in a direction he had never trained for. He had trained, all his life, for accuracy. Nobody had trained him for courage.

He stopped at the embankment rail. Below, the river ground its teeth. A barge lay moored in a black lead of open water, riding easy, waiting for the rest of the winter to let go.

He thought of her hands, red to the wrist from the boiling of bandages. He thought of the way she had read names aloud to him in the lamplight—not performing them, not mourning theatrically, just *recording* them, giving each one its full weight of syllables as though sound could be filed. He thought of Captain Merrick upstairs in the loft, dreaming his orders, and of the fishing boats going out into fog with silent cargo. He thought: *whatever version of myself survives this spring, it will be the one I choose in the next ten minutes.*

Because that was the real shape of Quill's offer, once you held it to the light. Not immunity versus testimony. Not safety versus ruin. It was simpler and more terrible than that. Sergeant Quill had offered him the chance to decide, right now, in the fog, with no witness, which Elias Wren would be permitted to go on existing—the one who kept his head down and his columns true and his grief folded away in a government drawer, or the one who had stood in a sailmaker's workshop and asked to see the ledger and known, in that instant, that he was crossing a line with no walking back.

Quill thought he had offered a bargain. He had actually offered a mirror.

Elias took out his notebook—the Bureau-issue notebook, ruled, dated, stamped—and turned to the page where he logged his calls. Six visits to Corder's Slip, each entered in his neat hand. Evidence, Quill would call it. Corroboration, the tribunal would call it. He looked at it a long moment.

Then he tore the page out, folded it small, and put it in his breast pocket—not to burn, not to surrender, but to carry, the way you carry a debt. And he turned up his collar toward Corder's Slip and began to walk, because there was something he had to tell her before the sergeant's men came at dawn, something about warrants requested and a board convening early and a traitor closer than either of them had guessed—

—because Quill, in his satisfaction, had said one sentence too many. He had said *a man on my list traded his information freely*, and had smiled at Elias's shock, and had never realized that in naming the informer so casually—*your fisherman friend, the one with the bad lung*—he had told Elias exactly which of Tamsin's hidden men had sold them all for a berth home.
