## 12. The Workshop Stands

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a blow.

Tamsin was awake before the second strike of the fist on the workshop's outer door. She had learned to sleep the way a cat sleeps—half-sunk, one ear above water—and she had been sleeping less than that for six days, ever since Elias told her about Quill's warrant requests and the clerk who'd promised to warn them. The clerk had not warned them. There had been no warning at all, only boots on the cobbles outside, many boots, and the flat metallic voice of a man reading something aloud through a speaking trumpet: *By order of the Military Provost, this district is subject to inspection. All persons will remain indoors. All persons will be counted.*

*Counted.* The word went through her like a needle finding the old hole in cloth.

She was out of her cot and moving before her feet decided to, pulling on her shoes without untying them, crossing the loft where six men slept in the smell of lamp oil and salt wool. The fog had come in off the river overnight and pressed against the high windows like a hand laid over a mouth. In that grey half-light the men looked younger than they were—Prewitt with his arm bound to his chest, the Hollis boy curled small as if he could fold himself out of existence, Okafor already awake and watching her from under the workbench with eyes that had seen this kind of morning before, somewhere else, in another uniform.

"Under," she breathed. "All of you. Now."

They knew the drill. They had rehearsed it twice, badly, laughing the first time because laughter was easier than fear, and the second time not laughing at all. The false wall behind the sail-loft swung on hinges greased with tallow; the crawl space beyond was three feet high and smelled of rat droppings and old hemp. Prewitt went first, then the boy, then the others, Okafor last, pausing at the threshold to look at her the way he always did—a long, level look, as if filing her away somewhere nothing could burn.

"Captain?" she whispered toward Merrick's corner.

He was already up. Of course he was already up; he had been an officer for twenty years and officers woke to trouble the way other men woke to birdsong. He stood in his civilian coat—the one they'd bought him off a ragman, too short in the sleeve—and buttoned it with steady hands.

"I know my part," he said quietly. "Go be a nurse, Miss Sandoval. That is all you are this morning. A nurse with too many patients and bad bookkeeping."

"And you?"

"I am exactly what the paperwork says I am." Something moved across his face—not quite a smile. "For once in my life, I intend to be."

* * *

The third blow took the door off its latch entirely.

They came in four at a time, rifles slung but hands near them, and behind the soldiers came Sergeant Quill in his grey greatcoat with the collar turned up against the fog, carrying a leather document case the way a priest carries a pyx. He was smaller than Tamsin remembered, or perhaps the workshop made everyone small—it had been built for canvas and rope, not for men, and its low rafters bent even the tall ones into apology.

Quill did not apologize. He stood in the middle of the floor and let his eyes move over everything with the patience of a man counting money that was not yet his.

The workshop had been prepared, as much as anything could be prepared. The beds were stripped to three. The bandage boiler stood cold, its copper still smelling faintly of carbolic beneath the honest stink of tarred twine. The ledger lay in its coffin across the harbor, under a dead gunner's name-plate, and the copy was sewn into the lining of Elias's census satchel, which was itself in a locker at the customs house. What remained here was nothing: lint, thread, a cracked teapot, six coats on their pegs that could belong to any poor household that dressed its men secondhand.

Quill saw the coats anyway. Tamsin watched him see them—one bed, six coats—and watched him file the arithmetic away behind those grey eyes, and understood that this man's entire talent was refusing to accept the answer that stood in front of him.

"Miss Tamsin Sandoval," he said, consulting a paper he plainly did not need to consult. "Registered nurse. Proprietress of record for this establishment since—" he paused, delicately, "—since such time as the records indicate, which is to say, since they do not. Curious thing, Miss Sandoval. This building appears in the parish rolls as a sailmaker's workshop. It appears in the port authority's rolls as vacant. It does not appear in the district census at all, except in your handwriting, which claims residence for one person." His gaze lifted to the coats. "One person wears six coats?"

"We take in washing," Tamsin said. "Sailors pay poorly and leave things behind. It's a known vice of the trade."

"It is indeed." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man who has heard every lie a waterfront can manufacture and has grown fond of them the way a taxidermist is fond of animals. "And the wounded officer convalescing in your sail-loft? Because I have here a report from the garrison hospital listing Captain A. Merrick as transferred to convalescent duty, location unspecified, and I have spent a very instructive night learning how much of this city is unspecified."

From the stairs at the back of the loft came the sound of measured footsteps, and Merrick descended into the lantern-light with one hand on the rail and the careful, deliberate limp of a man whose wounds the army had officially certified. He looked terrible in the way that only genuinely ill men can look well-used-by: grey at the mouth, the bandage visible at his collar, his bearing doing all the work his body refused.

"Sergeant," Merrick said, and his voice carried the flat authority that twenty years of giving orders pours into a man like lead into a mold. "You are making noise outside my sickroom at five in the morning. I assume there is a reason that will survive being reported."

Quill's posture changed. Not much. But Tamsin, watching, saw the soldier come up under the investigator's skin—the involuntary straightening, the reflex of rank meeting rank, even bogus rank, even a captain in a borrowed coat in a sailmaker's loft.

"Captain Merrick. You're a long way from a hospital ward."

"I am where my transfer orders put me, which is more than most of us can say." Merrick produced papers from inside his coat—papers Tamsin had never seen, typed on garrison letterhead, stamped, initialed, real-looking down to the coffee ring on the corner. "Convalescent detachment, harbor district, attached to the port medical auxiliary. Light duties. Records supervision." He held them out and let Quill read them under the lantern. "You'll find the countersignature. Colonel Hesse's own initials. I signed the roster myself on Tuesday. You are welcome to verify it, though I'd do it quietly, if I were you—the colonel dislikes having his signatures questioned by provost sergeants almost as much as he dislikes being woken before dawn."

It was a masterpiece. Tamsin kept her face still and her heart going like a hammer, and understood, with a vertigo that would arrive later when there was room for it, that Merrick had forged—or had arranged to have forged, or had simply *written*, in the fluent bureaucratic language that was his mother tongue—an entire administrative structure around them. The roster. The detachment. The auxiliary. The signature. The lie was not a lie at all; it was paperwork, and paperwork, in this army, in this war, in this fog-bound century, was more solid than flesh.

Quill read the pages twice. When he looked up, something had shifted in his face—not belief, but the professional's grudging recognition of professional work.

"Records supervision," he repeated.

"The port's casualty documentation is in disarray," Merrick said mildly. "Disgraceful disarray. Men listed twice, men not listed at all. I intend to spend my convalescence imposing some order on it. Which reminds me, Sergeant—" and here he stepped closer, close enough that Quill had to choose between stepping back and standing his ground, and chose, visibly, to stand it, "—if you are conducting an inspection of irregular residents, you may wish to begin with the fish-cannery sheds on the north quay. We've had reports. Desertion runs along the waterfront like rats along a wharf, and my information places several in the cannery district as recently as last week."

Tamsin did not breathe. The north quay was a fiction; she knew it was a fiction; but it was a fiction with sheds, and sheds with lofts, and lofts where frightened men sometimes slept, and Quill would have to send someone to look, because a sergeant who ignored a captain's intelligence and found nothing later would answer for it, while a sergeant who followed it and found vagrants would be commended.

Quill folded the transfer orders and handed them back with two fingers, a gesture that managed to be both correct and contemptuous.

"You'll forgive the disturbance, Captain."

"You'll forgive me if I don't," Merrick said. "I was shot in France. Disturbance disagrees with me."

* * *

They searched anyway. Of course they searched. Quill was too good to leave without searching, and for forty minutes the workshop rang with opened lockers, pulled-out drawers, the scrape of furniture dragged from walls. Two soldiers went up into the sail-loft and stood within four feet of the false wall, and Tamsin stood below them with her fists closed in her apron and counted her own heartbeats because it was that or scream. Behind the wall, she knew, the Hollis boy was breathing through his mouth the way she had taught him, slow and shallow, and Prewitt had his good hand over the boy's mouth besides, just in case, and Okafor sat perfectly still in the dark doing whatever Okafor did instead of being afraid.

A soldier pried at the boards near the loft window. One groaned.

"That board's been loose since the equinox gale," Tamsin said. Her voice came out bored, and she marveled at it from a distance, the way one marvels at a stranger's skill. "Whole building's rot. You're welcome to fall through the floor, but I'm not writing the report."

The soldier grinned sheepishly and left the board alone.

Quill, meanwhile, stood at the bandage boiler and lifted the lid and looked at the cold copper, and Tamsin watched him watch it, and knew he could smell what she could smell—that thin ghost of carbolic under the tar, the smell of boiled linen, the smell of men being kept alive off the books. He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed the lid.

"Your washing business," he said. "You boil the sailors' shirts in antiseptic?"

"Feet," Tamsin said. "You've never smelled a tramp steamer's crew."

"No," Quill said. "I've smelled worse." He set the lid down precisely, square with the stove edge, a small act of order imposed on a disorderly world, and Tamsin thought: *he knows.* Not proved. Not certain. But knowing the way a dog knows a buried bone under frozen ground—he would come back, and next time he would bring shovels, or fire, or law. "This district will be inspected again, Miss Sandoval. Routinely. The harbor is a security concern now that the ice means to break. You should keep better records."

"I keep excellent records," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point of a census, isn't it? Or does the army prefer we not count at all?"

Something flickered across Quill's face—annoyance, or possibly respect, or possibly the memory of his own name once lost in someone else's ledger. Then his heel clicked, and he was gone, and the boots went with him, out into the fog, northward, toward the cannery sheds and the fiction Merrick had built for them.

* * *

The sound of the patrol faded by degrees, boots dissolving into cobbles, cobbles into fog, until the only noise left was the river ice groaning far off—a long, deep complaint, like a door in a house the size of the world slowly being opened.

Then the false wall swung, and the men came out of the dark one by one, blinking in the lantern light, and nobody spoke for a moment because there are moments language cannot improve upon. The Hollis boy sat down abruptly on a coil of rope and laughed—one bark, high and cracked—and then put his face in his hands and shook. Prewitt put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

Okafor crossed the floor to Tamsin and stopped an arm's length away, and gave her the level look again, and said, in his careful, accented English:

"He knew the words to use. Your captain."

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"In my country," Okafor said, "the men who knew the words were also the men who wrote the lists." He did not say it with accusation. He said it the way a man reports weather. Then he went to help the boy up, and the moment closed over like water.

Merrick was standing by the window, looking out at nothing, one hand pressed flat against his bandaged side. He had gone greyer since the patrol left; the performance had drawn on reserves she suspected he could ill afford. She brought him brandy without asking, the good bottle she kept for stitching, and he took it and drank and did not thank her, because thanking would have made it a transaction between them and they were both pretending, for the sake of the fragile architecture of the morning, that it was not.

"How long have you been able to forge documents?" she asked.

"I didn't forge anything." He said it mildly, looking at the fog. "I wrote a duty roster. Whether it exists in any archive besides my own hand is a question of metaphysics, not forgery. Hesse's initials—I've watched the man initial things for eleven years. I could do it in my sleep. I may have done it in worse condition than sleep." He drank again. His hand, she noticed, was not quite steady. "You're angry with me."

"I'm—" She stopped. The anger was there, and it confused her, because it had no proper object. "You lied fluently. You lied the way I've been lying for two years, and it worked, and we're safe, and I'm sitting here owing my life to the same machine that wrote those men out of existence. That's what I'm angry at. Not you. The fact that the machine protects its own officers even when the officer is lying to it. It never once protected them." She gestured at the loft, at the hidden wall, at the whole trembling economy of hidden men. "A private goes missing and he's a deserter. A captain produces a piece of paper with the right initials on it and four soldiers walk out of my house without looking behind a single board."

"Yes," Merrick said.

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you have me say, Miss Sandoval? That it's shameful?" He turned from the window, and his face in the lantern light was older than his years, worn down to the grain. "I have known the system was rotten from the inside since before you wore a nurse's apron. Knowing it and being unable to be anything but a part of it—that is the particular hell they built for men like me, and I lived in it comfortably for twenty years, which is worse. This morning I used its own rotten timbers to shelter six men. I feel—" he considered, and the honesty of it startled her, "—cleaner than I have felt since 1914. And ashamed that it takes treachery to feel clean. Both things are true. They will go on being true. I suggest you make room for both, because I suspect you already have."

She wanted to argue. She found, examining herself the way she examined a wound—probing for depth, for what lay under—that she could not, because he was right, and because the shame he named was already living in her, had been living in her since the boots came up the street: the vertiginous, nauseating relief of being saved by the very thing her ledger existed to condemn. If the army were just, none of them would need hiding. If the army were just, there would be no ledger, and no ledger meant no names, and no names meant—

No. She would not think her way to the bottom of that well this morning. The men needed breakfast. The bandages needed boiling. The coffin across the harbor needed to stay where it was, undisturbed, until the inquiry.

"Eat something," she said to Merrick, which was not forgiveness but was adjacent to it, and he nodded, accepting it as such.

* * *

Elias came at noon, white-faced, having spent the morning being counted and recounted at the customs house while soldiers moved through the district like a comb through wet hair. He stood in the doorway taking inventory of her with his eyes—alive, unarrested, intact—and only then did he look past her at the workshop, at the mended latch, at the men sitting in the ordinary daylight drinking tea as if dawn had not nearly ended them.

"They hit the cannery sheds," he said quietly, when she'd told him. "Quill personally. Found two vagrants and a dog. No deserters. He knows he was played—he'll know it was Merrick the moment he cools down and reads that roster again with clear eyes. Hesse's initials won't survive scrutiny forever."

"Then we don't give him forever," Tamsin said.

Elias looked at her, and she saw the thing in his face that she supposed was mirrored in hers—the fear burned down to coals now, banked, usable. He set his satchel on the table, the satchel with the copy sewn into its lining, and sat down heavily beside Merrick, and reached for the teapot.

Outside, far down the river, the ice groaned again—longer this time, deeper, a sound like the earth clearing its throat before speech. Old Manet, the crippled sailmaker who owned the building and asked no questions, cocked his head at the window and spat expertly into the stove.

"Early," he said. "Ice don't usually talk till March. Talking in February means the river's in a hurry." He looked around the room, at the hidden men drinking tea in plain sight, at the nurse, at the census man, at the captain. "River's in a hurry, everybody in it better hurry too."

Tamsin looked at Merrick, who had gone very still, his teacup halfway to his mouth.

"Sergeant Quill will want that roster verified," Merrick said slowly. "And when it doesn't verify, he won't come back with four soldiers and a speaking trumpet. He'll come back with a court-martial detail and charges that fit the crime." He set the cup down untouched. "How many days does your clerk say we have?"

Elias didn't answer at once. Through the fogged window, the ice sang its low, grinding note, patient and immense, and Tamsin understood that the sound was not a warning anymore. It was a countdown.
