## 13. Terms of Surrender

Elias had rehearsed the confession eleven times on the walk from the census office, and by the time he reached the sailmaker's workshop he had abandoned all eleven versions in favor of the plainest one, which was the only kind Tamsin Sandoval would accept.

The fog had come in off the river that evening like something with intent, filling the streets to the second-story windows, swallowing the harbor bells. Inside the workshop the stove threw a low orange light across the sails hung from the rafters, and the smell was of tar, canvas, carbolic, and under it all the faint sweet rot of river ice beginning to think about spring. Two of the hidden men slept in the loft. A third sat propped against the far wall, whittling a peg leg for a toy soldier he'd been carving for a week, his left sleeve pinned at the shoulder. Captain Merrick was not there—he had returned to quarters three nights past, walking on legs that no longer needed an excuse—but his presence lingered anyway, in the folded uniform shirt hanging behind the stove like a shed skin.

Tamsin was at the long table, writing. She wrote every night now; he knew the shape of her evenings as well as he knew his own. When she heard the door she did not look up.

"You're late," she said. "The count's wrong again."

"The count is always wrong." He hung his coat on the peg. It was the sixth coat on six pegs, and he thought, not for the first time, of the day he'd stood in this doorway with a census form and counted boots, and how far past that man he had walked. "I need to tell you something. You'll want to put the pen down first."

Now she looked up. Whatever she read in his face made her set the pen in its groove with great care, the way one sets down a thing that might otherwise be thrown.

"Quill came to me," Elias said. "Tuesday. After the corrections I filed on the Marlowe Street households. He had me alone in the records annex and he offered me immunity—full immunity—for testimony against you. Harboring deserters, falsification of residence returns, aiding men absent without leave. He has enough already to make it stick. What he wants from me is the keystone."

The stove ticked. Upstairs, a board settled. Tamsin did not move, and Elias understood suddenly that stillness was her battlefield reflex, the same way his was counting—counting names, counting exits, counting the seconds between a question and the answer that would end him.

"And what did you say?" she asked. Her voice was level. Her hands were not; they had closed around each other on the table, knuckles white as river stones.

"I told him I would consider it. Which was a lie. But a lie that keeps a door open is worth more than a truth that closes one."

"Don't." The word came out fast and hard. She stood, and the chair scraped back, and the man whittling in the corner looked away with the practiced deafness of men who survive by hearing nothing. "Don't dress it up. You sat across from that man and let him believe there was a version of you that would sell us—all of them—" she flung her hand toward the loft, toward the sleeping men, toward the ledger beneath the floorboards, though he could not have known where it lay, except that he could, because he knew everything about her now, "—for a clean record and a pat on the head."

"Yes," Elias said. "That's exactly what I did. And I'm telling you about it standing in your house, unarmed, at night, when I could have gone anywhere else and been safe. That's the whole of my defense. Judge it however you like."

She stared at him. The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere out on the river the ice groaned—a long, low sound, like a door in an enormous house being opened by no one.

"He showed you papers," she said at last. Quieter. "He knew things. Names."

"He knew about Private Ansel Doyce. He knew the boat that moved two of your patients through the fog last month, or thought he did. He knows more than we assumed and less than he pretends. That's the arithmetic as I can make it." Elias took out his watch, an old habit, then closed it without looking. Time-telling felt obscene tonight. "Tamsin. I didn't come here to be forgiven. I came here because you deserve the whole of it, unedited, before anything else happens. If Quill comes back with a better offer—if he threatens me directly—I want there to be no version of events where you learn it from someone other than me."

"That's it?" Her voice cracked on it. "That's your defense? Honesty?"

"It's not a defense. It's a term of surrender."

Something shifted in her face then—the fury finding nowhere to land, because he would not fight her. He watched her look for the flaw in him, the trick, the soft place where betrayal usually hid, and find instead only a tired man with cold hands who had counted her boots once upon a time and never stopped counting since.

She sat down slowly.

"If you had taken it," she said, "if you had gone to him and given them this workshop—"

"I know."

"No. You don't know." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "You know the ledger. You've seen pages. But you don't know Doyce's mother, who writes me letters addressed to a dead man because the army sent her a dead man's letter and I keep answering so she won't stop. You don't know that Halloran in the loft cries out for a sergeant who is on my list twice—once as dead, once as never enlisted—and if the list dies, those two entries die, and Halloran spends whatever's left of his life shouting into silence." Her hands came down. Her eyes were terrible and clear. "If the ledger dies with me, Elias, the names die twice. Once when the army killed them on paper and once when I fail. Quill isn't offering you immunity. He's offering you a funeral for sixty-one men and a receipt for the burial."

The words went into him the way cold water goes into a boot—finding every seam.

"I know what he's offering," Elias said. "I lost a brother to that arithmetic. Thomas died at the Marne and then died again in a filing cabinet somewhere, and my mother got a form with a stamp on it instead of a son. There isn't a night I don't do sums with that, Tamsin. Every hour I spend in that annex touching clean paper, I am aware that paper is what killed him the second time." He pulled out the chair opposite her and sat, so that the table—her table, covered in her small precise script—lay between them like a border. "So here is what I propose. Not absolution. Arithmetic."

She waited.

"The copy you're making. Split it. Half the pages stay with you, half go with me—to my rooms, then somewhere better, somewhere Quill wouldn't think to look because it belongs to a man with no history of trouble. If they take you, they take half a ledger and I walk into the inquiry with the rest. If they take me, you hold the other half. Neither of us can be made to bury all of it."

"They could burn both halves."

"They could burn everything. They could burn the harbor." He shook his head. "But two fires draw more eyes than one. And neither of us lights ours willingly. That's the point. Not that it's safe. That it requires two betrayals instead of one, and neither of us will pay that price."

For a long moment Tamsin studied him across the table, and Elias felt the examination go through him like light through glass—not judging his words but their weight, the way she must judge a wound, deciding whether it would hold suture.

"You realize what this is," she said finally. "You realize this isn't insurance. This is you putting your neck where mine is, permanently, with no way to take it back."

"I realized it somewhere around the fourth rehearsal of my confession. All eleven drafts were worse."

Despite herself—one saw it happen, the mouth losing its war with itself despite everything—something crossed her face that was almost a smile and was gone too fast to name.

* * *

They worked until the small hours.

It was decided quickly and without ceremony, the way all their most important decisions had been made: practically, quietly, in the language of lists. Tamsin pried up the floorboard behind the sail chest and brought out the original ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and beside it the copy-in-progress, forty pages done and twenty-one to go. She would finish the copying tomorrow and the day after; the ice was talking more every night, and they both heard the deadline in it.

"We split by units, not by sequence," she said, laying the pages out. "If one half is destroyed, the other should still show the pattern—the omissions across regiments, dates, orders. A single page proves nothing. The pattern is the proof."

"You've thought about this."

"I think about everything. It's my worst quality and the only reason any of us are alive."

She copied with the lamp turned low, and Elias sat across from her doing what he could—drying pages, checking names against his own memory of the annex files, keeping the ink bottle full. It was domestic work, and the strangeness of it kept catching in his chest like a fishbone: the two of them bent over a table at midnight, conspirators in a crime whose only victims were oblivion itself. Outside, the fog thinned and reformed. One of the men upstairs coughed twice and was silent.

"Your brother," Tamsin said, not looking up from her work. "Thomas. Tell me the unit."

"Second Battalion, Royal Fusiliers. Why?"

She stopped writing. She sat very still for a moment, and then she reached beneath the ledger's oilcloth and turned, delicately, to a page near the middle, and turned the book toward him without a word.

Elias read the entry. He read it four times. The room went very far away and then came back.

*Pvt. T. Wren, 2/R. Fus. Reported killed in action 14 Sept., withdrawal from the Marne sector. No remains processed. No next-of-kin circular issued. Unit clerk's error noted and corrected by order of Capt. E. Merrick — name struck from casualty roll, reclassified 'administrative resolution.' Body unaccounted.*

"My God," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Merrick. It was Merrick's order."

"He signed men out of existence," Tamsin said softly. "Dozens. Your brother among them, by clerical convenience after a rout. Elias—" she reached across the table, hesitated, and then took his hand, which was lying flat on the page as though pinning Thomas down. "He's been trying to remember. He remembers more every week. When he's ready, he'll testify, and your brother's name will be said aloud in front of a naval board with the power to restore it."

Elias could not speak for a while. He found that his thumb had begun moving over her knuckles, back and forth, an unconscious motion, and she did not withdraw her hand, and that was its own sentence in whatever grammar they had built between them out of ink and floorboards and fear.

"This is why you stayed," he said finally. "All these years of it. Not duty. Not even conscience at the start. Someone handed you a list and you couldn't put it down."

"Someone handed me a list of men the world had agreed to forget," she said. "And I discovered that I am constitutionally incapable of forgetting on command. It's not heroism, Elias. It's a defect." She withdrew her hand gently and capped the ink. "Finish drying that page."

Near dawn the copying was done. She cut the pages apart with a sailmaker's knife, straight and true, and divided them by unit as promised, wrapping each half in oilcloth with the neatness of a woman dressing a wound. Then she held both bundles a moment, weighing them, and he understood she was weighing something else entirely.

"When this is over," she said, "whatever it costs—you understand that I will stand up in that inquiry room and say my own name. Not yours. Mine. If you try to take authorship of my ledger, I will correct the record publicly, in front of Quill, in front of God and the Admiralty. I've spent five years being invisible on purpose. I'm done with it as a strategy. So don't sacrifice yourself for me, Elias Wren. It's the one gift I will refuse."

"Noted," he said. "Filed under objections overruled."

"Elias."

"I heard you." He took his bundle from her hand. Their fingers overlapped on the oilcloth a moment longer than the transaction required, and neither of them pretended otherwise. "Then we say our own names. Both of us. Side by side, if it comes to that. Agreed?"

"Agreed," she said, and it sounded less like a promise than like a surrender—both of theirs, mutual, unconditional.

He tucked the bundle inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every heartbeat like a second, heavier heart. At the door he paused with his hand on the latch. Fog had crept back into the streets; the workshop doorway framed a world erased down to ten feet of gray.

"The ice is early this year," he said. "You hear it at night—it's not groaning anymore. It's cracking. Clean cracks, like shots."

Tamsin came to stand beside him in the doorway, her shoulder just touching his. Out in the darkness the river spoke again—a sharp, splintering report, followed by a long settling sigh.

"Quill requested warrants today," Elias said quietly. "The clerk who owes me a favor told me at closing. For the harbor district. All of it."

Tamsin watched the fog, and Elias watched her, and beneath both of them, under the frozen river, the water pushed relentlessly toward spring.

"How many days?" she asked.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'd stop sleeping deeply if I were you."

"I stopped in 1915," she said, and shut the door.
