## 14. What Merrick Knows

The lamp had burned low before Merrick spoke, and by then Tamsin had learned to read the silences between his words the way she once read a pulse—by pressure, by rhythm, by what it refused to say.

He sat propped against the wall in the loft's corner bed, his left arm bound in fresh linen where the shrapnel had torn through at Ypres, his right hand resting on the blanket with a stillness that did not come naturally to him. She had dressed that arm eleven times now. She knew the geography of his wound better than he did. What she did not know—and had begun, in the small hours of the night watches, to fear—was what else he carried beneath the bandages and the laudanum haze: not just shrapnel, but memory.

"Miss Sandoval." His voice was rough from disuse. The other men slept below, six bodies breathing in the dark of the workshop floor, their presence a secret stacked like cordwood under the rafters. "Come here a moment. If you would."

She set down the basin she'd been carrying. Water for morning dressings. A small domestic lie she told herself every night: tomorrow will be ordinary.

She climbed the ladder to the loft.

"I'm not sleeping," he said. "I know you've been doubling my draughts so I will. I don't object. But tonight I'd rather have the truth than the sleep."

Tamsin stood at the top of the ladder with her hand on the rail. Outside, the river ice groaned—a long, low sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep. It had been doing that for three nights. Elias said the breakup was early this year. Elias said many things now; they had crossed some border together that neither of them named.

"What truth is that, Captain?"

"You know which." He turned his head on the pillow. In the lamplight his face looked older than his thirty-four years—the face of a man who had been reading documents no one should read. "You keep a ledger."

The word landed in the loft like a dropped instrument. Tamsin kept her face still. Eleven years of nursing had taught her that; her hands could shake and her face could not.

"I keep accounts," she said. "Bandages. Morphine. Bread."

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse. "Please don't. I've spent two weeks watching you lie to me beautifully—you're very good at it, I'll grant you—but I heard you last night. Through the floor. You were reading names aloud. And I—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I recognized one of them."

* * *

She should have denied it. Every instinct honed since the first name went into the ledger, in ink so faint it could pass for a stain, told her to deny it. Deny, deflect, dim the lamp, descend the ladder, and let him heal or die without ever knowing what was written above his head.

Instead she asked: "Which one?"

Merrick closed his eyes. When he opened them again there was something in them she had never seen in an officer's eyes—something stripped and level, like water after a storm has passed over it.

"Prewitt," he said. "Corporal Daniel Prewitt. Second Battalion, Royal Fusiliers. Listed as killed in action, March the ninth, 1915. Burial record filed. Next of kin notified." He recited it flatly, the way men recited things they had memorized against their will. "Except he wasn't killed. He was wounded at Neuve Chapelle, evacuated to a casualty clearing station, misidentified when a man beside him took his tags, and by the time the error surfaced, someone had already decided it was tidier to leave him dead. A living Daniel Prewitt meant paperwork. Meant questions about why he'd been posted missing for five weeks while his battalion moved twice. Meant an inquiry into how the identification failed. So the error was allowed to stand. The dead man stayed dead. And the living man—" Merrick's voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, "the living man became a clerk in a records office in Rouen who signed himself D. Prentiss, because using his own name had become a court-martial offense."

Tamsin's hand found the ladder rail behind her. "How do you know that."

"Because I countersigned the burial record." He said it simply. No flinch, no armor. "And because eighteen months later, a man calling himself Prentiss brought a misfiled casualty return to my desk, and I looked at him, and I knew. And I signed the correction order that erased the correction. I sent him back into his own erasure with my signature on it." He laughed then—one dry, terrible note with nothing in it of laughter. "I have been dreaming about that man's face every night since you brought me up here, Miss Sandoval. Do you understand? Your patients talk in their sleep. The fog carries voices off the water. And every voice is a man I put into the ground while he was still breathing."

* * *

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the loft window. Below them, someone coughed—the deep, wet cough of Hollis, the gunner whose lungs had been gassed at Passchendaele and whose name appeared in no regimental roll anywhere in France. Tamsin thought of him sleeping ten feet beneath them, listening perhaps, or pretending to sleep, and made herself speak quietly.

"There are nineteen names," she said. "Nineteen in the ledger, and four more men in this building tonight who aren't in it yet because I haven't finished verifying their units. Some are deserters in the way the army means it—men who walked away after the third time over the top. Most aren't. Most were lost by the army first. Misfiled. Misidentified. Written off as casualties who weren't, or as never-enlisted at all, because enlisting them twice was easier than admitting the first enlistment had gone wrong." She heard her own voice go cold and precise, the nurse's voice, the triage voice. "I write down what actually happened. That's all the ledger is. What happened, and to whom, and who signed the papers that made it otherwise."

"And you mean to take it somewhere." Not a question. "To the inquiry. Wren's been asking at the customs house—I hear things even sedated; the fog is a marvelous telegraph. There's a naval board convening when the ice breaks."

"Yes."

"They'll hang someone for it."

"Someone has already hanged those men," Tamsin said. "They've been hanged twice—once out of existence, and once more whenever their mothers write to the War Office and receive condolences for sons who are alive in Rouen or Boulogne or this loft. The inquiry can hang whomever it likes afterward. First it has to look at the names."

Merrick was silent for a long time. The ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound with movement in it, like a door being forced.

Then he said: "Read me the names."

"No."

"Read me the names, Miss Sandoval." He pushed himself upright against the wall, wincing as the wound pulled, and his eyes in the lamplight were fierce and clear and entirely sober. "Not to check them. Not to dispute them. I know what I'll find. I signed orders for at least half of what's in that book—I feel it in my bones the way you feel weather in an old break. Read them to me so I can tell you what I remember of each one. Dates. Signatures. Which desk, which clerk, which convenient fiction. You have a ledger of omissions. I can give you the machinery that produced them."

"Why?" The question came out of her harder than she intended. "Why would you do that? You'd be testifying against your own signature. Against the staff college, against half the officers you've drunk with since you got your commission. They'll take your rank. They may take considerably more than your rank. You'd be handing them a reason."

"Yes," Merrick said.

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"It's the only answer I have." He looked down at his bandaged arm, then at his right hand, the one that still worked, the one that had held the pen. "Do you know what I was doing before I was hit? Sitting in a dugout at Ypres writing letters of condolence. Forty-one of them in nine days. To mothers, wives, fathers. And I realized, somewhere around the thirtieth letter, that I could not vouch for a single death I was describing. Not one. I was signing sympathy onto paper for men whose deaths existed only as entries—entries made by tired clerks from other entries, errors compounding like interest. Some of those forty-one men might be alive. Some might be lying in a clearing station under another man's name, exactly as I am lying here under the care of a woman who keeps the true count." His voice dropped. "I wrote men out of the world with a fountain pen and good intentions. That is the whole of my war, Miss Sandoval. And you are the first person who has shown me the bill."

Tamsin found she could not speak. She had imagined this moment in nightmares—Merrick discovering the ledger, Merrick reaching for it, Merrick's duty arriving like Quill's warrants. She had rehearsed lies for it, barricades for it, burning it rather than surrendering it. She had never once rehearsed this: the man asking to be added.

"The inquiry won't be kind to you," she said finally. "Even if the board is honest. Especially if it's honest. Honest boards need someone to blame, and you'll be standing in front of them wearing your own signature."

"I know what I'm worth to them," Merrick said. "I've been an officer long enough to know exactly how much an officer weighs when he stops being useful. But hear me—" and now he leaned forward despite the wound, and the lamplight carved his face into planes of certainty, "—I have spent two years being useful in precisely the wrong direction. If the choice is my career or those nineteen names, then my career was never anything but the mechanism of their disappearance, and it deserves exactly what it gets." He paused. "There's a condition."

Of course there was. Tamsin waited.

"Not immunity," he said. "Nothing for me. My condition is this: when the board reads the names—and if we do this properly, they will read them, aloud, into the record—they must also read the corrections. Where each man actually was. Where he can be found. Prewitt in Rouen, signing himself Prentiss. Whoever else survived whatever the ledger says they didn't. The point was never punishment. The point—" his voice frayed, and he pressed on through it, "—the point is that a mother in Leeds has been mourning a living son for two years, and no verdict against me brings her son back to her unless someone tells her where to write. Testimony that indicts me and finds the men. Both pages. Or I'm useless to you."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she went down the ladder, lifted the loose board beneath the sail-loft bench, and drew out the ledger—her ledger, the weight of two years in her hands, canvas-bound and salt-stained and heavier than any atlas.

When she returned to the loft, Merrick watched her settle onto the stool beside his bed with the book open on her knees, and something in his posture changed—not relief, exactly. Surrender. The particular peace of a man setting down a weight he had convinced himself he could carry alone.

"Daniel Prewitt," she read. "Corporal, Second Battalion, Royal Fusiliers. Entered in the official record as killed in action, ninth of March, 1915."

"Burial record countersigned by Captain E. Merrick," Merrick said, "on the twelfth. The body interred was Private Aldous Finch. I verified it myself last autumn, after Prentiss came to my desk. The grave in the Étaples cemetery holds the wrong man, and I have known since October."

Tamsin wrote it down. Her pen did not shake. Later—she knew this with absolute certainty—it would shake; later, when the loft was dark and Elias was asleep on his cot by the stove and the work was done, everything she was holding steady now would demand its price. But not yet. Not while the ink was wet.

They worked through the small hours. Name by name, entry by entry, Merrick reached into the wreckage of his memory and pulled out dates, signatures, the specific banalities of bureaucratic murder: the form number, the clerk's initial, the phrase *administrative resolution effected* which meant *we decided not to look*. Twice he stopped entirely, his face going white, unable to go on, and both times Tamsin waited without speaking until he could—because she knew that silence too. It was the silence of a man reading his own handwriting on the warrant of his own destruction.

Near dawn, the ice on the river gave a crack so sharp and close that the windows buzzed in their frames, and below them Hollis cried out in his sleep, and somewhere out in the fog a dog began to bark and would not stop.

Merrick laid his working hand flat on the page—the page of his own indictment, covered now in two handwritings, hers and his.

"There," he said quietly. "Now I exist in your book too. Write me down as what I am: witness against myself."

Tamsin blew out the lamp, because the light coming gray through the window meant the city would soon be stirring, meant Quill's men would soon be stirring, meant every hour now was borrowed. In the gray half-dark she heard Merrick lie back, and heard him say, almost to himself:

"She'll want to know where to write. Prewitt's mother. When this is done. See that she's told."

Downstairs, boots sounded on the cobbles—early, too early, more than one pair, moving toward the harbor district through the thinning fog.

Tamsin closed the ledger and slid it back beneath the floorboards, and stood listening, her heart counting the footsteps the way she had once counted pulses.
