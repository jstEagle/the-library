## 15. The Ice Groans

The sound woke him at four in the morning—a long, low groan from the river, like a door in an enormous house being forced open somewhere below the floor of the world. Elias Wren lay in his narrow bed above the census office and listened to it travel. The ice was talking. It had been muttering for a week, small complaints, the creak of a thing under strain, but this was different. This was the sound of something deciding.

He dressed in the dark and went down to the water.

The harbor district at that hour belonged to no one: gulls, the tide, a lamplighter making his last round with his collar up and his mind elsewhere. Elias stood on the Customs Stairs and watched the river work. Out past the moored lighters the ice sheet stretched gray-white to the far shore, and across its whole breadth ran cracks that hadn't been there at dusk—black seams opening and closing as the current beneath shouldered upward, breathing through them. When the ice moved, the pilings groaned in sympathy, and the whole waterfront answered with a shudder he felt through the soles of his boots.

It was early. Everyone said so. The old men who fished through holes cut in the ice had given it another three weeks, maybe four; the almanac agreed; the naval inquiry, when Elias had first learned of it, had been scheduled accordingly—convening "upon the opening of navigation," which everyone understood to mean mid-April, which meant time. Time to copy pages. Time to prepare Merrick's testimony. Time for Tamsin to decide, name by name, what could be risked and what must be held back.

But the winter had been a liar all along. It had come late, gone soft in January, hardened again out of spite, and now the thaw was arriving like everything else that year—before anyone was ready, without regard for what anyone had planned.

Elias counted the seams in the ice and did arithmetic he didn't want to do. If the river opened in days rather than weeks, the inquiry opened with it. And if Quill got his warrants before that—

He turned from the water and walked fast, not toward his office but away from it, along the crooked lane that climbed toward the sailmaker's workshop. He told himself he was only going to warn her. He knew, someplace honest in himself, that he was also going because the sound of the ice had frightened him, and fear made him want to stand where she stood, in the one room in the harbor where things were named and therefore real.

The fog was coming in off the river as the sky lightened, thickening around the lamp posts, and by the time he reached the workshop door the world had shrunk to thirty feet of cobbles and the smell of canvas and tar. He knocked twice, paused, once—their signal now, though neither had agreed aloud that it was a signal.

Tamsin opened the door with a candle in her hand and her sleeves rolled, and behind her the room was already awake: the smell of carbolic and boiled linen, the shape of a man coughing on the cot near the sail bench, the soft industry of a place pretending with all its might to be empty.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"The whole street heard it."

She let him in and barred the door behind him. Up close she looked the way he supposed he looked—slept in, steady-eyed, running on something that wasn't rest. She'd been boiling bandages; the pot still sat on the stove, and the steam had fogged the little window over the sink until the harbor outside was only a smear of gray.

"Three weeks," she said. "That's what we had. That's what you told me the clerk said."

"Almanacs say three weeks." He nodded at the window, toward the river they couldn't see but could both hear—the ice giving another long complaint, farther east, like something heavy dragged across floorboards overhead. "The river doesn't read almanacs."

* * *

Mr. Hollis the clerk kept the inquiry's schedule the way other men kept birds—in a cage of ruled paper, fed daily, sung to quietly, never meant to fly. His office was two floors below the Board of Trade rooms, down a corridor that smelled of sealing wax and cold pipe smoke, and when Elias pushed through the door at half past eight, Hollis looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man who had been expecting bad news and was relieved it had arrived wearing a familiar coat.

"Wren." He set down his pen. "You've heard the ice."

"I've heard the ice."

"Hah." Hollis rose, joints cracking, and went to the tall window that gave onto the harbor. The fog had lifted enough to show the river's face, and the seams in it, black as inked corrections. "Forty years I've worked this waterfront," he said. "I can tell you the exact day the ice goes out within forty-eight hours, every year, and I'm telling you it goes out Friday. Saturday at the latest. The board convened for April the seventeenth because April the seventeenth is what the schedule says, and the schedule"—he tapped the wall, where a printed calendar hung in its proper nail—"does not consult the river."

"Then convene early."

"On whose authority?" Hollis came back to his desk but didn't sit. "The board members are scattered across three counties. The senior judge is at his sister's. There are papers to circulate, witnesses to notify—" He stopped. He had a long, kind, tired face, and Elias watched something move across it, the flicker of a man remembering why he'd taken a clerk's wage in a port town instead of a lawyer's in the capital. "You're asking me to break procedure."

"I'm asking you to honor the reason procedure exists."

"That's a fine sentence, Wren. Sentences don't sign requisitions." Hollis lowered himself into his chair and was quiet a moment. Then he said, not looking up, "Your brother's name was Thomas."

Elias's breath went out of him slowly. "Yes."

"Casualty list, October 1915. Struck through in the second printing. I processed the correction myself." Hollis's pen lay where he'd set it, parallel to the ledger's edge, perfectly square, and he adjusted it a degree as he spoke, as if order in small things might excuse disorder in large ones. "I have spent thirty years making sure the record says what happened. And for thirty years I have watched men like Quill use the record to make it say the opposite." He looked up. "What is it you want filed?"

"A hearing. Convened at the earliest lawful opportunity upon the opening of navigation—not the board's convenience. The moment the pilot boats can cross the bar, the inquiry sits." Elias laid his prepared memorandum on the desk, written out fair overnight, arguments marshaled, precedents cited. He had rehearsed the speech that went with it. He found he didn't need it. "And I want it entered today, before the board reconvenes, so it stands in the record and cannot be quietly amended."

"Because you know about quiet amendments."

"Better than most."

Hollis drew the memorandum toward him and read it through, and Elias stood in the cold office and listened to the building settle and, beneath even that, faint as a pulse, the river. Every errand now carried the weight of everything—that was the arithmetic of these last days, the terrible exchange rate the ice had set. A signature here, a form there; each one either a door opening or a door closing, and no way to know until afterward which it had been, and no time to stand in any doorway grieving.

Hollis signed. He blotted it, squared the page in its tray, and said, "It'll be contested. Quill has friends on the board."

"Then it'll be contested loudly, in session, where he has to argue against hearing evidence in front of witnesses. Let him explain why the names shouldn't be read aloud. Let him do it into the record."

A thin smile crossed the clerk's face and vanished, embarrassed by its own existence. "Get your documents in order, Wren. If the river opens Friday, you sit Monday." He paused, pen hovering. "And Wren—file nothing else after hours. The corridors have ears this season. More than usual."

* * *

Quill requested the warrants at eleven.

Elias learned of it the way news traveled in the harbor district—which is to say sideways, incomplete, and faster than any official channel: the fishmonger's boy mentioned it, then the ferryman confirmed it, then Ada from the chandlery caught Elias's sleeve outside the post office and told him the shape of it entire. Sergeant Quill, in full uniform, at the provost marshal's office since nine o'clock, requesting search warrants for three properties on the waterfront. The sailmaker's workshop among them. The grounds: harboring deserters, unauthorized medical practice, possession of seditious documents.

Seditious documents. Elias stood in the street with Ada's hand still on his sleeve and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that Quill knew. Not suspected—knew. Knew there were documents. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had seen, or Quill had simply reasoned his way to the truth the way water finds the crack in a wall: patiently, along the path of least resistance, which in this case was a census taker who had filed too many corrections from one district.

Ada was saying something about the provost marshal's disposition, whether he'd sign same-day or wait for his superior, and Elias heard himself answer in a voice that seemed to come from slightly above his own head: Same-day. Quill wouldn't wait. Waiting was for men who believed the situation could improve.

He walked back toward the workshop and made himself take inventory instead of panic. Warrants took time to execute; Quill would need men, and men needed assembling, and even Quill would prefer daylight and a quiet street to a dawn raid on a district full of fishermen with opinions. Hours, then. Perhaps a day. Perhaps less.

At the top of the harbor stairs he met the ice going out of itself.

It happened while he watched. A seam that had been knitting and unknitting all morning gave way with a report like a distant gun, and a floe the size of a chapel roof swung loose from the main sheet and began to travel, grinding, downriver. The gulls rose screaming off the pilings. Somewhere a boy shouted. And the sound that followed—the true breakup beginning, the whole winter's armor cracking along its fault lines—rolled up through the water and the stones and into Elias's chest, and it did not sound like spring. It sounded like artillery. It sounded like the war arriving to collect.

* * *

Tamsin met him at the workshop door already dressed for weather, a sea bag at her feet, and for one vertiginous second he thought she was fleeing—thought everything they'd built had come apart in the hour since sunrise—and then he saw the bag was full of ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, and that she was not running but staging.

"Quill's at the provost marshal's," she said. "Merrick's friend on the harbor watch sent word ten minutes ago. Three warrants."

"I know. I've been to Hollis. We have a hearing date—the first day the pilot boats cross. Could be Monday."

"Could be Friday." She looked past him at the river, visible now through the lifting fog, white and broken and moving. "Listen to it. Friday, more likely." She stepped back to let him in and barred the door. Inside, the workshop had transformed overnight into something between a sickbay and a departure lounge: patients sorted into those well enough to move and those who weren't, bundles labeled in her small upright hand, the coffin—the one from the funeral undertaker's, the one place in the harbor no searcher looks—standing open by the sail bench with its false bottom waiting.

"The original stays here?" Elias asked, though he knew the answer.

"The original stays where Quill's imagination doesn't reach." She knelt and cinched a strap. "The copy goes with you. Today. Not to your rooms—" she looked up sharply, anticipating him, and he felt the strange warmth of being known completely by someone he'd known only weeks, "—not anywhere attached to either of us. You'll think of where."

He thought of where. He had been thinking of where since the fishmonger's boy, turning it over while his legs carried him through the streets: not the census office, not the bank, not any place with his name on a lease or a ledger. But there was one room in the city bound to no name at all, the reading room of the Sailors' Free Library, where any man of the sea could deposit a parcel in the public lockers, no questions, no registry, key left at the desk under any designation a man cared to invent. He'd passed it a hundred times filing census forms.

"The library lockers," he said. "Under a ship's name. Something forgettable."

Something crossed her face—not quite a smile, but the shadow of one, the muscle memory of a woman who had not had much to smile at. "Name it after something that floats," she said. "Everything else in this story sinks."

They worked. That was the mercy of the hours that followed—they demanded work, and work left no room for the sound of the ice, which came steadily now through the walls, crack and grind and boom, the river dismantling its own record of the winter. They divided the copied pages. They sealed them in oilcloth. Merrick sat propped on the far cot watching them with the particular stillness of a convalescent who has understood, before his nurses intended it, exactly how much hangs on the next two days, and when Tamsin brought him his broth he said quietly, "Friday, then. I'll be able to stand by Friday. I'll be able to testify standing, whatever it costs me," and Tamsin held his eye a moment longer than she needed to, and Elias saw the two of them arrive at something without a word being spent on it.

By dusk the patients who could travel had gone—two by fishing boat through the last of the fog, two overland in a market cart—and the workshop held only Tamsin, Merrick, the coffin, and the silence where six other men had been that morning. Elias stood at the door with the oilcloth bundle inside his coat, against his chest, heavier than paper had any right to be.

"Tamsin." He wanted to say a great many things. The ice chose that moment to deliver its largest report yet, a detonation upriver that rattled the little window in its frame, and whatever he had been about to say broke apart with the floe that caused it. What came out instead was the language they shared, the practical tongue in which all their tenderness was spoken: "If I'm not at the hearing, the locker key is under *Cormorant* at the library desk. Tell them that. Promise me."

She took his hand—did not shake it, took it, held it in both of hers over the bundle between them—and said, "There is no version of Monday that doesn't include you. I've decided that. I've decided it against all the evidence, the way I decide everything worth deciding."

He walked down through the harbor district in full dark with the river roaring now, unmistakably, irreversibly open in the middle channel, and the pilot lamps burning at the point, and behind him the workshop's single candle in its window like one lit name in a very long ledger. At the corner by the Customs House he passed a patrol he did not recognize—military boots, new wool, men bussed in from the garrison for the weekend—and understood, with the cold clarity the ice had given him at dawn, that Quill had his warrants signed, his men assembled, and his timetable.

The raid would come at dawn.

And dawn was only seven hours away, and the library closed at nine, and Elias Wren began to run.
