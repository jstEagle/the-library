## 16. Betrayal in the Fog

The boy's name was Wilfred Coyle, and he had been in the workshop for eleven weeks, long enough for the splint on his leg to come off and for Tamsin to teach him to walk the length of the loft without touching the rail. He was nineteen. He had a mother in Harwich who wrote letters to a son officially listed as killed in action at Ypres, and every week Tamsin read those letters aloud to him because his eyes had been bandaged for the first month and he'd learned to love her voice the way drowning men love rope.

She should have known. She should have known that a boy who loved her voice would one day trade it for his mother's arms.

It was Private Denny who noticed first—Denny, who missed nothing, who slept with one ear open like an old dog. "Coyle went out," he said at supper, not looking up from his bowl. "Back way. Said you sent him for camphor."

Tamsin set down her spoon. "I didn't send him."

The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when everyone understands at once and no one wants to say it. Six men around the table, six faces turning toward her, and outside the fog pressing against the glass like something listening.

"He'll be back by dark," said Corporal Ashe, but he didn't believe it, and neither did she.

He came back at half past ten, smelling of gin and river damp, and he wept before he got his boots off. He knelt there in the doorway of the loft with his face in his hands and told them everything: the sergeant in civilian clothes who'd found him limping along Rope Walk, the questions asked gently, gently, the photograph of Quill's own dead brother laid out like a card trick—*you want to go home, son, I can see it in your face*—and the promise. A medical discharge, papers drawn proper, a train ticket, passage home to Harwich by Friday.

All he had to do was say where the others slept.

"I didn't tell him nothing about the ledger," Wilfred sobbed, and Tamsin felt the floor tilt beneath her, because that sentence alone was a confession—he knew about the ledger, which meant he'd seen her write in it, which meant he'd understood what it was worth, which meant he'd understood what its discovery would be worth too. "I swear on my mum, missus, I never said a word about your book."

"But you drew him the workshop," Elias said quietly. He stood by the window, and even in the lamplight Tamsin could see how still he'd gone, that census-taker's stillness, the stillness of a man counting what remained.

Wilfred nodded into his hands.

"And the back stair," Denny said flatly. "And how many of us. And that Merrick's here." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible with a grief he couldn't spend anywhere. "Eleven weeks, Coyle. Eleven weeks we carried you up and down those stairs like you were made of glass."

"I want to go *home*," the boy said, and there it was—the whole war in four words, the thing no one could argue with, the hunger that had eaten better men than Wilfred Coyle. "They said I could go home. Nobody else was offering."

Tamsin knelt beside him. She could feel the others watching, waiting to see what she would do—the keeper of names, the woman who wrote down what the army erased. She thought about striking him. She thought about turning him out into the fog with nothing but his confession. Both impulses rose hot and vanished, because underneath them was only this: he was nineteen, and his mother thought he was dead, and fear had made him small enough to fit through any door the sergeant held open.

"Listen to me," she said. Her voice came out level, and she was distantly proud of that, the way a sailor is proud of his hands in a storm. "You're going to get your papers. You're going to get your train. But not tonight, and not because you earned it—because we're going to make sure that when you leave here, you leave nothing behind you but your own footprints."

He looked up at her, snot-nosed and wrecked. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Elias said from the window, "that Sergeant Quill is going to raid this building, and everything in it needs to be somewhere else before he does."

* * *

They worked until three in the morning, and the fog worked with them.

Two patients went out first, ferried by old Marta Kessler's fishing boat through water so thick with mist that the lamp on her bow seemed to burn underwater. Corporal Ashe went with them, refusing to be separated from the man whose gangrene he'd been dressing for a month. They slipped away from the coal stairs without a light, and Tamsin stood on the dock until the fog closed over the boat like a mouth, and then she turned and walked back up the hill and did not let herself think about whether she would ever see them again. Sentiment, she told herself, was a luxury. The work could not afford it. Neither, tonight, could she.

Merrick refused to go.

"Don't be absurd," he said, when she raised it. He was sitting up in his cot with his ruined shoulder propped against the wall, and he'd dressed himself fully, uniform and all, as though he intended to receive the military police standing. "A captain absent from his convalescence invites questions. A captain present answers them." His eyes moved over the emptying loft, the stripped cots, the gaps in the coat rack where six coats had hung and now two did. "How many hours?"

"Quill will move at dawn if he moves at all. Fog lifts with the tide."

"Then we have perhaps four." He flexed his good hand. "Give me paper. If they find me here, I'll be found with a duty roster in my pocket and a plausible reason for every soul who ever slept in this room. You—" he caught her wrist, and his grip was gentle but absolute, "—you take the book and you disappear. That's an order, Nurse Sandoval."

"I don't take orders from the army anymore."

"No," he agreed, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been respect or might have been shame; she had learned they wore the same face on him. "You write orders. There's a difference."

Elias came down the ladder with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and the sight of it in his hands stopped her breath for a moment—all those nights of ink, all those names, passing between strangers' hands in a room that smelled of canvas and carbolic. He had copied it twice already; one copy lay hidden in the census office under a false drawer, one in a Bible box at the chandlery. This was the original. This was the true book, the one her hand had written in the dark while men breathed above her head.

"Burn it?" he asked, and she knew he hoped she'd say yes—it would be safer, cleaner, and it would break something in both of them that could not be mended.

"No." She took it from him. "If they come, let them find ash and call it guilt. We are not guilty. We are *accurate*. There's a difference, Captain Merrick would say." She looked around the workshop—at the sailcloth, the tar pots, the great wooden forms where hulls had taken shape a lifetime ago—and her eye fell on the coffin.

It stood in the corner by the north wall where the undertaker's man had left it Tuesday: plain pine, naval issue, bound for the cemetery above the harbor, holding Able Seaman Thomas Greer, dead of pneumonia, awaiting burial with honors when the ground thawed enough to dig. No one searched coffins. No one had ever once, in the whole history of death, gone through a coffin looking for living evidence. The army feared many things, but it did not fear its dead, and its dead were the one cargo it never inspected.

"Help me lift the lid," she said.

They lined the bottom with sailcloth, tucked the ledger flat beneath the false floor of the coffin where the body-weight sat, and sealed it with pine pitch so that even a careful hand would find nothing but wood and a dead sailor. Then they lowered Greer back onto his bed, arranged his flag, and Tamsin rested her palm for a moment on the lid.

"You'll carry him safe," she murmured—not prayer, exactly; she'd lost the habit of prayer somewhere between Ypres and the harbor, but some words wanted saying anyway. "Him and forty-one others."

Elias watched her with an expression she couldn't name. "You just hid the most dangerous document on this coast inside a funeral."

"Where else?" She wiped pitch from her fingers. "No one interrogates the dead, Elias. It's the only honest company left."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thinning, the fog lifting off the river in rags.

They heard the boots first—the particular rhythm of military boots, not walking but arriving, a dozen pairs coming up Rope Walk in step. Denny blew out the stove. Merrick settled himself at the worktable with his fabricated duty roster squared before him, looking for all the world like a staff officer reviewing paperwork, which, in his way, he now was.

The door came open without a knock. Sergeant Quill filled it the way bad news fills a room—completely, without apology. Behind him stood four military policemen and, to Tamsin's private horror, Wilfred Coyle, in a clean hospital blanket, escorted like a prince, pointing with his chin at the loft stairs.

"Sergeant." Merrick rose, slow, pained, magnificent. "Captain Merrick, Fourth Division convalescent detachment. You're standing in a medical facility."

Quill's eyes never left Tamsin. "Am I, sir. Funny—I have a list of six men who were billeted here, none of whom appear on any roster in the district."

"Rosters lag, Sergeant. War does that." Merrick tapped the paper. "Transfers, evacuations, deaths. Administrative errors accumulate. If your list is accurate and mine is accurate, the discrepancy is a filing matter, and you may take it up with records."

Something flickered across Quill's face—doubt, quickly smothered. He gestured, and the policemen went through the workshop like a tide: cots turned over, floorboards sounded, the stove raked, the loft emptied shelf by shelf. Tamsin stood with her hands folded and her heart going like a hammer in a locked room, watching them search for a book that was currently keeping company with a dead man thirty feet away. Once, a policeman's boot struck the coffin's stand and the pine knocked softly, and she felt the sound go through her like ice water—but the man only steadied it with a bored hand and moved on.

They found nothing. Of course they found nothing. What they found was a wounded captain, a nurse with steady hands, a census taker with legitimate correction forms, and a room that had been, by every document present, legally clean for months.

Quill knew. She could see that he knew—the way a fisherman knows the fish is under the ice though the hole shows only black water. He stood in the center of the ruined workshop, breathing through his nose, and gave her a long look that contained, strangely, something almost like regret.

"You're a hard woman to catch, Miss Sandoval."

"I'm an easy woman to miscount, Sergeant. Your men keep proving it."

He smiled without warmth, and then his gaze slid to Wilfred Coyle, huddled in his blanket by the door, and Tamsin saw the boy understand—in one sickening flash—what he had actually purchased with his betrayal. Not freedom. Passage. And the boat he'd boarded belonged to Quill, and Quill's manifest had just grown one line longer.

"The boy comes with me," Quill said. "Medical discharge, as promised. Papers drawn proper." He let the words hang there, letting everyone hear how hollow they rang. "Unless anyone objects?"

No one objected. There was nothing left to object *with*.

At the door, Wilfred turned. His eyes found Tamsin's, and whatever he meant to say died in his throat; what came out instead was barely air. "Your mum's letters," he whispered. "They're in my kit. Under the—"

"Go home," Tamsin said. "Read them yourself."

And he went, down into the thinning fog, toward the train and the mother and the life he'd traded forty-one names to reach, and Tamsin watched him go and felt nothing she had a right to feel—not hatred, not forgiveness, only the cold clarity of a woman who has learned exactly what things cost and can no longer afford to weep at the price.

The policemen withdrew. The fog thinned. The river, far below, made a sound it should not have made in February—a deep groan, then a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, rolling up from the channel where the ice had begun, days early, to break apart.

Elias appeared at her elbow, white-faced. "That early? The inquiry convenes when the ice breaks—if it's moving now—"

"Then we've lost our days," Tamsin said. Her eyes went to the coffin in the corner, patient and sealed, carrying its double cargo toward the naval cemetery. "The board sits tomorrow. And our evidence is booked on a hearse."

She was already reaching for her coat.
