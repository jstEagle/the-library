## 1. The Weight of Minutes

The summons came on a Tuesday, which mattered, because Tuesdays were when the fishwives from the Low Quay came up through Candlemaker's Row and were not to be looked at by anyone with shopfront property. Lior Okafor was sweeping brass filings from the workbench when the knock came — three measured raps, official spacing — and stood very still the way their mother had taught them, or the way they imagined she had taught them; fifteen years is long enough to invent a mother's lessons out of nothing.

The man at the door wore the gray sash of the observatory's board of visitors, and behind him stood a boy of perhaps ten whose job was clearly to hold the umbrella and pretend not to exist.

"Master clock's losing time," the man said. "Four minutes in as many days. The astronomer wants it seen to before the equinox observations."

Lior wiped their hands on the apron already gray with the day's dirt, because a clean apron offered to an official suggested you had been idle. "The master clock is not mine," they said carefully. "It belongs to Herr Vasch's shop. I'm only the apprentice."

"Herr Vasch is dead."

"I know that he is dead." Everyone in Vellmark knew Herr Vasch was dead, because everyone in Vellmark knew what he was dead *of*, though no one said so above a whisper and never within earshot of a magistrate's clerk. "But his name is still on the shop lease, and the lease is still under restriction, and I am still — "

"You are nineteen years old and you are the only person left in this town who can read an escapement," the man said, without heat, as if reciting an inventory. "The observatory does not care about leases. Be there by noon. Bring your own tools."

He did not wait for an answer. The boy with the umbrella trotted after him down the Row, and Lior stood in the doorway watching the rain come off the harbor in long gray sheets, and thought about the word *restriction*, which in Vellmark meant many things depending on where you stood when it was spoken.

* * *

To walk from the clockmaker's shop to the observatory in 1911 was to walk through a map of who could speak to whom.

Lior took the long way, up through Tanner's Court, because the short way passed the magistrate's annex and there was a woman named Dagny Sorel who sold chestnuts at the corner there, and Dagny had once been the Okafors' neighbor, and the rule — unwritten, unspoken, understood by every child in the district the way birds understand frost — was that people who had known the erased must not be greeted by anyone who might someday also be erased, lest the knowing spread. So Lior walked past Dagny's brazier and looked at the chestnuts and did not look at her face, and Dagny looked at the coals and did not look at theirs, and between them passed four minutes of shared silence that contained, compressed inside it like a spring in a mainspring barrel, an entire decade of borrowed sugar and watched festivals and a funeral neither of them had been permitted to attend.

At the top of Tanner's Court the cobbles gave way to the switchback road, and the observatory rose out of the fog on its bald hill like something set down by a careless god — a white dome and a square tower, the tower crowned by the time ball that dropped each day at one o'clock so the ships in the harbor could set their chronometers. Lior had loved that ball since childhood. Every town child had. There was something holy about the exactness of it: the whole harbor holding its breath at noon-fifty-nine, and then the sphere falling, and every ship's clock in Vellmark agreeing on the same instant of existence.

They showed their summons slip at the gate. The sentry was young and bored and wet, and waved them through without reading it, which told Lior something about how seriously the magistrates took their own observatory: enough to guard it, not enough to read the passes.

Inside, the tower smelled of machine oil and cold stone and, beneath both, faintly, of the lamp-black they used to darken the instrument housings. The keeper's assistant — a nervous man named Pell who twisted his gloves while he talked — led them up the spiral stair to the clock room, talking the whole way in the low, careful voice people used in Vellmark when the walls were thin and the magistrates' clerks thinner.

"Losing four minutes over four days," Pell said. "Dr. Halloran says it's the pendulum rod. Says the humidity's got into it. But it's a gridiron rod, compensated, it shouldn't — well. You'll see. You'll know better than me."

"Who wound it last?" Lior asked.

Pell's hands stopped twisting. "We don't wind it anymore," he said. "Not since — " He caught himself, the way people in Vellmark always caught themselves, mid-word, the sentence amputated cleanly at the joint. "The old winder isn't with us. Dr. Halloran won't let anyone else touch the weights. But now it's drifting, so."

So. Lior nodded and asked no further questions, because asking further questions was another thing one did not do, and climbed the last of the stair into the clock room.

* * *

The master clock stood against the north wall in a case of black walnut taller than two men, its glass door clouded with age, its pendulum hanging still.

Still. That was the wrongness that met Lior first, before any detail of mechanism — the stillness. A stopped clock in a working observatory was like a stopped heart in a body that continued to walk around: everything else went about its business pretending not to notice. The room's other instruments — the transit telescope, the sidereal barometer, the bank of shelf clocks used for comparison runs — all ticked and breathed in their various rhythms, but the great clock stood silent, and its silence filled the room the way water fills a hull.

Lior opened the case and put their hands on the movement and forgot, for a little while, to be afraid.

This was the truth of their life that no summons or sash or silence could alter: they knew clocks the way priests know liturgy. Their fingers read the plates like scripture. They found the trouble in eleven minutes — a grain of grit no larger than a poppy seed, wedged in the pallet fork, wearing a bright crescent into the steel — and cleaned it, and oiled it, and set the pendulum swinging, and stood listening to the tick settle into its ancient stride, and felt the particular peace that only exact machines gave them. A clock did not lie. A clock did not erase anyone. A clock simply counted, faithfully, all the minutes alike, the ones that happened to someone and the ones that happened to no one.

It was while wiping down the inside of the case that their knuckles found the seam.

The back panel should have been fixed. It was not. It sat proud of the carcase by the width of a fingernail, along a line too straight to be warp, and when Lior pressed, the panel gave a soft click and swung inward on hidden pivots, counterweighted, silent as a held breath.

Behind it lay a compartment lined in green baize, and in the compartment lay a book.

Lior knew, even before touching it, that they should close the panel and wind nothing else and go home and never come back. Fifteen years of surviving in Vellmark had taught them that found things were worse than stolen things; stolen things merely ruined you, but found things implicated whoever had hidden them, and the dead could not defend their choices. Their hand went into the compartment anyway. Hands, Lior had long ago accepted, were not governed by the same survival instincts as the rest of them.

It was a ledger. Bound in gray boards, unmarked, the size of a merchant's account book. The paper inside was good stock, rag-laid, the kind the magistrate's office itself used. The hand was small and regular, a clerk's hand, and the entries were arranged in columns, and Lior — kneeling on the cold floor with the pendulum ticking behind them like a second heart — began to read.

Dates. Districts. Names.

*Elias Marn, cordwainer, Fischergasse. Removed 19 March 1904. Disposition: east convoy.*

*Anna Marn, same household. Removed 19 March 1904. Disposition: east convoy.*

*Petra Marn, aged six.*

Page after page. Not one name but hundreds, entered with the same unhurried precision Lior brought to their own repair log — *cleaned, adjusted, regulated, good for two years* — as if erasure were simply maintenance performed on a town. And in the margins, in a different ink, a second hand had made notes, tiny and fierce, cramped into every available space: *witnessed by the ferryman's wife. No convoy record exists. The east depot burned? Or nothing was ever sent.* And once, beside a name near the bottom of a page: *he kept the receipt. Ask V.*

Lior turned the pages more slowly now. The room had grown cold, or they had. The pendulum ticked on, counting out minutes that belonged to these names, minutes that had kept happening to the world after the world had stopped happening to them.

Near the middle of the ledger the columns changed character. The entries became sparser, more recent — 1907, 1908, 1909 — and the dispositions grew shorter, blunter. *Removed.* Not *east convoy*. Just *removed*, the way one removed a stain, a splinter, a name from a lease.

And then, under the date 14 September 1906, in the same small clerk's hand, with the same bureaucratic calm:

*Yara Okafor, clockmaker's wife, Kupfergasse. Removed. Disposition: withheld.*

The sound Lior made was not loud. It came out of them the way air leaves a cracked bell — shapeless, private, immediately regretted. They knelt on the floor of the observatory with the book open in their lap and their mother's name looking up at them in a stranger's handwriting, spelled correctly, dated precisely, filed between a cooper from the Salt Quarter and a widow nobody had spoken of in years, and the whole architecture of their survival — the silences, the lowered eyes, the four minutes of nothing exchanged with Dagny Sorel at her brazier — revealed itself at once for what it was: not prudence. Cover. The town had not been keeping safe those who remembered. It had been performing remembrance's absence, annually, diligently, like winding a clock.

*Withheld.* Lior said the word silently. Disposition: withheld. Not east. Not deported, perhaps. Not drowned, perhaps. Withheld — by whom, from what, toward where? No margin note attended this entry. The second hand, the fierce cramped hand, had left this page bare, as if even that pen had not dared.

Above them, muffled through floors of stone, the one o'clock ball began its descent, and Lior felt the building shudder faintly with the release of the mechanism, the whole tower committing itself to a single public instant while Lior knelt below in the company of the town's stolen dead.

They did not remember closing the ledger. They did remember sitting for a long time afterward with the green baize compartment empty and the panel clicked shut, the book now lying on their knee beneath their apron, its weight absurdly slight — less than two pounds, less than a brass movement, lighter than almost anything they had ever carried and heavier than the whole tower above them.

Pell's boots came up the stair at half past one, hesitant. "Is it running? Dr. Halloran will want — it's running?"

"It's running," Lior said. Their voice came out level, workshop-flat. Fifteen years of practice, and it worked even now. "Cleaned the pallets. It'll keep time."

"The old winder used to say — " Pell began, and stopped himself again at the joint, the usual amputation. He looked at the clock, ticking, faithful, and seemed to find it unsettling somehow, as if it had returned from somewhere it should not have gone. "You didn't find anything. In the case. Anything loose, anything misplaced?"

The lie assembled itself in Lior's mouth out of materials they had been born supplied with. "Nothing loose," they said. "Nothing misplaced."

Pell nodded, relieved, and paid them from an envelope, and saw them to the gate, and the rain had lifted and the harbor lay out below the hill, all its ships' clocks freshly set to the instant the falling ball had given them, every vessel in Vellmark agreeing on the hour while agreeing on nothing else.

Lior walked home the long way, past Tanner's Court, past Dagny's cold brazier, with the ledger flat against their ribs under the apron, and all the way down the hill they were aware of only one thing with perfect, terrible clarity: the entry had said *withheld*, which meant the record believed she might have been alive after the date. After the date when Lior, aged four, had been told their mother had gone east and would not return, and had been raised on silence in place of her.

Someone had built this compartment. Someone had cut it into the case of the town's most faithful clock, behind glass, in plain sight, where it would be opened only by the hands of a repairer. And of all the apprentices Herr Vasch had trained in forty years at his bench, Lior could think of only one thing certain about the choice: Herr Vasch had died knowing exactly whose hands would one day be small enough, and patient enough, and quiet enough, to find what he had left inside the weight of the minutes.
