## 2. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt like a betrayal in itself. Tuesdays were market days, days of haggling and gossip, of the fishwives' arguments carrying up the hill to the observatory steps — and instead the harbor square filled with grey-green coats and the flat percussion of boots, and every voice in Vellmark dropped at once, as if the whole town had been struck mute by the same hand.

Lior watched from the workshop window, a jeweler's loupe still screwed into one eye so that the world came doubled: the street sharp and magnified, and beside it the same street blurred and distant. In the magnified half they could see the officers' gloves, the brass buttons polished to the color of old butter, the way the townspeople stepped back from the shopfronts without being told. Nobody ran. Running would have required acknowledging what was happening, and acknowledgment was the one currency Vellmark had learned not to spend.

They had occupied the town for eleven hours before anyone came for the observatory.

The knock, when it came, was wrong in three ways — too loud, too rhythmic, too high on the door — and Lior knew before opening it that their apprenticeship had just ended, whatever the papers said about conscription and essential trades.

"Okafor?" The officer was young, with the particular cleanliness of a man who had not yet been shot at. He held a clipboard the way a priest holds a missal. "You're the clockmaker's apprentice."

"I'm the clockmaker," Lior said, and heard how the sentence sat strangely in their mouth. Master Ferren had been dead eight months, but saying *I'm the clockmaker* aloud, to an officer, felt like a theft from him.

"The observatory is requisitioned effective this morning. Signals station, Third Army Corps." The pen moved. Somewhere behind the officer, a private shifted a rifle from one shoulder to the other with a sound like a branch breaking. "You're listed as having knowledge of the master timepiece. You'll report daily to maintain station clocks. Compensation per schedule C."

"And if I refuse?"

The officer looked up then, and his eyes were not unkind, which made it worse. Kindness and a clipboard could move mountains; cruelty only moved corpses. "Then we find someone who can't tell a fusee from a flywheel, and your master clock dies of neglect within the month, and I write down that you declined compensation. That's all that happens to you. Things happen to other people because of it."

Lior understood, then, that this was the new grammar of the town: no more threats, only consequences, arranged politely in advance.

"I'll come at nine," they said. "Clocks should be tended in the morning. They've run all night; you can hear what's wearing before the day's heat swells the works."

The officer wrote something down. It might have been *nine* or might have been *cooperative*. In the new grammar, those were probably the same word.

* * *

The observatory had changed overnight in the way a house changes when a stranger sleeps in it. Soldiers' kit hung where the star charts had been. A telegraph wire now ran through the meridian hall, stapled along the wall that held the tide records, and the clatter of the key came through the stone like a woodpecker that never tired. Someone had propped open the dome with a crate of ammunition so the signals men could see the harbor masts.

But the master clock still stood in its alcove at the end of the corridor, and when Lior put an ear to its case, it was still keeping time. That was the thing about clocks that people who didn't love them never understood: they did not care who won. The escapement released its tooth, the pendulum swung, and empire or armistice, it was all the same weight of minutes falling into the tray below.

Lior opened the case to check the oiling, and there was the compartment.

They had found it three weeks ago, in the first chapter of all this — the hidden drawer behind the strike train, the ledger with its watermarked pages and its columns of names. Three weeks of carrying it home wrapped in an oilcloth inside a hatbox, three weeks of sleeping with it under the floorboards beneath the lathe, three weeks of waking at four each morning certain that footsteps were crossing the yard. Their mother's name was in it. *Sefa Okafor*, in the clerk's careful hand, followed by a date and a word — *removed* — that meant nothing and everything, that was the whole of an official record for a woman who had sung Lior to sleep in a language the magistrates had banned from the schools.

And now the ledger lay under the floorboards of a workshop that soldiers walked past twice a day, and the observatory — the one building in Vellmark built to look at things far away — had become the one place designed to see nothing at all.

Lior closed the master clock's case gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and began to think about hiding places.

* * *

The archive was sealed on Thursday.

They did it with paper first — broadsheets pasted to the doors of the record office, the customs house cellar, the shipping registry: *SEALED BY ORDER OF THE THIRD ARMY CORPS FOR PURPOSES OF INVENTORY.* Then they did it with nails, and then with a sergeant and a padlock, and by Friday afternoon the entire documentary memory of Vellmark was behind army locks, pending an inventory that everyone understood was not really an inventory.

Old Tomas Brenner stood in front of the record office door reading the notice over and over, though he'd been literate for seventy years and needed one reading. His granddaughter tugged his sleeve. He said, loudly enough for the sentry to hear, "Well. Good. Those files were a disgrace of dust. About time someone organized them," and walked off whistling, badly, and the sentry almost smiled.

That was the town speaking its oldest language. Lior had been fluent since childhood — since the year the school roster shrank by six names between spring and autumn and no teacher ever explained where the desks had gone. Silence as shelter. Silence as courtesy. Silence as the mortar that kept a contested town from shaking itself apart. Lior had built a life inside that silence the way a hermit crab builds itself inside a borrowed shell: safe, and shaped by it, and unable to say anymore whether the shape was theirs.

But silence had a different flavor now. Standing before the sealed doors, watching Brenner whistle his loyalty to the dark, Lior felt the ledger's weight under their coat — they had brought it out today, needing to feel it, a madness — and understood that the silence they had always called survival was being collected, page by page, into evidence against them. Every name not spoken was a name the magistrates had successfully unmade. Their mother had not merely died in some unrecorded way; she had been *edited*, and every year of Lior's careful quiet had been another pass of the censor's pen across her life.

The old skill curdled in the stomach. Complicity wears the same coat as prudence until the day you finally read the label stitched inside.

"Move along," the sentry said, not unkindly.

Lior moved along.

* * *

The regulator clock had come in broken a year ago and never left. It belonged to the harbor pilot office originally — a wall regulator, weight-driven, deadbeat escapement, handsome walnut case gone dull at the shoulders where a century of sleeves had brushed past it. Its great wheel had a cracked tooth that threw the whole train off every eleven hours, and the pilot office had stopped waiting for its repair and bought a cheap German spring clock instead, and so the regulator sat in the corner of Lior's workshop like a retired sea captain, upright and useless and dignified.

Its case was deep. Deeper than a movement required. There was a false bottom in the trunk below the weights — not original work; Lior knew every joint in that case and this one was newer, pine where the rest was walnut, glued and stained by hands in a hurry. Some earlier owner had already used this clock to hide something. Lior had discovered the cavity while repairing the cracked wheel and had thought nothing of it then. Towns like Vellmark hid things. It was practically a civic trade.

Now Lior laid the ledger in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth, and fit the pine panel over it, and stood back.

It looked like nothing. It looked exactly like a broken clock.

There was a poetry in it that Lior did not want and could not refuse: the record of the erased, entombed inside an instrument whose entire purpose was to be honest about time. The magistrates had stolen years from people — whole lifetimes, subtracted — and here were the years, the names, the dates, sealed inside the machine that counted them out faithfully, second after second after second, whether anyone listened or not.

"You keep them," Lior told the regulator quietly. It was the kind of thing Master Ferren would have said to a movement he was leaving overnight, and Lior heard the old man's cadence come out of their own mouth and had to stop, hand pressed flat against the cold walnut, until their breathing steadied.

Then they hung the clock on the workshop wall, weights dangling, pendulum still, and went to bed, and slept worse than any of the previous twenty-one nights.

* * *

Conscription settled into routine the way silt settles in a harbor — gradually, then completely. Nine o'clock each morning Lior climbed the observatory hill past the sentry, who learned their face and then their name and then, eventually, their habit of bringing an apple for the corporal on the telegraph desk. Inside, the army had grown over the instruments like kelp over a mooring. Lior wound and oiled and adjusted: the master clock, two chronometers locked in a cabinet the signals lieutenant believed were navigation instruments rather than the town's standard time (they were both), the turret clock that drove the observatory dials, and the new army equipment — the timing relays for the wireless mast, which failed hourly and were beneath the dignity of any self-respecting mechanism.

The soldiers treated Lior with the neutral courtesy accorded to useful furniture. The lieutenant called them "the civilian." One young signaller asked once, shyly, whether the stars through the telescope were good for anything military, and seemed genuinely disappointed to learn they were not.

Lior answered every question correctly and volunteered nothing, and hated themselves a little more each day for how well it worked. The skill was intact. Fifteen years of knowing which silences keep people safe, and the silences were keeping Lior safe right now, efficiently, while under the floorboards at home — no. On the wall at home. While on the wall of the workshop hung the only true account of what this army's predecessors had done to this town, ticking away at nothing.

On the twelfth morning, winding the master clock, Lior noticed the fresh scratches around the strike-train access panel — not theirs. Someone else had opened the case recently. The screws were seated a quarter-turn off from how Lior had left them.

They stood very still in the alcove while the pendulum breathed its slow breath behind the glass, and down the corridor the telegraph chattered, and somewhere below in the sealed archive a soldier's boots crossed the floor where the town's memory lay in boxes.

The panel could mean nothing. Curious hands, a bored sentry, a search for spare brass.

Or the inventory had already begun, and inventories, Lior was learning, were the polite word for taking everything.

They finished the winding with steady hands — fifteen years of practice at steady hands — and walked down the hill past the notice boards, where a new broadsheet had been pasted up among the requisition orders and curfew rules:

**ALL PERSONS HOLDING DOCUMENTS PERTAINING TO THE ADMINISTRATIVE PERIOD 1898–1906 WILL SURRENDER THEM TO THE CORPS INTELLIGENCE OFFICER WITHIN SEVEN DAYS. FAILURE TO COMPLY CONSTITUTES SABOTAGE.**

*Sabotage.* Lior read the word twice, and the wind off the harbor smelled of tar and cold iron, and seven days suddenly had a weight and a taste, like minutes running out of a clock whose escapement had finally begun to fail.
