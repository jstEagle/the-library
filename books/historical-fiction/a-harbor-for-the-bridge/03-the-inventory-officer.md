## 3. The Inventory Officer

The observatory smelled of brass polish and cold stone, and Captain Ilse Brandt had been awake inside it for eleven hours when she first noticed the weight was wrong.

She noticed it the way she noticed everything — not all at once but in the accumulation of small dissonances that other officers dismissed as noise. Her father had been a customs surveyor in Kesselhafen, and he had taught her his trade's one true lesson before she was old enough to understand it: *the manifest lies; the cargo does not.* Weigh everything twice. Trust the needle of a good balance over the word of any man alive. She had been eight years old, standing on a crate to reach the scale, and the lesson had gone into her like a rivet into steel.

So it was habit, not suspicion, that made her lift the regulator clock from its shelf on the second morning of her inventory — habit and the fact that it was the last instrument on the eastern wall still unrecorded — and habit again that made her frown at the feel of it.

Regulators were built light. That was their purpose: a case of thin hardwood, a movement suspended free of the case walls so that no vibration from the room could reach the pendulum. A good regulator was a held breath. This one sat in her hands like a chest full of coins.

She set it down on the long table, turned it, examined the back panel. The joints were clean. Too clean. Every other surface in the room wore its age honestly — dust in the crevices, varnish gone amber at the edges where generations of fingers had touched — but the seams around this panel looked as though they had been cut within the year. Someone had opened this case and closed it again with care, gluing the joint so precisely that only a person who had spent her life listening to objects disagree with themselves would ever hear the lie.

Ilse stood very still in the cold dome-shadowed room and did not open it.

That was the thing about her orders. They were beautifully clear and perfectly empty. *Catalogue all instruments, records, and timepieces of the Vellmark Observatory. Assign inventory numbers. Prepare items for transport to the Divisional Archive.* Nothing about opening cases. Nothing about reading what was found. And beneath the clarity, like a current under ice, the thing her colonel had said when he handed her the assignment, almost gently, the way one speaks to a junior officer whose competence is an inconvenience: *We are not preserving history here, Brandt. We are closing an argument.*

She had saluted. She had always saluted. Seventeen years in the army, and the salute came out of her shoulder before thought could intervene, the way a sneeze does, the way tears do.

* * *

Vellmark under occupation was quieter than Vellmark conquered. Ilse had expected sullenness, sabotage, the low hum of hatred she'd been briefed to expect from a contested port. Instead the town gave its occupiers a courtesy so complete it chilled her more than hostility would have. Shopkeepers weighed her bread without meeting her eyes. Children stopped their games when her boots rang on the cobbles and resumed them only after she had passed, as if she were weather. At the harbor tavern where the officers ate, the landlady remembered every man's preferences and spoke to none of them except to answer questions, and her answers were smooth stones, worn featureless.

On her fourth evening she asked the landlady, idly, whether the town had suffered much in the border wars. The woman polished a glass that was already dry.

"We keep our own records, Captain," she said, "when we're permitted."

Then she went into the kitchen, and Ilse understood that the conversation had ended, and that it had perhaps never begun.

It was the phrase that stayed with her. *When we're permitted.* Not *we remember* or *we mourn*, both of which would have been defiance dressed in grief. *Permitted.* As if memory itself required a license, issued and revoked by whoever held the town.

Her orders held the same grammar. She began to hear it everywhere in them. *Items for transport to the Divisional Archive* — not the capital's public archive, not the university, but the Divisional Archive, which existed in a requisitioned granary behind wire and was spoken of by staff officers in the lowered voices men used for ammunition stores and venereal disease. *Closing an argument.*

An argument was closed two ways. You could win it. Or you could make sure no one remained alive to argue.

* * *

She worked through the observatory methodically, because method was the only prayer she trusted. The great meridian telescope, disassembled and crated. The chronometers — nine of them, beautiful, each in its gimbaled box, each running with a patience that shamed her. The transit instruments, the barometers, the library of ephemerides going back a hundred and forty years. And above it all, in the tower room, the master clock, the regulator of regulators, whose pendulum swung with the slow authority of something that had outlasted three governments already and expected to outlast hers.

The master clock's case bore a maker's plate: *Okafor & Weiss, Horologists, Vellmark.* She copied the name into her ledger without thinking, then sat looking at it. Okafor. There had been Okafors in the town once — she had seen the surname in a shipping manifest among the seized papers, attached to a family that had kept the harbor's lighthouse chronometers for two generations. Past tense. Everything here was past tense.

The apprentice who came twice a week to wind the station's clocks did not look like a man with a history. He was small and gray-eyed and moved through the occupied building with a softness that Ilse recognized, because she had grown up around soldiers' children who'd learned the same walk: the walk of someone for whom being unnoticed was not modesty but armor. He wound the clocks, oiled the escapements, answered her questions in a voice like dust settling, and left. His name, according to the work roster, was Lior Okafor.

She watched him work the day he serviced the station's secondary regulators, and she watched him pass over the eastern-wall regulator — *her* regulator, the wrong-weighted one — with a glance so brief and so controlled that it was the loudest thing in the room.

He knew. Whatever was inside that case, the clockmaker's apprentice knew it was there, and he knew she had catalogued the instrument, and he was waiting — with a patience finer than anything in the tower — to learn whether she would open it.

She did not open it. Not yet. But that night she carried the inventory sheet back to her quarters in the customs house and added, in the remarks column beside item 114, the single word *anomalous,* and found that writing it felt less like bookkeeping than like planting a flag.

* * *

The seized records were stored in the observatory's lower gallery, boxed and numbered in her own hand, awaiting transport. Regulations forbade anyone but the inventory officer to enter. No regulation obliged the inventory officer to stay out after midnight.

She told herself the first night it was diligence — that a proper catalogue required sampling contents, verifying box labels against actual holdings. It was even true. What was also true was that at half past one in the morning, alone in a stone room lit by a single shaded lamp, with the harbor fog pressing white against the windows, she broke the seal on the first box of magistrates' papers and began to read, and did not stop until the lamp oil burned low and the fog had thinned toward dawn.

The early boxes were dull and human: tax rolls, harbor fees, a decade of complaints about fish prices. Then the ledgers changed character. She could mark the year precisely, because the handwriting changed — a new clerk, or a new policy — and because the entries acquired a bureaucratic delicacy that was worse than brutality. *Removed to the interior for resettlement.* *Deceased, cause unrecorded.* *Name struck from the rolls pursuant to Ordinance 12.* Page after page. Families reduced to lines. Lines reduced to strokes of a pen, and after the pen, nothing — no destination confirmed, no grave recorded, no estate administered. People did not die in these pages. They were *discontinued*, like a product line, like a subscription.

Ilse sat in the cold with her arms wrapped around herself and thought of her father's scales. *The manifest lies; the cargo does not.* Somewhere in the world there existed the cargo corresponding to every line in these books — actual bodies, actual griefs, mothers and sons and whole households — and someone in her chain of command intended for the manifest and the cargo to be separated forever, so that in fifty years the official record would show a quiet port, orderly transfers, no argument worth reopening.

Her regiment had garrisoned this district during the deportations. She had known that when she took the commission; everyone in the division knew it the way you know the name of a street you've never had occasion to visit. She had never once wondered what the garrison had done besides garrison.

Now she wondered, and the wondering had teeth.

* * *

On the ninth night she found the loose page.

It slipped from between the boards of a mislabeled folder — a single leaf, different paper from the rest, older, softer, covered in a cramped hand that was not the magistrates' clerk's. A list of names. Not struck-through names; *restored* names, each written beside its official erasure like a correction in a proofreader's margin. Beside one entry, in ink that had gone brown, someone had added a note in the same cramped hand: *witnessed the loading, pier three, they took the mother and both girls, the husband already gone. May God and the record hold what the magistrates will not.*

And beneath the entry, the erased name itself, restored in that careful hand:

*Okafor.*

Ilse read it four times. The lamp hissed. Out in the harbor, fog bells marked the channel buoys, patient as metronomes.

She knew — she knew with the certainty of her father's balances — that this page belonged to the ledger hidden somewhere in this building, the ledger whose absence she had been feeling for days the way you feel a missing tooth. And she knew that the apprentice who wound the clocks each Tuesday and Friday was the son, or the nephew, or the ghost-kin of the names on this page, and that he had been watching her with those gray eyes not to see whether she was dangerous but to see whether she was *honest* — a question, she realized, that she had never once been required to ask about herself, and that now would not leave her alone.

She folded the page along its original crease, returned it to the folder, and resealed the box with wax from her own kit, pressing her signet into it — the seal that certified, on her honor as an officer of the Empire, that the contents were unchanged.

Outside the window the fog was lifting off the water, and the first gray light found the observatory tower, and inside it, she knew, the master clock was striking six with perfect, indifferent accuracy, keeping the true time over a town built on a false one.

Two days until the next winding. Two days to decide what she would say to the clockmaker's apprentice — and, harder, what she would allow him to see when he looked at her and asked, without a word, the only question that mattered.
