## 4. What the Magistrates Kept

The tobacconist's shop on Cordwainer Lane still smelled of the year 1903. That was the thing about Marta Vey's establishment — the years did not pass through it so much as settle into it, layer upon layer, Virginia leaf and Latakia and the sweet dark shag her late husband had blended by hand, until the air itself had become an archive. Lior stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, letting their eyes adjust to the amber gloom, letting their heart slow from its sprint across three streets and a courtyard.

They had run, actually run, from the observatory with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and only at the mouth of the lane had remembered themselves — remembered that in Vellmark a person who runs is a person who has something to hide, and a person with something to hide is a person the neighbors will remember for the magistrates. So they had walked the last hundred paces slowly, past the shuttered fishmonger's, past the widow Anselm's window with its geraniums arranged like witnesses, composing their face into the mild blankness of a clockmaker out on an errand.

Inside, Marta Vey sat behind her counter as she had sat behind it for forty years, small and upright as a finial, her hands folded on a ledger of her own — the shop's accounts, kept in a copperplate hand that had not wavered since the reign of the old duke.

"You're Okafor's apprentice," she said. Not a question. "The one he left the shop to."

"I am."

"Then you'd better lock the door."

Lior turned the key in the lock. The sound of it was very loud, and very final, like the last tick before a mechanism engages.

Marta rose without hurry, went to the door, and drew the blind. Then she pulled the lamp close, turned its wick low, and looked at Lior with eyes that had gone pale as skimmed milk but had lost none of their weight. "Show me what you found."

Not *what do you have*. Not *I don't know what you mean*. She had known. Lior unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be — fifteen years of practice, hands that did not shake where customers could see them — and laid the ledger on the counter between the tins of pipe tobacco and the glass jars of snuff.

Marta Vey looked at it the way other people look at graves.

She did not touch it at first. She read the spine, the corners, the water stain along the lower edge, and then she opened it with both hands, flat-palmed, reverent, the way one opens a book in a church. The pages gave off a faint smell of iron gall ink and something older beneath it, camphor perhaps, or the memory of camphor.

"Page forty," she said quietly. "Read me page forty."

Lior turned to it. They knew it already; they had read it a dozen times through the night, by candlelight, with the shutters closed. But they read it aloud because she asked, and because saying the names felt different from reading them — each one a small detonation in the amber quiet of the shop.

*Vey, Marta. Tobacconist, Cordwainer Lane. Struck from the rolls, spring 1906. Reason entered: sedition (unproven). Property: transferred.*

"That's my name," Marta said, when Lior finished. "In my own hand, if you look closely. I wrote the entries for the last two years of it. Did your master tell you that? No. Of course he didn't. He never told anyone anything that could keep them safe." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "It was his worst quality and his greatest virtue, depending on which side of the wall you were standing on."

"My master," Lior said. "Master Aldous. He hid this? In the clock?"

"He built the hiding place into the case himself, the winter before he died. I watched him plane the compartment. He told me it was a drawer for spare escapements." She laughed, a dry rustle, like paper turning. "A drawer for spare escapements. And all the while he was deciding which of us would live long enough to be lied about."

* * *

What Marta Vey told him then took the better part of two hours, and Lior listened the way they had been trained to listen — completely, without interruption, filing every word into the deep drawers of memory where a person keeps the things they may someday need but must never appear to possess.

The purges had not begun with soldiers. That was the first thing. They had begun with clerks.

"There were three magistrates in those years — Corvus, Halm, and the young one, Dreyse, who everyone thought was soft. It was Dreyse who was worst, because he believed in what he did. Corvus drank and Halm took bribes, but Dreyse kept immaculate records. He believed the town could be *corrected*, the way you'd correct a watch running fast. Small adjustments. Regular maintenance." Marta's folded hands tightened on the counter. "First the names came off the tax rolls. Then off the guild registers. A name off the rolls can't hold property, and property reverts to the town. A name off the guild register can't work at a trade. You'd wake up and find you had never been a cooper, never been a shipwright. Your tools were confiscated as unclaimed goods."

"And after the rolls—"

"After the rolls came the ships." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "There were four transports out of the inner harbor between '04 and '08. Officially: resettlement of persons of uncertain allegiance. Unofficially—" She stopped. Started again. "My husband was on the second one. Bren. He sold tobacco beside me for nineteen years and corrected my arithmetic and sang badly on feast days, and one morning there were men at the door with a list, and the list said he had never existed, and eight months later a clerk handed me a receipt for his effects. One crate. His coat was in it. They had folded the coat."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a cart rattled past, and neither of them moved until the sound had gone.

"The ledger," Marta said at last. "How it worked was this. Every erasure generated paperwork — that's the obscenity of it, child, erasure is *bureaucracy*. The strike order, the property transfer, the transport manifest. Three copies of everything. Two copies went to the magistrate's office and the garrison archive. The third copy was supposed to be destroyed." Her pale eyes lifted. "Dreyse's junior clerk destroyed nothing. He copied the third copies into a single book, in order, with dates, and passed it page by page to people he trusted. Your master was one. I was another. There were eleven of us who knew the whole shape of it, and we called it nothing, because names are dangerous even for dangerous things."

"What happened to the clerk?"

"He was erased himself, in the end. Third transport. His own system ate him." Marta touched the ledger's edge with one fingertip, lightly, the way you touch a scar. "But before they took him, he brought the book to Aldous and said: put it somewhere no one looks. And Aldous said: everyone looks everywhere eventually. And the clerk said: then put it somewhere they'll only find when they're looking for something else."

Lior's breath caught. "The clock. He sent me to repair the clock."

"He did more than send you." Marta reached beneath the counter and produced a folded paper, yellowed soft as cloth, and opened it with great care. It was a letter, brief, in the unmistakable cramped hand Lior had seen ten thousand times on work tickets and chalk boards and the margins of technical drawings. Master Aldous's hand.

*To whoever finds the book,* Lior read silently, the letters swimming and sharpening by turns. *If it is you, Lior, then I am dead and the time has come, and I am sorry for the weight of it. I taught you silences because silences kept us alive. But a silence kept too long becomes a grave, and I have dug enough graves. The book is the town's memory. Memory is the only court that will ever sit for these people. Read it aloud somewhere they cannot stop you, or bury it with me unfinished. Either way, forgive me for choosing you. You were the steadiest hands I ever trained, and the best hider, and I hated myself for being glad of both.*

Lior set the letter down. Their hands, the steadiest hands, were not steady now.

"He meant it for me," they said. "All those years. The drills — *don't repeat what you overhear, boy, don't write anything down, don't let your face know what you know.* I thought he was teaching me cowardice. He was teaching me how to carry this."

"He was teaching you how to survive long enough to testify," Marta said. "There's a difference, though it takes most of us thirty years to see it." She straightened on her stool, and for a moment Lior saw her as she must have been at thirty — the woman whose name was struck from the rolls who had gone on selling tobacco under her dead husband's license, day after day, in plain sight, the most patient act of defiance Vellmark had ever contained. "Now. The important question. Who else knows you have it?"

"No one. Except—" Lior stopped. The regulator clock in the signals station. The weight that was wrong. The inventory officer with the careful eyes who had looked at the case a half-second too long. "Possibly an imperial captain."

Marta Vey closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer, or arithmetic. "Then you have less time than I thought. Listen to me. When the army leaves — and armies always leave, child, that is the one law above all others — they will burn what they cannot carry. It happened in Kesselring, it happened in the Marches. They call it leaving no disputed evidence. Your book is disputed evidence. The moment the armistice stalls, the moment some colonel decides the record must be clean, that book goes into a stove, and every name in it dies a second time, permanently, officially, and no one will ever sit in any court for any of them."

"So I take it out of the town."

"You take it *past* the town. Past the harbor. To someone who outranks the burning." Marta pushed the ledger gently back across the counter toward Lior, and her hand lingered on it a moment, on the page with her own name, her own handwriting, her own erased life. "And you must decide soon. Because I am seventy-one years old, Lior Okafor, and I have kept my silence for fifteen years, and I will tell you what silence has bought me: a shop full of smoke and a coat in a crate. If you come back here and ask me to stand in an assembly and say my husband's name aloud, I will do it, and gladly, and they can erase me a second time over my own counter. But I won't choose it for you. That choice your master already made, God rest his stubborn soul, and made it yours."

Lior wrapped the ledger again, slowly, each fold precise, a clockmaker's folds. Grief and fury had braided themselves together somewhere inside them during the last two hours, so tightly woven that they could no longer be told apart, and the braid had a single end, and the end pointed toward the harbor.

At the door, with the key in the lock, Lior paused.

"Marta. The transports. Is there a manifest for the second one? For Bren?"

"Page sixty-one," she said. "He's there. They're all there. That's the whole point of the book."

Lior nodded, unable to speak, and stepped out into the grey afternoon of Cordwainer Lane, where the neighbors' curtains twitched in their familiar way and the garrison's new flag hung limp over the customs house, and walked home the long way, past the harbor, where at anchor in the roadstead lay the troop transports of the occupying army — and moored among them, flying a merchant flag of a neutral nation, a single tramp steamer taking on cargo.

Lior stopped at the quay rail and looked at it for a long time.

Somewhere behind them, up the hill, in the sealed observatory, a broken regulator clock held a false weight and a hidden book, and an imperial officer was cataloguing instruments with careful, patient hands.

Three days, Marta had guessed. Perhaps less.

Lior turned up the collar of their coat and began, unhurried, to plan.
