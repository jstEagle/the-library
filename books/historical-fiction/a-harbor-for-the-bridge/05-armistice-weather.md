## 5. Armistice Weather

The weather came out of the capital like everything else did — by wire, by rumor, by the smell of it before either.

Captain Ilse Brandt stood at the observatory's north window and watched the fog come up the harbor road in a slow grey wall, swallowing the fishing boats first, then the breakwater, then the customs house with its imperial pennant hanging limp and sodden. Rain had been threatening for two days without falling, and the air had the taste of struck flint. In the capital, eleven hundred kilometers to the west, men were sitting around a table in a room she had once been shown on a tour as a cadet — walnut paneling, a map of the coast, carafes of water no one drank — deciding whether this town belonged to one empire or another, or to neither, or to whoever held it longest when the pens ran dry.

The talks had stalled. She knew that before the dispatches confirmed it, because the dispatches always arrived a day behind the mood of the garrison, and the garrison's mood had curdled. The quartermaster's clerks had begun burning correspondence that should have been kept for seven years. The provost marshal had stopped logging minor infractions. Soldiers who had spent four months pretending to be an administration were remembering, all at once, that they were an army, and armies withdrawing from contested ground did not leave things behind for other people to read.

"No disputed evidence." She had heard the phrase twice now. Once from Major Kesselring, over brandy she had not wanted, delivered with the lightness of a man describing a routine hygiene measure: *when we go, the archive goes. Paper is ammunition, Captain, and we do not arm the enemy.* And once, more quietly, from Lieutenant Fassbinder, who was twenty-three and had begun sleeping badly: *they say the provisionalists are sending observers, that if anything's still standing when the armistice signs—* and then he had looked at her and stopped, because he had realized he was saying too much to the wrong person, or possibly exactly enough to the right one, and had not yet decided which.

The fog reached the foot of the hill. It climbed.

She turned back to the inventory.

The work itself was the only thing that still behaved as it ought. Instruments, serial numbers, condition notes: a transit telescope with a corroded altitude scale; three marine chronometers, two sound; a case of drawing instruments missing its dividers. Her handwriting in the ledger was small and upright and could not be mistaken for anyone else's, and there was a stubborn comfort in that — in producing, line after line, a record that said *this existed, here, on this date*, while all around her the machinery of erasure warmed its engines.

She had been reading the seized records at night for eleven days now. She told herself it was diligence — a good inventory officer understood the context of what she catalogued — but diligence did not require a shaded lamp and the door of the map room wedged so it would not click shut behind her. Diligence did not make her hands cold.

* * *

The town records of Vellmark filled forty-one crates and told their story the way a body tells the story of a beating: by what was missing. Magistrates' minutes with pages razored out but the stubs left, as though removal were less damning than disorder. A registry of harbor pilots that simply ended in the ninth year of the border wars and resumed four years later with different names. Petitions — dozens of petitions, in careful merchant's handwriting, asking after sons and husbands and ships, each one annotated in a magisterial hand with a single word: *received*. Not answered. Received. As though the paper itself were a thing to be taken into custody.

Ilse learned to read the silences the way Lior Okafor, she suspected, had learned them from birth. Which families vanished from the tax rolls mid-decade. Which street names changed and which stayed, and how the ones that stayed clustered in the quarter where nobody had been taken. The magistrates of Vellmark had not been subtle once you knew what to look for. They had simply never expected anyone to look.

On the eleventh night she found the file.

It was misfiled — or rather, filed correctly into a crate that should have contained nothing but requisition receipts, which meant someone had put it there deliberately, or carelessly, and she would spend a long time afterward wondering which. An army folder, grey cardboard, the stamp of her own regiment along the spine: *Third Signals and Garrison Regiment, Detachment Vellmark.* Inside, a single page, and folded within it, torn from a larger book, a leaf of heavy ruled stock in a hand she almost recognized.

She unfolded it under the lamp and the room went very quiet, the way rooms do when they understand something before you do.

It was a deportation order. Dated the fourteenth of October, nine years past. Seven names. No reason given beyond the formula at the head — *for the security of the district and in accordance with emergency provisions* — which was the empire's way of saying *because we wished it*. At the foot, three signatures. The magistrates had signed first, the local hand, eager. Then the garrison liaison. Then, third, in a small compressed script she had seen a hundred times on requisition forms and duty rosters and letters of commendation over the past four months —

Colonel Achren. Her colonel. Whose signature she had countersigned, in some trivial administrative capacity, at least a dozen times since the occupation began.

And among the seven names, listed fourth, between a pilot named Sander Vos and a woman called Reinhild Mesch:

*Okafor, Tessa — clockmaker's wife. Okafor, child dependent.*

Child dependent. They had written her neighbor's son down as a *dependent*, a line item, a weight to be transported, the way one wrote *one trunk, household goods*.

Ilse stood very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the clock room, the master clock ticked on with the idiot equanimity of machines, marking out seconds for a town whose people had been fed into wagons one second apiece, and it did not care, and she had loved it for that, once — the indifference of good mechanisms, the relief of being a well-made instrument yourself, doing exact work, answerable only to your calibration.

Her whole life she had believed the empire was crude but sound. Crude mechanisms, honestly maintained. You did your work; the work was legible; somewhere above you, someone signed, and the signature meant the thing had been thought about by someone who bore the weight of it. That was the entire moral architecture of her service: *someone above me has considered this.* She had leaned on it through the border patrols, through the winter quarters, through the requisition of this town and its observatory and its clocks.

But here was the consideration itself, on one page. Here was the signature. Three of them. And what they had considered, weighed, and authorized was Tessa Okafor, clockmaker's wife, and a child dependent, on the fourteenth of October, nine years ago — and no one had ever written down where the wagon went.

She thought of Lior Okafor's face. The careful stillness of it. The way they moved through the observatory touching the instruments like a doctor taking pulses, and the way they never, ever asked questions, and how she had registered that silence early on as the trained wariness of a subject population and thought nothing more of it. Child dependent. Fifteen years old now. Fifteen years of knowing which silences keep people safe, Marta Vey had said in the market, loud enough to be overheard, and Ilse had walked past and done her duty and catalogued the instruments.

The vertigo came then, properly, the way she had known it at sea once as a cadet — the moment when the horizon proves to be moving and your inner ear insists on a world that is not there. Duty pointed one way: report the discovered document through channels, log it, seal it, let the machinery grind forward toward the incinerator that Major Kesselring had already begun building in the courtyard of the old tannery, because she had seen the bricks delivered yesterday and had told herself they were for an oven, for bread, for ordinary soldier things. Conscience pointed the other way, and conscience had no rank, no uniform, no channel, no form to be filed in triplicate. It only had a name on a page. Seven names. Thousands of names, probably, in a book hidden somewhere in this building by a dead clockmaker who had trusted — what? That someone would eventually open the case? That the empire would eventually produce one officer whose hands shook?

Her hands were shaking. She noted it with clinical detachment, the way she'd note a fault in a movement: *balance staff intact; impulse erratic.*

She refolded the order along its original creases. She put it back in the grey folder. She did not return the folder to the crate.

Instead she carried it to her own quarters, and sat with it on her knees for a long time while the fog pressed against the shutters and the rain finally came, soft and endless, washing the hill, and the garrison lamps went out one by one below her window like a fleet putting itself to sleep.

There was a version of her — the woman she had been in September, four months and a lifetime ago — who would have filed it and slept. Ilse tried to find that woman and could not. Whatever had replaced her had started, she supposed, with a regulator clock whose weight was wrong, a case freshly resealed, glue barely cured, and a young apprentice clockmaker looking at her across a workbench with terror and hope so carefully balanced that neither showed — and Ilse had said nothing, that day, and every day since, and the silence had not been neutral. Silence was never neutral. She had known that her whole life and had only ever applied it to other people.

*No disputed evidence.* The phrase turned over in her mind like a coin worn smooth. But evidence was not the property of the disputing parties. Evidence belonged to the dead, who could not dispute anything, and to the living who had been erased from their own town's books, and if the empire burned the record then the empire was not merely defeating an enemy — it was unmaking the fact that these people had existed at all, and calling it order, and pinning a decoration on it.

Rain on the shutters. The master clock far below, striking two, patient as water.

Ilse set the folder inside her campaign chest, beneath her spare shirts, beneath the commission she might no longer deserve to wear, and closed the lid, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just made a decision she could not take back — that somewhere between the lamp and the chest, she had stopped being an officer of the occupation and become something the army had no form for.

Tomorrow she would go down to the clock room. Tomorrow she would stand across the workbench from Lior Okafor and say a name aloud that no one in Vellmark had dared speak in fifteen years.

She only hoped her voice would hold, and that the clockmaker would not run.
