## 6. A Bargain in the Clock Room

*Ilse*

The regulator clock in the east alcove weighed wrong.

Ilse had known it for eleven days now, and for eleven days she had done the arithmetic of silence: what a report would cost Lior Okafor, what a failure to report might cost her. She was a captain of the Imperial Signals Corps, and a captain who let an unlogged object sit in a requisitioned station was a captain who had already chosen a side — she simply hadn't admitted which one.

She waited until the evening shift changed and the wireless room emptied of its operators, their headphones leaving pale crescents pressed into their hair. Then she went down to the clock room, where Lior knelt at the master clock with an oiler in hand, working by lamplight, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms smudged to the wrist with graphite and brass dust.

"Close the case," Ilse said.

Lior did not startle. That was the first thing she noted, and it unsettled her more than a flinch would have. The apprentice set down the oiler with the deliberate care of someone buying time, and turned.

"The case is closed, Captain."

"The regulator's case." Ilse crossed the room and laid two fingers on the walnut door of the broken clock in the alcove. "Eleven days ago this movement weighed four hundred twelve grams short of its listed specification. I checked the manufacturer's plate against the inventory manifest from the town hall. There is no record of any part having been removed. And yet the case has been opened and resealed — shellac applied over old shellac, and the sealant is still soft enough to take a thumbnail."

She took out her thumbnail and demonstrated.

Lior watched her do it with an expression she could not immediately name. Not fear. Something older than fear, something that looked almost like recognition — as though Lior had been waiting eleven days for exactly this thumbnail, exactly this sentence, and had spent the waiting rehearsing how to survive it.

"You weigh clocks," Lior said.

"I inventory them. It is the same discipline." Ilse kept her voice level, officer-flat. "I am required to report an unlogged discrepancy in a requisitioned instrument. I am asking you instead to tell me what I would find if I opened that case tonight."

"And if I say nothing?"

"Then I open it myself, log it, and you are arrested before morning. You know this. You have known it since the day you hid whatever it is in there." She paused. "You did not hide it well. You hid it quickly. Those are different skills, and only one of them keeps people alive."

Something moved behind Lior's eyes — the flicker of a person recalculating, running the sums again with new numbers. Ilse knew that look. She wore it herself, these last weeks, every night in the records room with a lamp turned low and the seized files of Vellmark spread around her like a dealt hand.

"I'd like to know," Lior said quietly, "which army sent you. The one that signs deportation orders, or the one that weighs clocks."

* * *

*Lior*

They had taught Lior, in the fifteen years since the border wars began eating names out of Vellmark, that survival was a craft like any other. You learned which silences were load-bearing. You learned never to be the most interesting person in a room. You learned that a soldier's question was a hook dressed as a courtesy, and the only safe answer was the one that gave away nothing.

But standing in the clock room with Captain Brandt's thumbnail still resting on the resealed walnut, Lior found the old craft failing. Because the captain had not said *what is hidden there*. She had said *tell me what I would find* — and beneath the officer's flatness, Lior heard something that no soldier in Vellmark had ever bothered to fake: reluctance.

"You already know," Lior said. "Don't you. You've known for eleven days and you haven't reported it. So we're both standing here committing the same crime, and only one of us can be shot for it."

"That is not strictly true—"

"It's true enough." Lior rose, knees cracking, and went to the alcove. The lamp threw their shadow huge across the wall, over the pendulum's slow arc. "You want to know what I would find? Open it yourself. You've earned that much. I won't touch it while you're looking."

Ilse hesitated — a real hesitation, Lior saw, not theater — then drew her pocketknife and worked the soft shellac free. The door swung open on the dead movement, the gutted works, and the ledger lying where Lior had wedged it, wrapped in oilcloth, its corners softened by decades of some other hiding place before this one.

The captain lifted it out with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping child or a loaded shell. She unwrapped the cloth. She read the first page. Then the second. Her lips moved slightly, counting names, and Lior watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"How many?" she asked finally.

"Four hundred and six. As far as Master Havel got before they came for him too. His name is in there himself — third page, bottom. They erased him and he wrote himself back in before they caught him doing it." Lior's voice held steady, which surprised them; inside, everything was ringing like struck brass. "Every name the magistrates struck from the town rolls between '04 and '09. Deported. Disappeared. Dead, when anyone was brave enough to write *dead*."

Ilse turned pages. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the wireless room crackled through its nightly cipher traffic, the empire talking to itself in a language of clicks.

"Lior Okafor," Ilse said suddenly.

"What?"

"Page nine." She held the book out, but did not let go when Lior took the edge of it. Their fingers met on the paper and neither pulled away. "There is an Okafor family on page nine. Four names. Struck in March of '06, deported under escort of—" She stopped. Read it again. When she looked up, her face had gone very still, the stillness of a person standing at the edge of something with no railing. "Escort detail, Second Regiment, Third Company. My company. My regiment, before I transferred to signals."

The clock room seemed to tilt. Lior gripped the edge of the master clock's case to steady themselves, and felt the great machine's heartbeat come up through the wood, patient and indifferent.

"My mother's name is the second one on that line," Lior said. "Anya Okafor. I was eight years old. They told us she'd been resettled south. For fifteen years I have not said her name aloud in this town, because the magistrates' clerks used to fine people for speaking erased names, and after the fines came other things." Their throat closed; they pushed through it. "You're telling me your soldiers walked her to the trains."

"I'm telling you my signature may be on the order. I was a lieutenant then. I countersigned manifests." Ilse closed the ledger with a care that looked almost like reverence. "I don't remember her name. I signed hundreds of those pages. That is the worst thing I have ever said aloud, and I have said it now, and I cannot take it back, and I find I don't want to."

* * *

*Ilse*

Here was the arithmetic she could not make balance. If she arrested Lior Okafor now, sealed the ledger as evidence, filed her report — the machinery would do what machinery did. The book would go to the provost, the provost to the archive, the archive to the fire that everyone below the rank of colonel already knew was coming for Vellmark's paper past. Four hundred six names, ash by spring. And Anya Okafor would remain a line item in a document no one would ever read, countersigned by a lieutenant who could not remember her face.

If she did nothing — if she walked out of this room and un-knew what she knew — she was no longer a soldier. She was a woman in uniform, which was worse.

Or.

"There is a third option," she heard herself say, "and it is treason, so I will say it once and we will never say it again."

Lior looked up. In the lamplight their face was all angles and exhaustion, and yet underneath the fear there was something that might, in another life, in another town, have been hope.

"The armistice delegations are stalling in the capital. Every week they stall, the provisional assembly meets another session, and every session they argue about whether Vellmark's records count as evidence or spoils. If that ledger reaches the assembly before the treaty text is fixed — before the historians finish their clean little volume — then four hundred six people exist again. Officially. Legally. In the record that outlives all of us." Ilse wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, her hands steadier than she expected them to be. "I can move things through army channels. Crates, manifests, transport passes. No one searches a signals officer's freight; we carry cipher books, and no one wants to know what else. But I don't know this town. I don't know which harbormaster takes bribes and which reports them, which cellar connects to which street, which fisherman hates the magistrates enough to risk his boat. Fifteen years of your silences, apprentice — you know where the town keeps its truths."

"And when they discover it's gone?"

"They will discover it's gone regardless. The burn order is coming; I've seen the draft circulars. The only question is whether it comes for a full archive or an empty shelf." She set the wrapped ledger on the bench between them, equidistant, belonging to neither. "So. Do we have a bargain?"

Lior was quiet a long moment. The master clock ticked through it, forty beats, fifty — Lior counted without meaning to, an old habit, the way other people prayed.

"If we do this," Lior said at last, "then I need to hear you say one thing first. Not the bargain. The name."

"Okafor—"

"No. *Her* name. Anya Okafor. Say it like a person, not a manifest line. Because if you can't, then this is just two criminals moving contraband, and I've survived enough of those. But if you can—" Lior's voice cracked and they let it. "If you can say it like a person, then maybe this is testimony. And testimony is worth dying for. Barely. Sometimes."

Ilse stood very straight in the lamplight, a soldier reporting to a court of one.

"Anya Okafor," she said. "Deported March 1906. Mother of the clockmaker standing in front of me. Countersigned by a fool who is finished being one."

The name hung in the clock room like a struck bell, refusing to decay. Lior put a hand flat on the master clock's case, feeling the vibration of it, and nodded once.

"Then we have a bargain. But understand what you're buying, Captain. This town hides things well because it has had practice. If you're going to move a truth through Vellmark, you'll need to see the places where it lives — and the first one is a tobacconist's shop on Rope Lane, run by a woman named Marta Vey, whose name appears in that ledger's margins as the only living witness." Lior picked up the oiler, returned to the master clock, and resumed work as though the world had not just tilted. "Come tomorrow at the change of shift. Wear civilian clothes. And Captain — walk past the shop once before you enter. In this town, even the doors watch."

That night, long after the station slept, Ilse sat in the records room with the lamp low and pulled the March 1906 deportation file for the second time. She smoothed the manifest flat. She found her own signature, young and obedient, at the bottom of the page.

And beneath it, in faded pencil, in a hand she did not recognize, someone had written a single word she had missed on her first reading:

*witnessed.*
