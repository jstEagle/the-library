## 7. The Historian's Draft

He arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing in particular and everything by arrangement, the way all official things arrive in an occupied town. Lior learned of it first through the bells — no, that was wrong, there were no bells anymore; the observatory's signal cannon had been silent since the requisition, and the station clock kept a time nobody was free to be late for. He learned of it the way one learns everything now: a soldier at the door of the workshop, not knocking so much as standing in the doorway until acknowledged.

"Master Okafor. The Herr Doktor will see you at four."

*Will see you.* As though seeing Lior were a thing the man did, a kindness dispensed like lamp oil.

The historian had taken rooms above the customs house, where the windows faced the harbor and the harbor faced away from everything that had happened behind it. His name was Doktor Emmerich Voss, and he was younger than Lior expected — thirty perhaps, with soft hands and the careful, unhurried movements of a man who had never needed to hurry for anything, whose boots had been polished by someone else every morning of his life. He wore civilian clothes with a military neatness, and on the table between them lay a leather portfolio stamped with the double-headed seal of the Provisional Historical Commission, and beside that a fountain pen, uncapped, patient as a knife.

"You are the clockmaker," Voss said. It was not a question. He consulted a card anyway, as if to confirm that Lior existed in the way the record said he did. "Apprenticed under Master Aldous Renk. Fifteen years at the trade. You maintained the observatory master clock before the occupation." He looked up, and his eyes were pale and friendly and utterly without warmth, the eyes of a man reading from a list. "You must know this town very well, then. Its habits. Its people."

"I know its clocks," Lior said.

Voss smiled as though that were the answer he'd hoped for. "Then you'll understand when I say that history is much the same work. Gears within gears. Everything turning on everything else. One is obliged only to record what actually *moved*."

Outside, gulls argued over something in the shallows. Lior sat very still, hands folded, the way one sits across from magistrates and quartermasters and men with seals on their portfolios — still enough that nothing could be read off them, still enough to disappear.

"I am writing the account of the border years," Voss went on, settling back. "The definitive account. When the armistice is signed — soon now, I'm told, days — there will need to be a single narrative both parties can live inside. Disputes over the past are how wars start again, Master Okafor. My task is to remove the disputes." He opened the portfolio and turned it toward Lior. "I have interviewed the harbormaster. The priest at Saint Anselm's. The widow Brandl. The schoolmaster. All of them tell me the same story: hard years, yes. Shortages. Some administrative relocations early in the war, regrettable but orderly. A town that endured." He tapped the page with one soft fingertip. "Does that accord with your understanding?"

The word hung in the air between them. *Relocations.* Lior's mother had been relocated. Their neighbors the Sefu family, relocated — father, mother, three children, gone east on a morning in March 1906 with paper tags tied to their wrists like parcels. Marta Vey's brother, relocated into a ditch outside the toll road, according to what she'd finally told Lior in her back room among the tobacco tins, her voice dropped to the register reserved for things that could get a person killed.

"My understanding," Lior said carefully, "is mostly of escapements and mainsprings, Herr Doktor."

"Of course." Voss wrote something. Lior watched the pen move and felt, absurdly, that each stroke was a small gear being cut — teeth that would mesh with other teeth, other pages, until some great machine of forgetting turned smoothly forever. "But you lived here through it. You heard things. Clockmakers hear everything; people talk in front of you as if you were furniture. I only ask that you confirm what others have confirmed. It would spare everyone difficulty."

There it was. Not a question at all. A form to be signed with a life instead of ink.

"The town endured," Lior said. "That part is true."

"And the rest?"

And the rest, Lior thought, is sitting in a broken regulator clock two floors below the signals room, wrapped in oilcloth, holding nine hundred names in its spine. And the rest is my mother. And the rest is why your pen has been moving since I walked in.

"The rest," Lior said, "was quiet."

Voss nodded, satisfied, and made a note, and Lior watched him do it and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the lie did not need to be forced on anyone. It only needed to be *available*. Fear would do the signing. Fear had always done the signing in Vellmark. Fifteen years Lior had survived by knowing which silences keep people safe, and now Lior sat in a warm room watching those same silences being harvested, bundled, bound into an official volume, and called history.

* * *

That evening Lior walked home the long way, past the harbor, past the fish market shuttered for the night, and saw the lamplight burning in the widow Brandl's front room and heard her voice through the window — she was telling the story again, to her sister, rehearsing it. *"They treated us decently, considering. There were relocations, but orderly ones."* She said it twice, the second time more smoothly than the first, the way one practices anything one intends to perform. Her son was in an imperial labor battalion. Everyone in Vellmark had someone in a labor battalion, or a cell, or a grave without a stone, and everyone knew who held the levers of that arithmetic.

At the corner by the chandlery, old Piet Halvard was mending net by lantern light, and he caught Lior's sleeve as they passed.

"You saw him? The historian?"

"This afternoon."

"He came to me Tuesday." Piet's hands kept working the twine while his eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance, on nothing. "Told me what the others had said. Told me my name would go in the acknowledgments, if I was helpful. Helpful." He laughed once, a dry sound like a knot pulling tight. "My daughter-in-law is from the other side of the border, Lior. She has no papers. One word from me about the deportations and she's relocated too. Orderly. Regrettable." He pulled the twine taut. "What would you have me do?"

Lior had no answer. That was the worst of it — not that the neighbors lied, but that Lior understood the lying completely, had practiced it professionally for fifteen years, and could offer them nothing better except a ledger hidden in a dead clock and a plan with no shape yet.

"They say the armistice may be signed within days," Piet said quietly. "A week, maybe less. After that—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. After that, the account would be published, the commission dissolved, the archives burned or crated away to the capital, and every name in the ledger would become a rumor, then a superstition, then nothing at all. Victors' history does not argue. It simply stops listening.

Lior walked the last stretch home beneath a sky going the color of pewter, and the thought that had been circling for days finally landed, clean and terrible as a gull taking a fish:

The ledger was worthless.

Not worthless — worse than worthless. Hidden, it was a dead weight, evidence entombed with the crime. Found by the army, it was fuel for the burn pile. It had value only in one place, at one time: in the chamber of the provisional assembly, in the mouths of living witnesses, before the ink dried on the treaty that would make the lie permanent. A window. Days wide. Perhaps less.

Everything Lior had done for months — hiding, waiting, keeping silence — had been aimed at preserving the thing, and preservation was suddenly not enough. A record that stays hidden records nothing.

* * *

The signals station after dark had a sound Lior had come to know too well: the tick of forty instruments, none of them quite agreeing, soldiers' boots on the stairs, the wireless crackling its coded weather from the capital. Lior climbed to the clock room under pretense of the evening winding, and there found Ilse already present, standing at the tall window with her cap in her hand, looking down at the harbor lights.

She heard the door and didn't turn. "Voss interviewed the signals staff today," she said. "He'll want you formally on record tomorrow. A deposition, witnessed, signed."

"I know. I sat with him today."

Now she turned. In the low lamplight her face had the look Lior had begun to recognize — the inventory officer's face, cataloguing damage. "And what did you give him?"

"What everyone gives him. Silence dressed up as agreement."

Something moved through her expression — not relief exactly; something wearier and more dangerous. She crossed to the regulator clock, the broken one, the one whose case she had reseal-checked twice and whose weight she had weighed in her hands and said nothing about, weeks ago, in another life. She laid her palm flat against its panel, over the place where the oilcloth bundle slept behind the false back, the way one lays a hand on a sleeping animal.

"I pulled the March file again last night," she said. "The deportation manifest. My regiment. My signature." Her voice stayed level, but her hand pressed harder against the wood. "Someone wrote *witnessed* under it, years ago. In pencil. I've been thinking about who writes a word like that. Not a protest — protests get torn out. Not a signature — signatures get hunted. Just... a marker. A person leaving a light in a window for someone coming later." She looked at Lior directly. "Your master clockmaker. Was it him?"

"It was him."

"And he left the ledger where a clockmaker would find it."

"He left it where *I* would find it." Saying it aloud, for the second time ever, still cost something; Lior's chest tightened around it. "Ilse — I went to see Voss's interviews happening all over this town. Piet Halvard. The widow Brandl. They're confirming the lie one by one because they're terrified, and they're right to be terrified, and it doesn't matter. The lie doesn't need believers. It only needs witnesses willing to say they saw nothing. By Friday there will be a finished draft. By the armistice, a printed account. And our nine hundred names become weather. Something people remember having happened, vaguely, and can't prove."

Ilse was quiet a long moment. Downstairs, the wireless spat and murmured. Then she said, in the flat voice she used for inventories:

"The assembly convenes in Vellmark the day before the signing. To ratify the harbor terms — it's in the protocol, the local delegation must hear the final articles read. One session. Maybe two. Every delegate from both sides, in the customs hall, with stenographers." She turned from the clock and faced Lior fully. "You're thinking what I'm thinking."

"The ledger has to be read *there*. Into their record. Before the treaty exists to bury it."

"That room will be the most guarded space in this province. And we are a disgraced-adjacent officer and a clockmaker with no standing at all."

"Yes," Lior said. "So we'd best stop planning alone, each of us, in separate rooms." Lior held her gaze. "Marta Vey knew the magistrates' methods because she watched them work. She knows who else survived, who else remembers, who might stand in that hall and speak a name out loud. Tomorrow I go to her. You find out when the delegates' names are posted, and which of them can be reached — there must be one who isn't afraid, or one whose fear points the right direction."

Ilse nodded slowly. Then, almost against her will, a ghost of something that was not quite a smile: "Fifteen years of knowing which silences keep people safe."

"And now," Lior said, "we learn which ones kill."

Outside, the tide was turning; Lior could feel it the way one feels any change in a town built on water, a long exhale beneath the cobbles. Three days, perhaps four, until the window closed. Somewhere in the dark the harbor waited with its crates and neutral flags and its one narrow channel of truth, and in the clock room the regulator ticked on, broken and honest, holding its breath around the names.
