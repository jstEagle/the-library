## 8. The Burn Order

The order arrived the way bad news always arrived in Vellmark — by a boy on a bicycle who did not look at anyone, pedaling hard past the shuttered fish market with a canvas dispatch case strapped across his chest like a bandolier.

Ilse Brandt was in the signals room when it came up to her, second-hand, through Lieutenant Fessler's mouth. He stood in the doorway of her inventory office with the paper held wrong — too loose, too far from his body, the way a man holds a document he hopes will stop being true if he doesn't grip it.

"Headquarters, Corps Command," he read, though she could see he had already read it twice. "All seized documentary material in occupied territories designated for immediate disposition upon withdrawal. Disposition to be effected by incineration. Inventory officers to certify completeness of destruction within forty-eight hours of receipt."

"Within forty-eight hours," Ilse said.

"Dawn after next, Captain." Fessler's throat moved. "They've requisitioned the boiler house at the cannery for the burning. There's a list of what goes in first."

"I imagine I'm on it."

"The archive is on it, ma'am. All of it. The magistrates' records, the port registers, the—" He hesitated over the last item as though it might bite him. "The observatory holdings. Everything under your seal."

Everything under my seal. She heard the sentence the way one hears a clock begin to run down — a small, precise sound that meant everything else had already been decided. Outside the window, the harbor lay gray and flat under a sky the color of wet slate, and the masts of the neutral merchantmen rode at anchor like a row of patient needles. Somewhere among them was the *Meridian Star*, out of Hallberg, flying no colors worth saluting, scheduled to take on ballast and chronometers Thursday night and be gone before the armistice was signed.

Thursday night. The burn was set for dawn Friday.

She had perhaps thirty hours between the loading of the crate and the lighting of the fire, and every hour of them would have to be stolen honestly enough to survive an audit.

* * *

She falsified the inventory at her own desk, by lamplight, with her own pen — because a forgery done in haste or in another hand would be found, and what she was about to do would only work if it looked like nothing at all.

That was the bitter joke of it, she thought as she ruled the columns: fifteen years of training in exactitude, and here was its final application. Not to record the truth but to misplace it so cleanly that no clerk could ever prove it had existed. Item 114, marine chronometer, eight-day, hallmarked, crated for return shipment per salvage directive. Item 115, packing case, empty. Item 116, dunnage, assorted. She signed each line in her small, upright script, initialed the margin, dated it, and felt nothing — that was the terrible part, how little she felt — except a cold, steady clarity, like the air that comes before snow.

Under army regulation she knew the penalty. Misuse of office in wartime carried a minimum of five years; if they called it aiding the enemy, which they would, once the ledger surfaced and someone traced the crate, it could carry worse. She wrote the false entries with the same calm she had once used to catalog a dead man's sextant, and understood that she was signing away not merely her commission but her name — that in the official history of this war, Captain Ilse Brandt would be a discrepancy. An error in the ledger. A line someone would eventually rule out.

Let them rule me out, she thought. Someone has to be the error, or the record comes out clean, and a clean record is the dirtiest thing there is.

She sealed the inventory, filed it, and sat for a moment in the dark listening to the observatory around her — the master clock ticking in its tower room above, steady as a pulse, keeping time for a town that had been ordered to forget itself.

* * *

Lior came at eleven, as arranged, slipping into the clock room through the service stair with the noiseless competence of someone who had spent fifteen years learning which floorboards spoke. They carried an oil lamp turned low and a canvas tool roll, and they said nothing until the door was barred behind them — then stood a moment in the lamplight, looking at her, reading her face the way they read a movement, for the fault in it.

"You've heard," Lior said.

"This afternoon." She laid the falsified inventory on the bench between them, and watched Lior understand it — watch the recognition move across their face, quick and grave, that she had already crossed the line and burned the bridge behind herself. "Thirty hours. The *Meridian Star* takes on cargo Thursday night, high tide at ten-forty. If the crate is aboard by midnight, she clears the roads before dawn inspection."

"And the burn?"

"Five o'clock. The boiler house at the cannery." Ilse kept her voice level. "Fessler is honest. He'll count crates against my inventory. Which means the crate that burns must exist on paper and not in fact — and the crate we ship must exist in fact and not on paper."

"A chronometer crate," Lior said slowly. "Weighted right."

"Weighted right. You told me once a regulator can hide a ledger because no one questions a clock's weight. No one questions a chronometer's either. It's packed in sawdust and lead, sealed, manifested, and bound for a neutral flag. I can move it through the quay on my own requisition — salvage instruments, returned to the trade for credit toward occupation costs. It's even true," she added, and something almost like a smile touched her. "The army does owe the trade."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. In the lamplight their hands rested on the bench beside the broken regulator — the old hiding place, now emptied of its secret — and Ilse saw them turn something over that was not a plan.

"My mother's name is in that book," Lior said at last. "Sefa Okafor, deported the ninth of March, '04. Destination unrecorded." Their voice did not shake; it had gone somewhere past shaking, into a flat country where words were laid down like rails. "For fifteen years I didn't say it. Not to customers, not to Marta Vey, not to myself after a while. Silence kept me safe, and I told myself safety was the same as survival, and survival was the same as loyalty to the dead." They looked up. "It wasn't. It was just quieter."

"Lior—"

"If we're caught, you lose everything you were. I want you to know I understand the size of it. I want you to say it anyway, so it's spoken between us before we do it."

Ilse looked at them across the bench, at this person the war had handed her as an adversary and conscience had converted into the only witness she trusted, and she found that all the speeches she might have made — about duty, about doubt, about the regiment she no longer believed in — had burned down to something very small and very plain.

"Ilse Brandt," she said. "Deported from her own life, effective dawn Friday. Destination unrecorded."

Lior's mouth curved — grief and humor in the same muscle, the way a face does when it has finally stopped pretending the two are different things.

Then neither of them said anything more, because there was a moment when planning ends and only looking remains, and they stood in it together, lamplight and clock-tick between them, and Ilse let herself be seen — entirely, without rank or uniform or the armor of procedure — and saw Lior the same way. It lasted three seconds. It was the longest honest transaction of her life. Neither of them named it. Naming it would have required a future, and futures were exactly what they were spending the night stealing back for other people.

"The route," she said at last, and drew the harbor on the bench in chalk dust.

* * *

They planned it the way Lior repaired clocks: part by part, in order, with no step skipped and no assumption left loose.

The quay road was closed after nine; Ilse could pass it on her own authority until midnight, but not with a companion. So Lior would go ahead on foot, through the tanner's yard where the fence had been down since the shelling — "the boards lean, they don't break; put your weight low and left" — and along the sea wall to the coal sheds, where Marta Vey's nephew kept a skiff and owed the old woman debts no army could cancel. The crate would travel separately, on a handcart under sacking, from the observatory yard at half past ten, pushed by Lior, escorted by Ilse in full uniform — an officer transporting salvage, which was the most invisible thing in any occupied town: a soldier doing paperwork.

At the pier, the *Meridian Star*'s mate had been paid in advance by the shipping agent — Ilse showed Lior the receipt — and expected one crate, manifest number 77-C, no questions, no bill of lading until sea. High tide at ten-forty; cast-off at twelve-fifteen to clear the boom gate before the dawn patrol.

"Checkpoint at the customs house," Ilse said, tapping the chalk. "One sentry, rotated hourly. At eleven the rotation changes and there's a gap of maybe four minutes while the new man finds his lantern and his temper. We go through in the gap."

"And if there's no gap?"

"Then I show my papers and you keep your head down and we are two soldiers moving government property, which is dull enough to be believed."

"And if it isn't believed?"

Ilse set down the chalk. Above them the master clock struck the quarter-hour, and they both listened to it finish — four strokes, unhurried, certain — before she answered.

"Then it goes badly," she said, "and the book still goes out, because you'll drop the cart and run and I'll stand there being an officer until you're around the corner. That's the order. It's not a request."

"It's a stupid order," Lior said.

"It's the only one I've ever been glad to give."

They finished the plan in silence, sealing each step like a case — checked, rechecked, oiled. When Lior gathered their tools and lifted the lamp, they paused at the door with their hand on the bar, and did not turn around, and said, quietly, to the wood:

"Eleven o'clock. The tanner's yard. Don't be early. Early looks like guilt."

Then they were gone, and Ilse stood alone in the clock room with her forged inventory and her stripped commission waiting for her somewhere in the future like a debt, and listened to the master clock tick down the hours she had bought — thirty of them, each one stolen, each one running.

Dawn would come in five hours. The fire would come the dawn after.

And at eleven o'clock tomorrow night, in a yard that smelled of hides and rain, she would find out whether the four minutes at the customs house were real — or whether the whole harbor route, and the ledger, and both of them, had already been given away.
