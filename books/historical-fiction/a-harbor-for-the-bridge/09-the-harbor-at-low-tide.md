## 9. The Harbor at Low Tide

The tide turned at 2:14.

Lior knew it the way they knew everything worth knowing — not from the almanac but from the harbor itself, from the smell of the mud flats exhaling as the water began its long crawl back toward the sea. For fifteen years they had set their days by clocks; tonight the whole town ran on one great escapement, the moon and the basin and the ship riding at anchor, all of it geared together, all of it counting down to a single moment when the *Meridian's* captain would weigh anchor on the ebb, neutral flag at her masthead, and Vellmark would have one chance and no second.

They had sealed the ledger into the chronometer crate at dusk. It had taken Lior an hour to do what should have taken ten minutes, because their hands would not stop shaking, because every turn of the screwdriver sounded in their ears like a boot heel on a stair. Ilse had finally covered their hand with hers and said nothing at all, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for them. Together they had bedded the ledger in oilcloth, packed it among the brass and mahogany of six marine chronometers bound for a merchant house in Halvenport, and closed the crate with honest nails and dishonest customs stamps. Then Lior had done the last thing, the thing only they could do: they had fitted the false bottom so precisely that when the crate was rapped upon — and it would be rapped upon — it would answer with the solid bell-note of wood alone, no hollow anywhere. A lie told in joinery. Their master would have wept, or laughed, or both.

Now the crate sat on a handcart under a fish-tarp, and the fog was coming in off the water in slow gray ropes, and Lior stood in the doorway of the dead clockmaker's shop listening to Vellmark breathe.

* * *

They went by the low streets, the ones that smelled of kelp and tar, because Ilse knew which checkpoints had been doubled and which had grown bored. She wore no uniform. She had left it folded on her bunk with her commission laid on top like a resignation letter written in a language the army did not read, and she walked now in a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too broad for her, and Lior kept looking sideways because it seemed impossible that a person could be so changed and so entirely herself. Her stride was still a soldier's stride. Her silence was still a garrison silence. When she raised two fingers at the corner of Salt Lane, Lior stopped as if the fingers were a lever dropped into a detent.

There was a checkpoint at the drawbridge. There was always a checkpoint at the drawbridge. But tonight the lanterns there burned steady and untroubled, the sentries passed a bottle between them, and Ilse took them through the shadow of the salt warehouse at a pace that was almost casual, her forged papers never leaving her coat.

"You've done this before," Lior whispered, when the bridge was behind them.

"No," Ilse said. "I've drilled for it my whole life. It isn't the same thing."

* * *

It was at the foot of Chandler's Row that the night broke.

Lior heard it first — not footsteps but a door, opening too quickly, closing too softly. Then lamplight spilling sudden and yellow across the cobbles ahead, and the shape of a man standing in it. Herr Kolb. The chandler. A neighbor of thirty years, who had sold Lior's mother tallow candles in the bad winters, who had survived every purge by being useful and careful and afraid.

He held the signal lamp the army had issued every household on the harbor streets. *Show a red light if you see anything.*

Kolb saw them. He saw the cart. He saw Ilse, and though she wore no uniform something in the way she stood read *army* to him the way a shape reads as a bird even in the dark, and his face performed the terrible arithmetic of a frightened man. Lior watched it happen. Watched him understand that the crate was the thing, that rumor of the burn order had reached even the chandlery, that here at last was the choice everyone in Vellmark believed they would recognize when it came, and none of them ever did.

"Herr Kolb," Lior said quietly. "Please."

"I'm sorry." His voice cracked in the middle of it. "I'm sorry, child. I have children."

The lamp came up. He was turning the wick to red.

Ilse moved faster than Lior had ever seen a person move. Not toward Kolb — past him, into the light, her hand closing over the lamp glass, and for one suspended moment the two of them stood locked together in that small yellow circle, officer and subject, occupier and occupied, both trembling, and Lior understood with a clarity that would never leave them that Ilse could have struck him down and chose instead only to hold his wrist.

"Look at me," she said, in her parade-ground voice, in her own tongue, and Kolb looked, and whatever he found there made his arm go slack.

The lamp fell. The glass burst on the cobbles and the flame guttered out in the sea-damp, and the darkness rushed back around them like water filling a hole.

"Run," Ilse said. "He'll find another way."

They ran.

* * *

The alarm came anyway. Of course it came. A whistle from the customhouse, thin and obscene against the foghorn's low lament; then a second whistle answering; then boots, many boots, on stone. Lior hauled the cart through the fish-market square with lungs full of broken glass, the wheels jolting over the cobbles, the crate shifting its honest weight behind them, and Ilse ran at their shoulder with her oilskin streaming and her breath coming ragged and even.

The pier lay ahead. The *Meridian* rode at her moorings beyond it, a dark mass with one riding-light, her boats already hoisted, waiting only for the tide and the crate. Two hundred meters of open quay between the square and the gangway. Two hundred meters with nowhere to hide.

"They'll reach the pier head before we do," Ilse said. It was not despair. It was navigation.

"Then what?"

"Then I hold it, and you load." She looked at Lior once, briefly, in the swinging light of the harbor beacon at the pier entrance, and her face was bare of everything except the thing neither of them would name, and Lior felt it go through them like a mainspring snapping. "Listen to me. The crate goes aboard. Whatever happens on this pier, the crate goes aboard. Do you understand me, clockmaker?"

"Ilse—"

"Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Lior said, and hated the yes, and meant it completely.

* * *

The soldiers came out of the fog at the pier head just as the cart hit the planks.

Six of them, then eight, rifles slung, bayonets catching the beacon light in little cold glitters, their sergeant shouting for the quay to clear in the name of the occupation authority. Ilse stepped off the moving cart and walked to meet them, and Lior heard her voice change into the voice of rank, pure and effortless, ordering the sergeant to hold position, claiming an authority she had burned hours ago — and it bought eleven seconds. Lior counted them. Eleven seconds was a pendulum swing and a half. Eleven seconds was enough to bring the cart to the gangway where the *Meridian's* bosun waited with a cargo hook and two swearing deckhands.

Then a lieutenant in the group knew her face.

"Brandt?" The word carried flat across the water. "Captain Brandt?"

And the eleven seconds ended.

The shot came out of the confusion without any order given — Lior would believe forever that no one had intended it, that it was simply the gun going off in the grapple of hands, the way a spring slips a pawl — and Ilse folded sideways against the bollard with a sound Lior felt in their own body more than heard, and the pier dissolved into shouting and fog and deckhands hauling at the crate, and Lior's hands took over.

That was the miracle they could never explain afterward, the thing they confessed only to Ilse, years later, on a different pier in the same rain: that in the worst moment of their life their hands went calm. The crate jammed at the gangway's turn, one runner catching the worn rail, and the soldiers were coming and Ilse was down, and Lior knelt in the lamplight and tilted the crate fifteen degrees to starboard and freed the runner with two fingers — exactly, precisely, the way they had been taught to free a stuck weight in a case without marring the wood — and the crate slid, and the bosun took it, and the deckhands took the bosun, and every erased name in Vellmark went up the gangway in the hands of strangers who did not know what they carried.

A second shot. Splinters from the pier post beside Lior's ear.

"Lift the gangway!" the bosun roared. "Tide's turning! Lift it or lose it!"

Lior looked back down the pier. Ilse was on one knee at the bollard, left hand pressed to her shoulder, blood black as tar between her fingers, and she was pointing — actually pointing, in the middle of everything, with her right hand — toward the ship. *Go,* said the gesture. *You gave your word.*

Lior did not go. Lior ran to her.

Afterward there would be no memory of those ten meters, only the fact of arriving, only their arms under hers and the shocking heat of her blood soaking instantly through the oilskin, and her cursing them fluently in three languages, and the gangway rising behind them while the bosun screamed that he would wait ten seconds and not one more. Soldiers at the pier head, rifles up, the fog swallowing everything, and Lior dragging two hundred pounds of stubbornness backward along planks that shuddered as the ship's screw bit the water beneath them.
