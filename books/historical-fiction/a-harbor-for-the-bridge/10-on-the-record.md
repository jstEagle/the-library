## 10. On the Record

*Lior*

The armistice was signed on a Thursday, in a capital none of us had ever seen, by men whose names would fill the schoolbooks. They signed it without the ledger. They signed it the way the magistrates had done everything in Vellmark — quickly, quietly, and with the ink already dry on the version of events they intended to keep.

I learned of it the way the town learned everything that winter: by rumor, carried up from the harbor through the rain. The war was over. The disputed territories would be administered jointly until some future commission decided. Vellmark would belong to everyone and no one, which is another way of saying it would belong to whoever wrote its history.

But the provisional assembly had convened anyway. They met in the customs house, that gray stone barn of a building with its salt-eaten windows, because the courthouse was still occupied and the town hall still bore the imperial crest over its doors, and no one in Vellmark had the heart or the hands to pry it down just yet. Forty delegates — fishermen, a schoolmistress, two lawyers of dubious allegiance, Marta Vey in a chair carried up the steps by four of her grandnephews. And on the third day of their session, they asked for the ledger.

I carried it myself. There was no one else left to carry it, and I had come to understand that this was not a burden but a post, the way a lighthouse is a post. You do not choose the light. You only choose whether you stand in it.

The customs house smelled of wet wool and tar and the ghost of tobacco. I set the ledger on the trestle table before the delegates, and my hands — the same hands that had spent fifteen years learning which silences keep people safe — were steady. Clockmaker's hands. Exact work.

"Read them," said Marta Vey, from her chair, in a voice like a door opening after years shut. "Read every name. Slowly. Let the town hear what was taken from it."

So they began.

* * *

*Ilse*

I stood in the rain with the rest of them.

I had no rank to stand anywhere else. The commission had been struck from me in a room in the barracks with the same efficiency I had once applied to inventories — Captain Ilse Brandt, relieved of duty, pending inquiry, her signature on the falsified manifest entered into evidence against her. The major who read the charges did not look at me. I understood that too. Looking at me would have meant looking at what I had carried out of Vellmark harbor, and what it said about the orders he had signed.

I expected to feel the loss like an amputation. I had been the army since I was seventeen; it had measured my days, fed me, named me, told me who I was. Instead I felt — lighter. As if a weight I had mistaken for my own body had been lifted away, and what remained was smaller, and colder, and mine.

The rain came down fine and gray, the kind that does not fall so much as hang in the air, and the whole town stood in it. The fishwives with their shawls over their heads. The dockworkers in their oilskins. Children held up on shoulders to see the customs house windows, though from the quay you could see nothing but lamplight and the shadows of the delegates moving behind the glass. It did not matter. No one was there to see. They were there to hear.

A clerk's voice carried thin through a window propped open with a broom handle, and someone near me repeated each name aloud, passing it back through the crowd the way buckets are passed on a fire line, until the whole quay was murmuring it — *Anselm Vey. Anselm Vey. Anselm Vey* — and then the next name, and the next, the syllables rolling through the rain like tide.

Three hundred and eleven names the first day. The reading stopped at dusk and would resume in the morning, and the crowd dispersed the way a congregation disperses, quietly, not looking at one another, each person carrying their portion of the sound home through the wet streets.

I stayed until the last. I had learned in the army how to stand a watch, and this was a watch — the last watch over a record that had spent thirty years in the dark of a clock's case. When the clerk's window finally closed, I turned and found Lior Okafor standing beside me in the rain. I had not heard them arrive. I rarely did.

"They'll finish tomorrow," Lior said. "Marta insists they go slowly. She says the magistrates were quick, so we will be slow."

"Three hundred and eleven," I said. "I counted."

"I know you did." A pause, and rain between us. "You could go home, Captain. Or whatever they call you now. There are ships."

I looked at the harbor, at the masts swaying against the gray, at the neutral merchantman that had carried a chronometer crate out of Vellmark and come back, weeks later, carrying the ledger home again like a tide that reverses. "I don't have a home," I said. "I had a regiment. It's gone." And then, because the rain made lying difficult and I had done enough of it: "And I have not finished my inventory."

* * *

*Lior*

They finished the names on the second day, and on the third day the assembly did something no one had planned. Marta Vey rose — was raised, rather, out of her chair — and asked that the record be amended with testimony. Anyone who remembered a face, a last day, a door knocked upon at night, was invited to stand and speak it into the record.

It took all day. It was the hardest thing I have ever witnessed, harder than the harbor, harder than the bullet I still carry near my collarbone as a scar like a second pulse. Old Henrik Sool stood up with his hat in his hands and admitted, weeping, that he had driven the cart to the north road and been paid to ask no questions. The schoolmistress read out the class register from 1898 and marked, name by name, which children had simply stopped coming. A woman I did not know described her husband's coat, found folded on the seawall, and the whole customs house was silent so long that the clerk had to strike his bell before anyone could go on.

And then a delegate — the fisherman, Brech, with his rope-scarred hands — said the name I had been waiting all my life to hear said in daylight.

"Adaeze Okafor," he said. "Clockmaker's wife. Taken in the spring of '94. No charge was ever entered. Her daughter was nine years old."

I did not stand. I could not have. But I was nine years old again for the length of one heartbeat, in the shop below our rooms, my mother's hands warm over mine on the key of the regulator, saying *listen — you feel it? When the escape wheel lets go, that little give — that's the whole secret. Everything runs on what it releases.* And then the heartbeat passed, and I was forty-one, and my mother's name was in the record of the provisional assembly of Vellmark, read aloud, witnessed, entered in ink that the rain could not reach.

The ledger was not the armistice's. It did not stop a war or draw a border or save a single life that could still be saved. But it was ours, and it was true, and on the third evening the delegates voted to have it copied in full, the original to rest in the customs house behind glass, open to the page of the day, so that anyone walking past could read a name for free, the way you can read the time off a church clock.

I thought of my teacher, old Master Ellery, who had built that hidden compartment into the observatory's master clock with his own hands and sealed it and said nothing for thirty years, and who had chosen me — the apprentice who knew which silences keep people safe — to be the one who broke the silence. He had understood something I was only now learning: that a clock does not make time. It only keeps faith with it. The record was the same. It did not bring anyone back. It kept faith.

* * *

*Ilse*

The master clock ran again in December.

I went up to the observatory on the morning Lior finished it — they had asked me to come, in their careful way, an invitation worded like a work order, which is how Lior Okafor says anything that matters. The signals equipment was gone, carted off with the last of the garrison in November. The hill was ours again, if a hill can be anyone's.

Lior stood before the open case with the tools laid out on a cloth, the way a surgeon stands, and together we watched the pendulum take up its old rhythm — that soft double heartbeat, tick and tock, that had governed Vellmark's harbor time for a hundred years and had gone silent under the army's occupation like everything else.

"You left the compartment empty," I said. I had noticed it at once; I had spent too many hours with my lantern inside that case not to notice.

"The record lives in the customs house now," Lior said. "And in the copy, and in the town. It doesn't need to hide anymore." They touched the empty space where the ledger had slept for thirty years, the little door swinging loose. "But I'm leaving it open. So people can see where it lived. So no one can ever say it was never there."

I understood then that this was the whole of Lior's philosophy, and perhaps the whole of what I had come to love in this town: not the hiding and not the revealing, but the exact, patient work of making things true again. A wheel re-seated. A weight corrected. A name read aloud.

"I received a letter," I said. "From the inquiry board. They've concluded. Stripped permanently. No charges — they don't want the manifest read aloud in any court." I had expected bitterness. What I felt was something more like the moment an escape wheel releases: a small, precise give, and then motion.

"Will you go back to the capital?"

"No." I looked out the observatory's long windows, down at Vellmark spread in its winter gray — the harbor, the customs house, the smoke rising from a thousand chimneys of a town that belonged to everyone and no one and, this morning, to itself. "I don't know how to be anything but an officer. But I know how to keep records. The assembly needs a registrar. Marta has already volunteered me." I paused. "She terrifies me."

"She terrifies everyone," Lior said, and for the first time since I had known them — through the clock room, the harbor, the long weeks of the reading — they smiled fully, and it changed their whole face, the way a struck bell changes a room. "Stay, then. Vellmark could use someone who counts things honestly."

"It could use someone who fixes them," I said.

We stood there a while longer, the two of us, in the cold dome of the observatory, listening to the clock keep its faith with the hour. Neither of us named what stood between us, that thing we had acknowledged once in a single look on a burning quay and had carried, unspoken, ever since — two people trained by opposing systems not to trust, learning it anyway, slowly, the way you learn a difficult movement of a mechanism, one tooth at a time. There was no hurry. We had both learned what hurry costs.

Below us, the town bell began to ring the hour, and the harbor answered with its own bells, and the sound went out over the water the way the names had gone out over the quay — passed from voice to voice, kept, kept, kept.

Lior closed the clock case but left the little door open, a dark square in the bright brass, empty and honest.

"Come down to the shop," they said. "I'll teach you to clean a movement. You have steady hands."

It was not a victory. It was better. It was a harbor, and we stayed.
