## 1. The Weight of Unasked Questions

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the tide turned, when the Meridian Trading Company's counting house smelled of wet rope and cold ink. Cyrus Yarrow saw him first from the loading dock—a man in a good coat the color of wet slate, walking the plank road with the unhurried gait of someone paid by the day rather than by the task. He carried a leather satchel and a folded umbrella he did not open, though the drizzle had been coming down since dawn like something the sky had forgotten to finish.

Cyrus set down the crate of cured cod he was carrying and made himself keep setting down things at a normal speed. Three years in Saltcross had taught him that the body betrayed what the mouth concealed. A man who moved too quickly when an official appeared might as well have rung the harbor bell.

"Boy." The census taker had reached him without seeming to hurry. He was perhaps fifty, with the ruddy, wind-scoured face of a man who took his meals in public houses and his opinions from newspapers. "Yarrow, is it? The indentured lad?"

"Yes, sir."

"Cyrus Yarrow, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, entered into service March of 'ninety-six. That's the record." He opened the satchel and produced a ledger bound in green canvas, its corners soft with handling. "I'm Prewitt. Crown census, special enumeration for the port districts. New regulations after the troubles." He said *the troubles* the way men in Saltcross said it—lightly, the way one steps over a grave.

"Yes, sir."

"You'll have been here three years. Good. You'll know the waterfront households better than most. These fishermen come and go; half of them can't be found twice in the same week." Prewett licked his thumb and turned pages. Cyrus watched the pages turn and felt the particular cold that came not from the rain but from beneath it, from under the floor of things.

"I do what I'm told," Cyrus said. "Carry crates. Mend nets when they're short-handed."

"Mm." Prewitt ran a finger down a column. "The old net-house. East of the salt sheds, past the breakwater path. Company property, I understand, standing empty these—" He glanced up. His eyes were pale and patient as a heron's. "How long would you say it's stood empty?"

*There it is.* Cyrus had known the question was coming for eleven days, ever since the notice went up on the customs house door announcing the special enumeration and the word *declared enemies* in the same paragraph, printed in the same ink, as if the two were naturally related. Eleven days was long enough to prepare an answer but not so long that an answer sounded prepared.

"Since before I came," Cyrus said. "It's falling in. Kids dare each other to go out to it. The roof's mostly gone on the north side." All true. All useless. Truth was a currency in Saltcross, and everyone spent it in small coins to avoid paying in large ones.

Prewitt wrote nothing. That was the worst part—the pen hovering above the line, the small silence stretching. "And the Ferreira family," he said. "Marta Ferreira, her son. They occupied that house until the spring of 'ninety-seven, according to the parish rolls. You'd have been here then. A year under your belt. You'd have seen them."

The rain ticked against the dock planks. Somewhere behind him, in the counting house, Halloway's abacus clicked—click, click, pause—like a slow heart learning to worry.

"I heard the name," Cyrus said. "Foreign family. They left when the fishing turned bad. Everyone was leaving that year."

"Everyone was *leaving*," Prewitt repeated, pleasantly. "Or being removed. There's a difference, and the crown is very interested in the difference just now." He closed the ledger partway, keeping his finger in it like a bookmark in a man's life. "You understand, Yarrow, that harboring declared persons is itself a declaration. Anyone with knowledge who fails to report it shares the offense. That's the new regulation. It's in the gazette. I can show you the paragraph."

"No need, sir."

"Good lad." Prewitt smiled, and the smile was the most frightening thing about him because it contained no threat at all—only the mild confidence of a man who believed the world would eventually sort itself into columns. "You've been useful. Invisible boys usually are. Nobody watches them, so they watch everything." He tucked the satchel under his arm. "I'll be in town through Friday. If anything occurs to you—anything at all—I'm lodging at the Anchor and Chain. Ask for me any hour."

He walked back up the plank road, opening his umbrella only when the drizzle thickened, as if even weather were a form he filled out selectively.

Cyrus stood a long moment among the crates. His hands were steady. He noted this with a strange detachment, the way he'd once noted, underwater, that his air was running low and there was nothing to do about it but continue.

*Invisible boys usually are.* Useful. That was the trap he'd built for himself plank by plank, year by year: learn everything, say nothing, become the boy nobody watched—and thereby become exactly the boy they'd want, when they wanted. His safety had always been a loan taken out against this day.

Behind him, the abacus had gone silent.

"You lied to him." Halloway stood in the counting house doorway, sleeves rolled, forearms freckled and thin. He was sixty if he was a day, with spectacles that magnified his eyes into two alarmed moons, and he had served the company longer than Cyrus had been alive. "Smoothly, I'll grant you. Better than most grown men could manage. But I keep the property rolls, boy, and I walked that net-house myself last autumn chasing a leak in the salt shed roof." He came down the step, lowering his voice though the dock stood empty. "There were fresh footprints in the ash. Small ones. A woman's boots, patched at the heel. And a straw pallet behind the net racks that had been slept in recently enough to still hold its shape."

The rain filled the pause.

"So either you don't know your own harbor," Halloway said, "which I doubt, or you know it better than the crown would like, which is worse—for you, and possibly for whoever sleeps in my net-house."

"Halloway—"

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Don't tell me. Hear me clearly: don't tell me. Whatever you say next becomes something I have to carry, and I'm an old man with bad shoulders." But his eyes, behind the moon-glasses, were not unkind, and that frightened Cyrus more than Prewitt's patience had. Kindness was a debt with no fixed terms. "What I will tell you is this. Prewitt lodges at the Anchor and Chain. He drinks in the taproom each evening between seven and nine, where the pilots gather, where the customs men gather, where every loose tongue in Saltcross goes to loosen further. He is building a map, boy. Not of houses—of people. Who owes whom. Who weeps for whom. He asked me yesterday whether Marta Ferreira had friends in the company."

Cyrus kept his face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a hull taking water. *Yesterday.* Before he'd ever spoken to Cyrus. The question about the Ferreiras hadn't been a question at all. It had been a test with the answer already written, waiting to see whose hand shook.

"And what did you tell him?" Cyrus asked quietly.

Halloway looked out at the gray water, at the hulks moored beyond the breakwater—the condemned ships waiting for the scrap dealers, their rigging hanging like cut hair. One of them, Cyrus knew, held more than ballast.

"I told him clerks don't have friends," Halloway said. "I told him the truth, which is that I keep the ledgers and the ledgers keep me." He turned back, and now his voice dropped to nearly nothing, lost under the rain. "But ledgers get audited, Yarrow. Papers get read by eyes above mine. Whatever you're carrying—and you are carrying something, you've carried it since the day you signed those papers—you should decide quickly what to do with it. Because that man will be back before Friday, and he doesn't ask questions twice. He asks them again, differently, to people who answer."

He went inside. After a moment the abacus resumed: click, click, pause. Click, click, pause.

Cyrus stood alone on the dock with the crates and the rain, doing the arithmetic that mattered more than any in the counting house. Prewitt would return. Halloway already suspected. And somewhere across the water, in the ribs of a rotting hulk, a boy named Tomas Ferreira was waiting for a decision Cyrus had promised to make in peacetime, back when promises were cheap and no one imagined the century itself would end before they came due.

Three days until Friday.

He picked up the crate of cod, because invisible boys must go on looking ordinary even while the ground opens under them, and began to walk—not toward the counting house, but toward the tide road east, where the salt sheds gave way to mudflats and the long gray ruin of the net-house waited with its secret breathing inside.
