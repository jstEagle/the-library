## 2. What the Tide Returned

Three days after the census taker left, the fog came back to Saltcross like a debt collector, patient and certain of its due. Cyrus Yarrow walked through it at dusk, taking the long way around the cooperage, past the tar-sheds where the smell would cover his passage, down to the strand below the old net-house. He had learned in three years of indenture that a man could be invisible only so long as he moved as though he had every right to be nowhere at all.

The net-house crouched at the tideline on rotting pilings, its windows dark, its roof sagging under a century of gull-droppings and salt. To anyone from the Meridian Trading Company it was condemned property, scheduled for demolition when the harbor works began. To Marta Ferreira it had been home for eleven years, and now it was the last place in Saltcross where her name did not cost money.

Cyrus knocked twice, paused, then once more — the pattern she had taught him the first winter, when he was sixteen and still flinched at raised voices. The door opened a hand's width. Her eyes found his in the gap, sharp and brown as a wren's, and the door swung wide.

"You came by the tanyard," she said. Not a question.

"The long way."

"The only way." She shut the door behind him and dropped the bar, and the room closed around them both: fish-oil lamp, mended nets hanging from the beams like shed skins, the smell of brine and woodsmoke that he had carried in his memory for three years without letting himself look at it directly.

Marta Ferreira had not grown older so much as harder-edged, the way driftwood does. Her hands were still quick. When she took his face in them and turned it toward the light, he felt thirteen again, half-drowned, coughing harbor water onto her floor while the tide went out of his ears.

"Let me look at you," she said. "They've fed you, at least."

"They feed everyone they own."

"And you let them." She released him and stepped back, studying him the way she used to study weather off the point. "You wear their silence like a coat now, Cyrus. I can hardly see the boy who slept in my loft."

"That boy kept you alive," he said, before he could stop himself. The words came out with an edge he hadn't intended, and he saw her register it — not wounded, but noting it, filing it away the way she filed everything. "I'm sorry. I didn't come here to quarrel."

"No," she said. "You came because of the census man."

The lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere along the strand, a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing.

"Halloway told me he'd been asking questions," Cyrus said carefully. "At the counting-house. Whether the Ferreira family still occupied this house. I told him no one had lived here since the war measures. I said the roof fell in two winters back."

"I heard about the fallen roof." Something moved at the corner of his eye — the boy, Tomas's younger brother Joaquim, slipping up the ladder to the loft, silent as smoke. Eleven years old and trained already. It made something cold settle in Cyrus's chest. Children shouldn't know how to disappear. He'd learned it at fifteen; he'd hoped it would die out of the world.

"Marta." He made himself say it plainly. "If the crown declares you enemies — if they find you here — I can't protect you. My papers belong to Meridian. If Halloway asks me again, and asks me in front of the factor, I don't know what my mouth will do. That frightens me more than the patrols do."

"It should." She poured him tea he didn't want, in a cup chipped at the rim, and pressed it into his hands anyway because refusing Marta Ferreira's tea had never once succeeded. "Sit down, Cyrus. There's something worse than questions."

* * *

*Saltcross, 14th of October.*

*My friend — I will call you friend even on paper, where names are dangerous, because what else is there to call a boy who pulled my sons from the water and asked nothing?*

*The hulks have come into the outer roads. Three of them, black-hulled, flying the transport pennant. They say the condemned ships are for prisoners but the harbormaster's men have been seen taking families aboard at night. Senhor Alves went last Tuesday with his wife and daughters, and no one has written to us since. No one writes from a hulk. That is the whole of the arithmetic.*

*Tomas is not taken yet. But Tomas is not free either. You will understand when you come. Come soon, and come as the fog comes — without being seen to arrive.*

*— M.F.*

* * *

"He's on the *Corvina*," Marta said.

Cyrus set the cup down before his hand could shake it. The *Corvina* was a hulk moored beyond the chain in the outer roads — a condemned collier, stripped of everything but rust and rats, officially awaiting breaking. Officially empty.

"Awaiting deportation," Marta corrected herself, as though she'd heard his thought. "That's the word they use. Deported. As if the sea were a country that wanted him."

"How — how is he alive out there? How is he fed?"

"Feeding him is the easy part. The watchman on the chain buoy owes me for eleven years of mended nets and never asking why some boats go out heavy and come back light. Tomas comes ashore when the fog allows. He was here four nights ago." Her voice did something complicated around the word *here*, and Cyrus understood: the boy had slept under this roof within earshot of the patrols, and she had lain awake listening to him breathe, knowing each breath was borrowed.

"Why hasn't he run?" Cyrus asked. "There are coastal packets. Smugglers' boats. Half the fishermen in Saltcross would take silver and ask nothing—"

"He tried." She sat down across from him, and for the first time since he'd known her, Marta Ferreira looked tired all the way through. "In September. The packet out of Gull Reach was boarded inside the headland. They took everyone's papers, and they took the boy who couldn't produce any. He's on the *Corvina* now too. So Tomas waits, because waiting is the only thing that isn't drowning. And Cyrus—" she leaned forward, and the lamplight filled the hollows of her face, "—the deportation order comes with the new year. The harbormaster signs them in batches. After the turn of the century, there will be a ship, and it will carry every name on that list to a colony none of them chose. My son will be a line in a ledger. Do you understand? A line. In a ledger."

Cyrus understood ledgers. He was one.

He looked down at his hands — diver's son's hands, scarred at the knuckles, steady on rope and oar and ledger pen alike. Three years ago he had signed those hands over to the Meridian Trading Company for bread and a bunk and the right to keep breathing in Saltcross. The company fed him. The company clothed him. The company had taught him that the safest man in any room was the one nobody remembered seeing enter it. And in exchange he had surrendered the only currency he'd ever truly owned: the truth of what he knew.

And now the truth sat in this lamplit room wearing a wren-sharp gaze, invoking a debt three years old.

"You remember," Marta said quietly. "The night before they took your papers."

He remembered. The storm out of the southwest, the ferry swamped at the bar, two boys in the water and Cyrus diving off the net-house steps because standing on the shore watching was unthinkable. He remembered hauling Tomas Ferreira up the pilings with his lungs full of fire, and Marta wrapping them both in sailcloth, and her saying, *You will always have a harbor here, whatever the world decides about the rest of us.* And him saying — young, stupid, honest — *Anything you ever need of me. On my name, I swear it.*

On my name. He hadn't known then what a thin thing a name was, or how much a company could weigh against it.

"I remember," he said.

"I am not asking you to lie for us again," Marta said. "Lies are small coins; they spend fast. I'm asking for something else." She reached beneath the loose board by the hearth — he knew that board, had slept above it — and drew out a folded paper, oilcloth-wrapped, and put it in his hands. It was a chart. Hand-drawn, careful, annotated in her cramped script: the channel past the chain buoy, the patrol lines, the tide windows, the blind spot behind the breakwater where a rowing boat could sit like a floating log.

"Tomas must be off that hulk before the year turns," she said. "And it cannot be me, and it cannot be any boat from the fishing fleet — they're watched, every hull numbered. But a company clerk," and her eyes were terrible and gentle at once, "a company clerk knows the tide-ledgers. Knows which hours the patrols stand down. Knows the water better than the men paid to guard it."

"Marta—"

"On your name," she said. "You gave it to me in peacetime, when giving it cost nothing. I am collecting now, in wartime, and God forgive me for the asking — but I will ask. I have earned the asking."

The lamp burned steady. In the loft overhead, a floorboard creaked and went silent — Joaquim, listening, learning invisibility from the dark.

Cyrus thought of the census taker's pen moving across the page. Of Halloway's dry voice: *You notice things, Yarrow. That's rare. That's useful.* Of the harbor clock he could hear faintly even now, striking the hour, keeping time on a year that was running out.

Loyalty to the company had kept him fed. It had kept him nothing else.

"When?" he said.

"The next fog," Marta said. "There's always a next fog. Bring me the tide-ledger times, and bring yourself, and bring the promise you made." She covered his hands with hers, hard and warm. "Bring my son home, Cyrus. Whatever it costs."

Outside, the fog was thickening over the harbor, and far out in the black water, unseen, the *Corvina* rode at anchor with a boy aboard who was running out of year.
