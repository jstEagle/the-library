## 3. A Harbor for the Clock

The tide-ledger was a book of small lies by nature. Every entry in it was a guess dressed as a fact — the height of water, the hour of slack, the weight of what came ashore — and everyone at Meridian understood that the truth of the harbor lived somewhere between the lines. Cyrus had learned that in his first week. What he learned now, pen poised over the December pages, was how easily a lie could be dressed back into a fact.

He worked by lamplight in the counting room, long after Halloway had locked the strongbox and gone home to his wife and his cold supper. The ink had to match. That was the first discipline. The company's iron-gall ink darkened with age, so he brewed a fresh batch and let it oxidize on a saucer until it sat the same brown-black as October's entries, then November's, then the last week of December. He practiced on scrap paper until his hand stopped trembling.

The entries themselves were simple enough. A condemned hulk called the *Vigilant* lay at anchor beyond the mole, slated for breaking after New Year, her holds emptied of everything but rats and, since Tuesday, one boy of sixteen named Tomas Ferreira. The hulk kept no boat of her own. Anyone visiting her would need a tender, and every tender crossing the harbor after dark passed within hail of the patrol skiff, and every patrol logged its rounds against the tide-ledger's times.

So Cyrus did not erase anything. He added. A fog warning here — *visibility poor off Coal Quay, patrol recalled early*. A false slack there, shifting the safe window past midnight. He wrote in the clerk's cramped hand, which he could do better than Halloway himself, because Cyrus had spent three years copying that hand and thinking about nothing else while he did it. Invisibility, it turned out, was a kind of apprenticeship.

*December 30th. Fog dense from ebb. Patrol stood down 11:40.*

He blotted the line, closed the ledger, and sat listening to the building settle around him. Somewhere below, the harbor clock began its quarter-chime, each stroke falling through the floorboards like a stone dropped down a well.

Three days left in the year. Three days left in Tomas Ferreira's life as a free boy on English soil, if you could call Saltcross soil, which was mostly ash and fish scale. After the turn, the hulk would be towed out and her passengers scattered to whatever ports would take declared enemies of the crown, which was to say none, which was to say a prison hulk farther out, farther south, farther from Marta Ferreira and the net-house where she had once pulled a drowned boy's lungs clear and asked nothing but that he live.

Cyrus had promised her something in peacetime, before the war made promises dangerous. He had been fourteen and coughing seawater onto her floor, and she had held his head and said, *you owe me nothing*, and he had said, *I know*, because even then he knew which silences kept people safe.

The promise had been wordless. It had taken three years and a census taker to make him hear it.

---

Halloway knew something was wrong the way he knew most things: not by any single fact, but by the accumulation of small wrongnesses that other men mistook for weather.

The boy Yarrow had volunteered for the tide-ledger. In six years, no indentured hand had ever volunteered for the tide-ledger. And the boy's entries were flawless — too flawless, corrected where corrections should have shown, dates aligned with a neatness that suggested rehearsal rather than habit. Halloway had spent thirty years reading ledgers the way priests read scripture, and scripture copied by a guilty man has a particular shine to it.

There was also the matter of the *Vigilant*. Twice this week Yarrow had found reasons to be on the north quay at dusk. Twice the patrol skiff had reported fog that the harbor master's own instruments disputed. And once — this was the detail that sat in Halloway's chest like a swallowed hook — once the boy had looked up from the counter when the name Ferreira crossed the morning manifest, and his face had done nothing at all, which is the hardest face of all to fake.

On the thirtieth of December, Halloway stayed late without announcing it. He sat in the shadowed end of the counting room with an unlit pipe and watched Cyrus work by lamplight, watched the boy dip his pen, hesitate, and write.

"You've a steady hand," Halloway said.

Cyrus did not jump. That, too, was wrong; boys jumped. "Thank you, sir."

"Steadier than mine." Halloway struck a match, thought better of it, and set the pipe down unlit. "Come here."

Cyrus came. He moved like a man walking into deep water who had already decided to drown rather than swim back. Halloway opened the tide-ledger to the December pages and laid his finger under the fog entry of the thirtieth.

"Tell me about this fog."

"There was fog, sir."

"There was haze." Halloway turned the page. "There was haze on the twenty-ninth as well. And yet the patrol reports stand clear on both nights, and your ledger says otherwise. One error is a mistake, Yarrow. Two errors are a policy."

The lamplight caught the boy's eyes, and Halloway saw what he had half hoped never to see: not fear, exactly, but something older and more settled than fear. A decision already made.

"I'll ask you plainly," Halloway said. "You can tell me what you're doing, or I can take this ledger to the harbor master and let him ask. Those are the choices. There isn't a third one, though I expect you've already imagined one."

For a long moment the clock ticked between them, counting down the old year in its patient, indifferent way.

"The Ferreira boy," Cyrus said quietly. "Tomas. He's aboard the *Vigilant*. They mean to deport him with the rest after New Year. His mother sheltered me when I was drowning, and nobody else in this town so much as looked at me." He lifted his chin. "That's my confession, sir. Write it down however you like."

"And your betrayal?"

"That's yours to choose. Not mine."

Halloway looked at him for a long time. Then he took up the pen, and Cyrus's heart went still as ballast — until Halloway wrote beneath the false fog entry: *Confirmed. Visibility nil.* He initialed it in his own cramped hand.

"Midnight tomorrow," Halloway said, closing the ledger. "The patrol stands down at the turn of the year. Every man jack of them will be watching the fireworks on the point instead of the water." He stood, buttoned his coat. "If you're going to be a fool, Yarrow, be a punctual fool. And if anyone asks me, I saw nothing but a clerk correcting his own arithmetic."

"Sir—"

"Don't thank me." Halloway paused at the door, his back turned. "I spent nine years in a counting house in Cork before they marched me here. I know what it is to owe someone who isn't owed by the company." The door shut behind him.

Cyrus stood alone with the lamp and the ledger and the sound of his own breathing, which was the sound a man makes when the ground gives way and he discovers he can swim.

---

Midnight came down on Saltcross like a lid.

The harbor clock began striking the old year out — twelve strokes, slow as a funeral — and Cyrus pulled at the oars with Tomas Ferreira crouched in the bilge of a borrowed tender, wrapped in sacking, breathing through his mouth the way his mother had taught him. The patrol lines lay ahead, two lanterns bobbing off the mole, but the skiffs were empty of attention; along the point, rockets were already going up, red and gold, and the watchmen's faces were turned toward the light like sunflowers.

Between strokes of the clock, strokes of the oars. Cyrus counted them together without meaning to. Seven. Eight. The cold came up through the boards and into his wrists. Nine. A lantern swung toward them and hung there, a yellow eye in the fog — and then a rocket burst over the point and every head on the water turned to watch it die.

Ten. Eleven.

They slipped through the gap between the second skiff and the mooring buoys, close enough that Cyrus could hear a watchman laughing, could smell the rum on the wind off him. The harbor mouth opened ahead, black water, then greyer water, then the long swell of the open channel where a Breton fishing lugger rode at anchor with no papers and no questions.

Twelve.

The last stroke rolled across the water and faded, and in the silence after it, the new century arrived without ceremony, without permission, carrying them out on its first tide.

Behind them, on the quay, a tall figure in a clerk's coat stood at the rail with a signal lantern unlit in his hand. Halloway watched the tender shrink to a speck, then to nothing. Around him the fireworks bloomed and died, bloomed and died, and the town roared its greeting to the year nineteen hundred.

He put the lantern back in its bracket, unburned, and walked home through the celebrating streets with his collar up, composing in his head the careful, honest sentence he would offer the harbor master in the morning: *No irregularities observed.*

It was, he decided, the first entry in years he wouldn't have to correct.

And far out on the black water, rowing toward a lugger, a runaway, and a dawn with no master in it, Cyrus Yarrow heard the clock finish and did not look back.
