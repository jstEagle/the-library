## 4. The Century Turns

They took my name off the ledger on the second of January, in ink that dried darker than any entry Halloway had ever made.

I stood before the counting-room desk while the company clerk drew a line through *Yarrow, Cyrus, indentured, term seven years* and wrote beneath it, in his careful round hand: *Runaway. Papers void. Not to be harbored.* The word *runaway* sat on the page like a nail driven flush. Three years I had kept my head down so far and so long that my neck ached from it, and now the company had made me visible after all — visible as a warning to every other diver's son who might forget what he was.

Halloway did not look up while he wrote. His pen moved with the same patience it had shown on the tide-ledgers, the same patience it had shown the night the patrol boats swept past the darkened packet in the outer roads and he stood on the quay with his lantern unlit at his feet, watching the water where a rowboat slipped between the pilings. He could have called out then. One word, and the year would not have ended the way it ended.

He finished the entry, blotted it, and slid the ledger aside.

"Your wages are forfeit," he said. "Your kit stays. The company retains claim against any future earnings within the province." He recited it like weather. Then he looked up, and for a moment the clerk's face was gone and something older looked out of it — something tired and unhurried, like a man who has finished a long sum and found the remainder interesting. "You'll want to be gone before the harbor master's office opens. There is a packet leaving on the morning tide. Coastal run. They take men without papers if the coin is right."

"I have no coin."

"I know what you have." He opened his desk drawer and set three silver crowns on the wood between us, aligned them with one finger, and pushed them across. "Consider it an accounting error. The ledgers are full of them, if a man knows where to look."

I didn't touch the money right away. We looked at each other across the desk, across three years of silences I had chosen and one silence he had chosen for me, and neither of us explained anything. That was the currency we had always traded in.

"Mr. Halloway," I said. "Why?"

He picked up his pen again, dipped it, and found the next line of whatever column he was tending.

"The clock was wrong," he said. "It's been wrong since the company hung it. Somebody ought to be standing under the right time when it strikes." He waved the pen toward the door without looking. "Go. The tide doesn't argue."

I took the crowns. My hand didn't shake until I reached the street, and then it shook only a little, and only once.

* * *

Marta met me at the net-house in the gray hour, when the fog came in off the roads and made the town into a rumor of itself. She had heard already — of course she had; Saltcross heard everything twice, once wrongly and once through Marta Ferreira's kitchen. When I told her the papers were void and I was leaving on the morning packet, she didn't weep and she didn't thank me in the way people thank you when they mean to be done with a debt. She took my face in both her hands, the way she had the night she pulled me out of the surf at eleven years old with salt in my lungs and my father's body still somewhere below the tide-line, and she held me there and studied me like a chart.

"You know what they will call you," she said.

"Runaway."

"And what are you?"

I thought about lying to her, out of habit, out of three years' practice. It would have been easy. It would even have been kind, maybe. But the whole of what I'd done in December had been built on the discovery that some questions can't be left unasked forever, and Marta's eyes were the oldest question I knew.

"Cyrus Yarrow," I said. "Pearl diver's son. Free."

She smiled then — not the small guarded thing she wore in public, but the whole face, the one Tomas had inherited. "Say it again."

"Cyrus Yarrow."

"Louder. The fog is thick."

I said it louder. I said it until it stopped sounding like someone else's name, until it stopped sounding like a confession or an apology, until it sounded like what it was: mine, spoken by its owner, in a town where I had spent three years being *the boy*, *the indenture*, *Meridian's lad*, everything but myself. Somewhere behind the fog the harbor clock struck six, still wrong, still striking the company's hour, and we both laughed at the absurdity of it — two enemies of the crown standing in a condemned net-house, laughing because a boy had remembered his own name.

She pressed a bundle into my hands: bread wrapped in cloth, a letter sealed with wax though she knew I might never find anyone to read it to me in the ports I was bound for, and inside the fold of the cloth, small and cold, a single pearl. Gray, misshapen, worth almost nothing — the kind my father would have laughed at and kept anyway.

"He dove it himself," she said. "Off your father's old grounds, before the war closed them. I was saving it for Tomas's bride-price. I think he'd rather it bought your road."

"Marta—"

"It isn't payment." Her voice went hard, the old hard, the voice that had faced down the census taker on her own step. "Payment ends a thing. This continues it. You carry a piece of this family out into the world, Cyrus Yarrow, and wherever you stand, there is a harbor. Do you understand me? Belonging is not a place. It never was. It's the people who know your name and are glad of it."

Tomas was gone — south on the fishing boat two nights after the escape, bound for cousins in Vessany who owed Marta more than they feared the crown. She told me this with her chin up and her hands steady, and only the whiteness around her knuckles told me what it cost to say it in the past tense. He was safe. He was away. And she stayed, because she would not give Saltcross the satisfaction of flight, because the net-house was hers by deed and grief and stubbornness, and because someone had to be here when the century learned what it was going to be.

"When you can write," she said at the gate, "write to me. When you cannot write, live well and let that be the letter."

* * *

The packet was called the *Cormorant*, which felt like an omen I hadn't asked for — a diving bird, a thief of shells, a thing that feeds beneath the surface and rises into the air when it chooses. She was small, patched at the waterline, rigged carelessly, and beautiful to me in the way that only leaving-vessels are beautiful. Her master took Halloway's three crowns without counting them and pointed at a coil of rope without looking, and by the time the sun cleared the headland I was working my passage forward of the mast, hands on lines I knew better than I knew my own history.

Saltcross shrank behind us. The Meridian warehouse with its painted crest. The quay where Halloway had stood with his dead lantern. The net-house, smoke rising from its chimney, one figure at the door — too far to see a face, close enough to know it was a wave. The harbor clock struck eight as we cleared the point, and I counted the strokes out of habit, and then, deliberately, I stopped counting.

The wind came around with the morning. The coast unrolled ahead of us, gray-green and unknown, towns I couldn't name, work I couldn't imagine, a century nobody could imagine — and none of it owned my papers, because I had no papers. None of it knew my silences, because I had left them all in Saltcross, filed in Halloway's ledgers where they belonged. For the first time since my father's lungs filled with the sea, I was carrying nothing that wasn't mine.

I stood at the bow with the gray pearl in my fist and the wind pulling my hair back like a hand, and I said my name once more, quietly, just to hear it against the noise of the water — and the water answered, the way water does, taking the sound and giving back the future.

Somewhere ahead, past the headlands and the haze, the new century was already waiting. I meant to arrive as myself.
