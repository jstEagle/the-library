## 2. What the Ledger Remembers

The rain came that night the way bad news always came to the harbor: sideways, without apology. Odile stood at the kitchen window of the Voss house with a cup of chicory going cold in his hand and watched the lamplight of the rue des Salins gutter and drown, one by one, all the way down to where the print shop stood. There was no glow of the press there now. No smell of ink riding out over the wet cobbles. Whatever Captain Renard's men had wanted from Céline Aubry's shop, they had taken it in the dark, and the town had pretended not to hear.

He had not slept. Sleep required a certain arrangement with the world, and he had broken that arrangement years ago, the first time he lifted the flap of an envelope that was not addressed to him and read what a harbor clerk had written about his own brother's drowning. After that there was no unseeing. The letters came through his hands—Marguerite's correspondence, the committee's correspondence, the timid petitions of men who would never have signed their names aloud—and Odile read them all, and remembered everything, because forgetting was a luxury his position did not afford.

> *Entry, undated. The clerk Armand Pelletier writes to his cousin in the interior: they have ordered the manifests rewritten for the third quarter. What went out as forty tons goes into the record as twelve. The difference is eating. I do not know who to tell. I am telling you because you are far away.*

That entry was three years old. Pelletier had since been promoted.

Marguerite appeared in the doorway in her dressing gown, gray hair unbound, her eyes going at once to the window and then to him, in that order, because she had spent thirty years reading rooms before she read people. "You heard it too," she said.

"The shop," he said. "Yes."

"They'll come for us tomorrow." She said it the way she announced platform resolutions—flatly, so that no one could mistake it for fear. "Renard wants paper. All of it. A town that keeps no records is a town that never happened."

Odile set down the cup. His hands were steady. He had trained them to be steady the way other men trained their voices. "Then we should decide what paper exists," he said, "before someone decides for us."

* * *

In the morning the requisition notices went up on the customs house door like posters for a fair that had been cancelled: *By order of the military governor, all civic registers, dock ledgers, court filings, and correspondence of public bodies are hereby seized for inventory.* Beneath the official stamp, Renard himself had added a line in red ink, and Odile read it twice to be sure: *Private papers bearing on matters of state security are included under this order.*

*Matters of state security.* He turned the phrase over on the walk back, testing its edges. It could mean munitions manifests. It could mean Marguerite's speeches. It could mean, if Renard suspected—and the captain had eyes like a man counting change—a book bound in gray cloth that lived, since yesterday evening, inside the spare tire well of the Voss motorcar, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the jack.

They let him drive through the checkpoint without stopping him. That was the point of Odile Almeida: doors opened for drivers the way weather happened to them, without malice or attention. A soldier glanced at his cap, his gloves, his blank and serviceable face, and waved the car past the crates of seized records stacked on the quay like grave goods.

It was there, waiting while two lieutenants argued over a dolly, that he saw them. A clerk had left a folder open on top of a crate—one gust of harbor wind away from scattering—and Odile looked down only meaning to look, meaning nothing, and saw his own handwriting looking back at him.

Not his handwriting. His *transcription*. The careful schoolroom script he used when Marguerite dictated testimony for the committee's files, the depositions they'd gathered two winters ago from the families along the quay. He knew the page by its crease, by the water stain in the corner where he'd set it down too near a window. *Testimony of Lucien Ferrand, docker, aged nineteen, regarding the night of 14 March.* And below it, in an army clerk's indifferent hand, a catalog number, and the word **RETAINED**.

Lucien Ferrand, who had described the patrol, the lanterns, the sound his neighbor made when they were done asking their questions. Lucien, whose statement Odile had carried to the courthouse himself, whose name he had entered into a record he had believed—as he had believed in rain, in tides—in the simple physics of paper finding its way to justice.

The army had that paper now. And the only other copy of anything—the names, the dates, the shape of what had been done here—was wrapped in oilcloth behind him, riding in the dark of the tire well like a heart inside a chest.

* * *

She found him at the market that afternoon. He should have known; grief has a way of finding the man who is trying not to be findable.

"Almeida." Marie Ferrand stood in front of him with a basket of nothing in her hands, nineteen herself perhaps, or twenty, with her brother's same sharp bones and none of his caution. She did not lower her voice. Nobody lowered their voice anymore; the soldiers had taught the town that whispering sounded guilty. "The courthouse won't take my petition. They said the records are under military seal. They said there is no record of any complaint by a Lucien Ferrand."

"There was a record," Odile said.

"I know there was. You took it." Her eyes were dry, which was worse than tears. Dry eyes meant someone had cried until there was nothing left, and then kept standing. "I watched you carry it out of our kitchen like it was made of glass. So you tell me, Monsieur Almeida—you tell me where a piece of glass goes when the whole building comes down."

There it was, then. The question he had spent three days not asking himself. He could say: the committee is scattered, Marguerite is watched, I am a driver, I drive. He could say: what survives, survives; what is seized is lost; go home, girl, and live quietly, which is the whole of what I know how to teach anyone.

Or he could open the door he had kept shut his entire adult life.

"Come to the house at seven," he said. "Ask for the chauffeur. Don't say my name again in the street."

> *Entry, this date. They have taken the testimony of L.F., which I helped to gather, and called it nothing. A record can be stolen but it cannot be unborn. Everything written is still true somewhere. The somewhere must be me.*

* * *

Céline Aubry received them in the back room of the ruined print shop, among the splinters of the case and the type scattered across the floor like black seeds, and listened to Odile's proposal with her arms folded and ink still ghosted under her fingernails where the soldiers hadn't thought to look.

"You want me to print a pamphlet," she said, "with no press, no type worth the name, and every sheet of paper in this town under requisition."

"Yes."

"And when Renard finds out—and he will find out, he has a nose like a customs dog—what do you imagine happens to the widow Aubry?"

"Something has already happened to the widow Aubry," Odile said quietly. "They burned your press. The question is whether they also get to burn what it said."

For a long moment she looked at the wreckage of her trade. Then she crossed the room, pried up a floorboard near the cold stove, and drew out a bundle wrapped in sacking: a small hand press, the old proofing platen her husband had kept for private work, and three trays of type he had hidden the day the war started, saying, *A printer who owns nothing is a man with nothing to confiscate.* She had laughed at him then. She was not laughing now.

"My husband hid these against exactly this day," Céline said. "I had begun to think the day would never have the courtesy to arrive."

* * *

Anselme came last, because Anselme was hardest to convince and easiest to find. They met in the boat shed beyond the breakwater where the deserter had been living since the armistice on fish and charity, a big slow man with a soldier's shoulders and a boy's habit of flinching at boots. Odile laid it out plainly: a run to the rail junction, sixty kilometers, a car that no one stopped because no one ever stopped it, and a package that had to reach a newspaper office in the capital before Renard's version of the harbor reached it first.

"You're asking me to go back into the war," Anselme said.

"I'm asking you to end one," Odile said. "Yours."

The deserter turned a fish knife over in his hands, considering. "And if they stop the car?"

Odile thought of the tire well, the oilcloth, the weight of every name he had ever carried. "Then it stops being invisible," he said. "All of it. Including me."

Marie Ferrand was waiting at the Voss house when he returned, sitting very straight on the kitchen chair as though she had been practicing it. On the table between them Odile laid the ledger open at last—not to hide it, not to memorize it in secret and slide it away, but flat, in the light, its gray cloth binding plain to see.

"This is your brother's testimony," he said. "This is everyone's. If we do this, every name in this book becomes a target. Mine first. But yours too, and Céline's, and a man named Anselme whom you must never ask about." He looked at her, and found that his hands, for once, were not steady at all, and that he did not mind. "So before I write another word in it, I need to know that someone besides me is willing to be remembered."

Marie put her finger on the page, on her brother's name, and did not take it away.

It was nearly midnight when the telephone rang in the hall—wrong hour, wrong ring entirely—and Odile lifted the receiver to hear Marguerite's voice, level as a spirit gauge, saying only: "They've taken me at the station, Odile. A Captain Renard. He says he would like to discuss what the town remembers." A pause, and then, in the tone she used for closing a debate: "Don't come for me. Come *after* me. There's a difference, and it matters."

The line went dead. Outside, at the gate, a soldier struck a match, and in the brief orange light Odile could see the whole street already filling with trucks.
