## 3. Ninety-Six Hours

**Odile**

The knock came at four in the morning, and Odile knew before he opened the door that the world had tilted.

Two soldiers stood on the step of Marguerite Voss's house, their breath ghosting in the cold, and behind them, in the street, a staff car idled with its lamps burning yellow through the fog off the harbor. Captain Renard sat inside it like a chess piece waiting to be moved.

"Madame Voss is requested for questioning," the taller soldier said. "Regarding seditious materials."

"She's asleep," Odile said. His voice came out flat and steady, and he marveled at it from somewhere far away, the way a man might watch his own hands work without him. "She's an old woman."

"Then she can be questioned when she wakes." The soldier's eyes were young and unhappy. "In the citadel."

They did not drag her. That was the worst of it. Marguerite came down the stairs fully dressed — she had heard the trucks too; she had been awake since three, Odile would learn later, listening to the engines idling along the quay — and she buttoned her coat with hands that trembled only slightly, as though she were dressing for church in bad weather rather than for whatever Renard intended. At the door she paused beside Odile and pressed something small into his palm: her reading glasses, folded.

"Hold these," she said, loud enough for the soldiers to hear. "I'll want them back by Sunday."

Then she was in the car, and the fog swallowed it, and Odile stood on the step with her glasses in his fist until the cold made his knuckles ache.

* * *

**Renard**

Captain Étienne Renard did not sleep well in requisitioned houses. The beds were too soft, the curtains too heavy, the whole town too quiet in the way of places that had learned to lie still around soldiers. He sat at a desk that had belonged to some harbor clerk and turned over the inventory sheets again — every civic record in the town accounted for, seized, crated, catalogued. Every record except one.

A driver had signed for deliveries to the printer's shop eleven times between March and September. Eleven times. And Céline Aubry, the widow who ran the press, had produced no invoices, no correspondence, nothing but blank paper and a face like a shut door. Records did not move through a town like this one without leaving fingerprints. Someone had been keeping a book.

He had taken the old suffragist not because he believed she held the ledger herself — women like Voss were too clever for that — but because towns like this one had a particular weakness. They loved their grandmothers. Somewhere in the dark there was a man with careful hands and invisible habits, and men like that always came out of hiding when someone they served was in a cell.

Renard poured himself a glass of the clerk's wine and waited for morning. He was very good at waiting.

* * *

**Odile**

By dawn the pamphlet was half set.

Céline worked the composing stick with her jaw tight and her sleeves rolled, sliding type into the chase letter by letter while Anselme stood at the shuttered window with his ear cocked toward the street. The print shop smelled of ink and oil and cold iron, and the single lamp threw all their shadows huge against the rafters, so that they seemed a company of giants bent over a small bright page.

*THE HARBOR RECORD,* the masthead read. *An account of what was done here, in the names of those who cannot speak.*

Ninety-six hours, Anselme had calculated. That was how long before the morning train south, before the pamphlets could scatter beyond any captain's reach. Ninety-six hours to print eight hundred copies, distribute them in flour sacks and fish crates and hymnals across three parishes, and get the ledger itself onto the train in Céline's cousin's luggage. Ninety-six hours during which Marguerite Voss sat in the citadel because of them.

"We go to Renard tonight," Odile said.

Céline's hands stopped moving. "And give him exactly what he wants."

"I trade myself for her. I confess to everything — the ledger is mine, I wrote it, I copied it, I carried it. She knew nothing." He said it simply, the way you state a fare or a delivery address, and found that beneath the terror there was something almost like relief. He had spent six years being no one. Tonight he would finally be someone, and the price was only himself. "You print. Anselme holds the door. Nothing I say in that room matters if the train leaves tomorrow morning."

"Odile." Céline set down the composing stick. Her husband had died at this press — a press accident, the official record said, though everyone knew the gendarmes' boots had done the official record's work — and she looked at Odile now the way she must have looked at that press every morning: at a thing that could crush her and that she refused to stop touching. "If you walk into that citadel, you may not walk out."

"I know."

"Then say the rest of it. Say what happens to the ledger if you don't come back."

So he said it. He gave her the hiding place, the spare tire well, the oilcloth wrapping, the key to Marguerite's back door. He gave her the names in the margins — which grievances could be told openly and which silences still protected living people. He handed over six years of his own hand, letter by memorized letter, and watched it become hers.

* * *

**Renard**

The message arrived at dusk, delivered by a dockworker's boy who vanished into the alleys before the guard could question him. One line, written in a small precise hand:

*The ledger exists. Come alone to the Aubry print shop at midnight and you may have its author. Release Madame Voss first.*

Renard read it twice, smiling. So. The invisible man wished to be seen.

He released Voss within the hour — an old woman in the fog was worth more to him outside a cell than in it, walking free toward wherever her people gathered. He put two men on her trail at a discreet distance and took six more to the print shop, because a captain who went truly alone to a rendezvous was a captain asking to be a footnote. He told himself the trade was sound. A ledger's author, in custody, could lead him to every copy, every accomplice, every name.

What he did not tell himself was why the thought of the small precise handwriting pleased him so much. He had spent two years erasing records. It was strangely moving, he thought, to meet one that had volunteered.

* * *

**Odile**

Midnight smelled of lamp oil.

Odile noticed it as he crossed the empty square — the sweet, heavy reek of it pooled under the print shop door, soaked into the sawdust of the floor he could see through the gap. Renard had no intention of taking prisoners. The captain meant to burn the evidence and everyone attached to it, and Odile had walked into the pyre carrying the one thing that mattered.

Good, he thought, with a clarity that frightened him. Let them think the truth dies here.

Through the window he could see Céline still at the press, feeding sheet after sheet, the pages stacking up damp and black behind her like fallen birds. Anselme stood at the door with a crowbar and the flat calm of a man who had deserted one army already and did not intend to salute another. Eight hundred copies. Six hundred and forty done. The train left at 6:40.

At 12:20 the soldiers came.

Odile stepped out of the shadows with his hands visible and his heart going like a piston. "I'm the one you want," he said to Renard, whose face in the lamplight was long and gray and almost gentle. "The ledger is mine. Let the others go."

Behind him, inside, the press never stopped. Clack, clack, clack — the sound of a heart that refused to quit, and Renard's eyes flicked toward it, and something in his expression curdled.

"Burn it," Renard said quietly.

The first lantern went through the window, and then the world was orange and roaring, and Anselme was shouting, and soldiers were dragging Odile backward by both arms while the shop filled with light — beautiful, terrible light, the light of everything he'd hidden finally blazing where anyone could see. Through the flames he saw Céline emerge coughing, her arms full of the finished stack, and Anselme wrenching open the back door into the alley, and the last sheets going out into the night in flour sacks and fish crates, passed hand to hand down the dark street like water down a chain of buckets.

Six hundred and forty copies. Enough.

Renard watched the fire eat the press and felt, for one unaccountable moment, that he had lost something instead of won. Then he turned to the thin man in his grip, whose face was lit gold by the burning shop and who was watching the alley, not the fire, with an expression of terrible hope.

"Your name," Renard said. "For the record."

"Odile Almeida," he said. "Witness for the town of —"

The captain's fist ended the sentence, but not the words. Words, once loose, do not come home.

* * *

**Odile**

They put him in a cell below the citadel, where he could hear the sea through a grate and count the bells of the 6:40 train as it pulled out of the station southbound, eight hundred copies riding in the luggage van toward a country that did not yet know what was coming.

He pressed Marguerite's glasses flat against his chest and smiled in the dark.

Somewhere above him, a telephone rang, and rang, and rang — Paris calling, Renard's superiors asking questions about fires and trains and an old woman walking free — and Odile closed his eyes and began, silently, to compose the testimony he would give when they finally brought him into the light.
