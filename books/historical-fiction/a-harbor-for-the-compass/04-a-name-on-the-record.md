## 4. A Name on the Record

The pamphlets came back to us the way gulls return to a harbor after a storm—first one or two, then whole flocks of them, arriving from towns whose names Odile had only ever seen stamped in faded ink on envelopes he had steamed open at Marguerite Voss's kitchen table.

*Le Témoin*, they called it. The Witness. Sixteen pages, printed on paper Céline had bought from three different suppliers so no single merchant could trace the order, set in type she had locked away each night like a sleeping child. Someone had carried copies north on the morning train out of Sainte-Aubin while the print shop still smelled of smoke; someone else had passed them hand to hand through market squares and seminary corridors and the smoking rooms of deputies who pretended to be scandalized while reading every line twice.

Now a copy had come home. It lay on Marguerite's table—the same table where Odile had once held letters over a kettle's breath, softening glue until secrets gave themselves up—and it had been annotated in six different hands. Penciled asterisks in the margins. A question mark beside the account of the night the soldiers came to the Rue des Salines. Beside the list of names, someone had written, in a careful schoolteacher's script: *We knew some of this. We did not know all of it. Why did we not know?*

Odile read that question a dozen times. He had no answer that did not begin with himself: because I was invisible, because I thought invisibility was safety, because I believed a ledger hidden in a spare tire well could hold what a whole town refused to say aloud.

He had been wrong about the tire well. He had been right about the ledger.

* * *

The news of Captain Renard reached them by way of Anselme, who still moved through the town like a man walking on river ice, testing each step. He came in through the kitchen garden at dusk, coat collar up, and delivered it the way he delivered everything now—as if reporting weather.

"They've recalled him," he said. "Paris. A commission of inquiry. Three officers and two civilians." He paused, working his jaw. "Two civilians. That's something. That's more than anyone expected."

"And the charges?" Marguerite asked from her chair by the window. She had been released eleven days ago pending trial—released with an apology so thin it might have been written on cigarette paper—and she had spent those days refusing, with great courtesy and iron stubbornness, every offer of refuge from friends in Lyon and Marseille. *I will not run from my own town*, she kept saying. *I ran from nothing when they took me; I will run from nothing now.*

"Abuse of requisition powers," Anselme said. "The seizure of civic records without authority. And—" he glanced at Odile, and his voice gentled, which for Anselme was almost unbearable to witness— "the burning of a printing house. With persons inside."

With persons inside. Odile's hands remembered the heat of that night even now, weeks later: the press room glowing orange, Céline's arms full of the last wet signatures, Anselme holding the back door with a body that would not move though the captain's men shouted. They had escaped through the alley with the pamphlets and lost almost everything else—the type, the rollers, the framed portrait of Céline's husband above the case. She had not wept until they were safe, and then only briefly, and then she had said, wiping her eyes with an inky thumb: *He would have liked to go that way. In service of the work.*

Renard would answer for the fire. Whether he answered for the rest—for the dockworkers beaten at the quay, for the girl from the Rue des Salines, for the testimonies sealed into army crates and carted away—would depend on what the inquiry heard, and from whom, and whether the ones who knew dared to stand up in a hall in Paris and say it with their own mouths.

Justice, Odile was learning, was a partial thing. It arrived like tide, covering some ground and leaving other ground exposed and salt-stained. But tide could be argued with. Tide could be witnessed.

* * *

Céline found him in the old print shop a week before he was due to travel.

She had leased a smaller premises two streets over—a narrow shop with a hand press older than the war—but she came back to the burned building sometimes, and lately Odile had noticed she came when he was there, as though his presence made the ruins easier to look at. Together they walked the blackened floor, their boots raising small ghosts of ash.

"The register," she said, without preamble, the way she always spoke when she had decided something. "At the inquiry. Witnesses sign it. Name, town, occupation." She looked at him across the ruined type case. "You've been practicing how you'll say it. Don't think I haven't noticed. You say your name differently than you used to. Before, it was a thing you handed over quickly, like small change. Now you weigh it."

Odile said nothing. The light through the broken roof fell in dusty columns, and in one of them he could see the place where the ledger had lived, inside the wheel well of Marguerite's car, through all those months when he had believed a book could be safer than a man.

"You could stay home," Céline said. "Marguerite will testify. Anselme will testify, God help him, a deserter standing up in front of officers—I told him he's either the bravest fool or the foolishest brave man in France. Your testimony is already in the pamphlet. Every word. You could let the pages speak for you."

"I know."

"And?"

"And a page can be dismissed." His voice came out low and even, surprising him. "A page can be called anonymous slander. A page has no face to remember, no voice to doubt or believe. Renard taught me that. He didn't fear the ledger—he feared that somewhere there were people willing to attach themselves to it." He turned a charred piece of type between his fingers, the letter *O*, half-melted into something like a closed eye. "Truth needs names attached. Otherwise it's just rumor with good posture."

Céline smiled then, tired and fierce. "Her husband used to say something similar about signatures," she said. "'A print without a name is a shout in the dark. A name is a lamp.'" She touched his shoulder, briefly, the way one touches a doorframe entering a house one means to live in. "Then we go together. All three of us. Let Paris see what a harbor town looks like when it stops hiding."

* * *

The morning they left, Marguerite stood on her step in her best gray coat, the one she had worn to every suffrage meeting since before the war, and would not be persuaded to stay behind.

"I'm released, not retired," she said, settling herself into the motorcar as though taking possession of it. "And it is still, whatever the army may believe, my car. Odile—you'll drive. Some things should not change entirely. It steadies the nerves."

They drove north through country just turning toward autumn, hedgerows gold-edged, orchards heavy, the world performing its ordinary abundance as if no records had ever been seized, no shops burned, no women jailed for saying *vote*. Odile drove with both hands on the wheel and the pamphlet's sixteen pages folded in his breast pocket, against his heart, like a second heartbeat.

Paris received them with rain and indifference, its boulevards streaming, its great buildings wearing the victory like a borrowed coat that didn't quite fit. The inquiry sat in a hall near the Palais de Justice, and outside it a knot of journalists and onlookers had gathered beneath dripping umbrellas, because *Le Témoin* had made the word Sainte-Aubin mean something now, and everyone wanted to see the people behind the word.

Inside, the hall was oak and echo and cold light falling through high windows. Clerks moved along benches with registers under their arms. Somewhere at the front, behind a raised desk, the commissioners arranged their papers with the deliberate slowness of men who wished to seem impartial.

Anselme went first, chin up, hands visibly empty. Then Céline, smelling faintly of ink no ruin could wash out of her.

Then it was Odile's turn.

The clerk held out the register—an actual book, leather-bound, its pages ruled in careful lines—and Odile felt the whole strange arc of his life contract to the width of one ruled line. The steamed envelopes. The ledger in the dark of the tire well. The girl begging on the dock. The firelight on Céline's face. All of it funneled down toward a pen and a space barely wide enough for four words.

His hand did not shake. That was the miracle of it. He wrote slowly anyway, giving each letter its full weight:

**Odile Almeida — Sainte-Aubin — witness.**

Not driver. Not secretary. Not the quiet young man who was always around and never counted. Witness. He set down the pen and looked up, and found Céline watching him with her fierce tired smile, and Anselme standing straight as a mast, and beyond them the hall full of strangers who did not yet know his name but would, by evening, in every newspaper that mattered.

The bailiff called the session to order. Rain silvered the high windows.

And from the corridor behind them, unhurried, accompanied by the click of military heels on marble, came a voice Odile would have known anywhere, even burned, even drowned, even years from now—

Captain Renard had arrived to hear the witnesses.
