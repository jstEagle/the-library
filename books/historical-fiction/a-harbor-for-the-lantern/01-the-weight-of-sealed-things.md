## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The packet weighed less than a Bible and more than a man's life, though no one passing Wendell Galloway on the Rue des Fontaines could have said he carried anything at all. That was the point. That had always been the point.

He walked the way a man walks to buy bread. Not too fast—speed drew the eye. Not too slow—hesitation drew it faster. His coat was the grey of three winters ago, patched at the elbow in a way that suggested poverty but not desperation, and beneath it, sewn flat against his ribs, the diplomatic packet sat like a second heartbeat. He did not think about it. Thinking about it made a man touch his side, and touching his side made him memorable, and being remembered was the one sin from which there was no absolution.

His rules were three, and he recited them the way other men recited prayers.

Never read what you carry. Never linger where you deliver. Never be remembered by anyone.

He had learned them from Tomas Reyes, who had learned them from a courier drowned before either of them came to the work, and who used to say them over breakfast with such cheer that they sounded like jokes. *Wendell, my friend, you are a ghost with excellent posture.* Two years since anyone had said anything like that to him. Two years since the northern ferry went down with forty-one souls, Tomas Reyes's name third on the list, alphabetized even in death.

At the corner of the Rue des Fontaines and the Kolonelstraat, the occupation flag hung from the customs annex, wet and heavy, the way all the flags hung now—as if the cloth itself resented the duty. Wendell adjusted his pace to the traffic, joined a knot of fishwives grumbling about the price of oil, and let their noise carry him through the first checkpoint without slowing.

The guard there was new. That was the thing people didn't understand about checkpoints: they weren't walls, they were rhythms. Every post had a tempo—the shift change at six, the tea break at nine, the boredom that set in after noon when a man with a rifle would wave through anything he'd already seen twice. Wendell had memorized this post's rhythm down to the minute. The new guard broke it. He stopped a cart at half past eight, which meant he was either ambitious or frightened, and both kinds got people searched.

Wendell filed the face away—young, chapped hands, boots unpolished, which meant poor, which meant bribable eventually—and took the long way around by the tannery rather than risk him twice. The detour cost him eleven minutes. Eleven minutes was nothing. Eleven minutes was everything. A courier's whole career was an argument for taking the long way, every time, forever.

The tannery lane smelled the way it always smelled, like something dying slowly and refusing to finish, and Wendell breathed through his mouth and thought about nothing in particular. That was another discipline. Fear wanted to talk. It wanted to rehearse disasters, run simulations, whisper *what if they stop you, what if they weigh the packet, what if the seal cracks in the damp.* He let the voice run the way you let a dog bark behind a fence—noted it, kept walking. Competence was armor, but silence was the forge where the armor was made. He had not survived four years of this by being brave. He had survived by being boring, punctual, forgettable, and very, very good.

* * *

The Chancellery of the Last Loyal Port occupied a building that had once housed a shipping insurance concern, and it still looked like one—brass fittings, marble worn to a soft shine, a plaque out front that someone had neglected to pry off. Inside, the republic conducted its business in three rooms and a corridor, with a staff that had shrunk each autumn like a tree preparing for a winter it understood better than its masters did.

Wendell delivered packets to the second room, always the second room, to a clerk named Verhoeven who had been old when the republic was young and who now spent his days stamping documents in an office heated by a stove that ate more coal than honesty.

"You're late," Verhoeven said, not looking up.

"Tannery lane."

"Trouble at the Kolonelstraat post?"

"New guard."

That made the old man look up. His eyes were the pale, watery blue of ice over deep water, and they went through Wendell the way they always did—a professional inventory, coat, hands, face, the flatness of the pockets. Satisfied, he held out his palm for the packet.

Wendell worked it free of the inner seam, laid it on the desk, and stepped back. Rule one lived in that step back. He did not watch seals being broken. He did not see what was inside. Whatever the paper said—whoever it condemned, whatever it surrendered—he was the man who carried, not the man who knew, and the difference between those two men was the difference between a long life and a short interrogation.

Verhoeven broke the wax with a bone folder, ran his eyes down the first page, and Wendell saw something he had never seen in four years of deliveries.

He saw the old man's hands shake.

Not much. A tremor, fine as a hair, caught and mastered. But Verhoeven had stamped surrender terms, retreat orders, the evacuation manifests for two islands and a peninsula, and Wendell had never once seen those hands admit to anything.

"Wait," Verhoeven said.

"I have a return—"

"*Wait.*"

Wendell waited. Outside, a patrol went by, boots striking in that unhurried cadence the occupiers favored, the sound of men who had decided the war was already won and were merely collecting it. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the harbor gong sounded the quarter hour, muffled by fog coming off the water in the grey way it came every morning now, as if the sea itself were trying to hide the port from what was coming for it.

Verhoeven read the whole packet. It took him a long time. When he finished, he sat with both hands flat on the desk, as if holding it down in a wind only he could feel.

"There will be a reply," he said finally. "But not today. Come tomorrow, before the tide. And Galloway—" He stopped. He was choosing words the way a man in a minefield chooses footprints. "You've carried for the Foreign Office since the second year of the war."

"I carry," Wendell said. "I don't catalogue."

"Yes." A thin smile, gone as fast as it came. "Well. Catalogue this much: the channel office is closing. The last of the diplomatic correspondence goes north on Thursday's ship, and after that there is no Foreign Office. There is no one to carry to." He slid the packet into a drawer and locked it, and his hands were steady again, which somehow was worse. "What you brought me this morning may be the last dispatch the republic ever sends out of this harbor. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Wendell understood the shape of it before the words were done. He had been watching the shape assemble itself for months—the ministries emptying, the pay arriving late, the checkpoints multiplying like frost across a windowpane. You can know a thing is dying and still be surprised by the announcement.

"No," Wendell said. "Tell me plainly."

Verhoeven studied him for a moment, and something in the old clerk's face shifted—from the professional blankness to something older and tireder, the face he must have worn in the first war, or whichever war had taught him that plain speech was a luxury purchased with blood.

"The government in exile has negotiated recognition abroad. There are men in this city—men with titles and harborside offices—who have been selling berth space on evacuation ships to the occupiers' agents. Names. Routes. The schedules of every loyal vessel left to us. This packet is the proof, bound for the free press and the foreign ministry together." He unlocked the drawer again, just to look at the sealed thing lying there, and closed it. "If it arrives, there may be a reckoning, someday, somewhere. If it doesn't, then the republic doesn't fall, because it has already fallen, and no one will ever be able to say when."

The stove ticked. The fog pressed at the tall windows.

"Why tell me?" Wendell asked. It was not a question he should have asked. Rule one existed precisely so that he would never need to ask it.

Verhoeven looked at him with those ice-water eyes, and for a moment Wendell thought the old man wouldn't answer. Then he said, quietly, "Because Reyes vouched for you once, in writing, and I keep faith with the recommendations of dead men. And because tomorrow, when you come for the reply, you'll want to know what you're carrying. Not its contents. Its weight." He returned to his stamping. "Tomorrow. Before the tide. Don't take the tannery lane; they're watching it now."

Wendell left the way he'd come, past the brass fittings and the worn marble and the plaque no one had pried off, and out into the street where the fog was lifting and the city went about the business of surviving another day under flags it hadn't chosen.

* * *

He should have gone straight home. Home was Mrs. Okonkwo's boarding house on the Zeeschaal, a narrow brick building that smelled of cardamom and coal smoke, where a hot dinner appeared at seven whether the world was ending or not. He should have eaten, slept, risen before dawn, and walked back to the Chancellery with his face arranged into its usual nothing.

Instead he stood at the mouth of the harbor road for a long moment, watching the fishing boats come in through the last of the fog, and felt the morning rearrange itself around him.

Four years of carrying. He had smuggled retreat orders through burning towns and armistice drafts through streets where a wrong accent was a death sentence. He had memorized the faces of two hundred guards and forgotten, deliberately, the faces of everyone he'd ever delivered for. He had done it all on the understanding that the work meant something—that the sealed things he carried were threads holding a fabric together, and that as long as the threads moved, the fabric lived.

Now an old man with shaking hands had told him he was carrying the last thread.

And underneath the dread, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he refused to examine, another thought stirred, unwelcome as a stranger at a funeral: if the Foreign Office was closing, if Thursday's ship was the last going north—

Then the routes north were about to matter more than they had ever mattered. And everyone who wanted to control them—traitorous harbormasters, occupying officers, desperate exiles—would be watching the harbor with everything they had, in exactly the window of days when he would have to walk through it one final time.

A gull screamed somewhere above the breakwater. The fog finished lifting, all at once, the way it did, and the harbor lay open and bright and exposed, every mast and hull visible from the hills, from the customs house, from the eyes that were surely already counting ships.

Wendell turned up his collar and started home along the seawall, taking the long way, every time, forever.

He did not see the woman standing at the fish market in a coat cut in a northern style, watching the boarding-house district with the fixed patience of someone who had crossed open water in winter and was not going to be turned away by anything so small as an occupied city. He did not see her notice him, mark the direction he walked, and begin, unhurried, to follow.

But she had letters with her, written in a hand he would know anywhere.

And by nightfall, his rulebook would fail him completely.
