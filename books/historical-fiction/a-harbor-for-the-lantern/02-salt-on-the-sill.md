## 2. Salt on the Sill

The boarding house on Candlemaker's Row had a door that stuck in winter, and Wendell Galloway had learned to open it with his shoulder, his hip, and a particular twist of the wrist that made no sound. He used all three now, coming home through the last grey light of an afternoon that had never quite committed to being a day.

Home. He caught himself using the word and corrected it. *Lodging.* A man who carried what he carried did not get to have a home. A home was a thing that could be searched, seized, burned, or held over your head while men in garrison coats asked you questions with their hands. What he had was a room on the third floor with a view of a chimney, a lock he'd installed himself, and a floorboard under the bed that lifted.

He stamped the salt off his boots on the mat—Mrs. Okonkwo would know if he didn't, would know it in some way that bypassed her famous deafness—and climbed the stairs.

She was in the hall below, as she always was at this hour, winding wool. She didn't look up. She never looked up when he came in, which was its own kind of courtesy, the way she never looked up when any of her boarders came in at odd hours, or left before dawn, or came down smelling of river water they had no honest reason to have been near.

"Supper's at seven," she said, to the wool. "There's fish."

"I may be late."

"There'll be fish at eight, too."

He went up. Behind him he heard the needles click, steady as a clock, and felt—as he always felt, and always resented—the specific sensation of being expected.

* * *

His room smelled of tallow and cold plaster. He set his satchel on the table, checked the seal on the inner packet with a thumbnail, found it unbroken, and allowed himself the day's first full breath. Then he lifted the floorboard, laid the packet in the cavity between joists where the rats had once nested and now, by unspoken treaty, did not, and replaced the board. He put a boot on top of it. The boot was old and bad and nobody would steal it.

Downstairs, the house was doing what the house did at dusk: filling up.

Pip came through the front door like weather, twelve years old, all elbows and news, shedding sleet and somebody else's gossip in equal measure. "Mrs. O, Mrs. O, the customs clerks are striking again, half of them, the ones at the east counter, and there's a man at the Anchor asking after boats to the northern isles, offering silver, real silver—" He stopped when he saw Wendell on the stairs, because Pip always stopped when he saw Wendell, the way dogs stop for a whistle they can't hear but trust.

"Evening, Mr. Galloway."

"Evening, Pip."

"You look like a man who's been walking all day."

"What a remarkable deduction."

"It's not a deduction if I watched you come up the hill." Pip grinned, gap-toothed, entirely too alive for the city he lived in. "Supper's fish. There's always fish when the boats come in brave."

Wendell nodded and continued up, and felt, as he always felt, the boy's eyes on his back like a hand between his shoulder blades. Pip ran messages. Everyone knew that. What everyone didn't know—or pretended not to know, in the manner of this whole street, this whole city, this whole dying republic—was *for whom*, and how far, and past whose windows. Wendell had made a point of never knowing. Knowledge was a debt, and debts got collected, usually from whoever stood nearest when the collectors came.

In the kitchen, Anselm was already installed at the corner of the table, the corner nearest the stove and farthest from the window, because Anselm had spent forty years piloting ships through a harbor full of things that wanted them sunk and still sat like a man expecting torpedoes. He was drinking something amber out of a teacup, because Mrs. Okonkwo disapproved of bottles at the table, and he was doing the thing he did every evening: counting.

"Fourteen," Anselm said, without looking up. "Soldiers on the quay tonight. Fourteen. That's six more than Tuesday."

"You count boots?"

"I count everything. Boots, lanterns, launches, the interval between patrol boats—it was eleven minutes in autumn, it's nine now. They're tightening." He took a sip. "You'd know that, though. You're out in it."

"I keep to my errands."

"Everybody keeps to their errands." Anselm's eyes were bloodshot and quick and missed nothing, which was the trick of him: he performed the drunk, and the performance was good enough for soldiers, and it was even good enough for most of the street, but Wendell had watched him thread a needle at midnight without spilling a drop, and had understood that the drunkenness was a coat Anselm wore the way Wendell wore forgettability. "Errands, errands. Whole city full of errand-runners, and nobody ever asks where the errands go."

"That's rather the point of errands."

Anselm laughed, a wet bark. "Rather the point. Listen to him. Rather the point." He raised the teacup an inch—a toast, or a warning, hard to say. "One day, Mr. Galloway, someone will ask you a question you can't walk away from. And all your errands won't save you."

"They've saved me so far."

"So has luck. And luck, in my experience, runs out at exactly the moment you start believing in it."

Mrs. Okonkwo set down three bowls of fish stew without ceremony, and the conversation died the way conversations died in that kitchen, smothered under the authority of hot food.

* * *

They ate. This was the part Wendell hated most, if hate was the right word for it, and he had never found a better one.

Because the food was good. Because the stew was rich with pepper and the bread was warm and Mrs. Okonkwo pretended not to notice when he took a second piece, and then noticed, and said nothing, and there was more bread the next night too. Because Pip told stories about his day with the total shameless relish of a boy who had never yet lost anything, and Anselm heckled him, and Mrs. Okonkwo said "mind your elbows" to both of them in a voice that had raised children who were grown and gone and possibly dead, though no one asked, because no one asked anything in that house.

Because it was, in every particular except the name, a family.

And because Wendell Galloway sat at the edge of it every evening like a man warming his hands at a fire he refused to acknowledge, and every evening he counted the minutes until he could excuse himself, and every evening Mrs. Okonkwo saw him counting and let him go, and every evening he climbed the stairs and lay awake listening to the sounds of the house settling around other people's lives, and told himself this was safety.

It was safety. That was the unbearable part. It wasn't that he lacked the discipline to stay distant; it was that staying distant required discipline at all, which meant the distance cost him something, which meant—

He shut the thought in the same box as the packet and sat on the lid.

"Wendell." Mrs. Okonkwo, clearing bowls. Not looking at him. "You left your window latch broken again."

"I'll fix it."

"A man should fix what's broken in his own room." She stacked a bowl on a bowl. "Or ask for help fixing it. Either is permitted. Both are rare."

"I manage."

"Mm." She carried the bowls to the basin. "So does the lighthouse. Until the night it doesn't."

* * *

Later, past ten, the knock came. Three soft raps, spaced evenly—not Pip's machine-gun patter, not Anselm's knuckle-drag. Wendell opened his door a hand's width.

Mrs. Okonkwo stood in the hall with a candle and a small plate of seed cake he hadn't asked for and wouldn't eat and would find gone by morning anyway, because she always left food outside his door on the nights he came in late, and he always ate it, and they never spoke of it.

"Soldiers came by today," she said quietly. "While you were out. Two of them. Asking about lodgers."

Wendell's stomach went cold and professional. "What did you tell them?"

"I'm deaf," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "I've been deaf for twenty years. It's remarkable how much a deaf woman doesn't hear, and how long soldiers will shout at a deaf woman before they feel foolish." Her eyes, above the candle, were sharp and black and entirely unhurt. "They wrote in their book. I wrote back. They went away."

"What did you write?"

"That I take respectable gentlemen only, that my hearing is poor but my memory is worse, and that if they wished to inspect the rooms they were welcome to inspect the leak in the attic first." Almost, almost, a smile. "Men don't like attics that leak. It suggests the whole house is dishonest."

"And the ledger?"

"The ledger says what the ledger needs to say." She handed him the plate. "Eat something. You've gone thin at the edges, like paper left in rain."

"I'm fine, Mrs. Okonkwo."

"You are not fine. None of us are fine. But we are fed, and fed is a kind of fine." She turned to go, then paused, candlelight moving on her face. "Mr. Galloway. Whatever it is you carry—I don't ask. Twenty years, I don't ask. But I will tell you what I know, since knowing is free and silence costs extra these days. I know you stop eating when the packets come. I know you sleep facing the door. And I know that whatever you're carrying, it's getting heavier, because you're carrying it alone, and that's the only cargo that gains weight in transit."

"Goodnight, Mrs. Okonkwo."

"Goodnight, Wendell." At the stairs she paused again. "There's a woman coming tomorrow. Fisherman's boat out of the north, they say. Soaked through and asking after lodging. I told her I had a room."

"We're full."

"We're never full. That's the whole point of a harbor." And she went down the stairs, candle guttering, leaving the smell of seed cake and the sentence hanging in the dark behind him.

Wendell closed the door. He ate the cake standing up, hating himself for eating it, tasting salt on the sill of the window where the storm had come in under the loose latch—the sea reaching into the house, patient, inevitable, already inside.

Below, faintly, he heard Anselm counting boots again, and Pip laughing at something, and the needles clicking.

Tomorrow, a stranger.

He fixed the latch. It took four minutes. He had needed help for none of it, and told no one, and lay down facing the door, and did not sleep.
