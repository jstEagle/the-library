## 3. A Stranger with Familiar Ink

She came with the morning tide, which was the first thing wrong about her.

Wendell had learned the harbor's rhythms the way other men learned prayers: by repetition, without understanding, until they governed him. The fishing boats came in at dusk, heavy and low in the water. The mail packet came on Thursdays, when it came at all. Nobody came off open water at seven in the morning in November, not in that swell, not alone.

He heard her before he saw her—boots on the cobbles outside Mrs. Okonkwo's, uneven, the drag of someone who'd been braced against a deck for hours. He was at his window with the curtain pulled to two fingers' width, doing what he always did: cataloguing. One woman. Alone. No escort visible from this angle. She stopped at the gate, looked up at the house, looked down the street both ways—the way he did, the way couriers did—and then came through the gate without knocking on it, as if she'd been told the latch stuck and it was better not to touch it.

That was the second thing wrong. Nobody knew about the latch except people who'd been told.

He was downstairs and in the hall before her knuckles found the door, which was its own kind of mistake, and he knew it even as he opened it. Rule three, never be remembered, and here he was answering a knock before it finished falling. But there are rules for ordinary days, and something in the set of her shoulders said this wasn't one.

She was soaked. Not rain-soaked—sea-soaked, salt crusted white along the hem of a coat that had once been good, hair flattened against her skull, lips still blue at the edges. She stood in the doorway like a parcel someone had delivered wrong: upright, patient, waiting to be refused.

"Wendell Galloway," she said. Not a question. Her accent was northern, the vowels rounded soft, the kind of voice that carried over water out of habit.

"Wrong house."

"No," she said. "I don't think I am." And she held out an oilcloth package, tied with tarred twine, the knot done in the fisherman's hitch that could be undone one-handed in the dark.

He didn't take it. That was rule one, never touch what you haven't been paid to carry, and rule two, never let them see you want to. His hands stayed at his sides. His eyes went to the package anyway, because he was tired and cold and it was early, and she saw him look. He watched her understand that she'd seen him look, and watched her decide not to pretend otherwise.

"You'd better come in," he heard himself say, and hated every word of it.

* * *

Mrs. Okonkwo appeared in the kitchen doorway with the timing of a woman who had run a boarding house through two governments and understood that the important conversations happened within earshot of the kettle. She said nothing. She set a pot on the stove, put bread on the table, poured tea into the chipped cup that was always given to strangers because it made them feel less watched than the good china did, and then she went back upstairs with a look at Wendell that he chose to read as nothing at all.

The woman sat with her hands wrapped around the cup. Up close she was younger than her voice—younger than thirty, maybe, though the sea had aged her overnight. There was a rope burn across her left palm, fresh, and saltwater sores at the corner of her mouth from vomiting over a rail, if he had to guess, and he usually did have to guess, that being most of the work.

"Your name," he said.

"Ilsa Brandt."

"That's not what I asked you to prove."

"It's the only name I have left, Mr. Galloway. I'd like to keep it." She drank the tea like it was medicine, all at once, grimacing. "I was told you carry things."

"I'm a clerk."

"You were pointed at me by name from a boat forty miles north of here. Clerks don't get pointed at." She set the cup down. "I was told: find Galloway, tell him nothing until he asks the right question, and if he never asks it, walk away and drown your letters, because he'll have decided you're bait."

"And what's the right question?"

"I don't know. The man who told me wouldn't say. He said you'd know it when you needed it, and that needing it would hurt." She looked at him steadily, and there was something under the exhaustion in her face that he recognized, unwillingly: the flat calm of a person who has already spent everything and is now just waiting for the bill. "I've been on that boat four days, Mr. Galloway. I've got nothing left to perform. So either ask me or turn me out, but do it soon, because I think I'm going to shake apart either way."

He should have turned her out. Every rule he owned said turn her out: unknown courier, unverified cargo, arrived irregularly, visibly marked by the journey—a woman that wet, that exhausted, would be remembered by every eye between this door and the customs house. He could see the report already. *Female, northern accent, thirties, coat hem salt-stiffened, seen entering Okonkwo's.*

Instead he heard himself say, "Who sent you?"

"A dead man."

* * *

There is a particular quality to grief that those who haven't carried it mistake for weakness. It isn't. It's load-bearing. For two years Wendell had built his days on top of Tomas Reyes's death the way you build a house on rock: everything above it solid because the thing beneath it does not move. Tomas was drowned. Tomas was recovered, identified, buried in the naval plot behind the garrison with a marker and a folded flag and a lie for an epitaph. Tomas was the fixed point Wendell steered by, the proof that the work killed people and therefore the work must be done carefully, quietly, invisibly. He had arranged his entire survival around the fact of that grave.

So when Ilsa Brandt untied the oilcloth on his table and slid three letters across the wood, and the handwriting on them was slanted hard to starboard, cramped, urgent, with the *i*'s dotted like little crosses—the way Tomas dotted his *i*'s since they were eleven years old and copying each other's schoolwork in the back of the chandlery—Wendell did not feel surprise.

He felt vertigo. The whole house tilted. The rock moved.

"That's not possible," he said, and his voice came out level, which frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Read them."

"I don't read things." It was automatic, the rule recited like a rosary, and she looked at him with something close to pity.

"These aren't for carrying. These are for you."

His hand was moving before he'd decided anything. Later he would examine that—the way his fingers closed on the topmost letter, the way his thumb found the fold Tomas always made, twice over, left then right, so the paper would sit square in an inside pocket. Muscle memory is a traitor. It keeps faith with the dead against the living.

The letter was dated twenty-three days ago. He checked the date three times, as if it might change under inspection, as if dates were the kind of thing that could be argued with.

*Dated twenty-three days ago.* Dated by a man who had been in the ground for two years, in a grave Wendell had stood beside in the rain while an officer who smelled of brandy read the committal words wrong.

He didn't read the letter. Not yet. He read the envelope instead, because envelopes couldn't lie the way ink could. The address was his own, in full, including the room number, including the instruction *hold until asked*, written in the margin the way Tomas used to write marginal instructions when the message itself was meant to pass through hands that might read it. Which meant Tomas had expected this letter to travel. Had expected intermediaries. Had built it for interception and survived it.

"He's alive," Ilsa said. Not asking. Watching him the way you watch a structure you suspect is about to fail.

"He's dead," Wendell said. "I attended the funeral. I helped pay for the stone."

"The stone says Reyes?"

"Tomas Reyes."

"Then the stone's part of it too." She leaned forward. Salt was drying in lines on her forearms where the sleeves had been pushed up; he caught himself cataloguing again—rope burn left palm, salt sores, a scar on the right wrist old enough to be silvered—and made himself stop, because cataloguing was how you kept people at the length of a report, and some animal part of him wanted very badly to keep her there.

"Tell me," he said.

"I found the letters in a cache. A lighthouse on Skerry Vow—" she used the old name, the pre-Republic name, and noted that he noticed—"the light's been dark since the blockade started. There's a false floor under the lamp room. Fishermen have used it for sixty years. I go out in autumn, before the weather closes, to collect what people leave. Most of it's nothing. Letters to sweethearts, debts, a man's medals once." She paused. "This year there was a sealed tin, weighted, sunk in the cistern rather than left loose. Whoever hid it wanted it found by someone specific or no one at all. Inside were these letters and a set of instructions naming you. Your name, this street, this house."

"Anyone could write my name."

"On a tin sunk in a cistern on a rock nobody visits? They could. But they also wrote this." She slid a second sheet across—not a letter, a list. Dates, tide tables, safe landings along the northern coast, each annotated in the same slanted hand. "I've spent six weeks checking those tides against my own logs. They're accurate. Every one. Written by someone who knows that coast the way a pilot knows it, not the way a forger studies it."

"A forger with access to tide tables—"

"Mr. Galloway." She said it gently, which was worse than sharpness. "You've read the letters. You're holding them. Look at your own hands."

He looked down. He had, without any decision on his part, unfolded the top letter fully. He hadn't read it—he was holding it the way you hold a bird, loosely, ready to release—but the page was open, and the opening line sat there in the lamplight where his eyes went whether he willed it or not:

*Wendell. If you're reading this, then I owe you the truth and a grave-marker apology, and I'm going to give you one of them now and the other when I can.*

He folded it shut. His hands were steady. That was the training, and he clung to the steadiness like a rail in high seas, because underneath it something enormous had come loose.

"Say it plainly," he managed. "No performance. You said you had none left in you. So say it plain."

Ilsa Brandt looked at him across the table, in the kitchen of a boarding house that smelled of bread and kerosene, and said it plain.

"Tomas Reyes is alive. He's on the northern islands, at the herring station past Vaul, working under another name. He faked his drowning two years ago. He wrote these letters three weeks ago. And the reason he faked his death, the reason he's writing to you now instead of coming home—" she tapped the folded letter, once, lightly, like knocking on a door—"is in there. Names, Mr. Galloway. Men in this city selling passages out of the Republic to the enemy, selling them to the very people trying to drown us, and pocketing the fare. He found out. He couldn't stay alive and stay honest, so he stayed alive and stayed dead. Now he wants the record to survive him. Again."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, Anselm was counting boots—*forty-one, forty-two*—his litany, his rosary, the sound Wendell had fallen asleep to for a year without ever letting himself call it the sound of home.

"And you crossed open water in November to bring me this," Wendell said. "Why you? Who are you to him?"

Something crossed her face then—the first crack in the flat calm, quick as a fish turning under the surface.

"I'm nobody to him," she said. "I'm somebody to the Republic. My brother took one of those passages. Paid everything we had to a man with a harbor office and a kind voice. The boat went out. My brother didn't arrive anywhere." Her jaw tightened. "Tomas's letters say why. So no—I'm not his friend, or his kin, or his lover. I'm the sister of a man those letters can explain. That's my whole story, and it's the only currency I have, and I have spent it getting here, and now I'm sitting in your kitchen shaking, and I would like very much to know what you're going to do."

What he was going to do. He looked at the letters on the table—three of them, plus the list, plus whatever else the tin had held that she hadn't shown him yet, because there was more; he could tell by the way she kept her left hand flat on the oilcloth, guarding it, an instinct he recognized because it was his own. He looked at them and felt the two halves of himself grind against each other like millstones.

One half said: *bait.* The officers hunting republic sympathizers had grown sophisticated; everyone knew it; the arrests came in clusters now, timed to catch networks mid-passage. What better lure than a dead man resurrected? Grief was a hook that worked on the careful ones especially, because the careful ones had buried their caution with their friends. If this was a trap, it was a beautiful one, built from his own mourning, addressed in a hand designed to unlock him.

The other half said: *it's him.* The cross-tick *i*'s. The double fold. The marginal instructions. The tide tables checked against six weeks of her logs. And under all of it, beneath evidence, beneath argument: the sentence on the open page, the voice in it, dry and rueful and sorry, a voice he had heard across eleven years of shared cigarettes and shared silences and one night on the seawall when Tomas had told him, drunk, that if anything ever happened he hoped Wendell would remember he'd tried. He had thought that was talk about the war. Maybe it had been a rehearsal.

Both halves spoke at once and neither won, and Wendell Galloway sat in Mrs. Okonkwo's kitchen holding a dead man's handwriting and discovered that his rulebook—never read, never linger, never be remembered, never trust, never want, never belong—had nothing in it for this. The book assumed the world was hostile and simple. This was hostile and complicated. The book assumed the dead stayed dead. The dead, apparently, filed reports.

"Mrs. Okonkwo will make you a bed in the attic," he said finally. "It's warm up there and the window faces the yard, not the street. Don't leave the house. Don't wash that coat—it smells of the boat, and that's useful, but hang it where it can't be seen from the lane. If anyone knocks and asks about a woman arriving this morning, you've been here a week and you're a seamstress and you keep to yourself."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." He gathered the letters, squared them, folded the oilcloth over them with the fisherman's hitch undone one-handed in the dark. "I need to think."

"You need to read them."

"Yes," he said. "That too."

She studied him for a long moment, this woman who had bet her life on a stranger's memory of handwriting, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something, because she nodded once, curtly, and stood.

"For what it's worth," she said at the door, "he told me you'd fight it. He said, *tell Galloway the letters are real and he'll check the knots and count the stitches and argue with the tide tables, and then he'll read the last page last, because he always reads the last page last, and that's where I put the thing he can't argue with.*"

She went up the stairs. The attic door closed, softly, the way doors close in houses where people have learned to be quiet.

Wendell sat alone with the oilcloth package in front of him and did not touch it for a long time. Through the window, the tide was turning; the boats swung on their moorings, all together, like a congregation shifting in its pews. Somewhere out on that water, in the direction of the dark northern islands, a man he had mourned was breathing.

*The last page last.* Because Wendell always read the last page last. Of course he did. You saved the end for when you were ready, because endings were the part that changed you, and a courier learned early to control what changed him.

He untied the hitch. He separated the official letters from the rest—one sheet at the bottom, folded differently, addressed not to the record, not to history, not to the free press abroad, but to him. To Wendell. In a hand he'd have known anywhere, dated twenty-three days ago, by a corpse.

He put it in his inside pocket, unopened, over his heart, double-folded left then right so it sat square.

And went to find out whether he dared.
