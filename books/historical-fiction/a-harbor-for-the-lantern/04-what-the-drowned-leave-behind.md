## 4. What the Drowned Leave Behind

The letters sat between them on the table like something freshly killed.

Ilsa kept her hands flat on either side of them, palms down, the way you'd steady yourself on a gunwale. Across the kitchen, Wendell Galloway hadn't moved in a long time. He looked at the handwriting the way a man looks at a face he's seen in a coffin.

"Three weeks ago," he said again. He'd said it four times now. "That's not possible."

"I know what the date says." Her voice came out rougher than she intended; three days of salt spray and bad water would do that. "I know what it says better than anyone alive, because I'm the one who pulled it out of a wall."

Mrs. Okonkwo had gone upstairs with the tact of a woman who had cleared out of rooms before. The kitchen was theirs. Rain worked at the window. Somewhere below the hill, the occupied city went on with its curfewed, counted-out evening, and Ilsa understood that everything she had crossed forty miles of open winter sea to do was going to be decided in this room, by this gray-faced man who still hadn't touched the paper.

So she told him. Not the way she'd rehearsed it on the boat, muttering to the tiller with her hands frozen around the wood—but properly, from the beginning, because if he was going to call her a liar or worse, a tool, he deserved to hear all of it at once.

* * *

The lighthouse on Skerry Point had been dark for six years.

Everyone knew that. The navy stripped it when the Republic started losing the coast—pulled the lens, boarded the windows, left the tower to the gulls. It stood at the top of the island like a man with his eyes put out, and the fishing families gave it a wide berth, partly from superstition and partly because a garrison patrol checked its footpath twice a week.

Ilsa Brandt had been checking it too, but not on patrol days.

She'd been running supplies north since spring—cod liver oil, quinine, the kind of small heavy things that kept free-island clinics alive—and every run needed a cache point the patrols hadn't catalogued yet. A fisherman she trusted, a half-deaf old man named Sorrel who'd piloted boats back when the Republic still had a fleet worth naming, had told her once, drunk on aquavit at his daughter's wedding: *If they ever come for you, girl, go to the light. Tomas knew about the light.*

She'd thought he meant the lamp room. She'd searched it twice that autumn, finding nothing but rust and gull nests, and she'd stopped thinking about it until the third week of November, when the *Petrel* lost her rudder fitting in a squall and Ilsa put in at the island two days early with nothing to do but wait for parts.

Boredom. That was the whole of it, at first. Boredom and the memory of a drunk man's sentence rattling around her head.

*Tomas knew about the light.*

Tomas Reyes. Everyone in the northern network knew the name. The Republic's best courier, the one who could walk a diplomatic packet through three checkpoints without the guards remembering his face afterward—the same trick, people said, as Galloway, though nobody ever saw the two of them together. Then the war had eaten him. Two years ago, word came south: courier lost overboard in the strait, body recovered near the breakwater, identified and buried by the occupation authorities themselves. They'd made a ceremony of it. The occupiers liked burying their enemies' legends; it did more damage than killing them.

Ilsa had been nineteen when Tomas Reyes taught her to read a tide table. That was all. Six weeks one summer, her father working the same runs, and Reyes patient with a sharp-tongued girl who wanted to learn everything and resented being taught. He used to dot his i's like little crosses, and when she'd asked him why, he'd laughed and said, *So the letter knows it's carrying weight.*

She climbed the path to the lighthouse in a cold drizzle because there was nothing else to do, and because dead men's sentences were the only cargo she had that day.

* * *

"—and you're telling me," Wendell said, very quietly, "that you found these in a wall."

"Not a wall." She could feel the rain of that day still, sitting in her shoulders like a second skeleton. "The base of the tower. There's a false course of brick behind where the fuel store used to be—a cavity maybe a hand deep. You'd never find it unless you knew to take out the brick with the mason's mark. And even then—"

She reached across the table and turned the top letter over. On the back, in faded pencil, someone had drawn a small anchor with a crooked crossbar.

"—even then you'd need to know which brick. The mark is an anchor. Sorrel told me that much, years ago, and I thought he was talking nonsense. Masons' marks are just masons' marks." She heard her own voice flatten, the way it did when she was angry at the past. "It took me two hours to find the right brick, Mr. Galloway. I took apart half a wall with a boat knife in the rain, and when the packet came out I sat down on wet stone and I couldn't open it. Do you know why?"

He didn't answer. His eyes hadn't left the slanted hand.

"Because I recognized the writing," she said. "And Tomas Reyes has been dead for two years."

* * *

Here was what the packet held. She laid it out for him now, in the boarding house kitchen, piece by piece, because she had learned on the crossing that the story only worked if you gave it in order.

Eleven letters. Nine of them sealed, official, bearing the cipher marks of the Republican legation-in-exile's northern bureau—the rump government that still existed on paper, still sent dispatches, still pretended to be a state. Three years' worth, by their dates. Someone had been using the lighthouse as a dead-drop for the whole slow duration of the collapse, caching correspondence that could not safely travel, waiting for a courier who apparently never came.

And two of them—two—were personal. One addressed to *S.* Sorrel, presumably. And one addressed, in that slanted hand with its cross-tipped i's, to a name that had made Ilsa sit down harder on the wet stone than any wave ever had:

*W.G.*

But she was getting ahead of herself. In the lighthouse, shaking, she'd opened none of them. She'd wrapped the packet in oilcloth, put it inside her coat against her ribs, and climbed down, and it wasn't until the *Petrel* was under way again, parts fitted, running south through the long grey dusk, that she'd done the thing she'd sworn on the ladder she wouldn't do.

She'd broken the seal on one of the official letters.

"I know your rules," she said to Wendell now. "Never read. Sorrel told me yours and Reyes's rules both, more than once, usually while complaining about how secretive couriers are. Never read, never copy, carry only. I broke it." She met his eyes. "I'd do it again. You'll understand why."

* * *

The letter she'd opened was dated fourteen months ago. It was from a bureau clerk whose name meant nothing to her—a signature, a cipher stamp—and it was written in the careful, deniable language of men who have learned that every sentence might someday be read aloud in a courtroom or before a firing squad. But underneath the circumlocutions, the meaning came through like a body under a sheet.

Escape passages. The rich and the marked of the collapsing Republic were buying their way out—everyone knew that, it was the occupation's most profitable black market—and the letters alleged that the trade ran through the harbor itself. Not smugglers. Officials. Men whose signatures appeared on manifests and clearances, selling berths on refugee ships at ten thousand crowns a soul, and worse: selling lists. Names of passengers who could not pay. Names passed to the occupation authorities, who met certain ships at certain piers, and certain passengers never filed arrival papers anywhere at all.

The letter named three men.

One was dead already—one of the exile bureau's own ministers, dropped from a balcony in a neutral port last spring, an accident according to everyone whose job it was to say so. One had fled south beyond Ilsa's reach or interest.

And one was the harbormaster of the very port she was looking at across forty miles of winter sea. The port where the last loyal ships still called. The port through which, in the coming months, whoever remained of the Republic's people, records, and witnesses would try to pass.

*I write to no purpose,* the clerk had finished, *since there is no one left to act on it, and no courier to carry it. If these words ever reach a functioning hand, the writer will be dust. Let the record at least exist somewhere the water cannot take it.*

The record existed. It was sitting in a cavity behind a brick with an anchor on it, because the one courier reckless and loyal enough to keep a dead-drop alive in occupied waters had been officially drowned two years ago.

Except he hadn't been, had he. Because the dates on those letters ran to three weeks ago.

* * *

"So you see my problem," Ilsa said. Her tea had gone cold hours ago; she drank it anyway, because her hands wanted work. "Nine letters that could hang the men selling our people to the firing squads. Written evidence, signed, dated, cipher-stamped—real, Mr. Galloway, I know forgery when I hold it, I've carried enough of the real thing. And the only man alive who can confirm the drop is legitimate is a man whose funeral I've heard described in detail."

"And the tenth letter," Wendell said. His voice had changed somewhere in her account. It was smaller now. Careful, the way careful voices get when they're carrying something fragile downstairs. "You said eleven."

"Ten and eleven are the problem." She turned the packet toward him and tapped the last two envelopes without touching them. "One's to Sorrel. Fine. Sorrel vouched for me, I can explain that. But the other one—" She swallowed. "The other one's addressed to W.G. And every person in the northern network who knows anything knows that W.G. is Wendell Galloway, the ghost of the southern route, and that Tomas Reyes was—" She stopped. Chose the word. "His brother. Whatever the blood says."

"We weren't—" Wendell began, an old reflex.

"I know what the blood says. I also know what the road says, and the road says brother, and I am not going to stand here and argue genealogy with a man who looks like he's holding his own heart in a envelope." She pushed it toward him. The one marked *W.G.*, seal unbroken, edges soft from damp anyway. "I crossed the strait in winter with that letter against my ribs. Forty miles of open water, one fishing boat, a compass I don't fully trust, and a hull that's been patched twice since I've owned her. I did not come here for the nine letters, Mr. Galloway. Any competent runner could take those south. I came because of this one. Because if Tomas Reyes is alive—if he wrote this three weeks ago—then everything changes. And if he isn't alive, if this is bait, if somebody forged that hand to draw out the people who loved him—"

"Then you've led them straight to me."

"Then I've led them straight to you," she agreed. "Yes. I've had a lot of time at sea to think about that."

* * *

The truth—which she gave him, because lying to this man felt like lying to a church—was that she'd argued with herself the whole crossing, and lost.

Argument one: it's a trap. The occupation knew about the northern runners, was closing on them one cell at a time. What better lure than a dead legend resurrected? Plant letters in a cache, let them be found, watch who comes running. The hand could be copied. People forge love notes and bank drafts every day; a man's own sister couldn't always tell his hand from a forgery after two years.

Counterargument: the trap makes no sense. To bait it, the occupation would have to know the cache existed—and if they knew, they'd simply have taken the letters and watched the lighthouse, which any watcher would have spotted, because the footpath had one approach and Ilsa had walked it with her heart in her mouth. And the forgery itself: nine official letters, correctly sealed, correctly ciphered, spanning three years, planted in a hiding place whose location required a dead fisherman's drunken hint and two hours with a boat knife? You didn't build traps that elaborate for runners. You built them cheap. This was not cheap.

Argument two: he's alive. Faked the drowning—God knew the Republic's couriers had talked about exit strategies often enough, half-joking, the way tired men joke—and spent two years off the map, keeping the drop alive from wherever he was, feeding the record into the wall one letter at a time, waiting for the network to send someone.

Counterargument: then why not surface? Why write to Sorrel and to W.G. instead of walking into any northern safehouse and saying *here I am*? Unless he couldn't. Unless whatever had driven him under still drove him. Unless he was dying, or hunted, or—

Or the letters were older than their dates. Or he'd written them before the drowning and the dates were lies. Or.

Ilsa set the cold cup down.

"So I made a wager," she said. "Out past the shoals, Tuesday night, in a sea that would have killed me if it felt like it, I made a wager. I bet that the hand on that envelope is real. I bet that the man who taught me tide tables dotting his i's like crosses is alive, or was three weeks ago, or wanted someone to believe he was. And I bet that the only person in the world who can tell the difference—forgery or truth, trap or testament—is you." She slid the envelope the last inch across the table, until it rested against his folded hands. "Because I've read about you, Wendell Galloway. They say you memorize faces. They say you never forget a hand of writing. They say you could identify a man's penmanship from across a room, and that's why you're still breathing when everyone else on the southern route is in a yard somewhere wearing a number."

Wendell Galloway looked at the envelope the way drowning men look at a lantern on shore.

"That was two years ago," he said. "Before they started hunting us properly. Before I learned to stop being anyone." A muscle moved in his jaw. "You want me to be memorable. Just for an hour. Just for a dead man."

"I want you to be what you were," Ilsa said. "For one hour. For one hand of writing."

Outside, down the hill, a patrol bell rang the half-hour, and both of them flinched—old habit, shared reflex, the first thing they'd ever had in common. The rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere out in the dark harbor, the tide was turning, and Ilsa thought: whatever happens next happens fast.

Wendell picked up the letter. Turned it over once, twice, as if checking whether it weighed what a letter should weigh.

Then he broke the seal.
