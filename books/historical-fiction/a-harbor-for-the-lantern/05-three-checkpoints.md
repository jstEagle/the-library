## 5. Three Checkpoints

Wendell had spent six years learning the harbor the way other men learned scripture: by repetition, by rote, by walking it until the stones knew his feet. That morning he walked it as a stranger would—looking for what he'd spent years training himself not to see.

The customs house first.

It squatted at the mouth of Merchant Row like a fat toad, all sandstone and green copper roofing, its windows fogged with the breath of clerks who'd been counting other people's fortunes since before the war went bad. Two sentries at the door, but they were decoration. The real teeth were inside: the manifest desk, where every crate and satchel entering or leaving the harbor district was logged, weighed, and—since the autumn decrees—opened.

He joined a line of fishmongers and stood with his hands loose at his sides, doing what he did best: being furniture. The clerk at the desk was new. That was bad. A man three years in his post developed rhythms, blind spots, a tolerance for certain faces. This one was maybe twenty-four, with the careful handwriting of someone who'd been promoted from copying to judging, and he opened everything. Wendell watched him slice the twine on a widow's trunk of linens with a small knife he kept sharp enough to shave with. He watched him hold a bolt of cloth to the light looking for printed code between the threads. He watched him make an old man unload an entire cart of winter turnips onto wet stone while the queue muttered and the old man's hands shook.

Nothing in that room was getting through unopened. Not letters, not ledgers, not a woman's letters in a dead courier's hand.

The second problem was the questions. The manifest form asked *contents* and *consignee* and *purpose of conveyance*. Wendell had answered those questions truthfully for six years because his contents were always boring and his consignees were always fictitious in ways that checked out. But Ilsa's packet—the official letters, the ones that named names—had no consignee who could be produced on demand. If the clerk sent a runner to verify, the runner would come back with soldiers.

He bought a herring from a stall outside and ate it standing up, watching the door, cataloguing the shift change. Sentries turned over at noon; the new pair was younger, hungrier-looking. Hungry men took bribes. Hungry men also invented reasons to demand them. Neither kind of man could be trusted with something this heavy.

*Checkpoint one,* he wrote in his head, in the cipher of habit he used instead of paper: *impassable for documents. Possible only for people willing to become someone else entirely.*

* *

The garrison gate was worse, and Wendell had known it would be before he rounded the corner of Barracks Street and saw the queue.

The gate cut the harbor district off from the city proper—a double arch of blackened brick with a checkpoint house wedged into the middle of it, and since the decrees, every person passing from city to quay needed a harbor pass stamped by the occupation authority. Wendell had one. It had taken him four months and two bribes to get it, and it said his name was Wendell Galloway, commercial messenger, which was even true. He'd built his whole survival on the principle that the best forgery is the truth, properly framed.

But the pass was individual. One name, one face, one body through the arch at a time. And the sergeant working the morning queue was a man Wendell knew.

Corporal Voss—he'd been a corporal two years ago, when Wendell's route ran through this gate twice a week—was now a sergeant, thickened around the jaw, with a scar through one eyebrow he hadn't earned honestly. Voss had once waved Wendell through on a night when the pass regulations were changing and half the couriers in the city got stranded on the wrong side of their own jobs. He'd done it without being asked, without ever acknowledging afterward that he'd done it, and Wendell had never forgotten, because in this city a favor unpaid was a knife left in your back by your own hand.

Voss knew his face. Voss knew his name. If Wendell walked Ilsa Brandt—northern accent, northern boots, a woman whose description was probably already circulating through garrison channels—through that arch on his arm, then Voss wasn't a risk anymore. He was a certainty. Every eye in the checkpoint house would go where Sergeant Voss's eyes went.

He lingered near a water cart, watching. The queue moved slowly. A woman ahead of him argued about her pass and was made to empty her handbag into a wooden tray while two soldiers picked through her husband's letters. A boy selling chestnuts got his tray kicked over for drifting too close to the barrier. Nobody screamed. That was the worst part of the occupied city—it had stopped screaming months ago and settled into this quiet grinding compliance, everyone paying the toll of humiliation because the alternative was a cell under the barracks.

There was a second way through. Everyone knew there was a second way through, the way everyone knows things they're not supposed to know: a coal chute beneath the eastern tower, used by smugglers since before the republic, guarded by exactly one bored soldier whose wages came partly from the men he didn't search. Wendell had never used it. His rules forbade it—not explicitly, but in spirit. Invisible men don't crawl through holes that exist because somebody powerful permits them to.

But rules were for delivering dispatches. This was different. This was Tomas Reyes's handwriting, alive three weeks after Tomas Reyes drowned, and whatever it cost to get those letters to open water, he was beginning to understand he'd have to pay it.

Still. The chute was watched, permitted, and therefore a trap wearing a welcome mat. He filed it away as a last resort and hated how much relief that word—*last*—gave him.

* *

The pier inspection was the one that would kill them, and Wendell knew it the moment he saw the tables.

They'd set up long trestle boards along the quay itself, between the customs shed and the gangplanks, and every passenger boarding an outbound vessel now walked a gauntlet: papers checked at the table, luggage searched at the second table, body patted down at the third, and finally—an innovation from last month, cruel in its simplicity—a single officer at the gangplank who looked each traveler in the face and asked one question. *Where are you going?* The answer was compared against the ship's manifest, the traveler's papers, and whatever instinct the officer had cultivated in however many years of deciding other people's fates.

The officer at the gangplank that morning was Inspector Hale.

Wendell felt it in his stomach before his mind caught up—that cold drop, the body knowing danger before the brain agrees. Hale stood at the plank with his hands clasped behind his back, gray-coated, unhurried, asking his one question of each passenger in a voice too mild for the work it did. Wendell had watched Hale send back a merchant's wife two weeks ago over nothing anyone else could see—some hesitation in her answer, some flicker—and he'd watched the woman taken to the checkpoint house and not seen her come out.

And Hale knew him.

Not well. That was the horror of it—well enough. Three years ago, on the ice quay, a crane cable had parted and dropped a cargo net within a hand's width of crushing Wendell against the bollards, and Hale had hauled him back by the collar without breaking stride, then told him to watch his footing and walked on. They'd exchanged perhaps forty words total in six years. Hale remembered faces the way Wendell did. Two professionals recognizing each other across a crowded profession.

If Wendell put those letters on a ship, he would have to walk past Hale and answer one question while carrying the most dangerous paper in the republic. There was no bribing Hale. There was no charming him. There was only the question, and whatever truth or lie Wendell offered up, weighed by a man who weighed lies for a living.

He stood at the edge of the fish market for a long time, herring smoke stinging his eyes, and let the paralysis have him. It was honest, at least, the paralysis. Six years of competence had felt like armor, but armor only worked if you never tested it, and now every road out of the harbor ended in a table, a gate, or a gray coat, and behind him in Mrs. Okonkwo's attic sat a woman who had crossed open winter water on the strength of nothing but conviction, waiting for him to be more than a man who memorized exits.

He could burn the letters. The thought arrived quietly, reasonably, dressed as mercy. Burn them, tell Ilsa the sea took them, keep the household safe, keep his own name off whatever list the reprisals would build. He could go back to invisibility. Invisibility had kept him alive through things that had killed better men than him.

But Tomas Reyes had written *three weeks ago*. Which meant Tomas Reyes had been alive three weeks ago. Which meant the drowning—the recovery, the identification, the closed coffin Wendell had mourned over in this same harbor—had been arranged, performed, purchased. Someone had gone to enormous trouble to kill a man who wasn't dead, and you didn't spend that kind of trouble on a nobody. You spent it on what a man carried, or what a man knew. And the letters knew.

He turned away from the quay and walked toward the taverns.

* *

Anselm held court at the Anchor & Chain from noon until whenever the money ran out, occupying the corner table nearest the stove with the authority of a man who'd piloted ships through these shallows for forty years and considered the harbor his personal property, currently mismanaged. Wendell found him exactly where the afternoon light and the smell of burnt coffee said he'd be: alone except for a bottle of plum brandy, a stack of coasters he was building into a tower, and eyes that tracked Wendell across the room with the sober precision of a sextant taking a reading.

"Galloway." Anselm nudged a chair out with his boot. "Sit. You've got the look of a man pricing funerals."

"Yours, possibly."

"They've been pricing mine since the armistice. Sit."

Wendell sat. Anselm poured two fingers into the second cup without asking, and Wendell drank it because refusing Anselm's brandy was a negotiation that took longer than the war. Around them the tavern filled its afternoon hours: dockhands off shift, a pair of pilots arguing tide tables, the barkeep polishing glasses that were already clean. Nobody looked at them. In this room, Wendell and the old pilot were scenery.

"You've been walking the harbor all day," Anselm said. "I heard. Pip hears everything, and Pip tells me everything, and you should be nicer to that boy, because right now he's the only intelligence service in this city worth the name." He turned a coaster square in front of him, aligning it with the table edge. "You walked the customs house this morning. Stood in the fish queue twenty minutes. Then the gate. Then the quay." He looked up. "You're mapping checkpoints. So. Somebody's leaving, or something is."

Wendell said nothing. Anselm nodded as if silence were an answer, which it was.

"I'll save us both the dance," the old man said. "You can't get through the customs house because the new clerk opens everything. Can't use the gate because Voss knows your face, and even if he didn't, the pass system is one name, one body. And the pier—" he tapped the coaster, "—the pier has Hale, and Hale is the reason half the smugglers in this port are either dead or retired, and the other half are working for him." He smiled without any warmth in it. "Did I leave anything out?"

"How do you know about Hale?"

"Because I taught half his instructors, boy. Because forty years ago I brought contraband through this harbor when the republic was young and honest and still worth smuggling *for*, and I know every channel, every chute, every blind eye and bought soul from the breakwater to the arsenal. And because—" Anselm drank, and for a moment the drunkenness fell away from his face like a coat taken off, revealing something lean and old and very awake underneath. "Because I've been waiting six years for you to come ask me. You think I sit here drinking myself stupid because I'm stupid? I sit here drinking because drunk old men are furniture, and furniture survives occupations."

The stove ticked. Somewhere behind the bar a cork popped and someone laughed at something that wasn't funny.

"There's a channel," Anselm said. "Runs from the old oyster beds east of the breakwater, through the shallows the navy charts mark as impassable, out past the bell buoy. Draft under two meters the whole way. I marked it myself, in the days when marking such things got you decorated instead of shot. No patrol boat can follow. No inspection touches it. A fishing boat that draws shallow enough can take aboard passengers and cargo off the oyster rocks at high water and be past the buoy before dawn." He turned the coaster again. "It's how your friend Brandt came in. She didn't tell you that? Northern boats know the channel. We taught them, back when we were allies instead of prey."

Wendell kept his voice level. "What does it cost?"

"Cost?" Anselm laughed, short and harsh. "It costs the thing you don't want to pay. It costs the truth." He leaned forward, and the stove light caught the network of broken veins in his face, and his eyes were not drunk at all. "I'll draw the chart tonight. I'll give you the channel, the bearings, the tide windows, the rock that isn't where the charts say it is. All of it, free, because some debts don't come in coin. But I'm an old man betting what's left of my neck on whatever you're carrying out of this harbor, and I don't bet blind. You want my chart, Galloway, you sit in my kitchen tonight and you tell me exactly what's in those letters. Not the shape of it. Not the size of the risk. The words. Who betrayed whom, and how badly, and why it's worth drowning me for."

"That's not how I work," Wendell said. "I don't read what I carry."

"I know." Anselm refilled both cups, unhurried, merciless. "That's how you've stayed alive. Never read, never linger, never be remembered—you think I haven't watched you do it for six years? It's beautiful. It's also finished. Because the moment a drowned man's letters showed up at your boarding house, you stopped being a courier and became a conspirator, and conspirators who don't trust each other die separately." He raised his cup. "So. Tonight. My kitchen. Everything you know. And in exchange—" the old pilot smiled, and this time there was warmth in it, dangerous warmth, the kind that pulled men toward rocks, "—you stop dying alone and start living with company. Worst trade you'll ever make. Take it."

Wendell looked into his cup. Six years of rules stood in a row behind him like the checkpoints themselves, each one a wall he'd built to keep the world at arm's length, and beyond the last wall, in a kitchen smelling of coffee and pipe smoke, an old drunk was offering him the only route through the harbor and demanding the only payment Wendell had never made.

He thought of Tomas's slanted hand. Of Ilsa, soaked to the bone, betting her life on a stranger's memory. Of Mrs. Okonkwo setting a fourth plate at the table without ever asking a question.

"All right," he said. "Tonight. Your kitchen."

Anselm knocked back the brandy and began, immediately, drunkenly, loudly, to sing something obscene about the harbormaster's mother—furniture again, invisible again, in plain sight of the whole room—but under the table his boot pressed once against Wendell's ankle, deliberate as a handshake, and Wendell understood that somewhere between the customs house and this corner chair, without noticing the exact moment it happened, he had stopped planning alone.

He just hadn't yet decided whether that meant he was saved, or doomed.

He'd find out tonight—in Anselm's kitchen, where the old pilot would want the words, all of them, including the ones Wendell himself had sworn never to read.
