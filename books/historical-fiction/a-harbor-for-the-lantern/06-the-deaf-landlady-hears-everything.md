## 6. The Deaf Landlady Hears Everything

Mrs. Okonkwo had been deaf for eleven years, and in that time she had heard everything worth hearing.

It was a discipline, like any other. The trick was not the cotton wool in the ears or the slight forward tilt of the head, though those helped. The trick was patience. Soldiers and clerks believed that a deaf woman was a wall they could speak in front of, and walls, they assumed, kept no records. So they talked. They talked about shift changes and bribes and which magistrate's wife had run off with a shipping clerk, and Mrs. Okonkwo stood with her hand cupped behind her ear and said *what's that, love?* until they gave up and shouted the important part, and by then she had already collected everything else.

She had been keeping this house on Lantern Row for twenty-six years and sheltering couriers for twenty of them, and in all that time she had lost only two guests to the authorities, both to bad luck rather than bad practice, and she had attended neither funeral because attending funerals made a name memorable.

Her first rule was simple: you learn people by their appetites.

The soldiers who came through had appetites like weather — loud, general, gone by morning. The frightened ones picked at their food. The liars ate too much, too fast, performing hunger. And couriers — couriers were a category all their own. They ate like men paying down a debt. Methodical. Grateful. Watching the door between bites.

Wendell Galloway had lived under her roof for three years, and he ate like a man reading a ledger. Every meal balanced. Nothing wasted. He thanked her for the herring the same way every Thursday, and when the bread was stale he said nothing at all, which told her more than any complaint would have.

So it meant something — it meant a great deal — when Wendell Galloway stopped eating.

* * *

She noticed it on the third evening after the northern woman arrived.

The household had settled into its new arithmetic. Pip took his supper standing up as always, one foot out the door, and Anselm drank his ration of gin and narrated the harbor tides to anyone or no one, and Wendell sat in his corner with his back to the wall — always the corner, always the wall; she'd stopped trying to cure him of it years ago — and across from him sat the woman, Ilsa Brandt, with salt still ghosted in the seams of her borrowed clothes.

Mrs. Okonkwo served the stew and watched.

Ilsa ate like a drowning woman being handed air. Fast, then forcing herself slower, then fast again. A woman who had crossed open water in winter and did not trust that there would be another meal. Fine. Noted.

But Wendell — Wendell lifted his spoon, held it over the bowl for the length of three breaths, and set it down again without tasting anything. He did it again. He arranged his bread into smaller pieces and moved them around the plate like a man solving a problem on his tabletop instead of in his head. When Pip asked him something about the customs house, he answered correctly, promptly, in the flat voice of a man reciting from memory while his mind stood somewhere else entirely.

Twenty years of feeding fugitives will teach you things no interrogator learns. Mrs. Okonkwo knew what an empty spoon meant. It meant weight. Something carried close to the body, something whose existence could not be spoken of even by silence, because silence itself might give it away.

He wasn't grieving. She'd seen grief at this table; grief eats either everything or nothing, but it does it honestly. This was different. This was a man doing arithmetic with his life and finding the sums didn't come out.

And the woman. The woman had looked at him twice that evening in a way that wasn't flirtation and wasn't fear. It was the look of someone carrying a message and waiting for permission to deliver it. Which meant whatever sat unspoken between them was heavier than soup, and it was going to break soon, and when it broke, soldiers would eventually follow. They always did. Soldiers were like gulls that way.

Mrs. Okonkwo refilled their bowls without being asked and said nothing, and heard everything.

* * *

The ledger lived in the drawer under the stairs, and it was, on its face, the most boring document in the Vessarine Republic.

*Guests of the house*, it said, in her own careful hand. Names, dates, payments. *Mr. Aldous Finch, cooper, arrived 14th of the Frost Moon, departed 19th.* *Mrs. Halloway and son, 3 nights.* Twenty years of cooperators and sons and wives who never existed, written in a hand so dull that a clerk skimming it would fall asleep before the second page.

This was her second rule: the best hiding place for a lie is inside a boring truth. The house really did take lodgers. Fishermen, widows, traveling tinkers, all real, all recorded. You buried your ghosts among honest customers and let the sheer tedium protect them.

That night, after the lamps were out, she took the ledger into the kitchen where the stove still held heat, and she turned back four pages, and she gave the northern woman a history.

*Ilsa Brandt* was too distinctive. Northern names drew northern questions. Instead she wrote: *Ilse Brandt, seamstress, arrived 9th of the Frost Moon* — nine days ago, safely before any fishing boat could have brought her, comfortably before any checkpoint log could contradict it. Paid in advance. Quiet guest, keeps to herself. She added a small detail, because details made lies breathe: *complains of the damp.*

Then she went up the stairs on her careful knees, past the second floor where Wendell's door stood closed and dark, up to the attic, and woke the woman gently, with a hand on the shoulder and a finger to her own lips.

Ilsa came awake the way soldiers' daughters come awake — instantly, silently, one hand already searching for whatever weapon the bedding hid. Good. Mrs. Okonkwo approved of that in a guest. It meant fewer surprises later.

"You'll sleep up here from now on," she whispered. "Second floor's for paying lodgers with nothing to hide. Up here you're just stored goods."

Ilsa blinked at her. "You're the landlady. The deaf one."

"That's right." She let the old smile move her mouth without reaching her eyes. "Deaf as a post. Never heard a word anyone's said to me in eleven years. Ask any soldier on this street." She handed the woman a blanket roll she'd carried up earlier, wool still warm from drying by the stove. "There's a board loose by the north eave. If you hear boots on the stair — my stairs creak in a particular way, you'll learn it — you go out onto the roof and across to the chandlery. Old Marta owes me. She'll stash you till it passes."

"Why are you helping me?" The question came out raw, unguarded, the voice of a woman too tired to perform gratitude properly. "You don't know me."

"I know you crossed the water in winter," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "Nobody does that for a small reason." She paused at the attic door. "One thing. The boy — Pip. If he offers to run messages for you, you tell him no, and if he insists anyway, you send him to me first. He thinks he's ten feet tall and made of luck. He is neither."

"He ran messages for us on the boat route," Ilsa said quietly. "Before. In the north."

"I know what he ran," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "I've known since the week he started. That's why I'm telling you." She closed the door on whatever face the woman made next, because some conversations were kinder unfinished.

Down the stairs, past the drawer, she paused and laid her palm flat against the ledger drawer once, the way another woman might touch a sleeping child. Twenty years of names in there. Some of those names had died anyway, in the end, in places she couldn't reach. But none of them had died because of her bookkeeping.

She intended to keep that record intact.

* * *

The soldiers came on the fifth day, which was faster than usual and slower than she'd feared.

She heard them from the kitchen — she always heard them first, the particular rhythm of military boots on cobbled streets, four pairs not two, unhurried, which meant a search rather than a chase — and by the time the knock landed on her door she had already done three things. She had sent Pip out the back with a basket and instructions to be loudly, tediously visible at the fish market for the next hour. She had put the kettle on. And she had knocked once, softly, on the second-floor landing, which in the grammar of this house meant *soldiers, twenty minutes, say nothing clever.*

Wendell opened his door before her knuckles left the wood. He was already dressed. Of course he was. He slept dressed half the time; she'd seen the creases in his coat at breakfast and never once remarked on it.

"The woman," he said.

"Is upstairs storing wool," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "And you are a courier who delivers sealed packets and knows nothing else, and I am a deaf old woman who cannot help the officers with anything, and Anselm—" she raised her voice slightly toward the landing below, "—is drunk, as usual."

From below, Anselm's voice floated up, agreeably slurred: "Drunker than usual, God willing."

The knock came again. She smoothed her apron, put the cotton wool more visibly into her ears — costume mattered, costume was armor — and opened the door to a lieutenant with a list in his hand and two soldiers fanning out behind him into her hall with their boots leaving wet prints on her clean floor.

"Madam. We're looking for a woman off a northern boat. May have passed through this quarter."

She cupped her hand behind her ear and leaned in with the whole performance of her body. "Eh? Speak up, love, I'm deaf."

"Loudly, then." He raised his voice with the weary patience of a man who had done this a dozen times today. "A WOMAN. FROM THE NORTH."

"A woman?" She shook her head. "I take lodgers, officer, respectable ones, it's all in my book—"

"We'll see the book."

And here was the moment, the same moment every time, the hinge on which twenty years had turned, and her heart did what it always did, which was nothing at all, because terror was a luxury for people with less practice. She fetched the ledger from under the stairs and set it open on the table before he could touch it, because a book offered freely looks innocent and a book seized looks evidence.

The lieutenant paged through it. His finger traveled down columns of coopers and tinkers and widows. *Ilse Brandt, seamstress, 9th of the Frost Moon, complains of the damp.* He stopped. Mrs. Okonkwo watched his eyes stop and felt the whole house balance on the point of that finger.

"Seamstress," he read aloud. "Brandt."

"From the midlands," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "Quiet as church-mice. Complains about the damp, if you can believe it, in this weather. Been here near two weeks. You can ask her yourself, she's out at her work most days, but she takes her supper here regular."

Two weeks. Before the boat. Before the winter crossing was even possible. The date sat in the ledger in her own patient handwriting like a small stone wall, and the lieutenant stood before it and found, as they all did, that the wall was exactly his height.

He glanced at his sergeant. The sergeant shrugged the shrug of a man paid by the day.

"Where is she now?"

"At her work, like I said. The dressmaker's off Cooper Lane. Madame Sefu's. She does fine work, you can ask—"

"No need." He closed the ledger. He was already bored; boredom was the true shield, better than any forged paper, and she had built hers page by tedious page. "If a northern woman comes asking for rooms, madam, you'll send word to the garrison."

"I never hear a thing," Mrs. Okonkwo said, tapping her ear, smiling her empty smile. "You'd have to write it down for me."

They tramped out. The kettle sang. She poured herself a cup with a steady hand and sat down at the kitchen table and permitted herself, for exactly the length of one cup of tea, to shake.

Then she got up, took the ledger back to its drawer, and added a new entry while the ink pot was warm: *Lieutenant, garrison, called regarding a missing person. Found nothing. Left boot-prints.*

Some histories you kept for yourself alone.

* * *

That evening the house reassembled itself around the table like water closing over a stone. Pip returned from the market smelling of herring and triumph, full of nothing because she'd instructed him to be full of nothing. Anselm drank and dozed. Wendell ate half his portion — half, an improvement — and the northern woman came down from the attic with lint on her sleeve and wool in her hair, playing the seamstress to the hilt, complaining charmingly about the damp.

Mrs. Okonkwo served the stew and listened to everything and said nothing, and what she heard was this: the boy and the woman exchanging glances over the candle about some plan half-formed; Anselm soberer than his act, watching the door; and Wendell and Ilsa circling each other like two people holding opposite ends of the same rope, pulling gently, testing, neither yet willing to haul.

Something was coming. Letters, probably — it was always letters, in the end. She had fed enough couriers to know that paper weighed more than lead, ounce for ounce, in the right hands.

After supper she caught Wendell alone in the hall, and she did what she almost never did, which was speak first.

"That girl upstairs," she said quietly, so quietly that only a hearing woman could have caught it, and only a deaf one would have dared say it. "Whatever she's carrying — it goes through this house clean, or it doesn't go through at all. Do you understand me?"

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Three years of Thursdays and herring and *thank you, Mrs. Okonkwo,* and behind his eyes she watched him decide, finally, how much of the wall to take down.

"She has letters," he said at last. "In a dead man's handwriting."

Mrs. Okonkwo stood very still. Somewhere out on Lantern Row a patrol went by, boots keeping time, and inside her chest twenty years of names turned over in their sleep.

"Well," she said. "Then you'd best bring them to the kitchen tomorrow, and we'll see what the dead man wanted. I'll make the good tea."

She turned back toward the stove before he could answer, because the offer had been made and the terms were set, and in her experience the dead man's handwriting could wait until morning — but the kettle, as always, could not.
