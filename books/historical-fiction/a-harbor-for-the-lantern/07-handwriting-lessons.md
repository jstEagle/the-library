## 7. Handwriting Lessons

Mrs. Okonkwo cleared the kitchen table after supper and then, with the tact of a woman who had been clearing tables for twenty years of secrets, took Pip upstairs to help with a leak that did not exist. Anselm followed, grumbling about his knees, and left the two of us alone with the lamp and the letters.

I laid them out in a row, the way I had once laid out dispatches for verification in the old chancery, before the chancery was a ruin and verification meant deciding who lived.

"Tell me about the scar," I said.

Ilsa Brandt sat with her hands folded, a woman who had crossed open water in winter and now submitted to a stranger's questions as if she had expected nothing less. "Left forearm. He fell out of a fig tree when he was nine. His cousin dared him."

"Which cousin?"

"Rafael. The one who drowned the following summer."

I hadn't asked that. She hadn't hesitated. Both facts were correct, and the correctness sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"The fig tree was behind the customs warehouse in Serrano," I said. "Not his family's garden. He told that story wrong on purpose, to see who corrected him."

Her eyes flickered—just once, just a candle guttering. "Then he told it wrong to me too."

"Or you heard it wrong from whoever coached you."

"Or," she said, "you are testing me with a correction you invented, and I have just failed it, and you know that, and you're watching to see if I know it."

I looked at her for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, the nonexistent leak was being attended to with a great deal of banging.

"You're either very good," I said, "or you're exactly what you claim to be. In my experience those are the same thing until proven otherwise."

* * *

I had known Tomas Reyes for eleven years. I had carried his letters, read his handwriting on ten thousand manifests and three hundred private notes, watched that handwriting change the way a man's walk changes—steadier after his mother died, cramped and hurried the winter of the purges, loose again in the last spring, as if some weight had finally come off him. I knew his hand the way I knew my own pulse.

So I did what I had trained myself never to do. I read.

Not the dispatches—Ilsa had those wrapped in oilcloth and I left them sealed, because the rule was the rule and the rule was the only thing between me and the wall of the garrison courtyard. But the letters she'd carried in a tin against her ribs, the ones dated three weeks ago, the ones in Tomas's slanted hand—those I spread under the lamp and interrogated the way I was interrogating her.

The *i*'s were dotted like crosses. Little perpendicular strokes, deliberate, a habit he'd picked up from the seminary schoolmaster who beat the alphabet into the harbor children. A forger who'd studied his hand would know that. It was in every official document he'd ever signed.

But look closer. The crosses leaned. In the real Tomas, they leaned left, always left, a left-handed man forcing himself into right-handed script. These leaned left too. But the ink pooled at the base of the strokes—pressed hard, the way a man presses when he's writing in the cold, or afraid, or both. Tomas had written fast and light, always. He used to say a courier's hand should leave no more impression than a courier's feet.

"He's on the northern islands," Ilsa said, watching me read. "The fishing camps past the light at Vell. He's been there two years."

"Two years." I didn't look up. "The body they pulled from the channel wore his coat."

"His coat, his papers, his ring." She said it flatly, recitation. "I asked him about that. He said—" and here she stopped, and for the first time since she'd walked into Mrs. Okonkwo's dripping seawater and ruin, she looked away from me. "He said he'd have to answer that one in person. That some things don't survive being written down."

"Convenient."

"Yes," she agreed. "It is. I thought so too. I've had three weeks at sea to think so."

* * *

I pushed the letters aside and tried a different door.

"His mother made a bread at midwinter," I said. "He brought it to the chancery every year. What was it called?"

Ilsa's brow creased. Not the smooth crease of performance—the genuine friction of someone reaching. "He never told me that."

"He never told anyone anything about his mother. That's the point. That's the answer."

"Then I've failed again." She spread her hands. "Wendell Galloway. You can build a test with no passing grade. I can't tell you things Tomas never told me, and I can't prove the things he did. All I can give you is this." She touched the tin. "And the fact that I nearly died getting it to you, and the fact that I could have sold it to the garrison for more money than a fisherman's daughter sees in a lifetime, and the fact that I'm sitting in a stranger's kitchen letting him pick through my story like a gull through refuse."

"Why?" I said. "Why me? There are a hundred men in this city who'd have taken those letters north for pay."

"Because Tomas said: *if anything happens, find the man who never reads. Galloway. He's the only one careful enough to be trusted and the only one stubborn enough to do it anyway.*" She recited it with the cadence of memorization, and then her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just humanly. "Those are almost his exact words. He laughed when he said it. He said you'd hate him for making you the last resort."

The lamp hissed. I discovered I was gripping the edge of the table.

"He told me the same thing about himself," I said quietly. "That if he ever went quiet, I wasn't to go looking. That quiet was the plan."

"Was it?"

"I don't know. That's the hell of it, Brandt. For two years I've told myself the silence was the plan, because the alternative was that he was dead in the channel, and I—" I stopped. Recalibrated. A courier does not hand over cargo. "It doesn't matter what I believed."

"It matters," she said. "It's the only thing that matters. Because if you believe he's alive, you'll carry these letters to the ship yourself, through three checkpoints, with your own hands. And if you believe he's dead, you'll hand them to me and go back to your room and your rules, and the letters will drown with me the first time a clerk looks at my boots."

She was right, and I hated her a little for it.

* * *

We worked until the candle burned to its collar. Not the interrogation anymore—or not only that. Something worse: two people building a man out of memory, and finding that the pieces fit, and being wounded by how well they fit.

She knew the story of the mule in the Serrano market—the one that ate half a diplomatic pouch and Tomas's hat with it, and how he'd chased it through four streets in his shirtsleeves while Wendell Galloway stood frozen with the surviving half of the dispatches. She knew it wrong in one detail. She said it was a Tuesday. It was a Thursday. I knew because Thursdays were the days the market tax collector drank, which was why Tomas had chosen Thursday to lose a diplomatic pouch.

"Thursday," I said.

"Are you sure?"

"Thursdays were the only days he could afford to be a fool."

She laughed—short, startled, wet-eyed—and pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "God. You did love him."

"I carried his mail."

"You know that's not an answer."

I looked at the letters. The crosses leaning left. The ink pressed hard by cold hands. *A courier's hand should leave no more impression than a courier's feet.* And yet these letters pressed hard. These letters wanted to be found. These letters had been left in a lighthouse cache like a lantern in a window.

"Show me the letter again," I said. "The one dated the nineteenth."

She passed it over. I held it to the lamp, tilted it, watched the light through the fibers. Good paper. Northern paper—the stock from the Vell mills, which meant nothing, because half the coast traded in it.

"Here," I said. "This word. *Forgive.*"

"What about it?"

"The cross on the *i* in *forgive*. It's straight. Every other cross in every letter leans left. Forgers are careful for a page and then they tire, or they don't, and one stroke goes true." I set it down. "Or a man writing the word *forgive* stopped being a forger of himself for one letter and wrote it the way he actually—"

I stopped. My throat had closed on the sentence.

Ilsa reached across the table. She didn't touch my hand. She put her fingers flat on the letter, on the word, the way you'd put a hand on a grave.

"I know," she said. "I've done the same thing every night for three weeks. Found one detail that proves him and one that unmakes him, and held them both until morning."

"And which do you believe?"

She took her hand back. When she answered, her voice was steady, and that steadiness cost her—I could see the cost, the way you can see the current under ice.

"I believe the letters are real. I believe the man who wrote them is alive, or was three weeks ago. And I believe that whether Tomas Reyes is breathing or at the bottom of the channel, the names in these dispatches sold escape passages to the enemy and let the couriers who found out drown for it. That part doesn't require my faith. That part is paper."

* * *

It was past midnight when we finished. Upstairs, the house had gone quiet in the particular way of a house that was listening. I gathered the letters, and then—because I was tired, and because the evening had stripped something out of me—I made the mistake of honesty.

"I can't tell if you're telling the truth," I said.

"I know."

"Your story has one seam in it. The cousin. Rafael drowned the summer after the fig tree—everyone in Serrano knew that. But the fig tree story as Tomas told it had the cousin catching him. Breaking the fall. That's the version I got, drunk, once, from a man who never spoke of his childhood sober." I watched her. "You told it with the cousin daring him. The version a child would remember from the ground."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Then she smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen all winter.

"He told it both ways," she said. "The falling version to strangers. The catching version to friends. He told me once—it was the night before I sailed, and he'd had wine, and he said: *when you find Galloway, he'll test you. Let him. And when he catches you in the cousin story, tell him Rafael caught me. He'll know what it means.*"

The lamp hissed. The house listened.

"He caught you," I said. "In the tree. You were there."

"I was eight years old. He was my neighbor. He pulled me down out of that tree after I climbed up to prove I could, and he told me never to tell anyone a girl had to be rescued, and he told the whole street a mule story instead." Her voice frayed. "I have been carrying that man's letters against my ribs for a month, Wendell. Ask me anything else you like. But ask it knowing that I'm not his forger. I'm just the person he trusted with the last true thing he had."

I sat back. Every test I'd built had a door in it I hadn't known I'd built, and she'd walked through all of them, and I no longer knew whether that meant she was honest or that Tomas had been thorough. Perhaps, with Tomas, those had always been the same thing.

"The dispatches stay sealed," I said finally. "I don't read them. That's the rule."

"I know."

"But the harbor has three checkpoints, and I can't get a northern woman with fisherman's boots through one of them, let alone three."

"Then I'll need someone who can."

I looked at her a long moment. Then I stood, took the lamp, and went to the stairs—and stopped, because Anselm was sitting on the bottom step in the dark, sober-eyed, a chart case across his knees.

"Been listening, have you?" I said.

"Deaf as a post," Anselm said, and unrolled the chart.
