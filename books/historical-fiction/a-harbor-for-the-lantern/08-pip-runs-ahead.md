## 8. Pip Runs Ahead

The thing about being twelve in Vessarine was that nobody looked at you twice. That was the whole trick of it. Grown men got stopped, searched, questioned, made to open their coats and turn out their pockets. Boys carrying bread got a nod at best. So when Wendell came to him on the third morning after the chart night and said, "I need eyes inside the customs house," Pip had already been smiling before the sentence finished.

"You want me to talk to someone," Pip said.

"I want you to *look*," Wendell said. "Talking's extra. Count the guards on the morning shift. Find out when they change. See if there's a side door that isn't watched." He said it the way he said everything—flat, careful, like he was reading off a list he'd written three times and burned twice.

"And if I talk?"

Wendell looked at him for a long moment. That look again, the one that made Pip feel like a locked drawer somebody was deciding whether to force. "If you talk," Wendell said, "talk about bread. Talk about the weather. Talk about anything except what you're actually doing."

"I know how it works."

"I know you think you do."

That stung, because Pip had been running messages since he was nine, back when the messages were just laundry lists and love notes between houses that didn't approve of each other. He'd graduated to real things last year—a folded paper sewn into his cap brim, carried past two soldiers who were too busy arguing about tobacco to notice a skinny boy whistling. Nobody had ever told him what was in the paper. He hadn't asked. Asking was for people who wanted to get caught caring.

So he said, "Bread and weather. Got it," and Wendell gave him a coin—not payment, Wendell never called it payment, always *provision*—and Pip went out into the gray morning with his collar up and his heart going like a drum.

* * *

The customs house sat where the harbor road bent toward the water, all pale stone and tall windows, built back when the republic had money enough to make its bureaucracy beautiful. Now half the windows were boarded against broken glass and the other half held the faces of men with ledgers. There were more soldiers than usual. Pip counted without seeming to count—four at the main doors, one pacing the quay side, two smoking by the coal shed. Seven. On a Tuesday. That was already wrong; there'd been five last month, and Anselm had grumbled about even those.

The shift change was the thing Wendell wanted most, so Pip did what he did best: he found work near the door. There was a fish cart stalled outside the customs house steps, its wheel sunk in a rut, and the carter was swearing at it in three languages. Pip offered to help lift. The carter took the help and gave Pip a herring for his trouble, and for an hour Pip was a boy wrestling a wheel out of mud, entirely unremarkable, positioned twenty feet from the door with nothing to do but watch.

He learned plenty. The morning guard changed at eight by the tower bell, but the *inside* men—the clerks with the stamping tables—changed later, nearer nine, and there was a gap between when the old shift signed out and the new one settled in when the inspection table stood empty except for a bored corporal who spent most of it picking at his nails. Twenty minutes, maybe less. A gap you could drive a cart through if the cart didn't mind lying.

It was good information. Clean information. Pip turned it over in his head like a coin, feeling its weight, and it was while he was still enjoying the weight of it that he saw the girl.

She came out of the customs house side entrance with a stack of folders nearly as tall as her, red hair escaping from under a plain wool cap, and she navigated by peeking around the edge of the pile, which meant she walked into the same loose cobblestone every courier in Vessarine knew about and went down in a spray of paper.

Pip got to her before the papers got away. He'd rescued documents from gutters before—it was practically his trade—and he had them gathered and squared and handed back before she'd finished standing up, cheeks blazing.

"Thank you," she said, not looking at him. "They'll have my head if any of these are wet."

"They're dry. I checked." He held up the top folder. "This one's got a boot print on it, but that happened before you dropped it. You can tell your head to relax."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "You're the Okonkwo boy. The errand one."

"Pip. Just Pip." He shifted the herring to his other hand. "You're the clerk's daughter. Marit, right? Your father shouts your name through the window sometimes. Whole quay knows it."

"He does not shout."

"He absolutely shouts. Last week he shouted it four times before noon. I counted."

Now she laughed, quick and surprised, like it had gotten out before she could stop it. She was maybe fifteen, a year or two older than him, with ink stains up both wrists and the tired eyes of somebody who did half her father's work and got none of the credit. Pip knew that arithmetic well. He filed it away—the way you file everything—under *useful*.

"Four times," she said. "You counted."

"Somebody has to keep records around here. Seems like your family's fallen behind." He nodded at the folders. "Those look heavy."

"They're the harbor manifests. Every ship in or out this quarter." She hugged them tighter, and something in the way she held them—protective, almost tender—made Pip's stomach do a small cold turn. These were the papers. These were the exact papers that would decide whether a certain satchel full of letters lived or drowned. "My father says next quarter they're doubling the inspections. New orders from the garrison. Everyone's nervous since the northern woman story started going round."

"The northern woman," Pip said lightly. "What northern woman?"

"That's the thing, nobody knows. Soldiers came asking at our house Sunday night. Wanted to know if we'd seen anyone with an accent." Marit glanced toward the boarded windows, lowered her voice. "My father thinks they'll post extra men at all three checkpoints by week's end. Maybe tonight. There was a messenger from the garrison an hour ago—I took the receipt myself."

Pip kept his face doing nothing, which was the hardest work he'd ever done. *By week's end. Maybe tonight.* The plan they'd spent three days building was already rotting, and here was the girl handing him the news wrapped in gossip, free as bread.

"Well," he said, "sounds like a lot of shouting in your future."

"My father says if I'm going to work in this office, I should learn to be invisible." She said it without bitterness, the way you say a thing you've heard so often it stops meaning anything. "Like the good clerks. Like—" she waved vaguely at the street, at the gray city, at everyone hurrying somewhere with their heads down, "—like everybody."

"Invisible's not so bad," Pip said. "I'm excellent at it."

"You?" She looked him up and down, and there was something in the look that wasn't teasing, exactly. Something sharper. "You're the opposite of invisible. You're the kind of person people remember. You talked to me for two minutes and I already know your name and where you live."

The herring was getting slippery in his hand. "Nobody remembers me."

"I remember you," Marit said simply. "From the bread line in October. You gave your spot to the old woman with the cane and then stood at the back like you hadn't. I remember that."

And then she was gone, folders and all, through the side door, and Pip stood alone in the street with a herring and a hole in his chest where his cleverness used to be.

* * *

He didn't go straight home. He went to the breakwater first, the long black arm of stones that reached into the harbor, and sat at the end where the wind came raw off the water, and ate the herring, and thought.

Here was the thing he couldn't stop turning over: Marit remembered him. Not just today—October, the bread line, a kindness he'd done and forgotten inside a day. She'd carried it for months. Which meant Wendell was wrong, or Wendell's rules were wrong, or—worse—they were right and Pip had already broken them so many times he was made of breaks.

*Be forgettable.* That was rule one, the rule under all the rules. Be forgettable, and you live. But you couldn't be forgettable and also be the boy adults sent into the customs house yard. You couldn't be forgettable and charming. Those were two different boys and only one of them got to survive, and Pip was starting to understand that the grown-ups had picked which one he'd be without ever telling him.

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing curdling under his pride like milk left out.

They needed him *because* he was a child. That was the whole architecture of it. Wendell with his careful face and Anselm with his charts and Ilsa Brandt upstairs in the attic speaking whispers—they were all too visible, too hunted, too heavy with consequence. If any of them were caught at the customs house asking questions, it was over, chains and cells and worse. But a boy with a herring and a grin? A boy could stand in the exact center of the enemy's business and be seen straight through. His usefulness *was* his invisibility was his childhood, and every coin of it got spent whether he agreed to the exchange or not.

Nobody had lied to him. That was the worst part. He searched back through the last few days and found no lies at all—just tasks shaped like games, questions shaped like errands. *Count the guards.* *Find out when they change.* Wendell had even given him the coin and called it provision. They'd let him believe he was choosing, the way you let a child believe the game is his idea. And he'd run ahead gladly, proudly, whistling, straight into the middle of it.

He thought about Marit's father shouting her name through a window, and about Mrs. Okonkwo's ledger, and about the way Anselm had said *deaf as a post* sitting sober-eyed in the dark. Everybody in that house was playing a part, and now he knew he was playing one too, except nobody had handed him a script—he'd written it himself out of wanting to matter, and that somehow made it worse. Wanting to matter. That was the hook in his cheek. That was how you caught a boy: not with money, not with threats, but with the chance to be the one who counted the guards, the one who knew things, the one the adults leaned down to listen to.

The tide was coming in below him, filling the harbor basin, lifting the fishing boats on their moorings. Somewhere out past the breakwater was open water, and beyond that the northern islands where a dead man might be writing letters, and beyond that whatever country still printed newspapers that told the truth. All of it depended on papers moving through checkpoints, and checkpoints depended on gaps in shifts, and gaps depended on boys who could count.

Pip threw the herring bones into the water and watched the gulls come down screaming.

When he finally walked home, he made himself do it slowly, practicing his face. Bored. Hungry. Twelve. By the time he reached Mrs. Okonkwo's door he had it perfect, and he went inside ready to deliver his report like a professional, clean and complete: seven guards, change at eight, twenty minutes at the inspection table, side door unwatched.

But there was something else in the kitchen when he got there, something that stopped him on the threshold with his report still folded neat as paper behind his teeth. Wendell stood at the window with his back to the room, very still. Anselm sat at the table, and the chart case was gone, and in its place lay a folded slip of garrison paper that Pip recognized instantly, because Marit had taken a receipt for one just like it an hour ago.

Mrs. Okonkwo looked up at him from the stove, and her eyes went soft and sharp at once, the way they did when she already knew.

"Garrison posted the new orders at noon," Anselm said quietly. "Checkpoints doubled. Starting tomorrow."

Wendell turned from the window, and his face was the locked-drawer face, but underneath it Pip could see something moving fast, recalculation, the whole plan tearing itself apart and rebuilding—and Wendell looked at him and said, "Pip. How fast can you find out what the new watch rotations are?"

And Pip, who had spent the whole walk home learning what it cost to be useful, heard himself answer, "Give me until dark."
