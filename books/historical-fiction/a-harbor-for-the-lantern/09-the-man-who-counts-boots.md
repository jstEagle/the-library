## 9. The Man Who Counts Boots

The summons came the way bad weather comes in winter—not as an event but as a change in pressure, a heaviness in the air that made the dogs stop barking and the fishwives look seaward. A boy in garrison gray appeared at Mrs. Okonkwo's door on Tuesday morning, handed her a folded paper, and left without waiting for a reply. She brought it to Wendell where he sat over his second cup of tea, and she set it down beside his elbow without comment, the way she might have set down the salt.

*Inspector Hale requests the pleasure of Mr. Galloway's company. Four o'clock. The customs house annex.*

There was no pleasure in it. There was never any pleasure in it.

Wendell read it twice, then folded it along its crease and tucked it into his coat, and spent the next six hours doing what he always did before walking into a room where a man might decide whether he existed or didn't: he prepared. He laid out his most forgettable clothes—the brown wool, the scuffed boots, the muffler the color of old dishwater. He rehearsed his answers to questions he couldn't predict until they sounded like they'd never been rehearsed at all. And he ate a full lunch, because hunger made a man's hands unsteady, and unsteady hands were a confession written on the body for anyone literate enough to read them.

At half past three he went downstairs. Mrs. Okonkwo was in the hall, ostensibly polishing the brass fittings on the doorframe, though Wendell had lived with her long enough to know that she polished brass only when she was worried about someone. She'd developed the habit during the purges of '41, when half her boarders were men the government wanted to forget, and she had never quite given it up.

"He counts your boots," she said, not looking up from her cloth.

Wendell stopped on the bottom stair. "I'm sorry?"

"Hale. Everyone in this quarter knows it. First thing he does, any inspection, is count the boots by the door and count the plates at supper and see if the numbers agree." She straightened, tucked the cloth into her apron. "A man who notices things like that isn't a man you can lie to with a clean collar and a dull face. You want to be careful how you sit in front of him."

"How should I sit?"

"Like a man with nothing in his pockets," she said, "because he'll already know what's there anyway."

He wanted to ask her what she meant—whether she knew something, whether she'd heard—but the door swung open behind him and Pip came barreling in from the street with cold-flushed cheeks and snow melting in his collar, and the moment folded itself up and put itself away, the way moments did in a house that survived by folding things away.

"Four o'clock," Wendell said.

Mrs. Okonkwo looked at him then, directly, and her eyes were the steadiest thing in the whole city. "Tea's still hot if you're back by five."

* * *

The customs house annex stood at the landward edge of the harbor district, a building that had once been a merchant's mansion and now wore its occupation like a borrowed coat, slightly too tight in the shoulders. Wendell passed the first checkpoint at ten minutes to four—a sentry who knew his face by now, or knew his papers, which amounted to nearly the same thing—and gave the man the flat nod that meant *nothing here, nothing to see*, and the sentry gave back the same nod, and Wendell added him silently to the ledger he kept behind his eyes: Corporal Voss, gate two, tired, underpaid, three weeks from rotation, could be bought with money or flattery but not yet desperate enough to sell anyone.

That was the work. That had always been the work. While other couriers memorized routes and passwords, Wendell memorized people—the small economies of their boredom, the specific weight of their suspicion, the exact point at which a guard's diligence curdled into resentment of his own superiors and became, therefore, useful. He had walked these streets for eleven years carrying sealed things, and he was alive because he had learned to see the men who were supposed to see him.

But Hale was different. Hale had never been supposed to see him. Hale simply did.

The inspector was at his desk in a first-floor room that had once been the mansion's library, shelves stripped of books and filled instead with ledgers, manifest copies, correspondence intercepted and copied and filed. He did not look up when Wendell entered. He poured tea from a pot on the sideboard—two cups, already waiting, which meant the invitation had been decided before the boy with the folded paper ever reached Mrs. Okonkwo's door—and he let the silence stretch until Wendell understood it was furniture, part of the room's design, meant to be sat in.

"You're punctual," Hale said at last.

"I try to be."

"Punctuality is honesty wearing a watch." Hale gestured at the chair across the desk. He was somewhere past sixty, built like the doorframe of a working building—nothing decorative, all load-bearing—with a face weathered down to its essential structures: brow, cheekbone, jaw. His eyes were pale gray and missed nothing, which everyone said, and which was true, and which was also the least important thing about them. What mattered was that they missed nothing *kindly*. Once. They used to.

Wendell sat. He put his hands loosely in his lap, empty, visible. He did not think about the letters hidden inside the lining of his trunk four streets away, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping like coals.

"You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to see you," Hale agreed. He slid one of the cups across. "Drink that. It's real leaf, not the acorn mash they're issuing. Consider it back pay."

"For what?"

Hale smiled, and the smile had no particular warmth in it, but it had precision, and precision from Hale was worse. "For the pier at Selkow, March of '43. For the patrol boat that came around the breakwater at dawn with its searchlight already lit. For the fact that you are sitting in that chair instead of at the bottom of the harbor with your dispatch case chained to your wrist, which is where the Republic's accounting would have put you, because dead couriers can't compromise their routes." He lifted his own cup. "I falsified the searchlight log that morning. I said it was unmanned. It wasn't unmanned. I was on it, and I saw you, and I chose not to see you, and I've been paying for that choice in installments ever since. So drink your tea, Mr. Galloway. You owe me at least that."

Wendell drank the tea. It was very good. His hands were steady, and he was proud of that in a distant, clinical way, the way a surgeon might be proud of an incision.

"That debt is settled," he said. "Whatever it cost you, tell me and I'll carry it, or pay it, or—"

"It cost me my certainty." Hale set his cup down with a small ceramic click. "Do you know what I was before all this? A harbor master's assistant in Corvess. Twenty-two years counting cargo. I know what a ship weighs by the depth of its waterline, Mr. Galloway. I know what a man carries by the way he crosses a room. And for eleven years you have been crossing rooms in this city like a man carrying nothing at all, and nobody carries nothing at all through three winters of war, so either you are the most innocent fool alive or you are the best courier the Republic ever fielded, and I have known for years which one I believed. What has cost me is deciding whether knowing it obligated me to do anything."

"And does it?"

"That depends entirely," Hale said, "on what you're carrying these days."

*Nothing*, Wendell's mouth offered. *Pension paperwork. Letters from my late aunt.* But he didn't say it. Saying it would be an insult between them, and insults between men who share a searchlight are heavier than lies. Instead he said, "You didn't bring me here to arrest me. If you could, you would have. You brought me here because you need something, and you're trying to find out whether I'm safe to ask."

Something moved behind the gray eyes—approval, maybe, or disappointment that approval was warranted. Hale rose, went to the stripped shelves, and pulled a ledger. He set it on the desk but did not open it.

"Two years ago," he said, "a body came out of the channel off the northern breakwater. Winter drowning. No papers—the tide took whatever he'd carried, or he'd gotten rid of it beforehand, we never established which. Fisherman found him tangled in net line near the light. Face in the water three days, so identification was—" He paused, and the pause was deliberate; Wendell could feel it shaped in the air between them like a hand. "Identification was partly dental, partly a ring. And partly a witness statement from a woman who claimed to know him."

Wendell kept his breathing level. Somewhere below his sternum, something old and heavy shifted its position, the way ice shifts on a river, making a sound only the river hears.

"The name on file," Hale continued, "was Reyes. Tomas Reyes. Courier. Presumed running documents north when the sea took him. There was a funeral of sorts—an administrative one, the kind where nobody cries because nobody's sure who they're mourning. The file was closed." He opened the ledger now, turned it so Wendell could see, though at that distance the columns were only columns, gray marks on gray paper. "Files don't stay closed anymore. Not since the reorganization. Everything gets reopened and reindexed, and last month a clerk going through the old records found a discrepancy. The witness statement. The woman described a scar—left forearm, from a boating winch, she said. She was very specific. Very certain."

Hale closed the ledger.

"When we exhumed the body last week to confirm the index entry, we found a left forearm without a mark on it. Smooth as a church candle. So now there is a question in my files, Mr. Galloway, and questions in my files have begun, lately, to require answers from men like you. The question is this: who drowned in Tomas Reyes's grave? Because somebody did drown—that part is certain. A mother in Corvess is missing a son who fits the description. But whoever he was, he was not wearing the ring we buried, or the woman lied, and either way—" Hale spread his hands, palms up, an old-fashioned gesture that belonged to some earlier and more honest century. "Either way, a courier's death record is false, and false death records are the currency of a very particular trade. Your trade."

The fire ticked in the grate. Out on the water, unseen, a foghorn spoke once and got no answer.

"I hadn't heard," Wendell said. "About Reyes."

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "You hadn't heard. Though you knew him. That's in your file too—or it would be, if your file weren't so suspiciously thin. Two men from the same quarter, same years, same trade, and no correspondence between the records. Thin files make me itch." He leaned back. "Here is what I will offer you, because I owed you a searchlight and I intend for us to be square after today. When the answer to my question surfaces—and it will surface; everything in this city floats up eventually—I would rather hear it from you than from an informant. Informants get people arrested. Friends get people warned. Come to me before I have to come to you, and we will both survive this winter. Wait until I knock on Mrs. Okonkwo's door, and neither of us may."

*Mrs. Okonkwo's door.* He had said it lightly, an aside, a detail. But Wendell had spent eleven years listening for details placed like caltrops on a road, and this one rang against his teeth. Hale knew the boarding house. Hale counted boots.

"I'll remember the offer," Wendell said, standing.

"See that you do." Hale remained seated. Then, as Wendell reached the door: "One more thing. Galloway."

Wendell turned.

"The woman who identified the body. Brandt, Ilsa Brandt, a schoolteacher from the northern islands. She signed the statement, collected a burial stipend, and vanished the same week. We assumed grief, or flight, or worse." Hale lifted his cup again, and over its rim his gray eyes were as patient as water wearing down stone. "This morning a fishing boat out of the northern islands put in at the fish quay before dawn. My men interviewed the crew, routine matter. One of them mentioned passengers they'd carried last week. A woman. Mid-thirties. Northern accent. Paid in old coin."

The fire ticked. The foghorn spoke again, closer now.

"Small world, the harbor," Hale said pleasantly. "Give my regards to Mrs. Okonkwo."
