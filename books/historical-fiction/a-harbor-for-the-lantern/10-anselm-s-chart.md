## 10. Anselm's Chart

Anselm Voss had not drawn a chart in eleven years, and his hands remembered it better than his prayers.

He worked by lamplight in the back room of the Anchor's Rest, the tavern where he'd run a tab since before the occupation and paid it never. The table was scarred oak, sticky with old beer, and he cleared a space with his forearm the way he used to clear a bridge before a pilotage—no hesitation, everything movable shoved aside. Then he unrolled the paper he'd bought that morning from the stationer on Cordwainer Street, who sold him wrapping paper and asked no questions because Anselm had bought wrapping paper from him every month for eleven years and never once wrapped anything.

The harbor came back to him under the pencil.

That was the thing about shallows. Charts lied—charts were drawn in fair weather by survey crews in clean boots—but memory didn't lie when the memory had been bought with groundings. The Middle Shoal had migrated east half a cable since the war began; dredging stopped, silt ran where it wanted. Anselm drew its new spine with three short strokes and put a cross where *Pelan* had touched bottom in '41, a night he still tasted in his teeth. The Gullet, between the shoal and the breakwater arm: passable at half tide, three knots of current at full flood, and the garrison's patrol boat couldn't follow through it without scraping her paint down to the iron. He marked the leading marks—a chimney, a broken mast, the white gate of the Catholic cemetery that wasn't white anymore but you could see it from the water all the same.

There. The smuggler's channel. His channel. The one he'd run silk and brandy and men through for thirty years, back when running things through this harbor was a trade and not treason.

His pencil hovered over the entrance and did not move.

Because here was the problem, the one he'd been chewing since Ilsa Brandt arrived dripping on Mrs. Okonkwo's step like something the sea had coughed up with an opinion: he did not know what he was carrying. Oh, he knew what Wendell carried in general—the man had been carrying sealed things into and out of this harbor since before the war had a name, and Anselm had piloted boats around him long enough to recognize the type. Quiet men with heavy coats. But this was different. This had the smell of endgame on it, and Anselm was too old, too fond of his own skin, and too honest with himself to run the Gullet at half tide on behalf of a mystery.

He'd told Wendell to come at nine. It was now twenty past, and Anselm had arranged and rearranged six pencils in a row and drunk nothing, which Wendell would notice, because Wendell noticed everything except when people were being kind to him.

The knock came. Two soft, one hard. Couriers knocked like that everywhere; Anselm had heard it in ports from here to the Equatorial Strait.

"Door's unlocked," Anselm called, "and the beer is warm, which is the only honest thing left in this city."

Wendell Galloway came in the way he always came in, as if the room were a checkpoint and he'd already memorized the exits. Thin man. Careful coat. Eyes that went lamp, window, door, Anselm's hands, then finally Anselm's face, and even then only briefly, like looking at the sun or an accusation. He sat across the table without being asked, which from Wendell was practically an embrace.

"You've been drawing," Wendell said.

"I've been remembering." Anselm turned the chart so it faced him. Watched Wendell read it upside down, fast, because of course he read upside down; couriers did. Watched him understand it—the flicker at the corner of his mouth when he traced the Gullet from the shoal to the cemetery gate. "Half tide, tomorrow morning, if your business sails when they say it sails. Patrol boat can't follow. Fort Vessarine's guns can't depress far enough to reach the middle of it. One hour of water where nobody official can touch you." He tapped the pencil against the entrance mark. "One hour. And I'll pilot it myself."

"Anselm—"

"For free," Anselm said, and enjoyed the way that landed on Wendell like a slap, because the man could accept a bed, a meal, a smuggled letter, but a gift sent him into some spiritual crisis he had to go sit alone about. "For free. Which should tell you there's a price after all. Just not money."

Wendell sat very still. It was a talent of his, sitting still. Anselm had once watched him stand in a bread line during a strafing run without shifting his weight, and had understood then that he was dealing with a man who had decided his body was government property and rationed accordingly.

"The letters," Anselm said.

"What about them."

"You know what about them. You've been carrying them around this house for four days like a hot coal in your coat. Pip says you sleep sitting up. Mrs. Okonkwo says—" he waved a hand— "well, she says plenty, but the point stands. I'll take you through the Gullet. I'll stake thirty years of knowing these shallows and my neck besides. But I'm not a courier, boy. I'm a pilot. A pilot has to know what's in his ship. If they board us, if Hale stops me on the quay and asks me what I'm running, I need to know whether I'm lying about fish or lying about lives. It changes how you lie. Everything about you changes, depending."

Wendell looked at the chart again. Anselm let him.

"That's the price?" Wendell said. "You want to know what's in them."

"I want you to know what's in them. Out loud. To me." Anselm leaned back and put both hands flat on the table, an old man's gesture, an honest man's gesture, rehearsed in front of a mirror out of vanity and then kept because it worked. "You've read them. Don't insult me. Four days sleeping sitting up—you don't do that over cargo, you do that over words. So here's my thinking, plain: I've got maybe two winters left in these knees and one good tide a week in these waters, and I'll spend neither carrying secrets for a man who doesn't trust me enough to say them out loud. Not because I'm sentimental. Because a secret that's never been spoken is a bomb, Wendell. The moment you say it, it becomes information, and information can be navigated by. I can't navigate a bomb."

Silence. The tavern noise came through the wall—someone playing a fiddle badly, someone worse at singing—and Anselm was grateful for it, because silence between two careful men is a contest and he'd already spent his patience on the chart.

"They're Tomas Reyes's letters," Wendell said at last. His voice was level. Too level, the way water is level right before it goes over the edge.

"I know whose letters. Ilsa Brandt nearly wept them at me over soup. Alive on the northern islands, she says. Writing dated three weeks ago. A drowned man with excellent penmanship." Anselm kept his voice dry. "I also know what Hale told everyone two years ago, because Hale likes telling people things. They pulled a courier out of the narrows. Identified him. There was a funeral, of sorts."

"There was a funeral," Wendell said quietly. "I didn't go."

"Why not?"

"Because I couldn't decide whether going would be loyalty or suicide." Wendell's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I stayed home. Mrs. Okonkwo made a fish pie that night. Nobody mentioned him. It was the loneliest meal of my life, and I've eaten alone in four countries."

Anselm felt something move in his chest that he declined to examine. He tapped the chart instead. "So. Are they his? Truly?"

"I don't know."

"Then say what you do know. Start at the beginning and don't skip the parts that shame you. Those are usually the load-bearing parts."

And so Wendell Galloway, who had spent years perfecting the art of being forgotten while standing in plain view, broke the only rule Anselm had ever seen him keep.

He reached into his coat and brought out the letters—not the diplomatic packet, the other ones, the private ones, wrapped in oilcloth—and unwrapped them on the scarred oak table next to a chart of everything Anselm loved about the sea.

* * *

"Wendell Galloway, courier fourth class," Wendell said, as if introducing himself to a stranger, "carries sealed dispatches and does not read them. That's the whole discipline. Never read, never linger, never be remembered. The seal is sacred. You protect the seal with your life and you never learn what your life purchased. Twenty-two years."

"I know your rules. I've watched you keep them."

"Then watch me break them." He unfolded the topmost letter with two fingers, delicately, the way Anselm imagined he'd handle a live shell. "This one is dated the ninth of last month. It's addressed to the Assembly-in-Exile care of a fishing cooperative in the north. It's signed T. Reyes."

Anselm leaned in. The ink was brown-black, the slant of it aggressive, leaning right like a man walking into wind. Even upside down, it looked alive.

"It names names," Wendell said. "Harbormaster Corvus. Deputy Quartermaster Lenz. It says—" and here his voice changed, dropped into something flatter, recitation, armor of tone, and Anselm understood he must have read this part forty times already and still needed the armor— "it says that for fourteen months the escape routes out of this harbor have been sold. Not compromised. Sold. Families pay for passage, the passage puts them aboard, and the boats are met outside the lights by enemy picket ships that were told exactly where to wait. The passengers are taken. The fees are kept. Some of the boats come back empty and are reported lost with all hands."

Anselm sat back slowly. Thirty years of harbor knowledge rearranged itself in his head, click by click, like a lock opening. The lost boats. He'd heard about the lost boats. Everyone had. The *Merrow*'s daughter. The family of seven off the *Kindly Fortune*. Weather, said the official reports. Winter weather.

"How many," he said.

"The letter counts eleven boats. Forty-three people." Wendell's thumb pressed white against the paper's edge. "It lists the dates. The tides they left on. It lists which pilot bookings were cancelled afterward—cancelled by Corvus's office, retroactively, so no licensed pilot would be on record having been near them."

Anselm looked at his own chart. At the neat penciled cross where the *Pelan* had touched ground. At the Gullet, the channel he'd offered to run tomorrow morning, free of charge, for a mystery.

"So the northern islands," he said. "If Reyes is alive up there—"

"Then he's been collecting testimony from survivors. Fishermen who found wreckage. People who saw the picket ships waiting. Every name in these letters is corroborated somewhere else, by someone else, in another hand. Ilsa brought those too. Eleven letters. It's a ledger, Anselm. It's a court of inquiry written in advance, addressed to the free press abroad, and it will hang Corvus and Lenz in whatever courts still exist after this war ends—which is why Corvus wants it, and why Hale's garrison wants it, and why we're carrying it through a harbor where every third person would sell us for a warm coat."

"And the forgery question."

Wendell laughed once, short, no humor in it. "Yes. The forgery question. A dead man writing letters. Convenient, isn't it—evidence arrives precisely when the republic needs discrediting most, in the hand of its most famous lost courier. If it's bait, then whoever planted it knows exactly what I'll do, because Tomas trained half of us, and I am walking, step by step, into the shape of the trap they built for me specifically." He rubbed his eyes. "I've run it a hundred times. The hand is right. The dates are right. The details are right in ways a forger wouldn't bother with—he dots his i's like crosses, he crosses his sevens, he uses the old republican double-dashes in his signatures, which haven't been taught since the Academy closed. Forgers forge what gets attention. Nobody forges punctuation."

"Unless they wanted you to conclude exactly that."

"Yes," Wendell said. "Unless that. Thank you for saying it out loud so I didn't have to."

* * *

The candle had burned down two inches. Anselm got up, found the bottle behind the loose brick—he kept it there on principle, since hiding drink from oneself seemed the only honest drunkenness left—and poured two glasses without asking. Wendell took his and held it and drank nothing, which figured.

"There's more," Wendell said. "You've earned the rest, God help us both."

He drew out the last letter. Smaller fold. Different paper—cheap stuff, the kind schoolchildren use. He held it a moment before opening, and Anselm watched a man of twenty-two years' discipline come apart at the seams in total silence, stitch by stitch, like watching a hull fail in slow motion.

"This one isn't addressed to the Assembly," Wendell said. "It's addressed to me. It was sewn inside the lining of the official packet. Ilsa didn't know it was there. Nobody knew. I found it the first night, and I have read it every night since, and I have told no one, because telling makes it real."

"Tomas was your teacher," Anselm said. Not a guess.

"Tomas was everyone's teacher. But me especially. I was nineteen and starving and he taught me the three rules. Never read, never linger, never be remembered. He said the rules were armor, and grief was the arrow that got through armor, so: no attachments, no partners, no graves to visit." Wendell opened the cheap paper. His hands were steady, which was somehow worse. "Two years ago they pulled a courier out of the narrows and identified him as Reyes and buried him, and I followed the rules, and I did not go to the funeral, and I ate fish pie."

"Read it," Anselm said gently. "Out loud. That's the price, remember. All of it."

And Wendell read.

"*Wendell. If you're reading this, then Ilsa found you, which means I won her argument eventually, and I'm sorry for that, she argues like a riptide.*"

His voice caught on the second sentence and he steadied it and went on, and Anselm listened the way he listened to the sea in bad weather, for what was underneath the sound.

"*I did not drown. I want that in your head plainly, before anything else: I am alive, and I chose to disappear, and the choice was made in the worst hour of my life and I have regretted it in the best hours since. The officers were closing on the northern network. There was a list. My death removed a name from it and bought eleven people their escape, and I told myself that was worth it, and some days I still believe it.*

*I did not tell you because you would have come with me, and you were better used where you were. That is the truth and it is also a coward's arithmetic, and I apologize for both.*

*Here is what matters. The betrayals are not ours. Read the enclosed names. Corvus sells the passages; Lenz supplies the schedules; the picket captains are paid in recovered cargo. I have spent two years gathering proof from the survivors who made it to the islands, and Ilsa Brandt carried it south because I can no longer travel—I'll spare you the details, but the sea takes payment for what it gives, and I have paid in full.*

*Wendell, listen. You will distrust this letter. Good. Distrust everything, verify everything, that is what I taught you. But when you finish verifying, get it aboard whatever ship is leaving, and do not stay in that harbor. The rules kept you alive, but hear your old teacher now: the rules were for wartime, and the war is ending, and a man who is only armor when the fighting stops is just an empty suit clanking around.*

*Forgive me or don't. Dot your i's properly. —T.R.*"

The room was very quiet. Downstairs the bad fiddler had given up; a clock somewhere struck ten with the reluctance of all clocks in occupied cities.

"He's dying," Wendell said. His voice had gone rough. "Or crippled, or exiled on some rock, but he wrote *the sea takes payment*, and Tomas never wasted a phrase. He's not coming back. These letters aren't a ghost waving. They're a testament. He wrote them the way a man writes a will, and he trusted Ilsa Brandt—total stranger to him before this year—to carry his will through three checkpoints, because the couriers are all dead or gone, and I'm what's left." He set the letter down on the chart, on the penciled channel, on the cross marking a grounding eleven winters old. "And I don't know if it's really him, Anselm. I want it to be him so badly that I cannot trust my own judgment, which is the one instrument I've relied on for twenty-two years. That's where you find me. That's what's in the boat."

Anselm drank his glass down and poured another and considered, for a long moment, saying nothing further, which was his usual policy regarding other men's griefs.

"To hell with it," he said instead. "I'll tell you what I know, since we're confessing. Two years ago, October, I was coming in through the Gullet at dawn, running a quiet little contraband of coffee, and I saw the recovery boat bring in what they pulled from the narrows. I was close enough to see the body before they covered it. It was not Tomas Reyes. Wrong build. Reyes carried his weight in the shoulders—all that Academy rowing. This man was narrow as a herring. Same coat, though. Someone dressed a corpse in a courier's coat and gave it to the city for a funeral, and Inspector Hale stood at the quay looking at that body with an expression I have thought about ever since. He looked like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like." He shrugged. "Maybe that means nothing. Maybe Hale dressed the corpse himself. But I've piloted this harbor for fifty years, Wendell, and I know what every drowned man looks like, and that one wasn't your teacher."

Wendell had gone very pale, and very still, and then—not steady, but something deeper than steady. Settled. Like ballast finding the bottom of the hold.

"Then it's real," he said. "Or real enough to bet on. The letters are his, the betrayal is real, and the harbormaster sold forty-three people to the enemy." He folded the letters along their old creases, wrapped them in oilcloth, and put them away inside his coat, next to whatever was left of his heart. "All right. Pilot, what do you need from me?"

"You just gave it to me." Anselm rolled the chart, quick and expert, and tied it with twine. "Now I know what I'm lying about if they stop us, and it's worth lying well for. Half tide, day after tomorrow. We go in with the fishing fleet—eleven boats, who counts eleven boats—and you and the Brandt woman walk aboard as manifest clerks, which Mrs. Okonkwo is already scheming about, don't ask me how I know." He stood, knees cracking like rigging in a blow, and clapped Wendell on the shoulder, feeling the man flinch and then, remarkably, not pull away. "One more thing, gratis. Tomas told you not to stay in the harbor. When this is done, think hard about whether obeying him one last time is grief or wisdom. There's a difference. Pilots learn to tell them apart or they die."

Wendell paused at the door, hand on the latch, and for once he looked back directly, eyes to eyes, no memorizing involved.

"Anselm. Why did you offer the channel before you knew any of this?"

"Because four days ago I watched you stop eating," Anselm said, "and I'm an old fool who's tired of watching careful men carry bombs. Now get out. I've got a chart to memorize and a tide to pray at, and you've got a harbormaster's dinner to think about—because if you believe those letters, then Corvus is going to be standing in a ballroom this week congratulating himself on forty-three murders, and I'd think very hard, lad, about whether you can look at that face and keep your courier's calm."

He heard Wendell go. The outer door, then the street, then nothing—Wendell even walked forgettably, the magnificent bastard.

Anselm spread the chart again under the lamp and began, from memory, to draw the patrol boat's schedule in the margin, and thought about narrow corpses in borrowed coats, and about inspectors doing arithmetic they didn't like, and about the fact that in four days' time everything any of them loved would fit inside one satchel crossing one shallow channel at half tide.

Outside, far off toward the water, the foghorn sounded once, low and patient, like something waiting.
