## 11. Midnight at the Customs House

The fishwife's name was Marta, and she had been dead for six years.

That was the first thing Ilsa liked about her. A dead woman's papers were safer than a living woman's—no one could write to Marta Voss, no one could invite her to a cousin's wedding, no one could stand in line behind her at the customs house and say, *Marta? Is that you? You've put on weight.* The second thing Ilsa liked was that Marta had been, by all accounts, unpleasant: loud, argumentative, known for haggling over half-coppers and winning. A woman nobody missed was a woman nobody looked for.

Anselm had procured the identity from a fishmonger on the west quay who owed him money and feared him slightly more than he owed. Mrs. Okonkwo had provided the rest—the smell of it, mostly. For two days Ilsa had slept in a room above the kitchen where the boarding of the walls had soaked up thirty years of brine, tallow, and fish guts, until her own skin began to believe it belonged to the trade. She had rubbed fish oil into her knuckles. She had learned to swear in the dock cant, which was almost but not quite the northern dialect she'd grown up with, the vowels flattened like stones in a riverbed.

Now she stood in the queue outside the customs house at quarter past midnight, a basket of herring on her hip, waiting to be weighed, taxed, and waved through to the pier, and she discovered that all the rehearsal in the world does not prepare you for the moment a soldier's lantern swings toward your face.

*You are Marta Voss. You are forty-nine years old. Your husband drowned off the Sable Rocks. You hate the garrison because they tax you like thieves and you have never once been polite about saying so.*

The queue moved. Ilsa moved with it.

There were perhaps twenty people ahead of her, most of them night-market porters hauling crates of winter stock down to the last boats before the freeze, a few fishermen with the day's catch gone gray and stiff in the cold. The customs house itself was a squat granite building that had once been a counting office for the republic's grain fleet; now the republic's seal above the door had been chiseled over with the garrison's cross-and-keys, badly, so that both marks showed like a scar over an old wound. Lanterns hung from iron brackets on either side of the entrance. Behind a plank table set across the doorway sat three clerks and a soldier, and behind them, through the open doors, the great hall yawned with filing cabinets and the smell of lamp oil.

Three clerks. That was worse than Wendell's map had promised. He had drawn it for her on the back of a laundry list two nights ago, his handwriting small and exact as everything else about him: *Clerk one is old, half-blind, waves people through if their papers are in order. Clerk two is young and bored. Clerk three—* he had tapped the pencil twice *—clerk three reads faces. Avoid clerk three.*

The queue advanced. One step. Another.

Ilsa made herself count herrings instead of heartbeats. Thirty-one in the basket, counted by hand at the boarding house, because a fishwife who didn't know her own catch was a fishwife with something else to hide. The satchel was beneath the herring, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in sacking, flat as a hymnal and about as heavy. The letters of Tomas Reyes—dead man's living hand—lay against her hip through all that padding, and she could feel them the way you feel a burn: not constantly, but every time you forgot, all at once.

She had crossed open water in a winter storm to bring them here. She would not lose them at a plank table to a boy with a stamp.

*Next.*

The old clerk beckoned her forward with two fingers, unhurried, and Ilsa shuffled up on Marta's bad knee—which she had practiced, too, a lurching gait that kept her head down and her face angled away—and set the basket on the table with a thump.

"Herrings," she said, in the dock cant, flat vowels, "thirty-one, and don't tell me thirty because your scale's been drinking."

The old clerk snorted. Good. Marta Voss would have said exactly that. He hooked the basket with one finger, glanced at it with eyes gone milky at the edges, and reached for his ledger without really seeing her at all.

Then the second clerk leaned over.

He was younger than Wendell by a few years, with the particular alertness of a man who has just been promoted and wants everyone to know it deserves keeping. His eyes went from the basket to her hands—she'd remembered to keep them grimy, knuckles cracked—and then down, past the table's edge, to her boots.

Ilsa felt the moment happen the way you feel ice crack underfoot.

Her boots were wrong. She had known this, distantly, in the way you know a thing and file it under *later*. Northern boots. Stitched in the northern style, double-seamed at the toe, the leather oiled dark instead of waxed brown, because in the north you dressed for sleet, not salt spray. Every fishing wife on this coast wore boots that came apart at the seams inside two winters and got mended with tar and twine. Her boots would outlive the war.

"Those are good boots," the young clerk said.

The old clerk kept writing. The soldier by the door shifted his weight, bored, lantern swinging.

"Marta Voss," Ilsa said, and gave him the dead woman's address on Tanner's Row, and watched his eyes come up from her boots to her face with the slow interest of a cat that has heard something move in the wall.

"Where'd a fishwife get boots stitched like that?"

"My sister sent them." This was true, or true enough; the boots had been Ilsa's sister's idea, bought in a northern market town the week before everything fell. "Before the roads shut. She always thought money grows on trees, my sister. God rest her."

The lie about the sister arrived smoothly, and Ilsa understood, with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with courage, that lying well was its own kind of drowning—you did it once and then the current had you and all you could do was keep swimming and hope the far shore came before your lungs did.

"Your sister," the clerk said. "Northern?"

"Tanner's Row," Ilsa said. "Born and bred."

"I mean the accent." He smiled. It wasn't an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. Kind men were the dangerous ones; cruel men you could predict. "It slides a bit, doesn't it. When you're tired."

Behind him, through the open doors, a clock in the customs hall struck the half hour. Twelve thirty. The tide turned at two ten. The ship that mattered would take on its final manifest between one and one forty, and after that the window closed for another day, and after enough days there would be no window at all, only winter, only the reprisals Hale had promised were coming, only the long silence of a city that had stopped writing things down.

*I have come too far to drown in the shallows.*

"Everyone's tired at midnight," Ilsa said, and let Marta's voice crack on the word *midnight*, let her shoulders slump, let the basket's handle grind into her hip the way it genuinely had been doing for twenty minutes. "Are you going to tax my fish or interview it? Because I've got a boat waiting and a husband who's already spent what I'm earning tonight."

The old clerk looked up from his ledger for the first time. Something passed between him and the young one—not agreement, exactly. Assessment. The old man had been weighing fish and women for thirty years, and Ilsa watched him weigh hers: the grime, the knee, the genuine fury, the boots.

The boots again.

"Open the basket," the young clerk said.

* * *

Here is what Ilsa Brandt knew about terror, having met it several times now, formally, like an acquaintance you keep running into at funerals: terror does not feel like running or screaming. Terror feels like standing very still in a loud room while your body tries to do arithmetic without you. *Thirty-one herrings. Oilcloth. Sacking. If he lifts the top layer, he sees fish. If he lifts the second layer, he sees a dead man's handwriting and I die in a customs house at midnight and everything Tomas wrote dies with me.*

The soldier drifted over from the door, drawn by nothing except the gravity of trouble. Lantern light pooled on the table between them.

The clerk took the basket's edges in both hands.

And Ilsa did the bravest and stupidest thing she had ever done, which was to speak first.

"Gently," she snapped. "Gently, boy, or you'll wear my supper home. Those are gutted and packed in salt layers like any sane woman packs them, and if you dig through them like a gull through a dustbin you'll have fish oil in your ledger ink and your supervisor will have your hide for ruining a page, because I've seen how you lot write, smudge-fingered, every one of you—"

She heard herself doing it and marveled. She was scolding him. She was becoming Marta Voss so completely that some shuttered back room of her mind had simply handed over the controls, and underneath the performance her heart was slamming so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

The clerk flushed. Twenty-two years old, promoted too recently, scolded by a fishwife in front of a soldier and an old man who was very carefully not smiling.

He dug anyway. Of course he dug. He plunged his hand into the salt and herring and pulled up a fistful of silver bodies, dripping, and Ilsa watched the oilcloth corner rise with them—one inch, two—before the weight of wet fish dragged it back down, and the clerk, revolted, shaking the herring off his fingers, said:

"Count them yourself if you want. Thirty-one. Tax's four coppers, and—" he wiped his hand on a rag with enormous dignity, "—four more for the mess."

"Four coppers for *your* mess," Ilsa said, digging in Marta's apron pocket, producing coins she had counted twice, "when you're the one elbow-deep in it. My sister was right about one thing. They'll let anyone sit at a desk these days."

The old clerk laughed. Short, surprised, gone as fast as it came, but it changed something in the room. The soldier lost interest and drifted back to the door. And the young clerk stamped Marta Voss's paper—once, hard, with the vindictive precision of a man who wished the stamp were a slap—and shoved the basket back across the table.

"Move along."

Ilsa took the basket. Took the stamped paper. Turned on the bad knee, the lurching gait, head down, toward the pier side of the checkpoint, where the night air came in off the black water smelling of tar and rope—

"Wait."

Every joint in her body locked.

"The boots," the clerk called after her. Not angry. Almost friendly. "Tell your sister next time to buy local. Half the north walks around this city in those double-stitched things like they own the place. We notice. Some of us notice."

Ilsa turned back, one shoulder dipped, and gave him Marta Voss's flattest look.

"You want my boots too, is that it? Four coppers for the fish, four for the mess, and now the shoes off my feet. There's a word for your customs house and I'll let you guess it." And she limped on, out into the wind, and did not breathe properly again until she had passed the coal sheds and rounded the first warehouse corner and the customs house lanterns were swallowed by the dark.

* * *

Wendell was waiting where he said he would be, in the lee of a beached dinghy overturned on blocks, and he read her face before she said anything, because of course he did. He was a man who had survived five years of occupation by reading rooms.

"He saw the boots."

"He saw the boots." Ilsa set the basket down and her hands, released from duty, began to shake all at once, delayed and useless. "He didn't find the letters. He counted my fish and he insulted my sister and he let me through. But he'll remember me, Wendell. Not my face—he barely looked at my face—but the boots, and the accent that *slides when I'm tired*, and Marta Voss of Tanner's Row who is going to walk through that checkpoint every time we need the pier route, and one night he'll ask a question in the northern dialect just to see if Marta answers it, and I will answer it, because I can't help it, because I'm from there."

Wendell was quiet a moment. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanked, patient and idiotic, marking a shoal nobody used anymore.

"Then we never use the customs house again," he said. "Not with you. Anselm's channel comes in past the garrison gate—that's checkpoint two, different clerks, different eyes. And the pier inspection is a formality for deck cargo, if the manifest's clean." He paused, and Ilsa watched him do the thing he did, the terrible competent arithmetic, adding up risks like coins. "But you can't go through any of them again. Checkpoint one remembers you now. Which means someone else carries the satchel, or we go around all three."

"Around?" Ilsa gestured at the harbor, black and glittering and ringed with lanterns, every approach stitched with patrols. "Through the water?"

"Under it, actually. If you believe Anselm. Some nights I do."

"And if we're wrong about him being drunk instead of sharp?"

"Then we've already lost," Wendell said, simply, and picked up the basket of herring as if it were groceries, as if they were two neighbors walking home, and Ilsa followed him into the dark and understood that this was what resistance looked like from the inside: not glory, but errands. Men carrying fish. Women learning to limp.

They were two streets from the boarding house when Wendell stopped under a broken lamp and said, quietly, without turning around:

"There's something you should know before we plan anything else. Inspector Hale sent for the customs house logs today. All of them. Including tonight's." A pause. "He knows someone came through at midnight wearing northern boots. By morning he'll know it was Marta Voss. And by tomorrow night, Ilsa, he'll be sitting in Mrs. Okonkwo's parlor, drinking her tea, asking me—as politely as a knife goes in—what my fishwife friend is carrying."
