## 12. The Ledger Burned

Adaeze Okonkwo had been pretending not to hear things for twenty years, and she had gotten very good at it.

The trick was in the hands. A deaf woman's hands were always busy—shelling beans, mending a seam, scrubbing a step—and her eyes stayed on her work while her ears did the real labor. So when the boots came up Salt Lane at half past four in the afternoon, six pairs of them, too heavy and too together to be anything but soldiers, her hands went straight to the beans and her ears went to the door.

She counted them by sound. Six men, maybe seven. One officer—the lighter step, the one who expected doors to open for him. They stopped outside her gate.

She did not look up when they knocked. She let them knock twice, three times, the way a deaf woman would, and only then rose from her chair with the slow, stiff dignity of her sixty-one years and shuffled to the door, opening it a hand's width, blinking at them like a woman startled out of a nap she hadn't been having.

"Yes?" she said, loud, too loud, the way deaf people spoke. "I don't hear so good. You'll have to say it slow."

The officer was young. They were always young now; the old ones had either risen high enough to stay behind desks or died somewhere in the provinces. This one had a clean jaw and a paper in his gloved hand and the particular patience of a man who believed he was being merciful.

"A woman," he said slowly, as if volume were the same as clarity. "Northern. Came off a fishing boat two nights past. We have reason to believe she is lodging on this street."

Adaeze squinted at him. "A woman," she repeated. "I have women. I have five lodgers. Two are women." She held up fingers, miscounted deliberately, corrected herself. "But none came off no boat."

"May we look?"

She stood aside. It was what they wanted and refusing would cost more than admitting them. Besides, there was nothing to find—not anymore, not since last night, when she had moved the Brandt girl into the attic behind the false wall Anselm had built during the embargo years for brandy crates and had never quite taken down. The girl was up there now with a candle stub and instructions not to cough, and above her, through the attic's single north window, she could see the customs house roof, which meant she could also be seen from it if anyone thought to look up. Adaeze had thought about this all day. There was nothing more to do about it.

The soldiers went through her house the way soldiers always did, which was to say badly and thoroughly at the same time. They overturned the bean basket. They looked inside the flour bin and put their thumbs in it. One of them opened the piano bench and seemed disappointed to find sheet music. The officer walked the halls reading names off his paper aloud—Brandt, he said, Brandt, Brandt—and Adaeze followed him saying *what? what?* until even he tired of it.

It was the youngest soldier who found the ledger.

He pulled it off the shelf in the parlor where it lived beside the family Bible—a fat book bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked white from thirty years of opening. He weighed it in his hand like something he'd caught.

"What's this?"

"Guest book," Adaeze said. "Names. What they pay. When they come, when they go." She shrugged, a big shrug, a nothing-shrug. "You want to read it? Read it. Boring as weather."

And here was the moment. Here was where twenty years of practice met its examination.

The soldier opened it. The officer drifted over. And Adaeze Okonkwo watched a stranger's thumb move down a column of names that were, in a sense that no clerk or commissar would ever understand, the truest record of everything she had ever done with her life.

There was Marta Voss in the spring of '19, who had come to her with bruised wrists and a name that wasn't hers and stayed eleven months until passage could be found. There were the Ferreira brothers, '21, smugglers of a kind but smugglers of people, who had slept three to a bed and left her a barrel of salt cod as thanks. There was old Yusef, who had taught piano until the conservatory purged him, and the girl called Lark whose real name Adaeze had never learned, and a courier named Dennick who had stayed one night and been shot at the garrison gate by morning, and whose page she had never had the heart to cross out because crossing him out felt like letting them win twice.

Page after page after page. Every soul the republic had failed, passing through her house like water through a channel, and her ledger holding each one the way the riverbed holds the river: without comment, without complaint, simply bearing the shape of what had flowed over it.

If they took that book upstairs to the garrison and sat a literate man with time and grudge over it, they would learn the shape of everything. Not just Ilsa Brandt. All of it. Twenty years of small treasons written in her own careful hand, dated, initialed, cross-referenced. Enough rope to hang the whole street.

The soldier turned a page. The paper whispered.

"The rent book," Adaeze said loudly. "You find somebody interesting in there? Some of them owe me money. If you're collecting debts, start with the tall one, room three, he owes me six weeks."

The soldier didn't laugh. The officer took the book from him, though. That was the thing about officers—they couldn't leave a document alone. Paper called to them the way blood calls to sharks. He flipped back to the beginning, and Adaeze saw him register the dates, saw the arithmetic happen behind his clean jaw: this book goes back before the war. This book goes back to the old republic.

"This will need to be examined," he said. "At the garrison."

"Examined," Adaeze repeated, and laughed her deaf-woman's laugh, too loud, unbothered. "For what? Rent arrears? Take it. Take the whole shelf. Half those books are cookery."

But her heart had gone cold and quiet, the way it did before hard decisions, and while they finished ransacking her kitchen she stood at the stove and began, very calmly, to think.

* * *

They left at dusk, taking the ledger under the young soldier's arm like a confiscated schoolbook, and promising—because officers always promised—to return it once it had been cleared.

Adaeze locked the door behind them and stood with her forehead against the wood for the length of ten breaths. Then she turned and looked at her parlor, at the empty place on the shelf between the Bible and the cookbook, at the pale rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where the ledger's spine had leaned for thirty years.

Upstairs, Wendell Galloway was standing in the hall with the particular stillness of a man who has spent an afternoon listening to soldiers walk through the rooms below him and has survived it by becoming furniture.

"They took it," she said, dropping the deafness from her voice the way she dropped it every night after dark, her real voice, low and river-deep. "My ledger."

"I heard." His face was gray. "Mrs. Okonkwo, I'm sorry. If we'd known—if Ilsa had never come here—"

"If wishes were boats." She brushed past him toward the kitchen. "Come away from that window. Sit. Eat something. You think worse hungry."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are always not hungry. Eat anyway." She set bread and the bean stew in front of him whether he wanted it or not, and then she sat across from him and told him the truth, because the hour for anything less was over. "That book has everyone in it, Wendell. Everyone I ever sheltered, every year, every name. Twenty years of it. If a clever man reads it at the garrison, he doesn't need to find Ilsa Brandt. He finds the pattern. He finds *me*, and then he finds everyone who ever passed through my door, including you, including Anselm, including half the harbor that thinks itself invisible." She folded her hands. "They'll bring it back eventually, if it's cleared. Books like that come home. But cleared takes weeks, and weeks is longer than we have."

Wendell set down his spoon. She watched him arrive at it a half-second after she had, watch the understanding land on his face like weather.

"You're going to get it back tonight."

"I'm going to get it back tonight," she agreed.

"That's—" He stopped. Recalibrated. She had watched this boy—for he was a boy to her, whatever his age, all of them were boys and girls to her—she had watched him spend two years perfecting the art of doing nothing, and now the nothing was failing him all at once. "That's the garrison. You can't walk into the garrison."

"No," Adaeze said. "I can't. But Sergeant Pellikin can be walked out of it, and Sergeant Pellikin walks with the property inventory."

"Pellikin." He knew the name. Of course he knew the name; knowing names was his whole trade. "The one who takes eggs from Mrs. Duval's cart every Thursday without paying."

"The same. A man who steals eggs will steal anything, and a man who steals anything can be sold anything." She stood and began wrapping the stew pot in cloth. "He has a wife who wants a stove. There's a stove at the ironmonger's on Cooper Street, secondhand, good iron, forty crowns, which is robbery, which is why nobody's bought it. Tonight that stove costs twenty-five, and Sergeant Pellikin finds himself holding a property inventory with a loose page in it, and pages go missing from inventories all the time, especially pages describing books nobody important asked for."

Wendell stared at her. "You've done this before."

"I've been doing this since before your mother met your father, probably." She tied the cloth. "Eat your stew. Then go tell Anselm I need him sober enough to stand in a doorway and look drunk, which is his finest performance. And tell Pip—" she paused, weighing it, hating it, deciding— "tell Pip to stay in tonight. Whatever else happens. Tell him the streets are bad."

* * *

The stove cost twenty-three crowns in the end, because Adaeze drove a bargain the way other women prayed—with total absorption and no expectation of mercy—and by nine o'clock Sergeant Pellikin was carrying a secondhand stove receipt in one pocket and a page torn from a property inventory in the other, whistling on his way home to a wife who would kiss him for the first time in months.

By ten, the page was ash in Adaeze's kitchen stove, and the rest of the evening belonged to fire.

Because here was the thing she had understood while the soldiers' boots faded down Salt Lane, the thing that had settled in her chest like a swallowed stone: getting the ledger back was only half of it. The book existed. The garrison clerk had seen it, logged it, perhaps copied a line or two before the inventory page vanished. Even returned, even whole, it was a confession sitting on her parlor shelf, waiting for the next raid, the next clever man, the next turn of the screw. The republic was dying and dying things lashed out, and she could not spend the rest of her life one inspection ahead of her own history.

So Adaeze Okonkwo carried the ledger to the stove, and she opened it, and she began to burn it.

Not all at once. Pages fed to a fire make noise and light and smell, and a deaf woman burning papers at midnight must still be a deaf woman burning nothing at all, should anyone look through a window. So she burned it in the quiet way: a page at a time, pressed flat against the coals with the poker, each sheet going brown at the edges first, then black, then lifting off in flakes that she stirred down into the ash before the next one followed. The ink went first. Names dissolved before paper did—Marta Voss's bruises, the Ferreira brothers' cod barrel, old Yusef's hands on the keys, little Lark whose name was not Lark, Dennick dead at the garrison gate, all of it curling and graying and gone.

She worked by feel more than sight. Her eyes were not what they had been, and the firelight made the handwriting swim, but she knew the book by weight and thickness and by the places certain pages had gone soft from handling. Page for the winter of '22, when there had been nine lodgers and one lamp and Mrs. Duval had brought her a chicken when her own hen stopped laying. Page for the courier season, the busiest, the most dangerous, the one with three separate aliases for the same brave fool of a boy. Page after page after page.

Somewhere around the middle—around the year the war turned, the year the names started coming faster—her hands slowed.

She sat back in her chair and looked at what remained: half a book, green leather gone soft, spine cracked white. Thirty years of the only country she had ever truly governed, this house, these rooms, this rolling census of the hunted and the hidden. No archive anywhere held what this book held. No monument would ever be raised to any of them. The ledger was the monument, and she was feeding it to a stove one page at a time because a girl she had known for four days was hiding in her attic, and because some histories are only safe as smoke.

"You don't have to do it all," Wendell said quietly, from the doorway. She hadn't heard him come down; the boy moved like a debt collector. He was holding his coat, ready to go out into the dark again, and he had stopped because of the smell, she supposed. Burning ink smells like a schoolhouse dying.

"Yes, I do," Adaeze said. "Half a ledger is worse than none. Half tells them exactly which years to ask about."

"It's thirty years of people. People who trusted you."

"People who trusted me to keep them alive," she said, "not to remember them. Memory is a luxury, Wendell. Ask any refugee." She turned the next page against the coals. "Besides. I'm not losing them. I'm eighty-one pounds of stubbornness and I remember every name in that book better than the book does. Marta Voss. The Ferreira boys. Yusef, who played Chopin like he was apologizing to it." The page curled, blackened, lifted. "The book was for the ones who came after me. For whoever kept the house when I'm done. But there isn't going to be a peaceful after, not for a while. So the names live in me now, and I am considerably harder to steal."

Wendell crouched beside her, uninvited, and took the poker from her hand, and fed the pages himself—one at a time, pressed flat, stirred down to ash, exactly as she'd been doing. Neither of them spoke for a while. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, faintly, a floorboard shifted in the attic: the Brandt girl turning over in her sleep, or not sleeping, or listening.

"She asked me today why you do it," Wendell said at last. "Why you take people like us in. I didn't know what to tell her."

Adaeze almost smiled. "What did you tell her?"

"That I'd only been here two years myself. That I assumed it was the rent."

This time she did laugh, short and low, careful of the hour. "My daughter had a name for this house," she said. The words came harder than the burning did. She had not spoken of Amara to anyone in this room. "She called it the lantern house. Said I kept a light going so the lost ones could find the shore." Her daughter had been dead eleven years and had never seen a single one of the names in that book, and yet somehow the book had been hers, all of it, the whole long litany of shelter. "Well. Tonight I burned the lantern's logbook. But the light stays lit."

The last page went into the coals. Wendell stirred the ash until it was uniform, anonymous, indistinguishable from a winter's worth of ordinary fires, and closed the stove door, and the ledger of thirty years was a warm room and a faint smell and nothing else in all the world.

Adaeze sat back. Her hands, for the first time all day, were empty.

It was Wendell who broke the silence, and when he spoke his voice had changed—flatter, faster, the voice of a courier with a schedule.

"Anselm says the harbormaster is hosting a farewell dinner for the diplomatic staff. Day after tomorrow. The whole garrison command will be in one room, drinking paid-for wine." He looked at her across the dead ledger's ashes. "We're going to need your good dress, Mrs. Okonkwo. And everything you know about how the wealthy eat."

Adaeze Okonkwo looked at the boy crouched at her stove with ash on his knuckles and firelight in his eyes, this invisible man who had finally decided to be seen, and she thought: *Amara would have liked you.*

Then she got up to find the good dress.
