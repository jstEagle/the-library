## 14. Letters to a Ghost

The boarding house had gone quiet the way it only went quiet after midnight—Mrs. Okonkwo's clock ticking in the hall like a patient interrogator, the pipes in the walls settling, Pip's uneven breathing behind his door. Wendell sat at the table in the abandoned kitchen with the satchel open in front of him and a single candle burning down between his hands.

He had not meant to open it.

That was the thing about rules. You built them out of yourself, rib by rib, and for years they held you up the way a skeleton holds a man upright, and then some night like this one they were just bones on a table and you were alone with them, and you discovered that the rule had never been about protecting the republic, or the senders, or the recipients whose names he never learned. The rule—*never read*—had always been about protecting Wendell Galloway from knowing too much to survive.

He knew the arithmetic of it better than anyone alive. Couriers who read became witnesses. Witnesses became valuable. Valuable men got pulled into rooms with lamps and questions, or got pushed off piers with their wrists lashed. For six years he had carried sealed things through occupied streets like a man carrying lit fuses, and he had never once looked at the flame.

But Tomas was dead. Hale had said so with the flat certainty of an official record: *recovered two years ago, identified as Reyes*. And Ilsa Brandt sat in Mrs. Okonkwo's attic with three letters in a dead man's handwriting dated three weeks ago, and somewhere between those two facts—the corpse and the ink—Wendell's rules had quietly drowned.

He turned the official dispatches aside. Ministerial seals, harbormaster's countersignatures, the whole rotten bureaucracy of a government selling itself piece by piece. He'd deliver those because delivering them was what he did, the way a heart delivers blood without asking the body's permission.

It was the fourth letter he wanted. The one Ilsa hadn't described. The one folded differently—he'd seen it when she handed the bundle over, tucked inside the others like a splinter worked under skin, addressed in a slanting hand to no ministry at all.

*To W. Galloway, if he lives, if she finds him, if he has the courage to read this.*

His name. His actual name, not the half-dozen aliases he wore through checkpoints like borrowed coats. Nobody living was supposed to know it. He had buried it himself two years ago, at the same time he'd buried Tomas—or thought he had—and seeing it now in that slanted hand was like watching someone dig.

Wendell picked up the candle and carried it to his room, because some things you could not do at a shared table. He bolted the door, wedged the chair under the knob out of habit, and sat on the edge of the bed with the letter on his knee for a long time, the wax seal unbroken under his thumb.

Tomas Reyes had sealed his letters with a thumbnail instead of a stamp. It was a courier's affectation, a little vanity—*I don't need tools, I am the tool*—and it left a crescent dent in the wax that Wendell could feel now, plain as braille. He pressed his own thumb into the crescent, fitting it there, the way you'd fit your fingers into marks left by a friend's hand on a doorframe.

"You absolute bastard," he said softly, to the empty room, in the voice you used for the people you loved most. "You're really dead, aren't you. This is just you. This is the last thing you'll ever do to me."

Then he broke the seal and read.

*Wendell.*

*I'm going to assume you've already decided I'm alive and lying about it, because you were always the smarter of us and smarter men doubt first. So let me do this in order, the way you taught me. Facts before grief.*

*I am not alive. I want to be very clear about that, because whatever you're feeling right now—whatever hope is doing in your chest—I won't be the cause of it twice. The man who walked into the northern channel two years ago died there in every way that matters. What survived is a name on islands where nobody looks, a face going grey around the jaw, and a job I can't finish alone.*

*You remember the Verrin Channel run. You told me not to take it. You said the escort rotation had changed and the new gunboats ran dark, no lights, and I called you paranoid and went anyway. They found my coat forty yards from where the ferry went down. I wasn't on the ferry, Wendell. I put the coat in the water myself.*

He stopped reading.

The candle guttered in a draft from the window frame and he had to cup his hand around the flame, and his hand was shaking, which annoyed him in a distant way, the way you notice rain when someone is telling you terrible news. Not on the ferry. Not drowned. Had walked away from his own death with his coat floating behind him like a shed skin, while Wendell—

While Wendell had stood on the quay in the rain two days later watching them bring up what the tide returned, and had looked at a waterlogged coat with a torn shoulder seam and said, *yes, that's his,* and signed the identification papers with a hand that didn't work right for a week after. He had identified a coat. He had buried a coat. Two years of grief, poured out like oil on water, and every drop of it had landed on fabric and salt water while Tomas breathed air on some northern rock and let him.

And the anger came, hot and clean and welcome, because anger was so much easier than the other thing underneath it, which was relief so violent it felt like illness. He's alive. He's alive and he let you mourn him and you would have done the same, you know you would have, that's why you're angry—that's exactly why you're angry.

*You taught me the rule yourself,* the letter went on, as if Tomas could hear him thinking. *Never be remembered. Well. A remembered man is a hunted man, and I was hunted, Wendell, before I ever faked the drowning. I had found something in the harbormaster's office and the harbormaster found me finding it. Corvin Doss gave me a choice: disappear cleanly or be disappeared messily. I chose clean. I chose it badly and it cost you two years and I will pay that debt however you bill it, but I need you to hear the rest, because the rest is why Ilsa nearly froze crossing open water in winter to reach you.*

*The thing I found is real. There are men in the last government selling escape passages out of this harbor—passages that grieving families pay for in everything they have—and then selling those same families to the occupation authorities at the far end. The passages go north. The ships turn east. There's a rendezvous point past the lightship and a signal arrangement, and Doss runs it, and he doesn't run it alone, but he runs it, and his signature is on the manifests I stole.*

*I've enclosed copies. Ilsa has the originals sewn into her coat lining. She doesn't know all of it—she knows enough to die for it, which is more than enough—but you should know that she crossed for her brother, who paid for two berths and walked up a gangplank in October and hasn't been heard from since. Don't waste her courage by doubting her. Doubt me instead. Doubting me is free; I've already cost you everything else.*

*Doss. Say it out loud, Wendell. Harbormaster Corvin Doss, who shakes every hand at the pier, who wept at my memorial service—I know, I read the reports, I'm vain enough to have collected them. He is the reason the third checkpoint searches northern stitching. He is the reason the garrison gate asks about family. Every question those checkpoints ask, he wrote, and every answer feeds back to him.*

*Which brings me to Hale.*

*I know what Hale will have told you by now, or will tell you soon. He'll be careful about it—Hale is careful the way ice is careful—but he'll have mentioned me, my file, the recovery, and you'll have walked out of that tea wondering if the man who once pulled you off a pier at gunpoint is the same man selling couriers to the occupation. I watched you almost believe it once, over cards, when Anselm joked about inspectors and you went white. So: no. Listen to me. Hale isn't dirty. Hale is worse than dirty, for our purposes. Hale is loyal—to the city, he'd say, to the idea of order, to whatever lets him sleep—and a loyal man holding a collapsing fort does arithmetic. How many arrests keep the peace? How many sacrifices prevent reprisals? He'd take your letters and burn them, Wendell, not for money but for conscience, and he'd write you a pass out of the harbor with his own hand and consider it mercy. He saved your life once because that's who he is. He would bury the republic's testimony tomorrow because that's also who he is. Both true. Hold both. You were always good at holding both.*

*Doss is the traitor. Doss is the target. And here is the part where I ask you for the thing I have no right to ask.*

*Ilsa has to get those documents onto a ship, and every honest route out of that harbor is closed, and every dishonest route belongs to Doss. I can't come south—if I cross that water, Doss's people will see a dead man walking and everything burns at once, including her, including you. So it has to be you, and the strange family you've collected and won't admit you love. I know about them. I keep files. Old habits.*

*Read the enclosures aloud, Wendell. That's not sentiment. A courier who has never read a word in six years reading these names out loud to witnesses—that makes it real for them, and real things get carried properly. Besides, you always understood things better out loud. You narrated your card games under your breath for years and pretended you didn't.*

*Forgive me or don't. I mean that. I've written this letter eleven times trying to earn it and there is no version that earns it; two years is two years, and you stood in the rain over my empty grave. But whether you forgive me or not, finish this. Somewhere abroad there's a printing press with room in it for the truth about what happened to this republic and who sold it, and when that press runs, everyone we lost stops being weather and starts being evidence. That's the only immortality I believe in anymore, and you're the last courier who can carry me to it.*

*Dot your i's. Watch the third checkpoint. Trust the deaf landlady completely—she hears everything, that's rather the point of her.*

*T.*

P.S. The scar story Ilsa will tell you is true. Ask her about the eel. She tells it wrong. I fell, I did not scream, and the fishmonger laughed first.

Wendell read it again. Then a third time, slower, the way he'd once memorized faces at checkpoints—not the words anymore but the hand that made them, the crossbars on the i's, the way Tomas crossed his t's like a man signing something he meant. It was his. No forgery got the t's right; forgers reached for the famous details, the slant, the flourish, and missed the small violence of the crossings, the impatience in them. The man who wrote this letter had been impatient to live for thirty-one years and had finally found something worth being patient for.

Wendell set the pages on the blanket and put his face in his hands and did not move for a long time.

He had imagined this moment, in the worst hours of the past two years—imagened Tomas walking through Mrs. Okonkwo's door with salt in his hair and some idiot grin, imagined hitting him, imagined the embrace after, the shouting, the laughter that comes out of grief like blood out of a wound. He had rehearsed the reunion until it wore grooves in him.

Instead he got paper. Words in a dead man's living hand, asking for forgiveness across a distance no embrace could close, and Wendell sat with the letter on his knees and discovered that mourning, it turned out, had a shape after all. It wasn't the rain on the quay. It wasn't the coat in the tide box at the mortuary or the grave he'd visited once and never again. It was this: a man alone with a candle, choosing, deliberately, to be angry and grateful at the same time, and letting both be true, because Tomas had asked him to hold both and he had always been good at holding both.

"I forgive you," he said out loud, to the room, to the paper, to the grey-jawed man on his northern rock collecting reports about his own memorial service. "And I'm going to kill you myself someday, and then we'll be even."

His voice didn't shake. That surprised him. Six years of carrying sealed things had apparently built him for this after all.

He got up and fetched the official dispatches from the kitchen and laid them beside the personal letter, all four documents in a row on the blanket, and for the first time since Ilsa Brandt had dripping-wet walked into the boarding house, he looked at the enclosures—the copied manifests, the rendezvous coordinates, the signal arrangements, the ledger of sold passages in a neat bureaucratic hand that belonged to Corvin Doss and no one else.

He read them. All of them. Every name, every date, every family listed like freight.

It was worse than he'd prepared for and cleaner too, because treachery kept books, and books could be carried, and carried things could be published, and published things could not be drowned, no matter how dark the gunboats ran.

He thought about Hale then, really thought about him, and felt the strange grief of it settle into place. The man who had hauled him off a pier at nineteen with a grip like a dock winch. The man who poured tea like he was defusing something. Loyal. Conscientious. Willing to burn the truth in order to save the city from what the truth would cost, and to call it mercy while he struck the match. Wendell had spent three days afraid of Hale and one hour pitying him, and pity, he suspected, was the more dangerous thing to carry through a checkpoint.

Doss, though. Doss he would not pity. Doss shook hands at the pier and wept at memorials and sold widows' passages twice, and his signature sat on these pages in triplicate.

Wendell blew out the candle, because a man planning treason against a harbormaster didn't need light to see by—only the chart Anselm kept rolled in oilcloth under his mattress, the boy who knew the customs house shifts, the landlady who misdated ledgers, the woman in the attic who had bet her life on a stranger's memory of handwriting.

Tomorrow he would gather them. Tomorrow he would do the thing he had sworn never to do in six years of invisibility: he would tell them everything, out loud, in the kitchen, like a man with nothing left to lose—which was, he was beginning to understand, the only kind of man who could carry this cargo through three checkpoints and a farewell banquet hosted by the traitor himself.

Because that was the thought that surfaced last, as he lay down fully clothed and stared at the ceiling and let the plan assemble itself in the dark with the cold precision of a tide table: Doss was hosting a banquet for the departing diplomats. Everyone would be there. Everyone important, everyone guilty, every manifest and every clerk who touched them.

If you wanted to steal the truth out from under a thief, there were worse places to stand than at his own table, wearing your own face, carrying his own signatures.

Wendell closed his eyes. Somewhere north, a ghost was counting on him.

In the morning, the ghost would learn what kind of courier he'd chosen.
