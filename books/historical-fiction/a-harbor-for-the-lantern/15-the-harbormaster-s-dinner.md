## 15. The Harbormaster's Dinner

* * *

**Wendell**

The invitation arrived at Mrs. Okonkwo's house before noon, which meant the harbormaster wanted Wendell to see it coming.

It was a heavy card, cream stock, embossed with the port seal—a lantern over water, ironic as everything else in this city—and addressed to *Courier Galloway, bearer of diplomatic dispatches*, in a clerk's careful hand. *The Harbormaster requests your services at a farewell banquet honoring the departing delegation of the Vessarine Republic. Harbor House, eight o'clock.*

Farewell. As if the republic were a guest catching a train rather than a government bleeding out on its own floor.

Wendell set the card on the kitchen table and let the household gather around it the way they gathered around everything—Pip first, chin nearly on the tabletop; Anselm slower, one eye closed against the lamplight; Mrs. Okonkwo last, wiping her hands though they were already dry.

"They want their courier where the diplomats can see him," Anselm said. "Optics. The last loyal port, all that theater."

"They want me carrying papers," Wendell said. "Which means they'll know exactly what I'm carrying when I walk through their door."

"Or," Pip said, "they want everyone to think it's normal. So nobody looks."

Wendell looked at the boy. Sometimes Pip said things that made him wonder who had taught the child to think like that, and then he remembered: the streets had.

Ilsa had been listening from the doorway, still wearing the merchant's wife she was learning to be—a gray dress, a widow's cap, hands folded like a woman accustomed to waiting for men to finish talking. She'd been practicing the posture for three days. Tonight it would have to hold.

"The banquet is our checkpoint," she said quietly. "Every name that matters in this harbor will be in one room. If we walk out of it alive, we'll know exactly what we're walking past tomorrow morning."

"And if we don't walk out of it," Anselm said, "we'll know that too, briefly."

Mrs. Okonkwo took the card, held it at arm's length, and studied it as though her feigned deafness came with failing eyes. Then she set it down and pointed at Wendell, then at the stove, then made an eating motion. It was not a suggestion.

He ate. That was the deal he had made with himself weeks ago, without quite admitting it: he would eat what she cooked, sleep under her roof, accept the warmth of these people in small installments he could deny later. Tonight the installments were about to come due all at once.

"Roles," he said finally, because someone had to say them aloud and make them real. "I'm the courier. Officially invited, officially invisible. Ilsa goes in as Frau Weiss, widow of a grain merchant, invited by extension—I can add her to my card at the door; delegations always bring widows nobody checks twice." He looked at Pip, who straightened like a soldier. "You're not going."

"I'm going," Pip said.

"You're eleven."

"I'm small, I'm quick, I've carried trays at the Anchor since summer, and Herr Anselm says the harbor house hires extra hands from the fish quarter when the kitchen gets slammed. Which it will, tonight, because half the staff quit when the garrison doubled the guest list." Pip recited it flat, facts he had gathered the way other children gathered stones. "You need eyes inside the service passages. You need someone who can carry a message from the dining hall to the side door faster than any soldier can walk it. And nobody—" he shrugged, brutal and matter-of-fact— "looks at a serving boy's face. Ever."

Silence around the table. Mrs. Okonkwo's jaw was tight. Anselm turned his glass in slow circles.

"He's right," Ilsa said, and Wendell heard the apology in it before the agreement.

"He's right," Anselm echoed.

Wendell looked at the boy—at the collar worn thin, at the too-serious mouth—and understood that he had already lost the argument, probably days ago, probably the moment Pip had heard the word *banquet*. All he could do was build the cage around it.

"Rules," he said. "You carry nothing but trays. You speak to no one but us—if you must pass a message, it goes into the bread basket, folded twice. If anything turns wrong—a whistle, soldiers at the servants' door—you drop the tray and you run home. Not to warn us. Home. Swear it."

Pip swore it with his whole chest, bright-eyed, believing it. That was the part Wendell couldn't look at directly: the believing.

Mrs. Okonkwo touched his sleeve. When he turned, she pointed at herself, then made a shape in the air—an old gesture between them by now—and he shook his head. *Not you. Not this time.* Her hearing might fool soldiers, but the harbormaster's clerks checked papers with real questions, and a deaf landlady at a banquet would draw exactly the attention they couldn't afford.

She nodded slowly, once, and went back to the stove, and Wendell watched her shoulders and understood that obedience cost her more than defiance ever would.

By seven o'clock the household had transformed itself: Wendell in his courier's coat, brushed, brass buttons polished—the uniform of a man whose job was to be seen precisely enough to be ignored. Ilsa in gray, a borrowed brooch, twenty years of invented mourning settled over her face like frost. Pip in a server's apron two sizes large, hair slicked, practicing balancing a tray of empty glasses in the hallway until Mrs. Okonkwo took it from him and filled it with bread rolls so his practice would feed somebody.

At the door, Anselm caught Wendell's arm. His breath was wine-sweet, deliberate, theatrical—but his eyes were clear as glass.

"If the harbormaster is what the letters say he is," Anselm murmured, "then that room tonight is his confession, dressed in candlelight. Watch how he stands near the delegates. Watch which doors his men guard and which they leave open." A pause. "And watch Hale. Whatever Hale does in that room tells you what happens at the pier tomorrow."

Then he clapped Wendell on the shoulder hard enough to sting, said something loud and drunken and meaningless for anyone listening, and pushed them out into the dark.

* * *

**Ilsa**

Harbor House had been built by men who believed a republic should impress its enemies, and the ballroom did its dead country proud: chandeliers burning whale oil as if the fleets weren't scuttled, a string quartet playing to a crowd that mostly wanted drums, long tables laid with food that would have fed Pip's street for a month.

Ilsa entered on Wendell's arm, and the door clerk glanced at the card, glanced at her widow's cap, and waved them through without a question. Twenty years of being unremarkable had trained her well. Grief was the best disguise ever issued; no one interrogated sorrow.

Inside, she performed the role she had rehearsed: drifted toward the windows, accepted a glass of sherry she didn't drink, became furniture. Furniture saw everything.

The delegation stood near the hearth like museum pieces of themselves—five diplomats of the Vessarine Republic, gray-faced men and one sharp-eyed woman, making farewell speeches with their spines. Tomorrow they sailed on the same tide as her letters. Ilsa studied them and felt something twist: they didn't know. They believed the ship beneath them was loyal, the harbor beneath the ship was loyal, the escort arranged by the harbormaster was loyal. They were going to sail their own evidence straight into the enemy's net unless three nobodies in borrowed clothes pulled it out of the fire first.

And there was the harbormaster, moving among them like the room's landlord in every sense—Vosch, silver-haired, charming, shaking the delegate woman's hand with both of his own. Ilsa had read his name in Tomas's letters until the letters had gone soft at the folds. *He sells the escape passages,* Tomas had written. *He prices them per head and bills both sides.* Standing here, watching Vosch laugh at some ambassador's joke, Ilsa understood for the first time how treachery actually worked: it wasn't shadowed and furtive. It was the best-dressed man in the room.

A serving boy passed with a tray. She recognized the set of the shoulders before the face—Pip, jaw clenched with the effort of looking servile—and she took a glass from him just to touch the moment, to confirm the family was real and assembled.

His eyes flicked up, flicked toward the far wall, flicked down. Trained signal. She followed the glance.

Two guards at the main doors. One at the terrace. None—she counted twice, disbelieving—none at the corridor to the counting rooms, where the manifests lived, where Vosch's private ledger of sold passages presumably slept in a locked drawer.

None needed, probably. Who would rob a room in the middle of the man's own party?

*I would,* Ilsa thought, and sipped her untouched sherry, and smiled at nothing like a lonely widow remembering better years.

Vosch crossed the room toward her. She kept her face slack with surprise as he stopped beside her, gesturing courteously at the window and the black harbor beyond.

"You find us melancholy, madam?" His voice was warm, unhurried, a man enjoying the sound of his own generosity.

"Only thoughtful, sir. The lights on the water." She let her accent go soft northern, a merchant's widow far from home.

"Ah. Yes." He gazed out with her, companionable, and Ilsa's blood moved slow and cold. "We do what we can for those fleeing hard times. Every soul that needs a passage, my office finds one. For a fair fee, of course." A smile. "Fairness is the soul of commerce."

*Per head,* Tomas had written. *Billed both sides.*

"The republic is fortunate in its servants," she said.

"The republic," Vosch said pleasantly, "has been fortunate in everything, right up until it wasn't." He inclined his head and moved on, leaving Ilsa with her heart hammering a rhythm against her ribs and one thought ringing clean as a struck bell:

He isn't hiding. He thinks it's already finished.

* * *

**Pip**

The kitchen of Harbor House was the biggest room Pip had ever been inside that wasn't a church, and it ran on shouting. Cooks shouted at boys, boys shouted at each other, a man with a cleaver shouted general philosophy at everyone. Nobody questioned a new face with a tray; within ten minutes Pip had been kicked, handed a stack of plates, and absorbed into the machine.

He learned the layout the way water learns a floor: servants' corridor running behind the dining hall, three doors—the pantry, the cloakroom, and one locked door with a brass plate that read COUNTING HOUSE, which every adult walked past without glancing at, which meant it mattered. Two guards smoked in the yard beyond the kitchen door. The side gate to the lane was propped open with a crate because the wine deliveries were still arriving.

All of it filed away, neat as stamps in a drawer.

The strange thing, Pip discovered, was how boring evil looked up close. He'd imagined the harbormaster's traitor staff lurking, whispering. Instead the counting-room clerk sat eating pie with the wine steward, arguing about football. Traitors ate pie. Traitors argued about whether the Dockers' keeper should've saved the second goal. Pip filed that away too, somewhere deep, for later, for a time when he'd need to understand how the world actually worked instead of how it was supposed to.

Midway through the third course, the message came.

He spotted Wendell across the crowded hall—courier-still, plate of untouched food, stationed near the pillar like a man hired to hold it up—and caught the almost-nothing movement: hand to breast pocket, pause, hand away. *Satchel check. Proceed.* And a moment later Ilsa, drifting past the bread display, set down her napkin folded twice.

Pip collected the napkin with the bread basket, carried it through the servants' door with his pulse tapping in his ears like a second heartbeat, and unfolded it in the corridor's shadows.

A line in pencil, Ilsa's small hand: *Counting room unlocked 9. Clerk changes 9:15. Window latch broken — confirmed. Tell W: Vosch walks the delegates to the pier himself at dawn. Escort list posted in cloakroom.*

Escort list. Pip read it three times until the words surrendered their meaning: the names of the men assigned to guard the delegates' ship tomorrow. The men chosen by Vosch. Evidence, sitting on a hook behind a coat rack thirty feet away.

He could see it from here if the cloakroom door swung open. He could—

No. Bread basket. Folded twice. Rules.

But his feet had already taken two steps toward the cloakroom before his brain caught up, and he stood frozen in the corridor with a basket of breadrolls and a napkin full of treason, hating the rules and loving them.

He copied the list. That was all. Names and ranks, nine lines, into the memory he'd sharpened on two years of running messages—he read it once, twice, closed his eyes, said it back, opened his eyes, checked. Perfect. Then he put the paper back exactly as it hung, angled the pin the same way, and got out before the cloakroom attendant returned from wherever attendants went to smoke.

Back in the hall, he delivered the reply into the bread basket: *List memorized. Nine names. Counting room open at 9, clerk leaves 9:15. Side gate open.*

And because he was Pip, and because pride is a kind of hunger, he added one more line, small, in the corner, where only Wendell would look:

*Told you I should come.*

* * *

**Wendell**

At nine o'clock the quartet began a waltz nobody danced to, and the counting room stood open, and Wendell discovered that the hardest part of espionage was walking at a normal speed.

He had left Ilsa holding a position by the terrace doors—visible, respectable, ready to raise any alarm short of screaming—and slipped down the service corridor with the loose, bored gait of a courier hunting a privy. The counting-room door yielded to his hand. Inside: ledgers, lamplight, the smell of ink and tallow. Three minutes. He gave himself three minutes.

The manifest drawer was locked, but Vosch's private desk was not, and men who sell passages keep their prices somewhere. Wendell found it in the second ledger: columns of dates, boat names, numbers of souls—and beside each entry, in a tidy commercial shorthand, two figures. What the enemy paid. What the republic's fugitives paid.

Both sides billed. Tomas hadn't exaggerated. Wendell stared at the arithmetic of betrayal and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very cold—the silence that came before decisions.

He copied four entries onto the flyleaf of his own pocket notebook, tore the leaf free, folded it twice, and restored the ledger to the exact angle of its dust shadow. Then, because three minutes had become five and greed kills couriers, he allowed himself one glance at the drawer he couldn't open, committing the lock's shape to memory—brass, three-lever, old. Anselm would sigh with contempt and have opinions.

He was two steps into the corridor when the door at the far end opened and Inspector Hale came through it.

For one suspended second they simply looked at each other: the man who had once dragged Wendell out of the winter harbor, standing six feet from the man whose life he'd saved, in the house of the man both of them now knew was a traitor—or knew it, Wendell realized with a lurch, in wildly different ways.

Hale's eyes went from Wendell's face, to the corridor behind him, to the counting-room door standing innocently shut. The tea they'd shared weeks ago hung in the air between them, all its old debts and new suspicions.

"Courier Galloway." Hale's voice was mild. Too mild. "Lost?"

"Seeking the necessary room, Inspector. This house is a maze."

"It is." Hale didn't move. Under the lamplight his face was unreadable as a stamped form, and Wendell understood that whatever Hale had come to this corridor to do—check the lock? meet someone? steal or protect?—he was now recalculating everything at speed. "Mind the wine stewards on your way back. They resent couriers."

"Most people do."

Something flickered at the corner of Hale's mouth—not quite a smile, not quite grief. He stepped aside, gesturing Wendell past with a courtesy that terrified him more than a drawn pistol would have, and said, softly, as Wendell passed:

"The delegates embark at dawn. Everything else is weather."

Then he walked on toward the counting-room door, and Wendell walked toward the light and the music, and neither man looked back, because looking back is how couriers die.

* * *

**Pip**

The last hour was all noise and gold. Pip ferried trays through the glittering hall and watched his strange, borrowed family operate in plain sight: Wendell at his pillar, stone-still; Ilsa by the windows, gray and ghostly and perfect; and at the center of it all the harbormaster, raising his glass to the departing diplomats with a face like a coin—bright profile, dark reverse.

"To the Vessarine Republic," Vosch declared. "May what she built outlive what has broken her."

The room drank. Pip, refilling glasses along the far wall, drank nothing and memorized everything: which officers toasted loudest (the ones Vosch owned), which delegate flinched (the woman, twice), and how Inspector Hale stood apart from his fellow guards near the main doors, drinking nothing either, watching Wendell the way a man watches a fuse he lit himself.

At half past ten the party began to shed guests. Pip carried his final load of dirty glassware through the servants' door, out the propped side gate, into the lane where the night smelled of salt and coal smoke—and there they were, the whole impossible conspiracy, gathered in the dark like thieves dividing loot: Ilsa pulling off the widow's cap with relief, Wendell pale and composed, already unfolding bread-basket messages that no longer needed baskets.

"You got it," Pip said, breathless. "The escort list, the times, the—did you get the ledger—"

"We got enough." Wendell's voice was strange, flat-calm, the voice Pip had learned meant the opposite of calm. He held up a torn page. "Four transactions. Dates. Prices. Both sides paid, in his own hand."

Ilsa was staring at the harbor, black and patient beyond the rooftops. "Tomorrow at dawn he walks them to the pier himself," she said. "Into his own escort. Unless the letters are on that ship and gone before his men ever board."

"Then we're agreed." Wendell folded the page away, and Pip saw his jaw set, saw the fear get shoved down somewhere and locked, and thought, with a fierce ache, *that's what brave looks like from close up—just a man swallowing hard.* "Checkpoint three. The pier. We put the satchel aboard with the diplomatic mail, and we do it while Vosch is standing on the quay watching us do it."

Nobody said *and if we fail*. Nobody had to.

They walked home through the empty lanes, three shapes in the dark, and behind them Harbor House blazed on like a lantern—except, Pip thought, lanterns were supposed to mean safe.

At the boarding house, Mrs. Okonkwo was waiting in the doorway with the lamp held high, and she took one look at their faces and stepped aside to let them in—which was, Pip thought, its own kind of toast.

Above them, in her window, the lantern burned steady against the coming tide.
