## 16. Betrayal at the Garrison Gate

The route Anselm had drawn for us kept to the shadow of the tannery wall, where the smell of rendered hides was thick enough to make even soldiers walk faster. Ilsa Brandt moved through it with the satchel strapped flat against her ribs under Mrs. Okonkwo's daughter's coat, and she counted her heartbeats the way Tomas Reyes had taught her to count waves—without panic, without rhythm she could be accused of.

Anselm walked ten paces ahead of her, drunk-seeming, weaving just enough. He wore his pilot's coat with the brass buttons half undone and a bottle wrapped in sacking under one arm. He looked like what he was pretending to be: an old man going home the long way from a tavern that no longer had a name.

They had rehearsed it twice in the boarding house kitchen. If anything went wrong at the garrison gate, Anselm would go loud. That was the whole of his instruction, delivered while he cleaned his spectacles on his sleeve. *Loud is a gift, girl. Old men are invisible until they make noise. Then they're the only thing in the street worth looking at.*

She had argued. She remembered arguing. She remembered him smiling at her over his spectacles as though she were a student who had not yet learned that some arithmetic only came out one way.

*You're carrying the letters,* he'd said. *That makes you the cargo. And cargo doesn't argue with the ship.*

Now, in the tannery shadow, with the garrison gate visible ahead—a torchlit gap in the wall, two sentries, a sergeant with a lantern and a ledger—Ilsa understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming across a harbor, that he had planned this all along. Not the failure. The shape of it.

Because someone had sold them.

* * *

She knew it the way you know weather before it arrives: by the wrongness of small things.

The sentries at the gate should have been bored. It was past midnight, the third watch, the hour when men at checkpoints stop searching bodies and start searching their own thoughts. Instead they stood like men waiting for something specific. The sergeant held his lantern high, sweeping it along the wall instead of the road, as if he expected people to come out of the stones.

And there was a fourth man. Leaning against the guardhouse door, out of the torchlight, arms folded, doing nothing—which at a checkpoint in the last winter of a dying republic was the most suspicious occupation there was.

Anselm stopped walking. He didn't turn around. He simply stopped, the way a man stops when he's forgotten why he crossed a room, and he scratched his ear with two fingers—the signal, the one they'd agreed on, *turn back, abort, live to run again.*

Ilsa turned back. Three steps into the dark of the tannery lane, she heard the sergeant call out behind her—not to her, to the shadows generally—and then Anselm's voice, pitched high and slurred and magnificent:

"Evening, evening, don't mind me, gentlemen, only looking for my boat—"

"Your boat's been dry-docked six years, old man."

"So it has! So it has! You've a better memory than the harbormaster, and he can't remember which pockets to line—"

The laughter that followed was thin, forced, the laughter of men who had been told to expect trouble and were relieved to find only a nuisance. Ilsa pressed herself against the wet stone of the tannery wall, the satchel digging into her ribs, and told herself to move. To keep moving. Every second she stood there was a second Anselm's performance bought at interest.

But she couldn't make her feet obey. Because she could hear it now, underneath the drunken rambling—the sound she'd crossed open water in winter to escape and had somehow sailed straight back into. A man's voice, low and clipped and northern, saying words she couldn't catch but whose cadence she recognized the way you recognize a song your mother sang badly.

Someone from the islands. Someone who had known she would come this way.

*They sold the channel,* she thought. *Not me. The channel.* And then, worse, arriving like cold water down the back of her coat: *No. They sold me. Anselm knew. He drew the decoy route himself and gave me the real one and then walked the decoy anyway, because the decoy needed walking or the real one dies.*

At the gate, the performance curdled. The sergeant's voice lost its amusement.

"Search him. Careful—he's a pilot, they hide things in their coats."

"I hide nothing but my opinions," Anselm said, "and those are worthless, ask anyone—"

Then the sound she would carry longer than any scar: the flat crack of a man being put against a wall, the clatter of the bottle hitting cobbles, the small grunt of breath leaving an old body. Not screaming. Anselm did not scream. He laughed—one bark of it, genuine, delighted almost—and said something in the low mumble of a very drunk man, and whatever he said made the northern voice snap, *"Check his boots. Check the boot heels."*

They knew about boot heels. Someone had told them about boot heels, and Anselm didn't keep anything in his boot heels, which meant the information was real enough to check and wrong enough to waste their time, which meant whoever sold the route had sold it secondhand, diluted, imperfect—

*Ilsa. Move.*

Her own voice, inside her skull, wearing Tomas's accent. She moved.

* * *

The real route ran through the fish market's drainage cut, a channel of standing water and old scales that passed beneath the garrison wall through a grate Anselm had sworn hadn't been sealed since the flood year. She went alone. That had always been the design; she saw it now with terrible clarity, every piece of it laid out over three days of maps and muttering and Mrs. Okonkwo's soup: Pip scouting the customs house, Wendell mapping the pier inspection, Anselm drawing routes he never intended anyone to use. Each of them building a machine whose only purpose was to deliver one woman and one satchel through one gate, and each of them quietly positioning themselves between her and the parts of the machine that failed.

She waded the cut with her skirts bunched to her knees, the water so cold it burned, one hand on the slime-slick wall, the other pressed flat against the satchel as if it were a wound. Above her, muffled through the grate bars, she could hear the garrison yard: orders shouted, a whistle, boots running in the direction of the tannery wall. Running away from her.

*He's buying you minutes with his bones,* she thought, and gagged on it, and kept wading.

The grate was where Anselm promised—hinged, rusted, passable. Of course it was. He'd used it himself, probably forty years ago, probably with men whose names were already forgotten. She squeezed through into a yard stacked with crab pots, and from there into the lanes behind the chandlers' row, and from there she did what couriers do: she became furniture. She became a woman walking home from a late shift at the net-menders', shoulders rounded, eyes down, a person nobody would remember because nobody remembers furniture.

It worked. That was the obscene part. It worked perfectly.

She reached the safehouse—an attic above a sailmaker's loft that smelled of hemp and mouse droppings—and she climbed the ladder, and she sat down against the wall with the satchel in her lap, and only then did her body inform her that it had been screaming for hours.

Her hands shook so badly she couldn't work the buckle. She sat in the dark listening to herself breathe, and the breathing sounded like sobbing, and she decided to let it, because there was no one to hear and no one to lie to.

*He was meant to be caught. No. He meant to be caught. There's a difference. There has to be a difference.*

There wasn't. Or there was, and it didn't matter, because either way an old man with bad knees was sitting in a cell tonight answering questions he had prepared answers for, and she was sitting in an attic holding the thing all those answers were protecting. The network had paid in flesh for her freedom, exactly as networks always paid, exactly as Tomas had paid two years ago when he'd drowned himself on paper so that his letters could stay alive somewhere in a lighthouse cache—and she had come here believing she was the rescuer, the avenger, the woman who would carry the truth out of the drowning republic. She had never once considered that she might simply be the next payment owed.

She thought about the northern voice at the gate. She went through the day again, minute by minute, hunting the leak. Pip? No—the boy had charmed the customs clerk's daughter, not the garrison. Wendell? Her stomach rejected the idea before her mind finished forming it; whatever else was true about the courier, he had read the letters aloud in Anselm's kitchen with tears on his face, and men didn't weep convincingly over paper they intended to betray. Mrs. Okonkwo? The woman had burned her own ledger rather than let soldiers read it.

Which left the harbor itself. Which left the simple, brutal arithmetic that in a city full of hungry people, three days of preparation left footprints, and footprints had prices, and someone had sold the price without ever knowing what they were selling.

Or knowing. Or not caring.

She unbuckled the satchel at last and checked the letters by feel alone, counting them through the oilcloth like a rosary. All there. Tomas's slanted hand, his i's dotted like little crosses, the names of the men selling escape passages to the enemy—all of it intact, all of it hers to carry, purchased tonight at the exchange rate of one old pilot.

And beneath the official letters, wrapped separately, the one addressed to no office and no ministry. The one Wendell Galloway hadn't found yet—or had found, maybe, and hadn't said. She'd seen him looking at her sometimes with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't want finished.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *One checkpoint left. The pier. And everyone at that pier will know by morning what happened at the garrison gate tonight—they'll know the route is burned, they'll know she exists, they'll be watching for a northern woman with a satchel.*

Unless the plan changed. Unless the plan was never what she thought it was.

She lay back against the wall and stared into the dark and realized she had stopped trusting the map. Not Anselm's chart—Anselm was beyond charts now—but the larger one, the one drawn by Wendell's silences and Mrs. Okonkwo's misdated ledgers and the whole quiet architecture of people who had spent years surviving this city by never telling each other everything.

Tonight the architecture had saved her and cost Anselm everything, and she had no way of knowing which parts had been design and which had been luck, and that not-knowing was its own kind of cell.

Below her, in the sailmaker's loft, a floorboard creaked. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Then a voice, low, pitched for her ears alone, in an accent she had last heard across a kitchen table while an old man cleaned his spectacles:

"It's me. Don't scream. We have a problem—and you're not going to like how I mean to solve it."
