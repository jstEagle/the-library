## 17. What Anselm Won't Say

The garrison gate had never looked kind to Wendell, but in the gray light after the arrests it looked like a mouth. Two sentries flanked it, breath steaming, bayonets catching the first weak sun. Beyond them, the courtyard where men went in and did not always come out.

He had dressed for invisibility. Plain coat, courier's satchel with its dull leather worn soft at the corners, the face of a man carrying nothing worth remembering. His rules said walk past. His rules said never linger, never be remembered.

But Anselm was inside.

He gave his name to the sergeant at the desk—a false name, one of four he kept—and said he'd come about salvage claims. The harbor generated endless paperwork about wrecks and salvaged cargo, and clerks were too bored to care which courier asked after which claim. The sergeant waved him toward a bench without looking up from his tea.

An hour passed. Wendell counted boots. Forty-three pairs crossed the courtyard, and he memorized eleven of them out of habit before he caught himself and stopped. He wasn't here to map anything. He was here because an old man who smelled of pipe smoke and tar had unrolled a chart on Mrs. Okonkwo's kitchen table and said, *You'll have to tell me what you're carrying, boy, or find another fool.*

And Wendell had read the letters aloud. And the old man had listened with his eyes closed like a man at church, and when they were done he'd said only: "Then we sail them through."

Now Anselm sat in a cell for that chart, and Wendell could not stop doing arithmetic. Every calculation ended the same way: the old man had walked into the garrison gate knowing exactly what the gate did to people.

* * *

They let him see the prisoner at noon, on the pretense of a deposition about the confiscated skiff. The guard—young, chapped hands, bored eyes Wendell filed away as bribable if it ever came to that—walked him down a corridor that smelled of wet stone and disinfectant that had lost its argument years ago.

Anselm sat on the cot with his back against the wall, hands folded over his belly, and he was whistling.

Low, tuneless, more habit than music. He looked up when Wendell stopped at the bars, and his whole face creased into the grin of a man greeting an old friend across a tavern rather than a cell.

"There he is," Anselm said. "The invisible man. Come all this way through my door."

"You're whistling."

"I am."

"They beat you?"

"Offered to." The old man patted the cot beside him as if inviting him to sit at a fireside. "I told them my hips were older than their republic and twice as stubborn, and the sergeant decided paperwork was easier. They took my knife, my pipe, and forty years of charts. Left me the whistle." He tapped his temple. "Can't confiscate what's up here, though I expect some clerk is trying."

Wendell gripped the bars. The iron was cold enough to burn. "This wasn't supposed to happen. The decoy route was supposed to be empty. You were supposed to be long gone before anyone—"

"Before anyone noticed a one-armed pilot rowing a derelict skiff through the garrison shallows at midnight?" Anselm chuckled, and it turned into something closer to a cough. "Boy. Sit down. You're swaying like a mast in a crosswind, and I can't abide watching a man sway."

Wendell didn't sit. Anselm sighed and went on anyway.

"Ilsa told me afterward—she got clear, she's safe, don't make that face, I'd know if she weren't because Mrs. Okonkwo has eyes in every kitchen from here to the fish market." He paused. "She blames herself. You'll have to fix that. She was meant to be the one they caught, you understand. That was the design. A northern woman with northern stitching in her boots walks a route someone sold us out on—the route we *knew* was sold, because Pip heard the clerk's daughter say the shift changed an hour early, which meant someone upstairs wanted the pier route crowded and the customs house hungry."

Wendell went very still. "You knew the garrison route was bait."

"I knew the *pier* route was being left alone, which is worse. Nobody leaves a route alone by accident." Anselm spread his good hand, the missing arm making the gesture lopsided, a half-map of nothing. "So you put a loud thing on the quiet route and a quiet thing on the loud one. Ilsa walks the garrison road wearing those ridiculous boots, gets seen, gets chased—and every eye in this garrison goes to her while the real cargo sits under fish crates on the pier, stamped and boring and beneath notice." He shrugged. "Except the net closed early. Someone moved the timetable. So I stepped into her place."

"You—" Wendell's voice cracked and he hated it. "You let them arrest you."

"I *arranged* my arrest. There's a difference, and the difference is that I chose the hour of it." The grin faded into something steadier. "A man my age, boy, is worth most as a wall. You put him between the danger and the young ones, and you let him stand there. I've been standing in front of things since before your father learned to swim. It's the one trade I never retired from."

Wendell's throat worked. "Hale's people will hang you. Smuggling against the occupation—"

"They'll try." Anselm picked at a thread on the blanket. "But here's the arithmetic they haven't done. I'm a retired harbor pilot with a documented grudge against the customs house and no papers linking me to anything but a stolen skiff and a bad knee. They can hold me. They can't prove me. And they won't waste a hanging on an old man while there's a northern woman loose in their city—they'll want me talking, and I've been not-talking since 1809." His eyes glinted. "Besides. Hale remembers me. We drank together when his wife was alive. He knows I'm too mean to break and too useless to bother."

"Hale isn't sentimental anymore."

"No," Anselm agreed quietly. "But he's careful. And a careful man counts costs." He leaned forward, and the whistle-humor drained out of him entirely, leaving something Wendell had rarely seen: the pilot reading water. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once and then I'll go back to being a foolish old man in a cell. The ship sails on the morning tide. It sails *clean*. No incidents, no fires, no running, nothing that makes the harbormaster's men look twice at a single hull in that anchorage. If the departure is boring, I walk out of here in a week on a paperwork error. If there's so much as a shouted word on that quay, they tear every crate open, and everything Tomas bled for goes into a stove, and I die for nothing—which would be embarrassing, because I've planned very carefully to die for something else entirely, later, of old age, complaining."

"The pier inspection is tomorrow morning," Wendell said. "With Hale reading the manifest himself."

"Yes." Anselm watched him. "That will be a difficult conversation, I imagine."

"He offered me passage. Just me. To walk away and hand over the letters."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I'd think about it."

"Good answer. Never argue with a man holding a key; agree with him until the lock turns." Anselm settled back against the wall. "You'll say no, of course."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're here instead of packing." The old man smiled, gentle now. "Invisible men stay invisible right up until the moment they decide not to be. Then they're the bravest people alive, because they spent all those years learning exactly how much a life weighs."

Wendell stood in the cold corridor and felt something in his chest come loose—not breaking, but unclenching, like a fist he hadn't known he was making. Twenty years of rules. Never read, never linger, never be remembered. And here was a one-armed pilot in a cell, teaching him the last rule by example: *sometimes you are the wall.*

"Ilsa wants to trade herself for you," Wendell said. "She's already talking about walking into the garrison and confessing."

"Then she'll need distracting," Anselm said mildly, "which is why I'm telling *you* and not her. Keep her on the ship. That's not a request, boy, it's ballast placement—heavy things low and centered, or the whole vessel capsizes." He raised his voice slightly, pitched it to carry past Wendell's shoulder, and Wendell realized with a chill that the old man had been speaking for the benefit of the listening corridor all along. "And tell Mrs. Okonkwo my rent's paid through spring! And tell her the stew was over-salted!"

The guard appeared at the end of the hall, bored, keys jingling. "Time."

Anselm winked at Wendell. Under the noise of his own complaint, almost too low to catch, he added: "Tide turns at dawn. Don't be late for the tide."

* * *

Outside, the wind had shifted, dragging the smell of the sea up through the streets—brine and coal smoke and the faint rot of the fish market. Wendell walked fast, mind working the problem the way Anselm read channels: hazards marked, depths sounded, one line drawn clean through the shoals.

Keep Ilsa aboard. Keep the quay boring. Get the manifest stamped.

Simple. Impossible.

He cut down toward the boarding house and stopped halfway there, in the shadow of the cooper's yard, because a figure was waiting by the rain barrel—a boy in an oversized coat with a smudge of soot on his chin and an expression of enormous, suppressed urgency.

Pip.

"Pip," Wendell said carefully. "You're supposed to be home."

"I know." The boy bounced on his heels. "But there's news, and Mrs. O said run it straight to you, and I ran *fast*, faster than the baker's dog even." He gulped air. "The harbormaster's posted extra men for tomorrow. For the *dinner*. Not the pier—the dinner. He's invited the departing diplomats and he's ordered the quay cleared during the banquet, and Pip—" the boy dropped to a whisper, eyes shining with fear and glory in equal measure, "Pip heard the cook say the harbormaster keeps his real ledger locked in his study *during dinner*. When everyone's downstairs. Alone."

The wind off the harbor carried the clang of a buoy bell, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere behind Wendell, in a stone cell, an old man was whistling and counting on him to keep the water calm.

Tomorrow night: the banquet. Tomorrow dawn: the tide.

There wouldn't be time for both unless both happened at once.
