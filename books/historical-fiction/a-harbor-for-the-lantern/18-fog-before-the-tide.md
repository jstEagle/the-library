## 18. Fog Before the Tide

Adaeze Okonkwo had not slept a full night in twenty years, and she had learned to be grateful for it. Sleep was for people whose houses held nothing worth losing.

She woke at the third hour out of habit, dressed in the dark without striking a lamp, and went down through the sleeping boarding house by the stair she knew better than her own heartbeat—third step groans, seventh step silent, the banister loose where she'd never let Pip fix it because a loose banister told you when someone was creeping about who shouldn't be. The kitchen was warm already. Wendell sat at the table with a cup of tea gone cold in front of him, and he did not look up when she came in, which meant he had heard her from the moment her door opened and had decided, in his careful way, that pretending otherwise was a kindness.

"You'll want fresh tea," she said.

"I'll want it done," he said. "The tea's just somewhere to put my hands."

She took the cup anyway, poured it out, set the kettle on. This was the arrangement between them that neither of them had ever named: she fed him whether he wanted feeding or not, and he sat in her kitchen at strange hours as though it were his, and between those two stubbornnesses something had grown that she supposed other people would call family and that they called nothing at all.

"The fog's come in thick off the channel," Wendell said. "Anselm would've liked it."

"Anselm is in a cell," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "He'd have liked it drunk."

That earned her half a smile, which was more than she usually got before sunrise. She let it stand a moment and then took it away, because there was work to do and sentiment was flour you saved for bread.

* * *

By the fourth hour the whole household was awake without any of them admitting to being awake. Ilsa came down first, hair braided tight against the day, wearing the gray dress of a harbor clerk that Adaeze had altered two nights past while the house slept—taken in at the waist, sleeves cut to look worn at the cuff, ink stains applied with a walnut solution along the right forearm where a manifest clerk would rest her arm on the desk. Ilsa stood in the middle of the kitchen and turned when told to turn, patient as cloth on a mannequin, and Adaeze circled her and found her wanting in only one respect: she stood like a woman who had crossed open water in winter, weight low, ready to run. Clerks did not run. Clerks stood all day and complained about their feet.

"Chin up," Adaeze said. "Bored. You are bored. You have been bored since the tide came in and you will be bored until it goes out again."

Ilsa lifted her chin. It was almost right. There was a flicker underneath—the fear, banked but burning—but fear read differently in different women, and Adaeze had spent two decades learning to tell the difference. In Ilsa it read as alertness, and alertness was the one thing no garrison clerk ever showed at dawn.

"Pip," Adaeze called.

Pip appeared from the pantry, where he had absolutely not been stealing preserved plums, holding a jar of ink and a fistful of manifests copied fair in his own round hand. The boy had done good work. The boy always did good work, and it broke Adaeze's heart a little more each time, watching him spend his cleverness on their behalf the way other children spent marbles. He was eleven now and he moved through soldiers like smoke, and every time he came home safe she caught herself doing arithmetic she refused to finish—how many more runs, how much longer before his luck—

She stopped the thought the way she stopped a kettle boiling over: lid on, heat down.

"Read me the manifest," Wendell said.

Pip read it: deck cargo, paper stock and ships' stores, four crates consigned to the *Meridian Bell*, cleared under the name of a shipping agent three days dead whose signature nobody living remembered well enough to dispute. The letters themselves were sewn into the false bottom of the second crate, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed twice. Wendell would walk the crates onto the pier as the courier of record; Ilsa would follow ten minutes behind as the clerk sent to countersign; and the fog, if God was paying attention this morning, would keep the pier inspection to one man with one lantern and a stamp.

"And if Hale asks why the clerk is a woman?" Adaeze asked.

"He won't ask," Wendell said. "Half the customs clerks on the east quay are women since the conscriptions. That's the point of the disguise—it isn't a disguise."

It was good reasoning. Adaeze nodded once, which was all the approval she issued in company, and turned to the stove, because there was one more thing to do and she had been putting it off since midnight, and she was a woman who did hard things at the hour appointed even when the hour arrived too soon.

* * *

The coat hung in the back of her own wardrobe, wrapped in muslin, smelling faintly of cedar and of years. Adaeze climbed the stairs to fetch it slowly, one hand on the rail—not from age; age was a rumor she declined to confirm—but because her knees had opinions and because some errands deserve to be walked, not run.

Her daughter Chiamaka had been gone eleven years. Not dead—dead would have been simpler, dead would have been an ending she could carry like a stone in a pocket and know its weight forever. Chiamaka had sailed for the free ports with a printing press in pieces packed as church vestments, and the last letter had come nine years ago, and after nine years a mother learns to hold both truths at once without letting either crush the other: alive until proven otherwise, and gone regardless. The coat had gone into the wardrobe the week the letters stopped. Adaeze had taken it out twice a year to brush it and put it back, the way you visit a grave that might not be a grave.

It was a traveling coat, deep green wool, made for a tall girl with broad shoulders, lined in flannel because Chiamaka ran cold and laughed at anyone who mentioned it. There was a burn mark on the left sleeve where a spark from the press had caught her, and Adaeze had never mended it, because mending it would have been lying, and the coat was the truest thing she owned.

She carried it down and laid it across the kitchen table, and the room went quiet the way rooms do around certain objects, the way they go quiet around a Bible or a blade.

"What's this?" Ilsa asked.

"Wear it," Adaeze said. "Under the clerk's jacket. The fog is a sea fog; it comes in cold enough to ache, and clerks on the morning shift stand still for hours. A woman shivering reads honest. A woman frozen stiff reads frightened." All of this was true. None of it was why.

Ilsa touched the wool with two fingers, the way you'd test a stove. "It's too fine for a clerk."

"It's too fine for a lot of things. Wear it anyway."

Wendell understood first. She watched it land in him—he was quick, quicker than he let anyone see, which was the whole architecture of the man—and he looked away toward the window and the fog, giving the moment privacy the way he gave everything privacy.

"Whose is it?" Ilsa asked, and her voice had changed, dropped its boredom entirely.

"My daughter's."

Ilsa's hand came off the coat. "I can't."

"You can and you will." Adaeze folded it neatly, held it out. "It kept my Chiamaka warm crossing water in winter. It knows the job." She heard her own voice go dry as ledger paper and was glad of it. Tears were currency and she did not spend them on herself before dawn. "It has a burn on the left sleeve. Don't mend it. If you're ever inclined to mend it, don't."

"Why not?"

"Because it's how I'll know it, if it ever comes back to me." She pushed the coat across the table. "And it's how Chiamaka will know it, if she ever does."

The kitchen held its breath. Even the kettle, coming to the boil, seemed to do it quietly.

Ilsa took the coat and put it on, and Adaeze looked at her—a stranger, a northern woman soaked out of the sea three weeks ago carrying a drowned man's letters, a girl she had known barely a month—and felt the old terrible thing move in her chest, the thing that had nothing to do with blood and everything to do with feeding people at strange hours until they were yours. Mothers were not made, she had decided long ago. Mothers assembled themselves, piece by piece, out of whoever kept showing up hungry.

"It fits," Ilsa said, unsteady.

"It should. You've got her shoulders." Adaeze turned to the stove before her face could report anything to the room. "Sit down, both of you. Nobody walks into that fog on an empty stomach."

* * *

They ate in near silence: eggs fried hard, bread fried harder, tea strong enough to strip paint. Wendell ate mechanically, eyes elsewhere, running the checkpoints again behind his face. Pip ate fast, the way boys eat, and kept glancing at Ilsa's coat with the particular hunger children have for stories they haven't been told yet. Adaeze caught the glance.

"Later," she said, and Pip, who knew exactly one speed—fast—nodded and slowed down, which for Pip was practically obedience.

At the fifth hour the fog pressed against the kitchen windows like a live thing, and the lamplight only pushed it back a foot or two. Good. Sea fogs like this could swallow a pier at forty paces. Somewhere out in it, the *Meridian Bell* was taking on her last stores, and Anselm's smugglers' channel lay shallow and unwatched, and Inspector Hale—Wendell had told her about Hale, in the flat voice he used for things that mattered most—was somewhere between his bed and his duty, carrying whatever decision he had been carrying for days now.

Adaeze began packing the food: a sack of rolls for the road, hard cheese, a flask of chicory because coffee was beyond even treason's budget. Practical things. Her hands needed practical things or they'd start shaking, and she had a reputation in this house for never shaking.

"You could come with us to the quay," Ilsa said quietly. "Walk partway."

"No."

"Mrs. Okonkwo—"

"I don't watch departures." She tied the sack off harder than the bread required. "Twenty years. I have fed couriers and kissed their foreheads and waved them out my door, and I have buried four of them, and I will tell you what I've learned: the ones who get seen off safe come back safe, and the ones who get watched leaving—" She stopped. Set the sack down. Started again, quieter. "You go out my door while I'm kneading bread. That's how it works. I'll be here when you come back, or I'll be here when you don't, and either way the bread gets kneaded."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment across the table, and then she did the thing that undid Adaeze worse than any argument could have: she nodded, and accepted it, and understood it, the way only someone who had left people behind on a northern shore in winter could understand it.

"The attic room," Ilsa said. "If I don't come back—"

"The attic room stays made up," Adaeze said. "Indefinitely. As far as my ledger is concerned, you checked out Tuesday last and paid in full." The ledger, or what remained of it after the raid—half its pages ash, its history of sheltered souls reduced to a smell of smoke in the parlor that still hadn't faded. She had burned her own record to save one woman, and she did not regret it, and she did grieve it, both at once, the way she held everything these days.

Pip slid off his bench, dug in his pocket, and produced—of course—a plum, the preserved kind from the pantry, slightly squashed. He set it next to Ilsa's plate with tremendous dignity, as though conferring a knighthood.

"For the boat," he said. "In case the rations are bad."

"Thank you, Pip." Ilsa tucked it in her coat pocket—the coat, Chiamaka's coat, plum in pocket—and Adaeze had to turn to the window and study the fog very carefully for a moment, because it was suddenly extremely important to monitor the weather.

* * *

At half past the fifth hour it was time.

They gathered in the front hall, and the household arranged itself the way it always did for departures, which was to say badly: Wendell with the satchel and the shipping papers, checking seals he'd already checked; Ilsa in the green coat under the gray clerk's jacket, manifest case under her arm; Pip vibrating faintly with the importance of running ahead to give the all-clear from the corner by the cooperage. The fog stood in the street beyond the door like a wall built overnight, and somewhere on the other side of it waited a pier, a stamp, a man with old debts in his eyes.

Wendell opened the door. Fog leaned in, cold and salt-smelling.

He paused on the threshold and looked back at Adaeze—looked at her properly, which he almost never allowed himself—and she saw in his face the question he was too disciplined to ask: whether this was the last time she'd feed him, whether the tide that carried the letters out would carry the reprisals in.

"Bread'll be on at noon," she said.

He nodded, and that was the whole conversation, and it was sufficient.

Ilsa embraced her quickly, hard, the embrace of someone who had learned not to linger over goodbyes, and whispered something in a language Adaeze didn't speak but understood anyway. Then they were out the door and into the white, and their footsteps faded—two sets, then one, Pip peeling off toward the cooperage—and then none, and the street belonged to the fog again.

Adaeze stood in the doorway a moment longer than her rules permitted. Just a moment. Twenty years of discipline, spent freely, all at once.

Then she shut the door, lit the kitchen fire, and put her hands in the flour.

She was on the second kneading, arms aching, mind fixed with deliberate violence on the dough, when the knock came at the front door—three slow blows, military, patient—and through the fog-thick glass of the transom came a voice she knew from the raid, a voice attached to boots that had stood in her parlor counting her spoons:

"Madam Okonkwo. Open up. We're told there's a clerk missing from the east quay, and we're told she was seen coming from this house."
