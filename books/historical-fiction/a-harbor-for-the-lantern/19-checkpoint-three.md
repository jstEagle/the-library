## 19. Checkpoint Three

The fog came in off the water at four o'clock and did not leave.

Wendell stood at the mouth of Pier Nine with the manifest tube under his coat and watched the lanterns of the customs barge blur into smears of amber. The tide would turn at six-forty. The *Meridian Star* had steam up. Between him and her gangway lay forty yards of wet planking, one inspection table, one oil lamp, one folding stool—and Inspector Hale.

Of course it was Hale. Wendell had known it would be Hale since Mrs. Okonkwo pressed her daughter's coat around Ilsa's shoulders and said, *Go now, before I change my mind about letting any of you go.* The garrison had pulled its regular pier detail for the harbormaster's banquet—every man who could be spared was upstairs drinking toasts to men who were already sold—and the only officer who never drank on duty was the one who'd spent two years counting Wendell's boots.

Anselm's arrest at the garrison gate had done what Anselm intended it to do. The soldiers who should have been walking the piers were instead writing reports about a drunk old pilot with a forged pass and a suspiciously detailed knowledge of patrol rotations. They had a prisoner. Prisoners were satisfying. Piers were cold. Wendell had walked past three of them already tonight, empty as church pews on a Wednesday, and understood that he was walking through the space his friend had bought with his own wrists in irons.

He could not waste it.

*Never be remembered.* The rule had carried him through four years of occupation, through every checkpoint from the river gate to the breakwater. But rules were like boats; they got you across water, and then you burned them or they sank you. He had already broken the first rule—never read—hunched over Anselm's chart by candlelight, reading Tomas's letters aloud while the old pilot listened with his eyes closed like a man hearing music. He had broken the second rule—never linger—by staying at Mrs. Okonkwo's long past any sane courier's departure. He had broken the third rule so thoroughly, so catastrophically, that there was nothing left of it: he had let himself be loved by deaf landladies and message-running boys and exiled pilots, had let them feed him and name him and wait up for him, until invisibility stopped being armor and started being a kind of starvation.

So there was only one rule left, and it was this: *the documents reach the ship.*

Everything else was negotiable. Even him.

* * *

Hale saw him before he reached the table. Wendell knew because the inspector's lantern came up—a slow, deliberate lift, the way a man raises a glass rather than a weapon—and because Hale said his name into the fog without raising his voice.

"Galloway."

"Inspector."

"You're out late." Hale set the lantern down on the inspection table. Up close, in the yellow light, he looked older than he had at their tea six days ago—the lines around his eyes deeper, the gray at his temples gone to white at the ears. Or maybe Wendell had simply needed him to look older then and younger now, and the truth was somewhere in between, where all truths lived. "Or early. Depends which side of the tide you're standing on."

"I'm delivering a manifest," Wendell said. "Final cargo papers for the *Meridian Star*. She clears with the tide."

"At this hour?"

"The harbormaster's office stamped the schedule Tuesday." This was true. The harbormaster had stamped a great many things that week, quick and careless, a man already counting his money. "You can verify it in the morning."

"I won't need to verify it in the morning." Hale held out his hand. "The tube."

Wendell gave it to him.

There was a moment—it lasted perhaps two seconds, though Wendell would remember it for the rest of his life as lasting much longer—when the manifest tube sat in Hale's gloved hand and neither of them moved. The fog pressed against the lantern light like something alive. Somewhere behind them, in the dark of the quay, a rat moved along the pilings with the sound of small hurried feet. Down the pier, the *Meridian Star*'s watchman paced her deck, visible between fog banks as a suggestion of motion.

"Hale," Wendell said quietly. "Before you open it. Whatever you think is inside—"

"Don't." Hale's voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, down to the register he'd used that night two years ago when he'd dragged Wendell half-drowned out of the harbor current, when he'd stood over him dripping on the customs steps and said, *You're nobody, whoever you are, and tonight that saves you.* "Don't tell me what to think is inside. You've lied to me by omission for four years, Galloway, and I've permitted it, and we both know why, and we are not going to stand on this pier at five in the morning and make it sentimental."

He broke the seal.

Wendell breathed. In, out. Ilsa's face floated behind his eyes—her hands wrapped around Mrs. Okonkwo's tin cup, saying, *If it goes wrong, it goes wrong on me, not on the papers.* Pip asleep in the kitchen chair, still in his serving boy's uniform, smelling of wine dregs and candle wax because no one had the heart to wake him and make him change. Anselm in a cell somewhere, serene as an abbot. Mrs. Okonkwo's ledger, twenty years of names, a third of its pages ash.

The manifest itself was genuine—that was Ilsa's work, three nights of it, copied from a real cargo list in a real clerk's hand until even Wendell couldn't have told them apart. Baled cotton. Salt fish. Machine parts, crated and consigned. The *Meridian Star* was genuinely carrying all of those things. What she was also carrying, sewn into the false bottom of a crate of Bibles—which even the enemy's censors rarely opened, God being the one cargo everyone pretended to respect—were forty-one pages in Tomas Reyes's hand. Names. Dates. Sums paid. Escape passages sold, ships' routes traded, loyal officers listed like inventory. The republic's death warrant, signed by the men who claimed to mourn it.

Hale read.

Wendell watched him read. He had planned to look away—he had told himself on the walk down that whatever Hale's face did, he would not watch, would keep his own expression flat and courier-blank—but he found he couldn't. Four years of memorizing faces at checkpoints, of cataloguing which guards were bribable and which were believers, had made him a student of faces whether he wished it or not. And Hale's face was doing something Wendell had never seen it do.

It was aging. Line by line, as the eyes went down the page, the inspector grew old.

"The harbormaster signed off on this shipment," Hale said at last. His voice was very steady, the way voices go steady when a man is holding something down with both hands.

"Yes."

"And these crates—" His finger stopped on the line. *Bibles, crated, twelve.* "These crates were inspected where?"

"They weren't. The office waved them through. Religious consignments are exempt."

"Exempt." Hale almost laughed. It came out as a single exhaled breath, white in the cold. "Yes. Everything's exempt now, isn't it. Exempt for the right people." He looked up, and his eyes in the lantern light were the color of wet slate. "You know what's in these crates."

It was not a question, but it required an answer, and Wendell found—to his own surprise—that he was tired of answers shaped like nothing. He had spent four years being smoke. The fog itself seemed to lean in and wait.

"I know what's in them," Wendell said. "I've read every word. I read them aloud to an old man in a boarding house kitchen while a boy slept in the corner, and none of us could sleep afterward, and I would do it again. Names, Hale. Dates. Every passage sold, every route given, every officer marked for arrest the week after the fleet sails. It's all in there, in the handwriting of a man your people pulled out of the harbor two years ago and buried under the wrong name."

Something flickered across Hale's face at that—*wrong name*—and Wendell seized it, leaned into it, because if there was one coin left in his purse it was this.

"Tomas Reyes," Wendell said. "Your report called him a sympathizer drowned in the escape attempt. Your report was wrong, and someone above you knew it was wrong, and that someone is the same man whose signature authorized this manifest, and the same man hosting a farewell banquet tonight for diplomats he has already sold to the enemy. You've suspected me for two years, Inspector. Have you ever once asked yourself why you were never ordered to arrest me? A known courier, left walking free through your checkpoints? Men like me aren't tolerated, Hale. We're kept. We're the channel the water runs through, and when they want to flood a city, they don't close the channel—they widen it."

The fog drifted. The lantern flame bent and straightened.

"You could stop this," Hale said. "Right here. One stamp withheld, and the ship doesn't clear, and the crates get opened, and you spend the rest of your life in a cell instead of—" He gestured vaguely at the pier, the fog, the gray edge of the world. "Instead of whatever this is you think you're doing."

"And you spend the rest of yours knowing you helped burn the last honest record this country will produce for a generation." Wendell stepped closer. The lantern light caught them both now; there was no shadow left to hide in, and for once he didn't want one. "You saved my life once. Out of that current. I've never thanked you properly because thanking you meant admitting I needed saving. So here it is: thank you. And here is what I'm asking you to understand—I'm not asking you to betray your oath. I'm asking you to keep it. You swore to protect this port and the people in it. There's a list in that crate of people in this port who are going to disappear, and you are holding the only copy that anyone outside the conspiracy will ever see. Stamp it, and someday—maybe not soon, maybe not while either of us lives—somebody prints it, and somebody reads it, and somebody says: an inspector at the third checkpoint chose correctly. That's the whole of what I'm asking. One stamp. The smallest act on this entire pier, and the largest."

Hale looked down at the manifest again. Then, slowly, deliberately, he drew off one glove.

His bare hand was scarred across the knuckles—an old rope burn, Wendell remembered, from the rescue, from hauling Wendell's dead weight against the current. The hand hovered over the inkpad. Over the stamp. Over the space on the manifest where the harbormaster's office had already signed its careless, purchased approval.

"If that ship sinks," Hale said quietly, "or if those crates come back to me, I will say you forced them past me at knifepoint, and I will testify to it, and I will watch them hang you, and I will feel nothing."

"That's a lie," Wendell said. "But it's a useful one. Take it."

For a long moment there was only the sound of the water under the pilings and the far-off clank of the *Meridian Star*'s engine room turning over, getting ready.

Then Hale pressed the stamp to the paper, hard enough that the table jumped, and the sound of it went through Wendell like a bell through a cathedral—small, wooden, final. *Cleared for departure. Inspected. Approved.*

"Manifest's in order," Hale said, loud enough for any listening ear in the fog. He rolled the papers back into the tube and handed it over, and his bare hand shook, just slightly, just at the fingers. "Go on, then. Tide won't wait."

Wendell took the tube. He wanted to say something—wanted it badly, some words worthy of what had just happened—but everything he reached for was too large or too small, and in the end he settled for the truth, plain:

"Thank you, Inspector."

"Get off my pier, Galloway." But Hale's voice cracked on the second word, and he turned away toward the rail and the black water, and he did not watch Wendell go, and Wendell understood that this was the gift: not watching. Letting a man be invisible one last time, on purpose, out of respect.

* * *

The gangway of the *Meridian Star* rose out of the fog like a ladder into heaven. At the top, Ilsa stood in Mrs. Okonkwo's daughter's coat, manifest clerks' papers in her pocket, her face pale and set and more alive than Wendell had ever seen it. She had come aboard ahead of him with the second set of papers, playing her part to the last syllable, and now she broke it entirely—leaned over the rail, gripped his sleeve as he climbed, and searched his face.

"Well?" she whispered.

"He stamped it."

She closed her eyes. For one moment she was not a smuggler or a widow of the cause or a woman who had crossed winter water on a fishing boat; she was just Ilsa, thirty-one years old, exhausted, holding a rail. Then she opened them.

"Tomas would have liked him," she said. "In another life. If there'd been another life."

"In another life," Wendell agreed, "we'd all have been boring people, and it would have been wonderful."

Below them, at the foot of the gangway, the fog on the quay began to move wrong—lanterns swinging, boots striking planks in the quick cadence of men running, not walking. A voice carried up through the murmur of the tide, sharp with authority: the harbormaster's voice, or one of his men, shouting for the pier to be sealed, for the ship to hold, for papers to be produced—

Ilsa's hand closed hard on Wendell's arm.

"Wendell. That's not a routine check."

Down the quay, the running lanterns multiplied. The *Meridian Star*'s captain appeared at the bridge wing, shouting orders in two languages. The tide was turning. Six-forty was now.

They had perhaps three minutes, and the whole harbor had just woken up.
