## 20. The Morning Tide

The fog came off the water like something alive.

Ilsa Brandt stood at the edge of Pier Three with Mrs. Okonkwo's coat buttoned to her throat and the satchel flat against her belly, under the coat, under the shawl, under the lie that she was a manifest clerk's widow going out to settle her husband's accounts before the *Meridian* sailed. The fog was the first mercy the city had shown her since the fishing boat put her ashore six days ago. It swallowed the quay in gray wool, erased the garrison gate entirely, and turned every lantern into a smudge of gold, like the harbor had been drawn by someone who only half remembered it.

She had learned to count in this city. She counted now: forty steps from the warehouse door to the pier head. Two clerks at the manifest table, one of them yawning. A single soldier at the gangway, his rifle slung backward because he'd been standing since midnight and had stopped believing anything would happen tonight. And somewhere behind her, back up the hill where the boarding house sat dark except for one window—Mrs. Okonkwo's lantern, lit.

*Safe*, the lantern said. *Go.*

Ilsa went.

Her boots were wrong for the part—the northern stitching, the mistake that had nearly killed her at the customs house two nights ago—and so she walked the way she'd seen the fishwives walk, heels down first, heavy, a woman who had carried cod since she was nine and whose feet belonged to her trade and not to any tailor. She kept her eyes on the manifest table. She did not look at the gangway. She did not look at the water, where the *Meridian* rode at anchor with her cargo lights burning low and her crew pretending to be asleep.

At the table she set down the forged manifest Wendell had written in his small, immaculate hand—*never read, never linger, never be remembered*, but he'd broken every rule for her, hadn't he, and here was the proof, eleven lines of clerk's script naming bales of hemp and crates of salted herring that existed nowhere in the world—and she said, in her careful harbor accent, "Final tally for the Meridian. My husband started it. He'd want it closed proper."

The yawning clerk took the page without really seeing it. That was the whole secret, Ilsa had learned: people saw what exhaustion told them to see. His pen scratched. The stamp came down—a thud she felt in her teeth, the same sound as the stamp at checkpoint three, the same sound as everything that had ever decided her life—and he waved her toward the gangway without looking up.

One soldier. Forty pounds of wet rope between her and the deck.

"Evening, missus," the soldier said, not moving.

"Morning," she corrected him gently, and showed him nothing, asked for nothing, gave him no seam to pull at. Just a tired woman, a stamped paper, a tide waiting for nobody.

He stepped aside.

The gangway moved under her feet the way gangways do, breathing with the ship, and then the deck was solid under her boots and the fog was above her instead of around her and Ilsa Brandt—who had crossed open winter water in a fishing smack with letters sewn into her bodice, who had walked through three checkpoints on a dead man's handwriting—leaned against the rail and discovered that her knees were shaking.

She laughed once, quietly, so as not to wake the pretending crew.

Then she counted again, out of habit: the satchel, still flat against her belly. Eleven lines of manifest. One packet of letters in Tomas Reyes's slanted hand, dated three weeks ago from an island where drowned men apparently lived long enough to write. One name inside them—the harbormaster's name, written plain, with dates and sums—that would hang him if it ever reached a printing press beyond the reach of this dying republic's officers.

It was going to reach. In four hours the *Meridian* would clear the shallows, and in six days a free press in another country would set type, and the record would exist somewhere no raid could burn it, no ledger could misdate it, no inspector's kindness or treachery could touch it.

Ilsa looked back at the quay through the fog and began to wait for morning.

* * *

Morning came the way betrayal comes—suddenly, and from the direction you weren't watching.

She heard the horses first. Hooves on cobbles carry differently than cart wheels, faster, more arrogant, and there were too many of them for the hour. Down on the quay, the fog was thinning into rags, and through the gaps she saw riders coming down Harbor Street at a trot, six of them, eight, blue coats and bare sabers, and leading them a man on a gray horse whom she recognized even at that distance because she had spent one glittering evening memorizing his face across a banquet table.

The harbormaster.

He had figured it out. Late—but he had figured it out, and he had brought soldiers to a pier where the *Meridian* still sat inside the harbor shallows, still inside his jurisdiction, with his evidence in her satchel and forty minutes until high tide.

Ilsa pushed off the rail. Her body made the decision before her mind finished the arithmetic; she was already moving toward the stern, already looking for the mate she'd been told to trust, when she heard the harbormaster's voice crack across the water, young and furious and afraid:

"That ship does not sail! Board her! Nobody leaves this harbor!"

And that was when Pip came out of the customs house like the answer to a prayer nobody had dared write down.

She saw it all from the rail afterward, in fragments, the way you see a storm: the boy—fourteen, all elbows, wearing a serving boy's livery from last night's banquet because nobody had thought to take it off him—running along the quay with a bucket in one hand and his face arranged into pure theatrical horror. She heard him scream "FIRE!" in a voice that cracked beautifully in the middle, and she saw him hurl the contents of the bucket—not water, she realized later, but lamp oil and a rag—against the shuttered front of the customs house annex, and she saw the rag catch.

It wasn't much of a fire. It didn't need to be.

It needed to be *there*, on the quay, between eight soldiers and their orders, and it was. Smoke rolled fat and black off the old tarred timbers, and the alarm bell in the customs house tower began to swing—Pip must have paid someone, or charmed someone, or simply rung it himself while everyone else watched the flames—and the quay emptied the way quays do when fire bells ring: clerks pouring out, dockhands dropping cargo, the soldiers at the gangway turning as one body toward the smoke because a fire is a thing you can see and an order about a ship is only a thing you've been told.

"Board her!" the harbormaster screamed again, but his horse was sidling away from the smoke, and two of his men were already running the wrong way with buckets, and the third looked at the burning annex, and at the fog, and at the tide clock, and made the private calculation that men in collapsing republics make every day.

The gangway stood empty.

The *Meridian*'s mate appeared beside Ilsa as if conjured. "Cap'n says tide's ours," he said. "Anchor's coming up. You'll want to hold something."

The anchor chain began to pay out below her feet with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, link after link after link, and the fog tore open along the harbor mouth, and the wind arrived all at once the way wind does when the sea has decided something. The sails filled with a series of cracks like distant gunfire. The pier began, very slowly, to slide away beneath her.

From the rail, Ilsa Brandt watched the city that had tried to kill her grow smaller.

She found Pip immediately—she couldn't help it, her eyes went straight to him—standing in the middle of the chaos he'd made, watching his own fire burn with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life. Not triumph. Something older and sadder, a boy discovering the exact weight of what he was worth to the adults around him, and deciding, visibly, to carry it anyway. Then Anselm's arrest warrant was being waved in some sergeant's face—no, not Anselm; Anselm was free, Wendell had told her, Hale's report had named the smuggling ring dismantled—and Pip caught sight of the ship, of her, of the rail, and raised one hand.

Not waving. Saluting. Fourteen years old, saluting her like a soldier.

Ilsa pressed her hand to the rail and held it there until the smoke took the quay.

* * *

An hour out, with the harbor forts reduced to gray geometry behind them and the open water running long and cold under the bow, the mate brought her tea in a tin cup and told her she could come down from the rail whenever she liked. She didn't come down.

She opened the satchel instead.

The letters were dry. That seemed impossible—six days of fog and fear and a night spent in an attic that smelled of apples and dust—but Mrs. Okonkwo had wrapped them in oilcloth, and oilcloth keeps its promises. Ilsa untied the string with numb fingers and looked, one more time, at the topmost page.

Tomas Reyes's hand. Slanted, urgent, the i's dotted like little crosses, exactly as Wendell had demanded she prove over and over during those interrogation evenings in the boarding house parlor, both of them circling the same ghost like priests around an empty tomb. *You'll forgive me the drowning,* the letter began, because Tomas had never once in his life begun anything with an apology he meant less. *The alternative was being useful to them, and I couldn't be that and be yours.*

She had never been his. That was the strange, quiet grief of it. She had loved a man through his handwriting, through a cache in a lighthouse wall, through other people's memories of his scar and his laugh and his infuriating habit of underlining things twice. She had bet her life on his ink. And the ink had held.

Below the letter to her, the official pages waited: names, dates, sums. The harbormaster selling escape passages to the enemy and filling the empty berths with stones and lies. The list of the drowned who had never drowned, and the worse list—the ones who had. Wendell's name was nowhere in it, which was its own kind of miracle. Anselm's was, marked *paid*. Mrs. Okonkwo's boarding house appeared twice as a waystation, described with a bureaucratic precision that made Ilsa's chest ache: *safe house, reliable, landlady deaf (feigned), twenty years' service.*

Twenty years of small treasons, reduced to one line in a traitor's ledger. Somewhere abroad, a typesetter was going to make that line permanent. Somewhere abroad, it was going to become history instead of evidence.

"Land ho in six days," the mate said behind her. "Free press office is two streets off the quay. Cap'n's cousin runs it."

"I know," Ilsa said. "I'm expected."

She retied the string. She wrapped the oilcloth. And then, because there was nothing left to do with her hands, she finally allowed herself to look back.

* * *

The city was a smudge by then, hills and rooftops dissolving into the haze, but the harbor mouth was still legible, and one figure on the quay was still legible within it, because she had spent six days learning to find him in any crowd: a tall man standing very still among all that motion, the way he always stood, as though stillness were the only country he trusted.

Wendell Galloway had not boarded. Of course he hadn't. She had known it since the night he'd told her, in his flat courier's voice, *the ship needs clean water, and clean water is bought with a man on the shore*. He was staying to surrender himself, or to stand witness, or simply because a harbor needs someone in it—she didn't know which, and she suspected he didn't either yet.

But he was visible. That was the thing that undid her, standing at the rail with salt drying on her lips. For six days she had watched him practice invisibility like a monk practices prayer, and now he stood on an open quay in full daylight, in front of soldiers, unmistakable, unhidden, having chosen to be remembered by everyone—including, at this distance, by her.

He wasn't looking at the ship. He was looking at the burning annex, at the smoke, at the small figure of Pip being gathered up by Mrs. Okonkwo's neighbors, doing whatever arithmetic Wendell did instead of feeling. But just as the *Meridian* slid past the breakwater and the last of the harbor began to close behind her, his head came up—some sailor's sense, some courier's instinct for being watched—and across half a mile of cold water his eyes found hers.

She raised her hand. Not saluting. Waving. Let him have the difference.

He didn't wave back. Wendell Galloway, she thought, might never wave at anyone in his life. But he lifted his hand—just slightly, just enough, a man touching the brim of a hat he wasn't wearing—and held it there while the distance grew, and Ilsa Brandt stood at the rail of the *Meridian* with a dead man's letters against her heart and cried the way she did everything: quietly, efficiently, without letting it interfere with the work.

The breakwater passed. The harbor closed. Wendell Galloway became a smudge, then a memory, then gone.

* * *

By noon the fog had burned away entirely and the sea ran blue and enormous, and there was nothing left between the *Meridian* and the free coast but weather.

Ilsa went below and did the last of the counting. Satchel: present. Letters: dry, sealed, tied. Manifest: burned, per instructions, the moment the harbor forts dropped astern—she fed the pages to the galley stove herself and watched the stamp mark curl and go black. Nothing aboard this ship connected the documents to the people who had carried them. Nothing except what lived in her own head, and that, she decided, watching the flames, was not evidence. That was debt.

Six days to the free press. Six days to set type. Six days until the harbormaster's name stopped being a secret and started being a sentence.

She climbed back on deck. The wind had freshened and the crew moved with the loose, whistling ease of men who had made their money and knew it. The mate touched his cap to her. Even the sea seemed different—or perhaps it was only that she was seeing it without checkpoints for the first time since the fishing boat, without papers, without a cover story, without a single person on this vessel who might sell her.

It should have felt like victory. Some of it did. She let herself hold that part honestly: they had done it. The record would survive. Tomas's words—*you'll forgive me the drowning*—were going to do their work at last, in print, in daylight, beyond the reach of every officer and inspector and burning ledger in the republic that had betrayed them.

But the rest of it was this: a quay shrinking behind her, a boy saluting through smoke, an old pilot sleeping off an interrogation in a cell he had walked into on purpose, a landlady's ledger full of ash, and a tall man on the shore lifting his hand to a ship he would not be on.

*We scatter,* Ilsa thought, *so the words can travel.* That was the shape of it. That had always been the shape of it. Every network pays in flesh and distance, and theirs had paid in everything except failure.

She stayed at the rail until dark. When she finally went below, she took the letters with her, and slept with the satchel strapped to her wrist like a promise.

In the morning, the first of the crew asked her what was in it.

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment—at his honest, tired, curious face, the face of a man who had carried stranger cargo than this and knew better than to press—and she heard herself give an answer that six days ago she would never have believed could come out of her mouth:

"The truth," she said. "All of it. Every word."

And ahead of them, six days out past the horizon, a printing press waited in a free city, and a typesetter's cousin had already been sent word, and somewhere behind them a lantern was burning in a boarding house window over a man who had chosen to stay—

—while here, in her hands, the republic's last testament rocked gently with the tide, ready to be given to the world.
