## 1. The Weight of Small Numbers

The lamp on Sable Nystrom's desk had a habit of guttering at precisely the hour when the numbers turned against her, and she had long ago decided this was not coincidence but character. Lamps, like ledgers, like people, revealed their true nature under pressure.

She adjusted the wick with a pencil stub, listened to the flame steady itself, and returned to the column that had been lying to her for the better part of an hour.

*Received: forty-one casks, Meridian Trading Company, consigned Greywater to Port Solenne. Gross weight as marked.*

The trouble was in the *as marked*. The casks had come off the *Kestrel's Pride* three days ago smelling of brine and someone else's arithmetic, and Sable had spent the intervening evenings proving what her fingers had told her at first touch: the weights were wrong. Not wildly wrong. Wrong the way fog is wrong—present everywhere, obvious to no one, impossible to grab hold of. Eleven ounces here. A pound and a half there. Spread across forty-one casks like butter across too much bread.

Somewhere between the divers' baskets and the company's scales, four pounds of Greywater pearls had evaporated.

Sable knew exactly where they had gone. That was the trouble with being the person who balanced the books: you learned things you could not unlearn. Harbormaster Quill took his tithe the way a gull takes bread—openly, without shame, convinced the sea provided it for him personally. And above Quill, somewhere in the warm offices of Port Solenne where men wore coats without salt stains on them, other hands reached into other casks. The chain was old and well-oiled. It had been running since before Sable could write, probably since before her father—

Well. Since before.

She wrote the figure in the ledger. The marked weight, not the true one. Her pen moved with the smoothness of seven years' practice, and she hated it a little less than she used to, which was its own kind of sorrow.

"You're doing the face," said Tam from the loft.

"I don't have a face."

"You've got a whole weather system of faces. Right now it's the one where you're deciding whether to be honest or employed."

Sable looked up. Tam hung over the loft rail of their rooms above the weighing house, chin on his forearms, hair sticking up in every direction like he'd been fighting the pillow and losing. He was seventeen and all elbows, and he had their mother's way of looking at you sideways until you confessed something.

"The second one," she said. "Obviously."

"Obviously." He coughed then—the deep, wet cough that had been living in his chest since the equinox gales, the kind that started in the lungs and ended somewhere behind the ribs. She heard him swallow it back down, the way he did now, careful, so she wouldn't hear how bad it had gotten. As if she didn't count his breaths at night through the floorboards. As if she hadn't been counting them for two months.

"Tea's cold," she said.

"Tea's tea." He came down the ladder slowly, which he'd also started doing recently, and she pretended not to notice that too. There was a growing catalogue of things Sable pretended not to notice: the cough, the ladder, the way Tam had stopped arguing about going out on the boats because he couldn't argue and breathe at the same time. The catalogue lived in the same drawer of her mind as the eleven missing ounces. She kept it locked. She kept everything locked. It was practically a profession.

Tam settled across the desk from her and pulled the cold tea toward himself. "How bad?"

"The shipment?"

"Everything."

Sable considered lying to him, which was a family tradition of late, though he didn't know his half of it. "The shipment is fine," she said. "It will sail on time. Nobody will ever know there was anything wrong with it, including, eventually, me."

"And that doesn't itch?"

"It used to itch." She turned the page. "Now it's just the job. History happens to other people, Tam. Wars, scandals, shipwrecks, fortunes made and lost—all of it happens to people whose names aren't in our ledger. We keep the numbers straight. Or straight enough. We stay small enough that nobody looks twice at us, and in exchange we get to grow old in a town where the most exciting thing is whether the ice closes early."

"That's not living, Sable. That's hiding with extra steps."

"It's *surviving* with extra steps, and the steps are comfortable." She tapped the ledger. "You want history? History is what took Da. History is what put me indentured to Meridian at fifteen years old because the company needed somebody to blame for his debts and I was the nearest thing with a steady hand. I have had quite enough history, thank you. I'm retired from it."

Tam didn't answer right away. He drank his terrible cold tea and watched her over the rim, and the lamplight caught the fever-bright shine in his eyes that she was choosing, actively, deliberately, with her whole professional willpower, to file under *tired* rather than *sick*.

"You count my breathing at night," he said.

Sable's pen stopped.

"Through the floor," Tam went on, gently, mercilessly. "You think I don't wake up. You count, and then in the morning you ask if I slept well, and we both pretend the answer has anything to do with sleep." He set down the cup. "We're very good at pretending in this family. It's the one trade Da left us."

"Da left us nothing," Sable said, before she could stop herself, and the words came out with more edge than she'd intended, sharp enough that Tam flinched, and she immediately wanted them back, because whatever her father had left them—and it wasn't much, and it wasn't good—it wasn't fair to hand the boy the whole weight of it in one sentence over cold tea.

"He left us stories," Tam said quietly. "Bad ones, mostly. But some were good. He taught you to hold your breath longer than anyone on this coast. That's not nothing."

"No," Sable admitted. "That's not nothing." She softened it with a look, the sister-look, the truce-look. "Go back to bed. You've got the morning shift at the drying racks, and Mrs. Osk will report your absence to Quill faster than she reports her own children's births."

"She would too. That woman has filed more reports than God has filed sins." But Tam was smiling as he climbed back up, and the smile cost him a cough at the top of the ladder, muffled into his sleeve, and Sable sat very still and listened to it and did not let her face do anything at all.

* * *

Morning came to Greywater the way it always did: not arriving so much as leaking in, fog pressing against the windows until the harbor was a rumor of itself. Sable walked the short distance downstairs to the weighing house floor, past the great brass scale that was older than the company and twice as honest, and began the day's ritual.

The divers came in at seven. Old Marta first, as always, with her basket of shell and her opinions about everyone, followed by the Voss brothers, who fought like cats and dove like seals, and then the trickle of others, each basket weighed, each weight entered, each pearl lot graded by lamplight because Greywater fog did not distinguish between morning and evening and neither did the company's schedule.

"Underweight again," Marta announced, watching Sable log her basket. "Third week running. You'd think the oysters were on a hunger strike."

"The beds are tired," Sable said. "Everyone says so."

"Everyone says so because Quill says so." Marta leaned on the counter, lowering her voice to a conspiratorial rumble. "Beds are fine. I've been diving these beds since before you were a gleam in your father's eye, girl, and the beds are *fine*. Something else is light."

Sable kept her eyes on the ledger. "The weights are the weights, Marta."

"Mm." Marta straightened up, and for a moment her eyes—shrewd, salt-cured, missing nothing—rested on Sable with an expression that was almost pity. "Your father used to say the same thing. 'The weights are the weights.' Right up until the day they weren't."

Then she hoisted her empty basket and stumped out into the fog, leaving that sentence hanging in the air like something dripping.

Sable finished the morning's entries with a steadier hand than she felt. *The weights are the weights.* Yes. And the books were the books, and the fog was the fog, and a clerk who noticed too much in Greywater had a short career and a shorter life expectancy. She knew the catechism. She'd written half of it herself.

At noon the mail boat came in—not the supply ship, that came once a year and was already three weeks overdue according to the almanac, but the little mail skiff that ran the coast whenever the weather permitted, which was rarely. Sable watched from the window while Quill took delivery of the satchel, sorted through it with the proprietary air of a man handling his personal correspondence, and set aside exactly three items for the weighing house.

Three letters. Sable raised her eyebrows. They got perhaps six letters a year, mostly invoices and one annual Christmas card from a cousin in Port Solenne who could not spell.

Quill brought them over himself, which was unusual. "For the Nystroms," he said, dropping them on the counter. His eyes lingered on her a moment too long, the way they'd been lingering lately, and Sable reminded herself that paranoia was just attention with anxiety added, and she had no budget for either. "Company business takes priority. Two of these are company letters."

"Thank you, Mr. Quill."

He shuffled away. Sable waited until his office door closed before examining them.

The first was an invoice, misaddressed, meant for the Voss brothers' account. The second was a company circular about revised docking fees, stamped twice, interesting to no one alive. The third was heavier paper. Cream-colored, travel-worn, postmarked from nowhere she recognized—a coastal town so far north it was practically a rumor, a fishing settlement that appeared on charts only as a smudge and a warning about rocks.

No return address. Just a name, written in ink gone brownish with age and damp:

*Mrs. E. Nystrom.*

Their mother had been dead nine years. Whoever had addressed this envelope had either made a terrible error or was writing to a ghost, and Sable stood in the fog-gray light of the weighing house holding a letter meant for the dead, and felt the first cold thread of something that was not yet fear but was definitely shopping for it.

She should give it to Quill. Unclaimed correspondence was company property; it was rule fourteen; she had recited rule fourteen to divers a dozen times. She should set it on the counter and walk away and let the machinery of the Meridian Trading Company grind it into its proper place.

Instead she slipped it into her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone, like a coal not yet lit, like the beginning of a fire.

She told herself she'd open it after hours. She told herself it was probably a debt collector writing to a dead woman's name out of sheer bureaucratic stupidity. She told herself that history happened to other people, that she was retired from it, that the letter would prove to be nothing, nothing, nothing.

But her father had taught her to read water, long ago, before the word *father* became a thing you said only in past tense. And reading water, the first lesson, the lesson under all the other lessons, was this: when the surface goes flat and glassy and quiet, that's not calm. That's the sea holding its breath.

Something was coming. She could feel it in the flatness of the day, in the weight of cream-colored paper against her hip, in the fog that refused, all morning, to lift.
