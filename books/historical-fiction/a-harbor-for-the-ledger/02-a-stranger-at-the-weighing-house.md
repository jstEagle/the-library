## 2. A Stranger at the Weighing House

The weighing house smelled of brine and lamp oil and, on Tuesdays, of the herring that Old Piet inevitably spilled on the floorboards while arguing about his share. Sable had learned to account for all of it—the brine in the ledger's margins, the oil on her cuffs, the herring as a recurring line item under *Losses, Miscellaneous, Piet*. Seven years of Tuesdays had taught her that everything in Greywater could be entered in a column if you were patient enough, and that patience was the only currency the Meridian Trading Company respected.

She was three entries deep into the morning's receipts when the door opened and the fog walked in wearing a coat.

That was Sable's first impression, and she stood by it later. The woman who crossed the threshold was tall and thin in the way of things the sea had tried to take and failed—gaunt at the cheek, gray at the temple, with a cough she swallowed the moment it started, as though coughing were a luxury she hadn't paid for. Her coat was good wool gone soft at the elbows, southern-made, and entirely wrong for Greywater, which meant she'd come from somewhere warmer and hadn't planned to stay.

Sable knew the type. Auditors came through every autumn with that same look: *I am here briefly, and briefly is all you deserve.* But auditors carried satchels. This woman carried nothing but a canvas satchel worn to the color of old rope and a way of looking at the room as if it were a sentence she was parsing.

"Good morning," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, with an accent that had been sanded down by years of travel until only the grain of it showed. "I'm looking for the clerk."

Sable, who was the clerk, who was the *only* clerk, and who wore the company's brass pin on her collar like a target she'd chosen herself, said, "He's out."

The woman's eyes came to rest on her. They were pale eyes, the gray of a harbor before a storm, and they did not blink so much as *file*.

"Are you," she said.

"I'm the under-clerk. Assistant. Junior assistant." Sable heard herself inventing ranks and made herself stop. "The senior clerk returns Thursday."

"Thursday," the woman repeated, in the tone of someone writing it down inside her own head, in a column of her own. "Then I'll trouble you. My questions are simple ones."

"Company business?"

"Family business." She set her satchel on the counter with a soft thump, the way a doctor sets down a bag. "I'm looking for the Nystrom family."

The fog outside pressed against the windows. Somewhere across the harbor a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would ever concede.

"Never heard of them," Sable said.

She said it well. She was rather proud of how well she said it—no rise of pitch, no dart of the eyes, just the flat professional blankness of a clerk informing a customer that the company does not extend credit. Seven years of practice. Seven years of keeping her head down and her ledgers clean and her name out of every conversation that mattered, because in Greywater the people who mattered were the people the company noticed, and the company noticed people the way a net notices fish.

The woman did not argue. That was the first unsettling thing. She simply reached into her coat and produced a small notebook, and Sable's stomach performed a slow, cold roll, because notebooks were auditor language, and Sable was fluent in auditor language.

"Let me save us both some time," the woman said, opening it. "Nystrom. Edvard Nystrom, pearl diver, late of the boat *Kestrel*, lost with all hands in the autumn of 1890. Widow: Maren, deceased 1893, lung fever. Two children." Her pale eyes lifted. "A son, Tam, seventeen. And a daughter who would now be twenty-three, who was entered into indenture to the Meridian Trading Company at fourteen years of age, contract number—"

"Stop." Sable's voice came out smaller than she wanted. She cleared her throat and rebuilt it. "That's—those are records. Anyone could read records."

"Anyone could," the woman agreed pleasantly. "I did. In the company archive at Meridian South, where I spent eleven days reading shipping manifests, which is a form of punishment I wouldn't wish on a customs officer." She turned a page. "The daughter is described in the 1890 personnel notes as 'small, quiet, quick with figures.' The notes for 1893 add: 'unremarkable. Keep on.' The notes for 1896—" she paused, and something almost like gentleness crossed her face, "—are blank. Which means either you stopped being worth writing about, or you got very good at it."

"I'm a clerk," Sable said. "Being worth writing about is against company policy."

The woman's mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "You have her way of talking, at least. According to a man in the northern camps."

The floor did something. Sable gripped the counter's edge, and the counter, being company property, did not care.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, "and I have work."

"You do. I've watched you do it through the window for the better part of an hour. You write fast and check twice, you shorted no one this morning, and when Piet tried to argue his herring weight down by two pounds you let him, because you'd already entered the difference under losses and decided it was cheaper than the argument." The woman closed the notebook. "I've spent a long time in places where people lie to each other for profit. You lie to protect. It's a different animal. It has different tracks."

"Who *are* you?"

"Ilse Varric." She said it the way other people said *no one important*, which is to say she said it and then watched to see what Sable would do with it. "I've come a long way. I've come further than you'd believe, and I'm not going to pretend the journey was pleasant, because I've decided to stop lying where it costs nothing." Her hand went to her coat's inner pocket, and Sable's whole body braced—but what Ilse Varric drew out was not a pistol or a writ or any of the instruments Sable's imagination supplied. It was a letter. Folded in thirds, softened at the creases, carried long enough to go furry at the edges.

She set it on the counter between them.

"I was told to put this in the daughter's hand," Ilse said. "The daughter, I'm told, is small, quiet, and quick with figures. The woman in front of me is all three, and she's also the only person in this town who went still at the name Nystrom instead of going sad. Sad is what the town does. Still is what family does." She stepped back from the counter. "I'll be at the Salt & Anchor until the ice closes. I'm told everything in Greywater happens before the ice closes, or never."

"Wait—" The word escaped before Sable could catch it and enter it anywhere. "Wait. Who told you? Told you what?"

But Ilse Varric only shook her head, once, slow, and there was something in her face that Sable would spend the rest of the week taking apart: not pity, exactly. Something more like the expression of a person handing over a heavy thing they'd carried so long their arms had forgotten how to hang.

"The letter explains what it explains," she said. "It doesn't explain everything. Nothing does." She coughed then—really coughed, deep and wet, and turned her head to do it, courteous even in that—and when she turned back her eyes had watered and her voice had roughened. "Read it somewhere private. Not here. Not where the walls have company ears."

And she left, the bell over the door giving its dull clank, the fog folding her up like a letter being put away.

* * *

Sable stood at the counter for a long moment.

The letter sat there. It was the most ordinary object in Greywater—cheap paper, a smudge of tar or pitch on one corner, folded the way working people fold things, in thirds and then in half and then jammed somewhere for a season. She had handled ten thousand such papers. She knew their whole taxonomy: the company's cream stock, the customs office's yellow, the cheap gray stuff the fishermen bought three sheets for a penny.

This was the gray penny paper. Her father had bought it by the block.

That thought arrived whole and uninvited, the way grief does, and Sable stood very still and let the weighing house go on around her—Piet's boots, the gulls, the foghorn out on the point moaning its one long note like a cow that had learned despair.

*He's dead,* she told herself. *He's seven years dead. The sea took the Kestrel and all hands, and the company sent a form, and the form said LOST WITH ALL HANDS, and I was fourteen, and I signed an indenture with the same pen I signed the condolence register, and that is the whole of it. Dead men do not write letters. Dead men do not buy penny paper. Dead men are the one category of account that balances and stays balanced.*

Her hand moved anyway.

That was the humiliating part, the part she would not tell anyone, ever: her hand reached for the letter the way Tam's hand, at four years old, had reached for the tide line where their father's boat was just going out—reached without asking the rest of the body's permission, without consulting any ledger at all.

She didn't open it at the counter. She was a clerk; she had rules, and one of them was *never open anything at the counter*, because the counter was where the company watched. She tucked it into her apron, against her stomach, where it lay like a stone with a heartbeat.

The rest of the morning was a farce she performed for an audience of one. She weighed Piet's second delivery of herring and entered it without argument, and Piet, sensing something wrong in the natural order, argued anyway, just to be kind. She checked a manifest twice and found no errors either time and checked it a third time out of pure superstition. She sent the office boy, Weevil—a ferret-faced twelve-year-old whose actual name was Ambrose but who had earned his nickname by getting into everything—to the harbormaster with a receipt, mostly because she needed the room empty for ninety seconds.

In those ninety seconds she took the letter out and held it, unopened, under the lamp, and looked at the outside of it.

There was no address. There was no name. There was only one word, in the top left corner, in a slanted, urgent, left-leaning hand that dragged its letters like a man walking into wind:

*Sable.*

Seven years. Seven years of forgetting a handwriting on purpose, the way you forget a smell, the way you forget the exact sound of a voice by telling yourself you have—and there it was, the whole of it, the *b* that never closed properly, the *l* that leaned like it was drunk, the final *e* that he always made too large, as though even his letters wanted to leave more of a mark than they'd earned. He used to write the shipping lists in that hand, and the company's southern clerks complained every single season, and her father used to laugh and say, *Let them squint. It builds character.*

Sable put the letter back in her apron and put her hands flat on the counter and breathed the way the company had taught her to breathe, which was shallowly and without complaint.

Weevil came back. Piet went home. The fog thickened. The lamp burned.

At half past four, when the light through the windows went the color of dirty pearl, Sable locked the ledger, locked the drawer, locked the door, and stood on the step with her key in her fist and understood that she was now a woman carrying a letter through a fog-bound town, and that every step of the walk home was going to feel like walking on the deck of the *Kestrel*, and that she was going to do it anyway.

* * *

Tam was asleep when she got home, which was the day's second mercy. He slept in the day now more than he should, and woke coughing in the night more than he admitted, and had taken to insisting he was "fine" with a bravado so transparent that Sable had begun entering it mentally under the same column as Piet's herring: *Losses, Miscellaneous.* Their landlady, Mrs. Grum, a widow shaped like a rain barrel and armed with opinions about lungs, had pronounced last week that the boy needed "warmth and fat and southern air," two of which were unaffordable and one of which did not exist in Greywater.

Sable lit the lamp, fed the stove, and sat down at the kitchen table with the letter in both hands.

She thought about burning it unread. She genuinely considered it—the way you consider, standing on a high place, the drop. Burn it, and the world stays balanced. Her father is dead, the company owns her years, Tam coughs, the fog rolls in, and history continues happening to other people, which is the only way Sable Nystrom has ever survived it.

She thought about her father's face, and found—with a small, guilty horror—that she had to work to summon it. She had his handwriting perfectly and his face only approximately. Seven years will do that. Seven years will take a man's face and leave you his penmanship, and there is no column for that, no entry, no way to balance it.

She opened the letter.

The hand inside was worse than she remembered—shakier, the letters leaning harder, as though the wind he'd always walked into had followed him north and grown teeth. The ink was cheap and blotchy. He had written it fast, or in the cold, or both.

*Sable. If you are reading this then Ilse found you, which means she got south of the ice, which means one stubborn thing went right for once. I don't know what she told you and I told her to tell you nothing, so whatever she said, half of it is true and the half that isn't is mine, not hers.*

*I am alive. I know what that sentence does. I am sorry for it and I am not taking it back, because taking it back would be a lie and you've had enough of those from me to last.*

*I am at the fishing camps past Sker Point, the herring stations. I am not well. None of us up here are well, the camps are rotting from the inside, but that is not your trouble and I won't make it so. My trouble is this: I have things here that need to come out of the north before the ice closes, and I need my daughter to help me do it, because you are the only person I have ever known who can keep a secret and a ledger at the same time, and I am in need of both.*

*Come before the ice closes. After the ice, nothing moves, and everything waits, and I have waited seven years already, which is seven more than I deserve.*

*Don't tell the boy. Not yet. Let me be the one to—*

The sentence stopped. There was a blot, as if the pen had been held too long in one place, thinking. Then, at the bottom of the page, smaller, the letters cramped together like a man hunching against weather:

*You'll have questions. I have answers, and some of them are even true. Come, and ask them.*

*Your father,*

*E. Nystrom*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times her eyes kept snagging on the same four words—I *am alive*—the way a boat snags on a reef, over and over, tearing a little more each pass.

The lamp hissed. Tam coughed in his sleep, once, a small wet sound that went through her like a hook. The foghorn moaned. And Sable Nystrom, who had built a life on the principle that history happens to other people, sat in a cold kitchen holding proof that history had been waiting for her all along, patient as a creditor, and had finally come to call.

She looked at the signature—*E. Nystrom*, that drunk leaning *l*—and heard herself say, out loud, to an empty kitchen, in a voice she didn't recognize:

"You absolute *bastard*."

It was the first dishonest entry of her adult life, because it wasn't true. He wasn't a bastard. He was worse. He was a dead man who wasn't dead, and he wanted something, and the letter—she read it a fourth time now, slowly, the clerk in her finally shouldering past the daughter—was not a letter at all.

It was a summons.

And somewhere across the harbor, in a room at the Salt & Anchor, a gray-eyed woman who read shipping manifests for punishment was waiting to see what she'd do about it.
