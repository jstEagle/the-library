## 3. Seven Years of Salt

Sable did not sleep.

She tried. She was a woman who could sleep standing up on a pitching deck and had once napped through a hailstorm against the weighing house wall, but sleep wanted nothing to do with her tonight. Every time she closed her eyes the letter floated up behind her lids like something drowned surfacing, and she would open them again and there it would be on the kitchen table, real as a debt, waiting to be read a fifth time.

She made tea instead. She made tea the way her mother used to, strong enough to trot a horse across, and she sat with her hands around the cup and let the seven years come, because they were coming anyway and it is a clerk's instinct to open the books when the auditors arrive—even when the books are your own.

* * *

The night the *Kindly Meridian* went down had been the kind of autumn evening that Greywater saved up for all year: clear, windless, the water lying flat as poured glass, so that the harbor looked less like water than like a second town sunk upside-down beneath the first, its masts and gulls and lamp posts doubled and made perfect. Sable was seventeen. She remembered that because she remembered thinking, standing on the dock with Tam's small mittened hand in hers, *what a beautiful night for anyone to be out on the water*, and how the thought had seemed like a blessing at the time and later like the cruelest kind of arithmetic—because beautiful nights are exactly what kill people here. Storms you can see coming. Calm is what lies.

Her father had gone out with only Anders Pell for crew, chasing a rumor of pearl beds off the old shoals that nobody had worked since before the company bought up the leases. He'd been drinking, but he was always drinking, and he'd been cheerful, which was rarer, and he'd kissed his palm and pressed it to the top of Tam's head—the gesture he fancied was a blessing and which Tam, aged nine, had received with the weary dignity of a boy being licked by a large dog—and then he'd said to Sable, "Keep the books straight, bird," which was what he always said, and pushed off, and that was the last thing.

They found the boat three days later, thirty miles south, swamped and drifting. They found one oar. They found Anders Pell, in the water, drowned with his boots on, and because they found Anders they stopped looking for anything else, because the sea does not usually take one man from a boat and leave the other, and grief is lazy the way current is lazy—it follows the easiest channel. The priest said the words over an empty box weighted with stones. Her father's slanted handwriting went into the family Bible between two pressed ferns, finished forever, and Sable learned within a month that a dead father is easier than a living one. A dead father cannot drink the rent. A dead father cannot stand in the market square telling loud stories about the pearls his daughter will dive up someday while everyone smiles the particular smile this town keeps for the Nystroms. Dead, he became a tragedy. Alive, he had been an expense.

The company had been almost gentle about it. That was the part she'd never examined closely until now, sitting at her kitchen table with the tea going cold. Within a week of the empty box, Meridian Trading had offered her a position. Not charity—they didn't call it that, the letter had been very careful not to call it that—but a *position*, clerk's apprenticeship, room above the weighing house included, terms generous given the circumstances. Given the circumstances. She had signed her name at sixteen-and-a-half years old to a document longer than her arm, and the word *indenture* had appeared exactly once, in the fourth paragraph, in print smaller than any other word on the page, and she had read it, and understood it, and signed anyway, because Tam needed feeding and grief makes everything look like a door.

She had spent seven years believing the company had thrown a rope to a drowning family.

Now, holding a dead man's letter, she let herself think, for the first time, the other thought: *generous terms given the circumstances*. What circumstances, exactly? A dead diver? Or a debt discharged—a liability settled, an account closed, a loose ledger line tidied away?

She put the thought down. It was too heavy to lift all at once. She picked up the letter instead.

* * *

The paper smelled of smoke and fish and some resinous northern pitch, as if it had been carried a long way against a body that ran hot. The handwriting was unmistakably her father's—the drunk leaning *l*, the *E* that looked like it had been blown sideways by weather—and yet wrong, older, cramped at the ends of lines as if the hand had grown stiff or the words had run out of room. She smoothed it flat on the table and read it slowly, aloud, because reading aloud is how clerks catch errors, and she wanted very much to catch this one in an error. She wanted it to say something that made no sense. It refused.

*Sable.*

*You will have questions and I haven't answers that fit on paper. I am alive. That is the whole of the news and it is news enough. I am north, past Kettle Inlet, at the Herring Mouth camps. Do not tell anyone, not even your brother, not yet. There are men who think I am useful drowned and I intend to go on disappointing them.*

*I know what you must think. I can guess most of what you think because I would think it too. But bird, listen: I did not choose this. Things were done that winter that left me no road but the water, and I took the water, and God forgive me I let you believe the worst because the worst was safer for you than the truth. Safer for Tam. That at least was true when I made it true.*

*I am sick. Not dying, before you get your hopes in order—sick, the lung-rot that is in all the camps now, slow but certain if a man stays cold. I need to move south before the ice closes the inlet, and I cannot move without help, and there is no one left in this world who owes me love except you and Tam, and I am not such a fool as to ask Tam.*

*There is a berth problem. There is always a berth problem in Greywater; there was a berth problem the day you were born, the midwife complained of it. The supply ship goes south before ice and it has room for exactly those the company permits. You work for the company. Work it, bird. One berth. That is all I ask. One berth, and I will vanish south and trouble no one's ledgers again, including yours.*

*I do not ask forgiveness. I would not know where to file it.*

*Your father,*
*E.N.*

P.S. *Tell the Varric woman nothing about the box. Whatever she says. Especially if she is kind.*

Sable read it twice more. Then she set it down, walked to the window, opened it, and breathed the harbor air for a while, because the alternative was to scream, and screaming carries in Greywater the way bells do.

*Not even your brother.* As if Tam hadn't spent seven years leaving a lamp burning in the window on the anniversary—as if Sable hadn't found him doing it every single November and pretended she hadn't, because taking it away felt like killing him twice. *I let you believe the worst because the worst was safer.* And there it was, the sentence she would carry now instead of grief, the stone replacing the stone: he had chosen. On a calm beautiful night with the water flat as glass, her father had looked at his children and chosen himself, and then he had written to her seven years later and called it safety, and asked her to commit fraud against her employer as though he were asking her to pass the salt.

And then—she laughed, actually laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it—the postscript. *Tell the Varric woman nothing about the box. Whatever she says. Especially if she is kind.*

What box? She turned back through the letter hunting for it. Nothing. No box mentioned anywhere except in warning. Which meant the box was real, and important, and her father assumed Ilse Varric knew about it and wanted it—which meant the gray-eyed stranger who had stood at her counter with unsettling precision was not delivering a dying wish but running an errand. For whom? For him? Against him? And kindness—*especially if she is kind*—kindness was listed right alongside whatever she might *say*, as a method of attack.

Sable closed the window. Somewhere below, the tide was turning; she knew it by sound alone, the soft wooden groan of the pilings, a noise she had slept inside of for twenty-four years. History happened to other people. That had been her creed, her whole creed, the entire architecture of her survival: keep the numbers small, keep the name clean, keep the door shut. And history had waited seven years like a patient creditor, and now it had not merely knocked—it had posted a letter through the gap under the door, in her dead father's hand, billing her for everything.

*One berth,* she thought. The annual supply ship. She did the sums automatically, because sums were where her mind went to hide: one berth, and three claimants already lined up in her chest like men outside a harbormaster's door. A father who wasn't dead. An errand-woman who might be dying and might be lying. And Tam, coughing in the next room with that wet deep rattle that had gotten worse since the equinox winds came, Tam whose face went translucent-gray after every climb up the weighing house stairs—

No. She put Tam aside. She put him carefully, deliberately, in a drawer and shut it, because if she opened that drawer tonight she would do arithmetic she wasn't ready to do.

What she allowed herself instead was anger, plain and hot, and it surprised her how good it tasted. For seven years she had mourned cleanly. She had kept his handwriting in the Bible and his good qualities polished and his faults filed under *illness*, the way you'd file a bad debt hoping the debtor would rise. And he had risen. He had risen exactly as far as his own convenience and no further, and the first thing he'd done upon rising was ask her for something.

"You absolute bastard," she said again, quieter this time, testing it. It still wasn't true. It was still better than crying.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over the harbor, and with it came sounds: gulls arguing over something at the fish stalls, the first cart wheels on the shingle, Tam stirring behind the partition with a cough that started wet and ended in that small exhausted silence afterward that frightened her more than the cough did. She fed him porridge and lied to him about how well he sounded, which was becoming a weekly entry in a ledger of lies she was no longer keeping honestly.

"I'm going in early," she said, buttoning her coat. "Company business."

"Since when do you go in early?" Tam asked, nine years dead of curiosity and reborn as a fourteen-year-old with a fever and a detective's instincts. "Since when does anyone go in early?"

"Since the auditor comes today."

That was true. She'd known it for a week—Meridian's regional office dispatching one Auditor Brandt to review the winter books, arriving on the morning packet—and she had filed the knowledge under *routine*, because auditors came every few years the way comets came, and were survived the same way: by keeping your head down. But she heard herself say it now and felt the word *routine* come loose in her hand.

"The one from the city," Tam said. "Is it true he once found eleven thousand crowns hidden in a church roof?"

"It's true he finds things," Sable said. "That's the whole job. Finding things."

"Well," said Tam, with the terrible clarity of the fevered, "you'd better hope he doesn't find anything."

She kissed the top of his head—her father's blessing-gesture, done properly, with actual love—and went out into the gray morning, down the stairs, along the quay, past the weighing house with its lamps still lit, and she did not walk toward it. She walked instead along the seawall, to the stretch of rail where the water lay quiet and doubled the town upside down beneath itself, and she stood looking down at that drowned mirror-town for a long moment, at the perfect inverted world where masts grew downward and gulls flew under the surface.

Then she took her father's letter from inside her coat, held it over the water, and very nearly let go. It would have been easy. Paper floats briefly and sinks thoroughly, and the sea has taken worse secrets.

But she was her father's daughter, and also her mother's, and mostly her own; and a clerk does not destroy an original document. A clerk files it.

She put the letter away, and turned up her collar against the wind coming off the water, and walked to work—past the Salt & Anchor, where a light burned in a second-floor window, gray-eyed behind the glass; and up the weighing house steps, where on the door, pinned neatly at eye level, someone had already posted the notice she had been dreading all week in small official type:

**MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY — OFFICE OF THE AUDITOR. ALL LEDGERS TO BE MADE AVAILABLE. NO EXCEPTIONS.**

Beneath it, in fresher ink, someone had added a single line, and Sable read it twice before her legs would carry her inside:

*Commencing with the pearl shipments.*
