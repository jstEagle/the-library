## 4. The Auditor's Shadow

Auditor Brandt arrived in Greywater on the morning tide, which was fitting, because Brandt considered himself a man of tides—inevitable, patient, and indifferent to what he carried away.

He came ashore before the fog had finished lifting, a thin man in a good coat that had been cut for a slightly larger one, carrying a single leather case and an umbrella he did not open despite the drizzle. The harbormaster's boy took his bag without being asked, which was correct; Brandt had that quality of men whom one assists without deciding to. He tipped the boy nothing, but remembered his name, and this unsettled the boy more than a coin would have.

"Meridian Trading Company," Brandt said to the first person who looked official enough to matter. "I am expected."

He was not, strictly speaking, expected until Thursday. It was Tuesday. This too was deliberate. An audit announced in advance is a courtesy; an audit arrived early is a truth.

* * *

The Meridian offices in Greywater occupied the second floor of the weighing house, above the great brass scales where divers' pearls were weighed and fishermen's catches were docked for "moisture loss," a phrase Brandt found charming in its honesty. Below, the floorboards were worn pale in two paths: one from the door to the scales, one from the scales to the company safe. Above, the paths continued—door to ledger room, ledger room to director's office. Brandt noted, within his first hour, that the path from the ledger room to the window was worn as well, and wider than it ought to be.

Someone stood at that window often. Someone who watched the harbor while pretending to work.

He began, as he always began, with the stationery.

This puzzled people when they learned of it—the junior clerks especially, who imagined auditing to be a matter of dramatic discovery, of slamming ledgers shut and crying *Aha!* In truth, accounting fraud was like rot in a ship's hull: you did not find it by looking for the hole. You found it by noticing where the paint bubbled. And paper, Brandt had learned over nineteen years and eleven postings, told on itself. A company that bought twice its usual ink in autumn was writing something it hadn't written before. A clerk who requested fresh blotting paper every week was hiding something wet.

The Greywater stationery log showed nothing unusual. Ink purchases: steady. Blotting paper: steady. But there, in the column of miscellaneous supplies—a category most clerks treated as a junk drawer—was an entry that made him set down his cup of coffee untasted.

*One (1) iron strongbox lock, replacement, 4s 6d.*

Greywater was a town of perhaps six hundred souls. Its company office kept its valuables in a wall safe older than the harbormaster. Who, in Greywater, had needed a strongbox lock?

He wrote the date in his notebook. Then he wrote the name of the clerk whose initials appeared beside the entry: *S.N.*

* * *

The ledger room smelled of lamp oil, cold tea, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by many anxious hands. There were four desks. Three of them belonged to clerks named Hobb, Merrick, and a boy called Fenwick who was chiefly employed, so far as Brandt could tell, at being seventeen.

The fourth desk sat beneath the window.

It was the tidiest desk Brandt had ever encountered outside of a photograph. Papers squared. Pens aligned. An inkwell positioned with geometric precision relative to the blotter. On the desk's corner, a small dish held three sea-worn pebbles, gray and white, arranged smallest to largest—an arrangement so faintly human that Brandt paused over it longer than he meant to.

The ledger itself was beautiful. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Brandt had audited forty-one branch offices of the Meridian Company, and their ledgers fell into types the way fish fall into species. There was the sloppy ledger, honest as a dog. There was the fussy ledger, usually kept by a mediocre man trying to seem excellent. And there was the fraudulent ledger, which always, always tried too hard—every column perfect, every total arriving on the first try, no smudges, no corrections, no evidence anywhere of a human being doing arithmetic under a bad lamp after a long day.

Sable Nystrom's ledger was none of these. It was immaculate, but not suspiciously so. There were corrections—small, honest ones, a transposed figure here, a misdated entry there, each initialed in a small slanted hand. It was the work of someone very good who understood that looking flawless was itself a kind of confession.

Brandt turned pages for two hours without moving anything on the desk except the ledger, and he returned each page exactly as he'd found it, because the first rule of hunting was that the prey should never hear your footsteps.

The pearls came through in shipments, as they did in all the northern offices. Divers brought in rough stock; the company weighed, graded, and shipped south. The weights were logged here, confirmed at the southern house, and reconciled quarterly.

He found the discrepancy on the third day, though he had sensed it on the first, the way one senses a draft from a door left ajar somewhere down a long corridor.

Shipment 114-G, logged October last. Gross weight at intake: 214 stone, 6 pounds. Weight received at the southern house: 213 stone, 11 pounds. Five pounds of pearl, gone between Greywater and the coast. Not impossible—pearls dried, sacks leaked, porters thieved—but five pounds was a great deal of pearl, and the southern house had filed no complaint, which meant either they hadn't noticed or they hadn't wanted to notice.

And here was the thing that made Brandt sit very still in the grey morning light: the intake weight had been written once, firmly, in Sable Nystrom's hand—and then, in the margin, in the same hand, smaller, almost apologetic, a tiny notation: *damp scale?*

Two words. She had filed around it. She had recorded the discrepancy, flagged it with the gentlest possible excuse, and moved on, and the notation was so unremarkable that any auditor skimming for fraud would pass right over it—which was, Brandt suspected, precisely why she had written it that way. You cannot be accused of concealment if the thing is technically in the book. You also cannot be accused of raising alarms if the alarm is a question mark about dampness.

It was, he had to admit, elegant. He wrote in his notebook: *S.N. — sees everything. Says nothing. Why?*

There were only ever two answers to that question, in his experience. Fear, or complicity. Sometimes both, wearing each other's coats.

* * *

On the fourth day he began pulling threads, and the threads kept leading back to the same small desk beneath the window.

Thread one: the Nystrom family account. Josiah Nystrom, pearl diver, deceased seven years by drowning, had owed the company a sum that Brandt calculated—crossing out and re-adding twice to be sure—at roughly nine years of a diver's best earnings. The debt had not been discharged by death. Instead, the company had done something Brandt had seen done before and never grown comfortable with: it had converted the debt into labor. The daughter's indenture. Seven years of a girl's life, priced against her father's failure, with the books showing the indenture as an *asset*—line 44, column B, valued annually, depreciating as she served.

Brandt read line 44 three times. Somewhere in the southern offices, a man he would likely never meet had decided that a drowned man's daughter was worth fourteen hundred marks a year, and had entered her into the asset register between a shipment of rope and the weighing house roof.

He found himself thinking about the pebbles on her desk. Smallest to largest. He did not know why he thought of them, and he disliked not knowing, so he wrote the thought down to examine later, the way he examined all things that snagged him.

Thread two: the letters. Outgoing correspondence, copied into the letterbook in the clerk's own fair hand. Most of it was routine—requisitions, confirmations, the polite menace companies used instead of guns. But there was a pattern in the addressees. Over the past year, copies of certain shipping manifests had been sent not only to the southern house but to Director Corvane directly, bypassing the regional office entirely. Six times. Each time, the manifest concerned high-grade pearl shipments. Each time, the copy bore the notation *per request of Director's office.*

Clerks did not write to directors. Directors' offices did not request copies of provincial manifests unless someone upstairs was watching this particular harbor very closely indeed—or unless someone downstairs had been asked to keep a record that could later serve as proof of innocence. Or guilt. The letterbook did not say which, and Brandt respected it for that.

Thread three: the missing predecessor. The ledger room had a shelf of closed books going back eleven years, and eleven years of books required eleven years of signatures. The signature for the year of Nystrom's drowning was present, but the ink was different from the surrounding entries, and the flourish on the capital letter hesitated mid-stroke, the way a signature does when the hand signing it is copying rather than writing. Someone had signed for a clerk who was, presumably, no longer available to sign for himself.

Brandt added it to the notebook. He did not yet know what it meant. He only knew that in Greywater, the year a diver drowned, a signature had been forged in the company's own books, and the company's current auditor had not been told.

* * *

He observed her before he spoke to her. This was policy, but it was also, if he was honest—and Brandt prided himself on a narrow, well-fenced honesty—curiosity.

She came in before the others, always. He learned this by arriving earlier, which cost him sleep and gave him pleasure in unequal measure. She lit the lamps, filled the inkwells, laid out the day's forms in the order they'd be needed, and then she stood at the window for exactly as long as it took the fog to show whether the fishing fleet had gone out. Then she sat and worked, and she worked the way water runs—without apparent effort, without pause, downhill toward whatever needed doing.

Once, he watched her catch an error in Fenwick's figures before the boy had finished writing them. She said nothing. She simply waited, pen hovering, until Fenwick frowned, went back two lines, found it himself, and corrected it, looking pleased with himself. She let him have it. She even smiled—a small, quick thing, like a fish surfacing.

*Generous,* Brandt wrote in the notebook, and then crossed it out, because generosity was data, not judgment, and he could not afford judgments yet. Though he noticed he had pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

On the sixth day, the discrepancy in Shipment 114-G was joined by a second: Shipment 121-C, underweight by three pounds, again flagged with a marginal note (*scale drift?*), again passed over by the southern house. Two discrepancies in one season was not drift. Two discrepancies in one season, both routed through the same clerk, both excused with the same soft little question mark, was a system.

That evening, Brandt stayed late in the ledger room with the lamp turned low, reading backward through five years of intake records, and built the pattern in his notebook like a man building a ship in a bottle, piece by piece with tweezers:

Underweight shipments: eleven in five years. Total shortfall: thirty-one pounds of graded pearl. Value, conservatively: a sum with four zeroes. All flagged by S.N. All excused. All accepted downstream without query.

Either Sable Nystrom was stealing pearls with a delicacy amounting to genius, or Sable Nystrom knew who was, and had chosen—with that same delicacy—to survive alongside the knowledge rather than die inside it.

Brandt blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the harbor work below: the clank of the scales, the shouts of men, the gulls conducting their eternal, corrupt negotiations over fish heads. He found, to his mild surprise, that he did not want her to be guilty. This was inconvenient. He examined the feeling, turned it over, and put it away in the drawer where he kept all feelings, which he opened perhaps twice a year for maintenance.

* * *

They met formally on the seventh day, in the director's absence, over tea that Merrick poured badly.

"Miss Nystrom." He said her name the way he said all names, as though confirming a figure against a source document. "Your ledgers are the finest I have audited in eleven postings."

He watched her receive the compliment. She didn't preen—that would have been ordinary. She went still, the way small animals go still, and said, "Thank you, Mr. Brandt," in a voice that offered him exactly nothing and dared him, very politely, to try taking more.

"I have questions," he said, "about Shipment 114-G."

Something happened behind her eyes then. It lasted less than a heartbeat, and ninety-nine auditors in a hundred would have missed it, but Brandt had spent nineteen years training himself to see heartbeats behind faces, and what he saw was not guilt. Guilt flinches. What he saw was recognition—the look of a woman who has been waiting, quietly, for years, for someone to finally ask.

"Damp scale," she said. "I noted it at the time."

"You did." He opened his notebook to the clean page he'd saved. "Tell me about the scale."

"It sticks at the low end when the weather turns. I reported it twice. There are requisitions in the file."

"There are." He let the silence do some work. "Miss Nystrom, in five years, eleven shipments have come up light through this office. Every one of them passed across your desk. Every one of them carries your tidy little question mark in the margin. And every one of them was waved through south without a murmur." He folded his hands. "You will understand that I am faced with a puzzle. Either you are the most accomplished thief on this coast, in which case I congratulate you and will nonetheless see you transported, or you have known for years exactly where those pearls went, and you wrote your question marks as insurance policies."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, the scales clanked, weighing somebody's honest living dishonestly.

"And the third possibility, Mr. Brandt?" she said.

He raised an eyebrow, genuinely pleased. "Which is?"

"That I'm a clerk," she said. "And clerks write what they see, and survive what they can't change. Ask your next question."

He did not ask the next question—not yet. Patience was the whole of his method; a hunter who fired early merely educated his quarry. Instead he thanked her for the tea, complimented the filing system, and gathered his coat, and at the door he paused as though struck by an afterthought.

"One small thing," he said. "The stationery log. Last spring. A strongbox lock, four shillings sixpence, initialled S.N." He watched her face with the attention other men gave to sunsets. "Whose strongbox, Miss Nystrom?"

And there it was again—that stillness, deeper now, the stillness of deep water. But beneath it, unmistakable, a flicker of something that was neither fear nor guilt nor recognition.

Relief. Faint and quickly buried, but relief, as if the question he'd asked was one she had already answered for herself, long ago, in the dark, standing at a window watching a harbor.

"A replacement," she said evenly. "For the old one. It rusted."

"Mm," said Brandt. "Of course it did."

He walked out into the fog with his umbrella furled and his case light on his shoulder, and in his notebook, under her name, he wrote a single line:

*She is protecting someone. Find out who died.*

Because that was the shape the threads had begun to make, though he could not yet prove it: every anomaly in Greywater's tidy books circled back, sooner or later, to a drowned diver named Josiah Nystrom—and to the daughter who kept his memory the way she kept the ledgers.

Perfectly. Quietly. With a question mark in the margin.
