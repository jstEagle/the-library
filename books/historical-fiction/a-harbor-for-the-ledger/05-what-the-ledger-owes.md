## 5. What the Ledger Owes

Sleep did not come to Sable Nystrom that night, which was not unusual, and did not bring her any answers, which was.

She lay in the narrow bed she shared with Tam's fever and listened to the foghorn out on the point, moaning its one long note every ninety seconds like a clerk sighing over an endless column. Her father's letter sat in the flour tin behind the stove, where she had moved it three times before midnight as though the tin were safer than the shelf and the shelf safer than her own hand. Slanted letters. Seven years drowned, and the man still couldn't hold his pen level.

*Come before the ice closes.*

She had read it forty times. She had read it the way she read everything now—looking for the entry that didn't balance. A dead man asking his daughter to visit. A stranger who knew too much delivering it with hands that shook. An auditor writing her name in a notebook. Somewhere beneath all of it there was a sum that refused to come out even, and Sable had spent nine years at the Meridian Trading Company learning one iron lesson: when the column won't balance, you have made an assumption somewhere, and the assumption is always worse than the error.

Her assumption, she was beginning to suspect, had been *my father drowned.*

Tam coughed in his sleep, a wet sound like a boot pulled from mud, and turned his face to the wall. Twelve years old and his shoulders already sharp as coat hangers. She tucked the blanket higher and made herself a promise of the only kind she trusted: tomorrow, she would stop assuming.

Tomorrow, she would ask the harbormaster.

* * *

Oskar Wenlock had been Meridian's harbormaster for longer than Greywater had possessed paved streets, possibly longer than it possessed streets at all. He occupied a office above the chandlery that smelled of tar, cold coffee, and the particular staleness of men who win arguments by outliving their opponents. He was seventy if he was a day, gout-ridden, fond of saying *the harbor keeps its own books*, and he had signed Sable's indenture papers personally when she was eleven years old.

He looked up when she came in without knocking, and his face performed the small surprise of a man who has never once in his life been interrupted.

"Miss Nystrom." He set down his pen with ceremony. "You'll forgive me. I don't recall summoning you."

"I know," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

She stood in front of his desk with her hands folded, wearing her company face—the mild, apologetic one that got ledgers approved—and she watched Wenlock decide how much of his morning she was worth. She had learned to watch men do that arithmetic. It usually took them two seconds. Wenlock took four, which she chose to find flattering.

"Sit, then," he said, waving at the chair, since standing visitors demanded more energy than sitting ones. "If this is about the quarter figures, Brandt's people will—"

"It isn't about the figures." She sat. "It's about my father."

Something crossed the harbormaster's face. It was quick, and it was not grief, and Sable felt the floor of her whole life shift half an inch, the way a wharf shifts when a heavy crate is set down in the wrong place.

"Josiah," Wenlock said carefully. "That would be seven years back, now."

"Seven years last March. The *Gullwing* went down off the north shoal with all hands." She recited it the way everyone in Greywater recited it, the way she had recited it to Ilse Varric across the weighing-house counter two days ago with her heart hammering and her voice perfectly flat. "Except it seems he may not have gone down with it. And I'd like to know what the company knows, Mr. Wenlock, because the company appears to know things my family does not."

"And what makes you think—"

"I've kept Meridian's books for nine years, sir. I know what a company looks like when it's surprised, and I know what it looks like when it isn't." She leaned forward. "You weren't surprised just now. You were *careful*. There's a difference, and it takes about four seconds to see."

Wenlock regarded her for a long moment. Outside the window, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would ever get. Then he did something she had never seen him do: he opened the bottom drawer of his desk, took out a bottle, and poured two measures of whatever lived in it.

"It's not yet ten," Sable said.

"It's well past ten somewhere," said Wenlock, and pushed the glass across the desk. "Drink it. You'll want it."

Sable did not drink it. She held it, because holding something gave her hands instructions, and waited.

"You want to know why your indenture was collateral," Wenlock said.

The room went very still, the way rooms do when they are waiting to see whether you will make a scene. Sable discovered she could hear her own pulse in her ears, patient and stupid, keeping time.

"I never said that's why I wanted to know," she said.

"No. But it's the question underneath the question. You've been carrying it since you were eleven." He settled back, and his gout complained audibly, and he ignored it with the dignity of long practice. "I signed those papers, girl. Me, not some agent. Because your father owed this company four hundred and twelve pounds, eleven shillings, and I wanted somebody honest on the other end of the debt."

The number went in like a fishhook. Four hundred twelve pounds. Sable's mind, traitorous and efficient, was already converting it: her wages came to fourteen pounds a year after board. Thirty years. She did the division twice, hoping for a different answer both times, which was the definition of madness according to every bookkeeper who ever lived.

"The *Gullwing* didn't sink," she said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else reading aloud. "Did it."

"The *Gullwing* is at the bottom of a lagoon two hundred miles up the coast with a fresh coat of paint on her and a different name," Wenlock said. "Your father bought her debt-deep, loaded her with goods that belonged to Meridian, and vanished north one night in March with the tide. There was no storm. There was no wreck. There was a man who ran, and a company left holding paper, and two children." He paused. "The company kept the children."

"Kept." The word tasted like pennies.

"That's the word we used." Wenlock had the grace, at least, to look at his desk rather than at her. "The directors converted the debt against your mother's estate. When she passed, it converted against the children. Tam's indenture matures when he's twenty-one. Yours—" he made a small gesture, a ledger-flavored apology— "yours was written to run until the principal is recovered or you reach majority of service. Nine years at your rate. You're better than halfway through, if that's a comfort."

It was not a comfort. Nothing in the arithmetic was a comfort. Sable sat very straight in the chair and thought, absurdly, of the shipment of pearls she had filed underweight three weeks ago, the discrepancy she had buried in a rounding figure because burying things was what she did, because staying invisible was her creed, because history happened to other people.

History had not happened to other people. History had happened to her while she balanced someone else's columns, and no one had told her because the telling would have cost the company nothing and benefited it less. She had spent nine years being grateful—grateful to Meridian for the roof, for Tam's schooling, for the very contract that was the price tag on her father's running away.

"Everyone in town thinks he drowned," she said. "There's a plaque. In the chapel. His name is on the wall with the other lost men."

"The plaque was a director's idea. Sentimental, cheap, and effective. Grief keeps a workforce quiet, Miss Nystrom. Resentment organizes them." Wenlock finally picked up his own glass and drank, wincing. "You think I'm a hard man. I'm the honest kind of hard. I keep accounts. Your father wrote fiction, and the directors published it."

"Why are you telling me?" It burst out of her louder than she intended. "Nine years, and today you decide honesty is convenient? What changed?"

Wenlock looked at her then, fully, and for a moment the gout and the years fell off him and she saw the man who had once run a harbor through two wars and an epidemic.

"Auditor Brandt happened," he said. "He pulled the *Gullwing*'s insurance record yesterday. Which means within a fortnight he'll pull the debt conversion, and he'll find your name on it, and he'll come to you smiling the way he does, and he'll offer you something that sounds like freedom and costs you everything." He refilled his glass, though hers sat untouched. "I'm telling you because you're the best clerk this company ever had and the only person in Greywater who checks my figures without flinching, and because somebody ought to tell you before he does. That's all. Don't mistake it for kindness. At my age, kindness is mostly just impatience with lies."

Sable rose. Her legs worked, which seemed overly generous of them under the circumstances. She set the untouched glass back on his desk with a click that sounded, to her own ears, exactly like a column refusing to balance.

"One more thing," she said. "The strongbox. If he took company goods—did he take the strongbox from the north station?"

Whatever was left of Oskar Wenlock's composure left with that question. He stared at her, and this time the surprise was genuine, unperformed, and terrible.

"What strongbox?" he said.

And because Sable Nystrom had kept honest books for liars for nine years, she knew a true answer when she heard one, and it frightened her far more than any lie would have.

* * *

She walked home along the seawall without remembering any of it. The fog had thinned to a gray gauze; the harbor lay flat and dull as pewter, and the divers' boats knocked together at their moorings with a sound like slow applause. She had walked this seawall a thousand times. She had walked it as a child hunting sea-glass with Tam on her shoulders, and as a widow's-orphan clerk with her collar buttoned to the chin, and she had never once—not once—thought of it as a road built on top of a debt.

Four hundred twelve pounds, eleven shillings.

Her father had taught her figures on this seawall. That was the thing that kept catching, the splinter in the mind: she could not separate the man from the arithmetic. He had shown her how to carry a remainder when she was six, drawing numbers in barnacle-scars with a bit of kelp stem, laughing his big easy laugh, saying *numbers don't lie, Sable-bird, that's what I love about them, give me an honest column over an honest man any day.*

She had thought it was whimsy. She understood now that it had been a confession, delivered early and in code, to an audience too young to read it.

Numbers don't lie. Men do. And she had inherited the numbers.

At the mouth of Lantern Row she stopped, because Mrs. Pellaby was scrubbing her step—as usual, at an hour designed to intercept gossip—and called out the customary greeting, and Sable heard herself answer it in the customary words, and realized midway through the exchange that her whole life had been conducted in customary words laid carefully over a ledger nobody had let her see. *Fine morning.* Fine. As though fine were a currency and she was rich in it. Nine years of fine mornings, purchased on credit, billed to an eleven-year-old girl.

She climbed the stairs to their rooms quietly, out of habit, though Tam was at the doctor's and the rooms were empty, and she stood in the middle of the small square parlor where everything smelled of coal oil and camphor and looked at her life the way you look at a page you've misread for years.

The stove her father had built. The shelf he'd cut crooked on purpose, so it would look handmade, so the inspectors wouldn't requisition it. The little brass diver's charm nailed over the door for luck—he'd laughed when he hammered it in, said luck was just odds you hadn't counted yet.

Every object in the room had been paid for by her servitude, and she had dusted each one lovingly, gratefully, for nine years.

Sable sat down at the table where she did the household accounts, took out the household ledger—a real one, hers, the only book in Greywater that had never lied to anyone—and turned to a blank page. This was what she did when the world broke. She wrote. She could not burn anything down or sail anywhere; she could write, and writing imposed a shape on ruin, and shape was nearly as good as sense.

At the top of the page, in her smallest, firmest hand, she wrote:

*Debts: Josiah Nystrom, 412£ 11s, payable in daughters.*

Then she sat with it. The foghorn sounded out on the point, ninety seconds between notes, faithful as ever. Downstairs, the chandlery bell rang for the midday tide. Somewhere north, past the shoals and the fishing camps and the closing ice, a man who drew numbers in barnacles was waiting for his daughter to come rescue him, and did not know—or worse, did know and had calculated—that the daughter had been the payment all along.

She should hate him. She reached for hatred the way you reach for a familiar tool, and found her hand closing on something else: the memory of his laugh, warm and low, teaching her remainders. That was the betrayal inside the betrayal. Not that he had run. That he had run and left her still loving him, still, infuriatingly, after everything, the way you love a bad column because you spent your childhood learning it.

A knock at the street door. Two knocks, precise, evenly spaced—with unsettling precision, some treacherous part of her noted, the way everything about that woman was precise.

Sable closed the ledger, put her hand flat on its cover, and breathed.

Ilse Varric stood on the landing with her thin shoulders up against the damp and a paper sack of oranges under her arm, as though visiting the daughter of the man whose letters she carried were the most ordinary errand in Greywater. Up close, in daylight, Sable could see what she had refused to see at the weighing house: the gray under the woman's skin, the careful way she held her ribs, breath budgeted like coins.

"They told me at the chandlery you'd gone to see Wenlock," Ilse said. Her eyes went to Sable's face and stopped there, reading. "Ah. So now you know."

"Do I?" Sable's voice came out cracked. "Because I've just spent the morning learning that my whole life is an invoice, and the man who wrote it is alive, and the only person who can explain any of it is you." She stepped back from the doorway, not quite an invitation, not quite anything else. "So no. I don't know. Come in and start balancing."
