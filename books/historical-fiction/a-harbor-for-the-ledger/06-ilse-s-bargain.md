## 6. Ilse's Bargain

Ilse Varric had delivered a great many things in her life. Cod to the salt houses at Skarnvik. A rifle wrapped in oilcloth through three days of sleet. Once, a live goose, which had complained the entire way and bitten her twice, and which she had come to regard, by journey's end, as the most honest traveling companion she'd ever had.

But she had never delivered anything like this: seven letters tied with waxed string, written in the slanted hand of a man who was supposed to be dead, addressed to a daughter who wanted nothing so much as for him to stay that way.

She sat in her rented room above the chandlery with the window cracked to the fog, because the fog was the only thing in Greywater that didn't stare at her. The town had been staring since she arrived. She understood why. She was tall where the harbor women were compact, blunt where they were quick, and she carried herself like someone who had walked out of a country that no longer appeared on maps. Which, in fairness, she had.

The cough came up from the bottom of her chest, the deep one, the one that meant business. She pressed her forearm against her mouth until it passed, then examined the cloth of her sleeve with the detachment of long practice. Nothing new. Good. The lung-rot advanced in seasons, not days, and she had made her peace with the arithmetic of it somewhere on the ice road, in a way that no longer frightened her but did occasionally annoy her. There was a difference, she had found, between being ready to die and being ready to be *inconvenienced* indefinitely.

She had promised Nystrom she would deliver the letters. She had promised him nothing else. He had asked her to say he was sorry, and she had told him what she always told him: that sorry was a coin he kept trying to spend from an account that had been empty for years, and that his daughter would see through it, because daughters always do.

He had wept, a little. Men like Nystrom wept the way ice melted—slowly, reluctantly, and only at the edges.

*Deliver the letters,* he'd said. *That's all. Don't plead my case. I haven't got one.*

For once in his life, the man had been honest.

* * *

The weighing house smelled of brine and ink and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by wet hands for forty years. Ilse liked it immediately. It reminded her of the factor's office at Skarnvik, except here the woman behind the counter looked up when the door opened, which the factor never had.

Sable Nystrom had taken the letter yesterday. Ilse had watched her face do something complicated—the way water does when you drop a stone into still water, rings going out and out and no way to call them back—and then Sable had folded herself shut again like a ledger closing, and said, "Thank you," in a voice flat as slate, and gone back to her columns as though the world had not just tilted.

Ilse respected that. She also recognized it, because it was exactly what she would have done.

She had spent the day telling herself she would leave Greywater on the morning boat north. There was no reason to stay. The errand was done. Her lungs hated the sea damp. Every sensible argument pointed the same direction, and Ilse had survived this long by listening to sensible arguments.

And yet here she was, at the end of the day, standing in the doorway of the weighing house with a loaf of dark bread under her arm and no defensible reason for it whatsoever.

"Miss Varric." Sable didn't look up from her figures. "We're closed."

"I know. I brought bread."

"We have bread."

"Not like this. This is rye, real rye, from the baker who used to work the northern route. Everything else in this town is white and soft and tastes of apology." Ilse set it on the counter anyway. "Also, I wanted to see if you'd burned it."

"Burned—"

"The letter. Half the people who get letters like that burn them unread. The other half read them and wish they hadn't. I've been taking odds all day."

Something flickered at the corner of Sable's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might eventually decide to live. "I read it."

"And?"

"And nothing. It changes nothing. A man writes 'come before the ice closes' after seven years of silence and expects—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Why are you here, Miss Varric? Truly. You've made your delivery. People don't linger in Greywater unless they owe it money or it owes them grief."

"Both, possibly," Ilse said. "But mostly I'm here because I promised myself I'd tell you the rest of it, and then I promised myself I wouldn't, and I've discovered I'm a person who keeps promises to other people more reliably than to myself. So. May I sit? My lungs and standing have reached an understanding that does not favor standing."

There was a stool behind the counter. Sable fetched it without being asked, which Ilse chose to interpret as hospitality rather than pity, because a woman could only absorb so much pity before breakfast went down sideways.

* * *

"You were with him," Sable said. It wasn't a question. She had done the arithmetic already; Ilse could see it happening behind her eyes, the dates lining up, the geography. "In the camps. In the north."

"Six years. Off and on. More on than off, the last four." Ilse tore the bread, handed half across the counter unasked, and ate her portion in the manner of someone for whom meals had often been uncertain. "I keep books for the camp at Hvalvik. Or I did. Same trade as yours, smaller numbers, more fish. Your father ran the net crews. He was good at it. He's good at any work where nobody asks him questions about the past."

"He told you about us?"

"He talked about you constantly and told me almost nothing, if that makes sense. Tam first. Tam's hands, Tam's cleverness, the model boats he used to build. Then you, last, always last, always longest." She chewed, swallowed. "He described your handwriting once. Said you write numbers the way other people pray."

Sable looked down at her own hand on the counter as though it had betrayed her.

"He said you'd hate him," Ilse went on. "He said it the way men say things they hope aren't true. 'She'll hate me.' And I said, well, yes, probably, that's what happens when you let people bury you. And he said, 'Better hate than shame,' and I said, 'That's a coward's proverb,' and he threw a boot at me. We got along fine."

The ghost of the smile again, fighting its way toward the surface. "You lived with him."

"Lived near him. Worked beside him. Shared a stove in winter because wood costs more than dignity up there and we'd both already sold ours." Ilse set down the bread. Here it was, then. The part she'd promised herself not to say, arriving in her mouth anyway, the way truths do when you've carried them too far. "I'll tell you plainly, because plainly is the only currency I have left. I'm dying. The lung-rot. It's in the camps—it's in everyone who worked the camps—and mine has moved past the point where the word 'maybe' applies. The company doctor in Skarnvik gave me a year. That was eight months ago, and doctors flatter themselves."

Sable had gone very still. It was a stillness Ilse knew intimately: the stillness of a person reorganizing their entire understanding of a conversation mid-conversation.

"That's why you came," Sable said slowly. "Not for him. Not even for the errand. You came because—"

"Because I wanted to see the daughter whose handwriting he described like scripture. Because I wanted to stand in the town he talked about with such loathing and such longing that I sometimes couldn't tell them apart. And because," and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, roughened slightly, "because a person delivering her own last letters might as well deliver someone else's while she's at it. It seemed efficient."

The fog pressed against the window. Somewhere outside, a gull made its argument to no one.

"You ask for nothing," Sable said. "That's what you said at the counter, the first day. You ask for nothing."

"I said that, yes."

"It wasn't true."

"No," Ilse admitted. "It wasn't. But it was close, and I'm fond of it, so I keep saying it." She turned her half of the bread over in her fingers. "Here is what I actually want, and you may throw me out for it. I want you to know that everything I've said to you is true except the parts I've left out, and the parts I've left out are about him, not about you. I want you to understand that I did not come here to be loved, or pitied, or forgiven on his behalf. I came because letters undelivered are a weight, and I have carried enough weight. And I want—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I want, before I go, to have known one person in this world who isn't connected to me by debt or duty or the rot. Just... known. However briefly. Whatever that turns out to be."

Silence. The kind of silence that has machinery in it.

Sable picked up her pen, set it down again, and looked at Ilse with an expression that was doing several jobs at once and doing none of them well.

"You realize," Sable said finally, "that I have known you for two days, and in those two days you have handed me a letter from a dead man, admitted you're dying, and asked for friendship. Most people spread that sort of thing over decades."

"I'm efficient."

"You're *terrifying.*"

"My mother said the same. She also said I'd die alone, so we'll see who wins that one." But the joke landed soft, and Ilse heard what was underneath it, which was: *stay.*

Sable blew out a breath and rubbed the bridge of her nose, the gesture of a woman whose ledgers had just developed a column she had no heading for. "There's an auditor coming," she said abruptly. "Company man. Brandt. He arrives within the week, and there's a discrepancy in the pearl shipments that runs straight through my desk, and my father's name is underneath all of it like a keel. If he finds what I think he'll find, it won't matter that I've kept my head down for seven years. My indenture, my brother, everything—we're collateral, Miss Varric. We always were. I learned that yesterday, from the harbormaster, and I have not yet decided how to feel about it, because feeling it would require stopping."

Ilse listened without moving. So that was the shape of it. Nystrom hadn't told her the half of it either—or perhaps he had, and she'd chosen not to hear, which amounted to the same thing.

"Then you need someone in your corner who has nothing left to lose," Ilse said. "Those are historically the best corners to have occupied. I speak from experience."

"Why?" Sable asked, and it was the sharpest question yet, aimed true. "Why my corner? You owe me nothing. You owe *him* something, clearly, but I'm not him. What do you get?"

Ilse considered lying, found she didn't have the energy for it, and told the truth instead, which was becoming a habit around this woman and one she couldn't currently afford.

"I get to be useful once more before the end," she said. "Useful matters more than comfortable, at this stage. Comfortable is just waiting with better furniture." She stood, took her stool back to its corner, and gathered her coat. At the door she paused. "The bread's for Tam. Rye's better for the chest than that white stuff. Steep it in hot milk if the coughing's bad at night—that was your father's remedy, and for once in his life he was right about something."

Sable stared at her. "How did you know Tam's coughing worse at night?"

"Because he's your brother," Ilse said, "and you're his sister, and I've been watching you carry him around in your face since the moment I walked in." She pulled her collar up against the fog. "Goodnight, Miss Nystrom. I'll be at the chandlery. I'm not leaving on the morning boat after all."

She was halfway down the steps when Sable's voice caught her, quiet and unwilling and absolutely certain:

"The ice closes in six weeks. If you mean to go north again—you'll want company on the road."

Ilse smiled into the fog, where no one could see it and make anything of it, and said, "Yes. I rather thought you might."
