## 7. The Berth on the Last Ship

The notice went up on the harbormaster's door on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day Sable did the coal accounts and coal accounts always put her in a mood to be disappointed by official documents.

She read it twice, standing in the thin rain while the harbor fog chewed on the masts behind her.

*NOTICE TO THE TOWN OF GREYWATER. The supply vessel* Meridian Star *will depart southward before ice. ONE (1) passenger berth is available to residents of Greywater, at the discretion of the Company. Applications to be submitted in writing to Harbormaster Quill.*

One berth. One. In a town of four hundred souls, half of whom had been talking about leaving since the day they were born and none of whom could afford it.

Sable stood there long enough that Old Piet, hauling his nets past, said, "You planning to marry it, girl?" and she said something civil without knowing what, and walked back to the weighing house with the notice printed on the inside of her eyelids.

One berth.

Her father was three hundred miles north, in the fishing camps, hiding behind seven years of his own death. Brandt was here, in Greywater, pulling threads with those pale unhurried fingers of his. And between those two facts sat a ship that sailed once a year, whether the world was ready or not.

* * *

By evening the whole town had an opinion, which was the natural state of Greywater on any subject larger than the weather and most subjects smaller.

"The berth'll go to the Vestergaard family," said Mrs. Lund at the chandlery, weighing out lamp oil with the gravity of a woman distributing prophecy. "Old man Vestergaard holds company shares. Well — *held*. Same thing."

"The berth will go to whoever bribes Quill," said Piet. "Quill would sell his own teeth if he thought there was a second set coming."

"The berth will go to nobody," said Ilse, from her corner by the stove, where she had taken up a semi-permanent residence at the chandlery like a cat who had decided the premises were now hers. "It'll rot empty while everyone argues about it."

Sable, counting stock at the counter, said nothing. She had gotten very good over twenty-three years at saying nothing in rooms where opinions flew. But Ilse's eyes came around to her anyway, sharp as a compass needle finding north, and stayed there until Sable had to look down at the ledger and pretend the tallow candles required her complete mathematical devotion.

"You've done sums already," Ilse said, when the others had gone and the fire had been banked low. It wasn't a question.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You've been doing sums since the notice went up. I can hear the abacus in your breathing."

Sable closed the ledger. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a debt collector. "There's one berth," she said, finally, because Ilse was going to sit there being right at her all night otherwise. "My father needs it more than anyone in this town. If he comes south before Brandt—"

"Brandt isn't looking for your father."

"Brandt is looking for *everything*, Ilse. He's an auditor. Looking for things is the entire profession." Sable heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, fold her hands on the ledger, be for a moment the clerk again, the girl whose ledgers balanced. "If my father sets foot in Greywater while Brandt is auditing Meridian's books, and anyone connects him to the missing shipments — and someone will, because he left debts like a snail leaves slime — then he doesn't sail anywhere except possibly the magistrate's docket. The berth isn't just passage. It's the only door out of this harbor before the ice shuts it for eight months."

Ilse turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "And how does your father learn about the berth? He can't apply in writing. He's dead, as far as the paperwork is concerned. Dead men write very poor applications."

"I'm going north anyway," Sable said. "We agreed. I tell him. We come back together. He takes the berth."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"Does the berth carry your father south while you stand on the dock watching it go?"

Sable had, in fact, done this sum too. It was the sum she kept doing and un-doing, the one that never balanced no matter how she shifted the numbers. "I have an indenture," she said. "I can't leave even if I wanted to. My contract belongs to Meridian until—" She stopped.

"Until Brandt decides it doesn't," Ilse finished quietly.

They looked at each other across the dying stove, and Sable understood that they were both thinking of the same thing: that everything now ran through Auditor Brandt like a river through a gorge, and neither of them knew yet which way he'd turn.

"Well then," Sable said briskly, because briskness was cheaper than honesty, "it's settled. Father gets the berth. I get a clear conscience and eight months of winter. Everyone weeps appropriately."

Ilse smiled — that crooked, private smile that suggested she found Sable's briskness both transparent and oddly dear. "Everyone weeps appropriately," she agreed. "I'll bring a handkerchief."

* * *

The trouble with hope, Sable discovered over the following week, was that it behaved exactly like an unpaid debt. It accrued interest. It woke her at three in the morning. It grew heavier the longer she carried it, until she was hauling it up the harbor steps every day like a sack of wet rope.

She began, without quite admitting to herself that she was doing it, to build the case for the berth the way she built any ledger entry: methodically, line by line, in a little notebook she kept in her apron pocket.

*Item:* The berth goes "at the discretion of the Company." Discretion was a currency, and Sable had spent nine years learning exactly what the Company valued. It valued quiet. It valued paperwork in perfect order. It valued, above all, not being embarrassed in front of shareholders.

*Item:* Harbormaster Quill processed applications. Quill drank. Quill owed money to the chandlery, which meant Quill owed money, indirectly, to Sable, who had recently begun keeping the chandlery's books on the two evenings Ilse didn't need her.

*Item:* Nobody else in Greywater wanted the berth as badly as a woman with a living father and an auditor circling the docks. This last item she wrote and then underlined, and then crossed out, and then underlined again, because wanting badly had never once in her life been sufficient grounds for anything.

She caught herself doing absurd things. She re-read the notice so many times she could recite it in her sleep, including the printer's error ("applications to be submitten," which she found obscurely comforting, as though a town careless enough to let a typo onto an official notice might also be careless enough to lose track of a berth). She calculated the *Meridian Star*'s likely tonnage from her memory of the manifest forms, worked out the crew complement, estimated provisions, and arrived at the conclusion that yes, technically, one could fit two people in a single berth if neither of them minded intimacy of a nautical extremity, and then she went red to the ears at her own thoughts and shut the notebook very firmly.

Ilse noticed everything, of course. That was the thing about Ilse. You could hide a letter from her, maybe, if you buried it deep enough — but not a hope. Hopes radiated off a person like heat off a stove.

"You're doing it again," Ilse observed one evening, as they walked back along the seawall. The tide was out and the mudflats breathed their cold salt breath around them.

"Doing what?"

"Building it. Your case. I've seen clerks do it with shipments and lawyers do it with men's lives. You're doing it with a boat ticket." Ilse walked a few steps, boots crunching on frozen shell. "What happens to a person, Sable, when she wants a thing that badly and doesn't get it?"

"She survives. She usually does."

"That's not surviving. That's just continuing." Ilse said it mildly, almost cheerfully, the way she said most devastating things. "There's a difference. I've continued through a great deal. Surviving is rarer."

Sable kicked a stone off the seawall and watched it vanish into the dark water. "You sound like someone who's given this lecture before."

"To myself. Frequently. With limited success." Ilse coughed then — the small dry catch that had become, in the weeks since her arrival, almost familiar, and which Sable had trained herself not to flinch at. "So. When do we leave for the north?"

"Soon. Before the weather turns. Two weeks, perhaps less."

"And the berth application?"

"Submitted." Sable pulled the notebook from her apron and held it up like evidence at a trial. "Three pages. Addressed to Quill. I cited my nine years of service, my brother's medical necessity, and — this is the part I'm proudest of — the Company's own charter, article eleven, regarding 'the welfare of persons in its employ.' I quoted it verbatim. With page number."

Ilse took the notebook, read the relevant page by feel in the dark, which shouldn't have been possible but was somehow very Ilse, and laughed — a real laugh, low and warm, the first one Sable had heard from her. "You wrote them a love letter in accounting."

"I wrote them *the only language they respect.*"

"No." Ilse handed it back. Their fingers touched briefly on the transfer, and neither of them hurried the moment along. "You wrote them the only language you had. Those aren't the same thing, whatever the Company thinks."

They walked on. Somewhere out in the flats a bird cried once and gave up.

"It won't work, will it," Sable said. Not really a question either.

"It might. You're frighteningly good at paperwork." A pause. "But if it doesn't—"

"Then I go north, I fetch him, and I find another way. There's always another way. There has to be." She heard how it sounded — young, stubborn, slightly desperate — and didn't care. Hope had sunk its hooks in properly now. She could feel it working deeper every day: in the way she checked the tide tables for the *Star*'s arrival window, in the way she'd started mentally drafting letters to her father in a hand that wasn't hers, in the way the phrase *one berth* had taken up permanent residence in her skull like a tenant who paid no rent and broke nothing but her sleep.

At the corner where their ways divided — Ilse to the chandlery, Sable home to Tam — Ilse stopped.

"One thing," she said. "When you make your pitch to Quill, and to the Company, and to God and the weather besides — remember that you told me the plan was your father's berth. Yours wasn't in the arithmetic at all. I want it written down somewhere, Miss Nystrom, that you chose that. So that later, when you're tempted to rewrite the entry, somebody remembers the original figures."

Sable stared at her. "You think I'll rewrite it."

"I think," Ilse said gently, "that you're a bookkeeper. And bookkeepers, when the column doesn't balance, are always tempted to move a number." She touched Sable's sleeve, once, lightly, and was gone into the fog.

* * *

That night Sable climbed the stairs to the room she shared with Tam and found him sitting up in bed, in the dark, breathing in that careful shallow way he'd developed — the way of a boy rationing his own lungs.

"You're late," he whispered. Talking cost him; she could hear it.

"Auditing the chandlery." She lit no lamp — light made him cough — and sat on the edge of the bed, feeling the heat come off him. Still feverish. Always feverish now. "You should be asleep."

"Couldn't." A pause, punctuated by the small wet sound of his chest doing its bad work. "Sable. The notice. On Quill's door. The berth."

Her stomach dropped, slow and cold, like an anchor paying out line. "What about it?"

"If it's real. If it actually sails south." His eyes found hers in the dark, burning bright with fever and something worse — hope, the same disease she'd caught, in a body far less able to survive the treatment. "Take it."

"It's not mine to take, Tam. It's—"

"Take it." He gripped her wrist. His hands, which used to mend nets and out-arm-wrestle boys twice his size, closed around her wrist like a child's. "You hate it here. You've always hated it here, you just file the feeling away with everything else. Go south where it's warm. Where you can breathe. Get away from the ledgers and the fog and—"

"Tam." Her voice cracked on his name, which infuriated her. "Stop. Stop it. You're not well, and you're talking nonsense, and I am not going to discuss this while you're burning up at—"

She reached for his forehead to check the heat and he flinched away from her hand, and in the flinch she felt something shift in the room, some load-bearing wall between them developing a crack.

"You'd give it to Da," Tam said. Flat. Fever-bright eyes boring into her. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? I've seen you walking around with your little notebook. You're going to spend our one door out of this town on the man who let us bury an empty box."

The words landed harder because they were, in the exact terms of her own secret notebook, true.

"He had reasons," she said, and heard immediately how hollow it rang — a clerk defending an entry she hadn't yet audited.

"Sure. Reasons." Tam lay back, carefully, the way the sick lie down, conserving even their bitterness. "Seven years of them. Go to bed, Sable."

She sat a moment longer in the dark, listening to her brother breathe — each breath a small negotiation, a ledger entry in red ink — and understood with terrible clarity that the arithmetic had just changed again. That the notebook in her apron was already obsolete. That the berth was no longer a question of her father, or of herself, or even of what was owed.

Because only a warmer climate would save him. The doctor had said it that afternoon, casually, over his spectacles, the way men deliver sentences.

She rose, and tucked the blanket around Tam's shoulders, and stood a long time in the dark watching him sleep, while downstairs in her chest the abacus started clicking all over again — and this time, no matter how she moved the beads, the column would not, would not balance.
