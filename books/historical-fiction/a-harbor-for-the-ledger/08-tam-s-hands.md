## 8. Tam's Hands

The cough came in threes now.

That was how Sable had learned to measure it—not by doctors' Latin or the color of things in handkerchiefs she refused to look at too closely, but by counting. In the spring it had come in ones, scattered and dry as gull-cry. By midsummer, twos. Now, in the first hard week of autumn, when the fog came off the harbor thick enough to chew, Tam coughed three times, paused to gather himself like a swimmer treading water, and then coughed three more.

She kept the count in the back of her ledger book, in pencil, in the margin where no auditor would ever think to look. It was the only column she'd ever kept that wasn't about money, and it was the only one that mattered.

"Four," Tam announced from his cot by the stove, with the grim satisfaction of a man reporting enemy movements. "Beat my record."

"You're supposed to be resting, not competing."

"I'm resting *and* competing. Multitalented." He pulled the blanket up to his chin. At nineteen he was all elbows and collarbone, a scaffolding of a boy with greywater-pale skin and their father's dark hair, which had gone lank and dull the way everything did in Greywater eventually—the paint on the boats, the brass on the weighing house counter, the people. "What's for supper?"

"What we always have."

"Fish?"

"There's a reason they call it Greywater and not Beefshire."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, four this time, and Sable turned back to the stove so he wouldn't see her face do what her face was doing.

* * *

Dr. Ellerby came on Thursday, which was unusual, because Dr. Ellerby came when the Meridian Company paid him to come, and the Meridian Company had never once paid him to look at Tam Nystrom. Indentured clerks' brothers were not company property. Sable had learned that the way she'd learned most things—by asking once, politely, and filing the refusal without comment.

So when his knock came at the door, her first thought was not *he's here for Tam*. Her first thought was *Brandt*.

But it was only Ellerby, hatless in the drizzle, his bag under his arm, looking older than he had in spring. He was a soft-spoken man with the permanent apologetic expression of someone who delivered bad news for a living and had long ago stopped apologizing for the apology.

"Miss Nystrom. I'll be quick about it, because I suspect you're the sort who prefers quick."

"That depends on the news."

"It usually does." He set his bag down inside the door without being invited, which was its own kind of message. "Your brother. May I?"

Tam had gone very still on his cot, the way young men went still when they understood that adults were about to discuss them in the third person. He'd been doing a great deal of stillness lately. It didn't suit him; Tam had been built for motion, for hauling nets and racing Ilse Varric along the seawall and losing cheerfully.

Ellerby listened to his chest for a long time. Longer than listening took. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at Sable, and she saw him deciding how much truth to spend, and she hated him a little for hesitating, because hesitation was expensive and she couldn't afford it.

"The lung's compromised," he said. "You knew that."

"Yes."

"And you know it's the camps' complaint. The rot. Half the northern fishermen have it by forty." He rubbed his jaw. "It's the damp, Miss Nystrom. Seven years of this harbor, breathing fog and coal smoke and wet wool. His lungs are full of it. There are things I can give him to ease the nights, and I will, gladly, and they will ease nothing that matters. What he needs—" He stopped. Started again. She watched a careful man choose carelessness out of respect. "What he needs is sun. Dry air. Somewhere south where winter is a rumor instead of a season."

Sable stood very still. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere outside a gull said something ugly to another gull.

"How long," she said, "if he stays here."

Ellerby looked at his hands. "Two winters ago, I'd have said years. This past spring I'd have said one. Now?" He shook his head. "I've seen it move fast when it decides to. I've seen a man go from coughing in threes to—" He caught himself, hearing what he'd almost named, and Sable felt the floor tilt under her in the old familiar way, the way it had tilted under a folded letter on a weighing house counter.

"In threes," she repeated quietly.

"Pardon?"

"Nothing. Go on."

"He needs the south before the ice closes. That's not a physician's flourish—it's arithmetic. The supply ship sails south within the month. After it goes, nothing leaves this harbor until thaw, and thaw comes late." He stood, gathering his bag, and at the door he paused. "There's talk the ship has one berth open for residents. You'd know more than I would, working where you work."

"Yes," Sable said. "I would."

"One berth," said Ellerby, not unkindly, and left.

* * *

Tam pretended to be asleep while Ellerby's footsteps faded down the lane. He was bad at it. He breathed like a man performing breathing.

"I heard," he said finally, eyes shut.

"You were meant to. He spoke loudly enough for the whole street."

"Sable." He opened his eyes then, and they were their father's eyes, which she had spent seven years not thinking about and could now think of nothing else. "The berth. On the ship."

"Don't."

"I'm just saying the words."

"Well, un-say them."

He struggled upright against the wall, and it cost him something—she saw it cost him something—and looked at her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the Nystrom stubbornness, the family inheritance that couldn't be repossessed. "You have one berth. I heard the harbormaster's boy shouting it across three streets. One berth, going to whoever's got money or friends, and you've got neither, but you've got *something*, because you've always got something, you terrifying little bookkeeper. So you get it. And you put me on that boat."

"No."

"No?"

"No," Sable said, and began scrubbing a pot that did not need scrubbing. "The berth is spoken for."

"Spoken for by whom?"

By our father, she thought. By a dead man who writes letters in slanted ink from the north and asks me to come before the ice closes, except I'm not coming for him—I'm coming to put *him* on that ship, because Brandt is pulling threads and every thread ends at a Nystrom, and if the auditor finds Elias Nystrom alive and hiding in the fishing camps with seven years of forged documents behind him—

"The berth is spoken for," she said aloud, which was technically true, in the way that most of her sentences were technically true, which was the whole art of bookkeeping.

"By Dad." Tam said it flatly. Not *father*, not *our father*—Dad, the word he'd used at nine years old, kept in a jar all these years, taken out and thrown like a stone. "Ilse told me."

Sable put down the pot. "When."

"When you were at the weighing house Tuesday. She doesn't lie to me, Sable. It's one of her best qualities. You should try it." He coughed—three—and pressed on before she could count them. "He's alive. He's been alive. For seven years he's been alive, and I've been—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it, fourteen years old again and furious. "I lit a candle for him every year. Every year, at the drowning memorial. Did you know that? Four candles. Mother let me. And he was up north eating fish."

"Tam—"

"He can have the berth," Tam said, with a bitterness that was worse than any cough. "I don't want it. Let him sail south and be warm and far away, that's the best use of it I can think of."

And then he did sleep, or something near it, and Sable sat by the stove with her hands flat on her knees and discovered what the arithmetic actually was.

* * *

Here is what she wrote in her head, because she could not stop writing it, because she was her father's daughter and her mother's daughter and the Meridian Company's creature, and numbers were the only language she had ever trusted:

One berth.
Three claimants.

Her brother, whose lungs were filling like a tide pool at slack water, who needed the south the way a drowning man needs a boat, whom Dr. Ellerby—a careful man who spent truth like coin—had measured in seasons.

Her father, who needed it less medically and more existentially, because Auditor Brandt was in town with clean cuffs and infinite patience, and every day Brandt stayed, the net drew tighter around the name Nystrom. If her father stayed north, Brandt would find him. If Brandt found him, it wouldn't be a cell—companies didn't bother with cells—it would be debt collectors and seized boats and a trial in a southern court, and the name Nystrom would be dragged through ledgers in three provinces.

And herself, though she refused to write herself in the column, because she had spent seven years practicing not appearing in columns. Though there was Ilse, dying by inches of the same lung-rot, who asked for nothing, who had walked out of the north carrying letters like coals in her bare hands, who slept in the loft above the weighing house and coughed in twos and never once mentioned it.

Three claimants. One berth. And Sable Nystrom holding the pen.

She caught herself doing it at the weighing house on Friday—doing the arithmetic in broad daylight, over other people's fish. A diver brought in a basket of pearls and Sable weighed them and thought, *the fare south is eleven crowns and the berth is free but the bribes won't be.* A fisherman argued about short-weight and Sable corrected the tally in his favor and thought, *if Father takes the berth, Tam has one winter, maybe two.* Mr. Pellow from the company office leaned over the counter to complain about the new audit slowing everything down, and Sable smiled her invisible smile and thought, *if Tam takes the berth, Father hangs.*

She caught herself, and was disgusted, and did it again five minutes later. That was the thing nobody told you about love and arithmetic: they used the same part of the mind. You could not stop the sums. You could only hate yourself honestly for running them.

*Give it to Tam* was the answer, obviously. Any stranger off the street would say it. A child would say it. The sick boy gets the boat; the grown man made his bed in the fishing camps seven years ago and can lie in it. It was so obviously the answer that Sable distrusted it immediately, because obvious answers were how companies got you—they offered you the obvious answer with one hand while the fine print took your other arm. Give it to Tam, and Brandt finds her father, and Brandt finds her father means Brandt finds *her*, and an indentured clerk who burned around a discrepancy, whose name was on documents she shouldn't have touched, whose brother was safely out of reach—

No. That wasn't it either. She was doing it again, dressing cowardice in strategy. She was good at that. She'd had practice.

The truth, which she examined late Friday night by stove-light while both her patients slept—Tam below, coughing in fours; Ilse above, coughing in twos, and Sable lying awake between them keeping count of the whole house like some mad little clerk of breaths—the truth was simpler and uglier:

She wanted to send Tam because Tam deserved it.

And she wanted to send her father because sending her father would end it. Would settle the account. Would close the ledger that had been open in her chest for seven years, the one with no honest total. If he sailed away warm and safe, she would never have to decide what she felt about him. She could file the question. Unresolved. Carried forward. She was very good at carried-forward.

And that, she thought, staring at the ceiling, was the most damning entry of all. She could forgive herself nearly anything except the suspicion that some small, cold, bookkeeping corner of her wanted her father *gone* not because it saved him but because it spared her—spared her the confrontation, the fury, the terrible conversation waiting at the end of the northern road like weather.

Tam got the candle. Tam got the grief. Tam should get the berth, and she should go north alone into whatever waited there, and Ilse—

Ilse, who asked for nothing. Ilse, who had become the sound the house made when it wasn't coughing: the scrape of her chair, the low humming of some northern song with only three notes, the way she said "*gut*" instead of *good* and had taught Tam to say it too, and now Tam said it, and Sable caught herself saying it. Ilse, who was dying of the same air Tam was dying of, who would not fit anywhere in the equation because there was no room for her, and Sable had caught herself—God help her—feeling *relief* about that, feeling relief that the woman she— 

She didn't finish the thought. Some columns she was not ready to total.

* * *

Saturday morning the fog lifted for the first time in a week, and Greywater emerged blinking like something hauled up from a deep bed, dripping and surprised. Tam insisted on walking to the seawall, and Sable let him, because Ellerby had said movement was good and because she was learning to let Tam have the small victories before the large defeat arrived.

They stood at the rail. The harbor lay flat and pewter-colored. Out at the anchorage, the supply ship—the *Corvus*, fat and patient as a sow—rode at her moorings, and men were already crawling over her rigging like ants preparing for a long picnic.

"She sails in three weeks," Tam said. "Less, if the ice comes early. It's coming early. Old Maren says the terns left a fortnight ahead of schedule."

"You've been talking to Old Maren."

"Someone in this family has to collect intelligence. You collect it too, you just file it where nobody can read it." He gripped the rail, and she watched his knuckles, watching for whiteness, for effort, cataloguing her brother the way she catalogued everything, and hating herself for that too, gently, the way you hate a habit rather than a sin.

"Sable." He didn't look at her. He watched the ship. "Whatever you're doing—all the pacing, and the lists, and the face you make when you think I'm asleep—I want you to know I've done the sum too."

"Tam."

"I'm better arithmetic than he is. That's just true. I'm worth more per crown invested. Even he'd agree, wherever he is, because he always liked sums more than sons." He turned to her then and smiled, and it was their mother's smile, sudden and crooked and unbearable. "But I'm going to tell you something, and then we're never going to speak of it again. If you give that berth to him—to *him*—I will understand. I will even, someday, maybe, forgive it. Because I think you need to see him. I think you need to stand in front of him and ask him why, and watch him fail to answer, and then come home and burn every candle in the house and start over. Some accounts you can't close from a distance." He coughed—three, four—and waved off her hand. "But if you give it to him, Sable, you'd better be certain you're spending it. Not saving it. Not carrying it forward." He tapped her ledger-hand, there on the rail, once, lightly. "You're the only person I know who could hide a whole life in a margin. Don't hide mine there."

Then he pushed off the rail and walked back toward the house, unhurried, a tall thin shadow in the bright cold air, and Sable stayed at the seawall a long time, doing the one sum she had no column for.

She was still standing there when a shadow fell across the planks beside her—crisp, unhurried, smelling faintly of ink and train-smoke—and a voice she had begun to hear in her sleep said, pleasantly:

"Miss Nystrom. What a coincidence. I find myself in need of a good conversation about ships."

Auditor Brandt smiled at the harbor like a man admiring a balance sheet, and Sable felt the ice begin to close.
