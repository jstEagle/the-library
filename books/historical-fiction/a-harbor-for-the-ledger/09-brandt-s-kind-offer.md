## 9. Brandt's Kind Offer

Auditor Elias Brandt had a theory about kindness, which was that it worked.

Not sentimentality—he had no use for sentimentality, which he considered a form of bookkeeping error, an entry made in the wrong column. But genuine, patient, well-timed courtesy? Courtesy opened doors that crowbars only scratched. He had audited forty-one companies in his career and had never once needed to raise his voice. Voices raised were voices that had run out of arithmetic.

He woke before dawn in the Meridian Company's guest quarters, a narrow room above the salt store that smelled of brine and old rope and, faintly, of the herring the previous occupant had evidently smuggled in against regulations. He noted the herring. Somewhere in Greywater there was a storeman skimming provisions, and Brandt intended to find him by Thursday, though not because it mattered. Because order mattered. A town that let its herring go unrecorded would let anything go unrecorded.

He dressed carefully—grey suit, grey waistcoat, the small silver chain of his watch laid across the waistcoat like a meridian line of its own—and went down to the weighing house an hour before it opened.

The fog was doing what Greywater fog did, which was everything. It erased the harbor masts, erased the far breakwater, erased the distinction between water and sky until the whole world seemed to be one vast undifferentiated ledger awaiting entry. Brandt found this restful. Other men complained about the fog. Brandt thought it was the most honest thing about the town: it showed you exactly how much you actually knew, which was nothing, unless you walked out into it carefully and took measurements.

The weighing house was unlocked. Of course it was. The clerk arrived early; she always arrived early. Miss Nystrom had been at her desk for—Brandt consulted his watch—forty minutes already, and through the frosted window he could see her lamp burning, and her silhouette bent over the books with the particular posture of someone who believed that if she made herself small enough and correct enough, the world would pass her by.

It was a reasonable strategy. It had worked for seven years. Brandt admired it almost as much as he intended to end it.

* * *

He did not knock immediately. He stood outside the door for three full minutes, hat in hand, listening to the sounds of honest work: the scratch of the pen, the soft flip of a page, once—a small human note—the clink of a teaspoon against a cup, hastily stilled. She was drinking tea at her desk, which was against company policy, which meant she was human, which meant she could be spoken to.

Then he knocked.

"Miss Nystrom. Good morning. I hope I'm not early."

"You're always early, Mr. Brandt." Her voice came level and cool through the door, and he heard the drawer slide shut—the tea cup, hidden, filed away. A tidy mind. "The office opens at six."

"And yet here we both are at five-twenty, keeping secrets from each other." He let the smile reach his voice if not yet his face. "May I come in?"

A pause. The pause of a person deciding whether refusing was survivable. Then: "Of course."

The weighing house interior was exactly as he remembered from yesterday, except colder and lit by one lamp. Sable Nystrom sat behind the counter with her hands folded, a young woman of perhaps four-and-twenty, dark-haired, sea-eyed, with the stillness of someone who had learned early that stillness was safety. Her father's eyes, he'd been told. He wouldn't know. He'd never met Josiah Nystrom, drowned man, debtor, ghost. But he had spent two weeks reading the man's forged signatures, and you came to know people that way—better than most wives knew their husbands, Brandt sometimes thought. You learned their hesitations. Where the ink thickened when they lied.

"Tea?" he asked pleasantly. "I smelled tea."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Miss Nystrom." He set his hat on the counter, precisely centered between them, like a boundary stone. "I am going to be frank with you, because I have observed over the past fortnight that frankness unsettles you less than politeness does. Politeness makes you wait for the blow. Frankness lets you see where I'm aiming. You strike me as a woman who prefers to see."

Her chin lifted a fraction. "Say your piece, then."

"The shipment of March last year. Pearl consignment, billed at four hundred eleven ounces, received at the southern office at three hundred sixty-two. Forty-nine ounces missing. The receiving documents bear your father's signature." He watched her face with the attention other men gave to card sharps. There: nothing moved, and the nothing itself was eloquent. "You found this discrepancy on the eleventh of last month. You filed it under 'clerical variance, resolved.'"

"It was a clerical variance," she said. "Weight loss in transit. Damp crates."

"Damp crates don't eat forty-nine ounces of pearl, Miss Nystrom. Nothing eats pearls. That is rather the point of them." He said it gently, almost apologetically, the way one points out a small stain on a guest's cuff. "Your father signed those documents. Your father then vanished into the sea—or so we're told—with a debt to this company of some nine thousand marks outstanding, secured against—" he let his gaze travel, unhurried, around the little room, and settle back on her "—certain assets. His indentured daughter, chiefly. Forgive the phrasing. I didn't write the contract. I merely read contracts, which is worse."

The lamp hissed softly. Out in the fog, a gull cried once, like a question no one wanted answered.

"My father is dead," Sable said.

"Yes," said Brandt. "That is what everyone says. It is remarkable how often it is also what everyone hopes."

* * *

Here is what Brandt knew, and he arranged it now in his mind the way he arranged all things, in columns, debit and credit:

Debit: the March shipment fraud pointed to Josiah Nystrom's hand, but the forgeries were good—very good—which argued practice, which argued either a long history or instruction. And there was one document among them, the third bill of lading, whose paper stock did not match the company's own supply. Someone inside Meridian had provided materials. Fraud of this quality in a company this size rarely swam alone.

Credit: the girl at the counter had found the discrepancy and buried it. Not reported it upward, as a loyal clerk would. Not exploited it, as a dishonest one would. Buried it—quietly, neatly, in the way of someone protecting something. Or someone.

And credit again, entered just this morning: the stranger woman, Varric, who had come off the northern packet asking questions at the taverns about the Nystrom family with a precision that made even the tavern keeper uneasy, and who had been seen—this from the boy who swept the fish market, paid a copper a week for his observations—slipping a folded letter across the weighing house counter two days ago. And who now lodged at the widow Hessel's, and who coughed in the night in a manner Brandt recognized from the fishing camps' reports. Lung-rot. The north was full of it. The company's ledgers were full of the cost of pretending otherwise.

So. The dead father was likely alive, and the living daughter knew it, and somewhere in the fog between them lay either the missing pearls, the missing documents, or both.

Most auditors would have gone straight to the director with suspicion. But suspicion was cheap, and Brandt did not deal in cheap things. Suspicion got you a frightened clerk, a sealed mouth, and a nine-thousand-mark hole in the accounts that stayed a hole forever. What Brandt dealt in was conversions. Turn liabilities into assets. Turn enemies into instruments. Turn a scared girl hiding behind a counter into a witness who handed you the whole case gift-wrapped, grateful for the privilege.

For that, you needed kindness. Real kindness, or its flawless counterfeit—he had practiced so long he honestly no longer knew which his was, and it had stopped mattering years ago.

* * *

"Miss Nystrom," he said, and drew up a stool, and sat, which surprised her—he saw it land, the surprise, the auditor lowering himself to her level like a large grey bird folding down onto a fence post. "I want to tell you a story. Indulge me. It's short."

She said nothing, which he took as indulgence.

"Seven years ago a man owed my company nine thousand marks. He loaded a boat with what he could carry and he disappeared, and the sea was kind enough to provide an ending that everyone wanted to believe. His children got a tragedy. My company got a loss. And a clerk named Sable Nystrom got an indenture—seven years of balancing books in a freezing weighing house to pay for a crime she didn't commit, against a debt she never signed." He paused. "I find that unjust. I want you to know that I genuinely find it unjust. I am not a sentimental man, but I believe accounts should balance, and hers do not. She has overpaid."

Something flickered in her face—not softening, exactly. Recognition. The dangerous kind.

"You've read my contract," she said quietly.

"I've memorized your contract." He smiled, briefly, and let the smile fade into something more earnest. "Now. The second part of the story. If Josiah Nystrom is alive—if he forged those documents, if he defrauded this company of forty-nine ounces of pearl and fled his debts—then the law is simple and I will pursue it to whatever rock he is hiding under, and I will find him, Miss Nystrom, because finding things is the only skill I have ever possessed and I have polished it for thirty years. When I find him, the company seizes everything he has, which is nothing, and everything securing his debt, which is you. Your indenture transfers to the southern office. The southern office uses indentured clerks the way ships use ballast."

"That isn't my problem," she said, too quickly. "My father's crimes aren't mine."

"No," Brandt agreed warmly. "They're merely yours to pay for. Which brings me to the offer."

He reached into his coat and produced, with a small ceremony he knew the value of, a single sheet of paper, folded once, and laid it on the counter beside his hat. He did not push it toward her. Pushing was for salesmen. He simply let it exist, the way you let a key exist in a locked room.

"Testimony," he said. "Sworn, witnessed, in your own hand if you prefer—that would spare you the indignity of saying it aloud. Your father forged the missing documents. You discovered the discrepancy, you investigated as any diligent clerk would, and you found evidence of the forgery. In exchange: Meridian releases your indenture. Today. Full and final settlement of the Nystrom debt against the value of your testimony. You walk out of this weighing house a free woman, Miss Nystrom. No southern office. No ballast. Seven years returned to you."

The silence stretched. Outside, the fog pressed at the windows like something listening.

"And if I refuse?"

Brandt spread his hands—an honest gesture, he'd been told, disarming, almost boyish on his lean frame. "Then nothing happens today. I continue my audit. Audits are thorough. Thoroughness has a way of touching everyone near a discrepancy—clerks especially. Clerks who filed false resolutions, notably. 'Clerical variance, resolved,' Miss Nystrom, when you knew it was neither. That has a name in the company code. Section eleven, subsection four. I won't recite the penalty; it's dull. Everything in the code is dull except the penalties." He said it without heat, almost kindly. "I don't want that outcome. Truly. Punishing you costs me a competent witness and gains the company nothing but a grudge, and grudges accrue interest. I'd rather free you. I am, in my way, offering to do you a kindness."

"You're offering to buy me," Sable said.

"I'm offering to *un*-buy you," said Brandt. "There's a difference, and it happens to favor you."

* * *

He gave her time. That was part of the craft—kindness included patience, patience included silence, and silence did more work than any speech. He watched her look at the paper without touching it. He watched her hands stay flat on the counter, steady, while her eyes did the arithmetic he knew was running behind them: freedom on one side, a name on the other. He had seen that arithmetic a hundred times in a hundred faces, and it always came out the same way eventually, because the self is a creditor that never forgives and always collects.

What he had not anticipated—and this irritated him later, the way a single misplaced figure irritates a clean page—was that she did not cry, or plead, or bargain. She looked up at him with her father's eyes, and they were calm, and calm in a cornered creature is a datum worth recording.

"You said evidence," she said. "If I testify, you'll need more than my word. Documents. Signatures compared. Where would I have found evidence of forgery, Mr. Brandt? My father drowned. His papers went down with him."

"Did they," said Brandt, mildly.

It was not a question. He let it sit there between them, small and round and terrible, like a pearl on a scale.

She held his gaze for three seconds—longer than most, he noted, entering it in the mental ledger under *Nystrom, Sable: composure, exceptional*—and then said, "I'll consider your offer. The office opens soon. Fishermen don't wait."

"They never do," Brandt agreed, rising, retrieving his hat. At the door he paused, and turned, and gave her the full measure of his courtesy, which was the most dangerous thing he owned. "Miss Nystrom. Whatever you decide—I'd decide before the weather turns. The ice doesn't negotiate, and neither, after a certain date, can I. Good morning."

He let himself out into the fog, pleased with the morning's work. The trap was laid, the bait generous, the exits mapped. By week's end he would have his testimony, his conversion of liability into asset, and the satisfaction—real, he insisted it was real—of having freed an innocent woman from her father's debts.

Behind him, in the weighing house, the lamp burned on.

And Sable Nystrom, alone with the folded paper and the fog, looked out past the harbor toward the black line of the old pearl beds beneath the grey water—where, though Auditor Brandt did not know it yet, an answer had been waiting at the bottom of the sea for seven years—and reached for her coat.
