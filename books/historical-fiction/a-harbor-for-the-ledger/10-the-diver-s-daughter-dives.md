## 10. The Diver's Daughter Dives

The tide at Greywater ran out around four in the morning, and by half past, the harbor floor lay bare as a scoured skillet for nearly a quarter mile. Sable had grown up thinking of that exposed stretch as her father's second parlor. He'd walked it every low tide in his rubber boots, hands clasped behind his back like a bishop inspecting his parish, and he would point with his chin at this rock or that channel and say things like *the water remembers where you're rude to it* and *a diver who hurries drowns politely.*

She had not thought of those sayings in years. Now they came back all at once, uninvited, like relatives at a funeral.

She stood at the edge of the shingle with her father's old diving belt coiled over one shoulder and Ilse's letter folded against her heart, and she told herself—firmly, the way she told Tam to take his medicine—that she was not doing anything foolish. She was taking a walk. A walk into very cold water, weighted, alone, before dawn, to retrieve something a drowned man had hidden seven years ago.

Perfectly ordinary.

"You're going to do it," Ilse said behind her. Not a question. Ilse had a way of stating facts so flatly they sounded like accusations, which Sable was beginning to suspect was simply what honesty sounded like when it had been left out in the weather too long.

"The tide turns in an hour," Sable said. "If I'm going to be a fool, I'd rather be a punctual fool."

Ilse crouched on the stones and began helping her buckle the belt without being asked. Her cough had been quieter these past two days—the dry spell before the autumn rains, she called it, though her eyes said otherwise—and her fingers were deft with the buckles in a way that suggested she had fastened diving gear before, or at least watched someone die slowly enough to learn patience.

"Your father dove here?" she asked.

"He dove everywhere. He said the harbor beds were picked clean by his grandmother's time, but he liked the practice of it." Sable pulled the rope through her palms, checking for rot. It held. "He used to say a diver's whole education is learning what the water takes and what it only borrows."

"And which is your father?"

Sable didn't answer. The question sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The water took her the way it always had: first the shock, then the forgiveness. Cold clamped her ribs, then relented into something almost companionable. Below the surface, Greywater stopped being a town of ledgers and became what it had been before anyone thought to weigh anything—a world of green light and drifting silt and the slow percussion of her own heartbeat in her ears.

Her father had taught her to dive when she was nine. He'd held her ankles in the shallows while she learned to let the water hold her back instead of fighting it. *Don't conquer the sea,* he'd said, spitting salt. *Conquering is for men with ships. You just visit. Be polite. Take nothing you can't carry up in one hand.* She had been good at it—better than him, eventually, because she was small and patient and could stay down until the world above forgot about her. Staying down. Staying invisible. Even then, even underwater, her one great talent.

She followed the memory of his map now. He had drawn no map, but he had talked, once, drunk on cheap brandy after a good season, about a shelf of basalt below the northern point where the current made a pocket of still water—"a room," he'd called it, "with no door but the one I make." She'd thought it a diver's boast. Divers boasted the way sailors did, about depths and holds and women who waited. But she remembered the shelf. She remembered, too, that three days before the *Meridian Star* reported him lost, her father had rowed out alone at dawn with a sea chest lashed in the bow, and had come back without it, and had whistled all the way up the dock.

At the time she'd assumed he'd sold the chest. Her father was forever selling things.

The northern point rose ahead of her, black rock furred with kelp. She swam along its shoulder, counting fin-kicks the way her father had taught her to count—one for every boat-length—and at forty she tipped down into the green dark and let herself sink.

The bottom came up soft and slow. Silt drifted like snow falling upward whenever her fins disturbed it. She found the shelf exactly where memory kept it, a lip of basalt jutting from the point's base, and beneath it, the current did make its pocket—still water, unnaturally clear, as if the sea had agreed to keep a secret and was embarrassed to have held it so long.

And there, wedged under the lip, wrapped in tarred canvas and crusted with seven years of barnacle, was a shape roughly the size of a hymnal.

Sable hung in the water and looked at it.

She should have felt triumph. What she felt instead was a strange, cold anger—not at her father, not yet, but at the sea itself, at the sheer *ease* of it. Seven years of grief. Seven years of her mother going gray and silent, of Tam growing up with a hole in every story, of Sable learning to keep her head down because the world took fathers who stood tall. And it had been sitting here the whole time, forty kicks from the dock, under a rock, waiting. The water hadn't taken him. It had only borrowed him, and then given him back to somewhere else entirely, and kept only this.

She worked the bundle loose. It was heavier than a hymnal. Iron-bound corners bit through the canvas—her father had sunk an actual strongbox, a small one, the kind merchants kept their best pearls in, and he had weighed it with a diver's care, just heavy enough to stay put, just light enough for one hand to carry up. *Take nothing you can't carry up in one hand.*

She kicked for the surface with it pressed to her chest, and the green light opened above her like a door.

* * *

She broke the surface gasping, and the gasp turned halfway into a laugh, which frightened her more than the dive had. She hauled herself onto the shingle and collapsed on her back among the stones, strongbox on her stomach, rising and falling with her breath, and the sky overhead was going the color of a bruise healing into peach.

Ilse's face appeared above her, upside down, framed by wet hair and the first grey light.

"Well," Ilse said.

"It was there," Sable said. Her voice came out cracked with salt. "Forty kicks from the dock. Under a rock. Like a spare house key."

"Your father sounds like a careful man."

"My father sounds like a man who planned his own drowning down to the tide table." She sat up, shivering, and began working at the barnacled seams with her knife. "Help me with this. My hands are useless."

They were, in fact, useless—white and pruned and shaking—but between the two of them and Ilse's small sharp belt knife, the canvas came away in rotten ribbons, and then the box was bare: iron-strapped oak, salt-welded shut, with a lock that looked at them smugly.

"No key," Sable said.

"There's a keyhole."

"There's a keyhole and no key, which is my father all over. He'd hide a thing perfectly and forget the part where you open it." She struck the lock with the flat of her knife, out of pure frustration, and the hasp—corroded half through—gave way with a crack that echoed off the point. They both froze. A gull screamed somewhere, mocking.

Inside, nested in wool gone grey with brine, were papers.

Not pearls. Not coin. Papers, wrapped in oilcloth, dozens of them, and when Sable peeled the wrapping back with numb fingers, the Meridian Company's crest stared up at her—the meridian line through the globe, embossed in gold, smug as ever.

"Bills of lading," Ilse said quietly, reading over her shoulder. "Shipment manifests. Signed."

"I know these forms. I file twenty of them a week." Sable turned the top sheet toward the light. It was a manifest for a pearl shipment out of Greywater, dated six weeks before her father vanished, and the declared weight was wrong—wrong in the specific, deliberate way she knew better than anyone alive, because she had spent seven years filing around exactly that kind of wrong. The pearls recorded were twice what the beds had produced that season. Someone had written numbers that did not exist and sent them south, and someone else had signed off.

The signature at the bottom was her father's. She recognized the slanted hand the way you recognize a voice in another room.

But it was the countersignature that stopped her breath.

Above her father's name, in a rounder, colder script, in the ink of authority, was the approval line reserved for the company's regional director. *Approved and ordered per instruction. E. Calloway, Director, Northern District, Meridian Trading Company.*

"That's not possible," Sable said.

"What isn't?"

"Calloway signed *above* him. Directors don't countersign clerks' manifests—they barely read them. This is—" She flipped the next sheet. Another manifest. Another impossible weight. Calloway again, *per instruction,* in that same round cold hand. And the next, and the next. Eleven sheets of it, plus letters—actual letters, in Calloway's hand, instructing her father to inflate the shipments, to book phantom weight, to move the difference south in unlisted crates. The fraud wasn't her father's invention. It was his *employment*. He hadn't stolen from Meridian. He had been Meridian's pen, and when he'd tried—or threatened, or merely hesitated—to stop being the pen, he had become a problem, and problems in Greywater were solved by becoming dead.

"They let us believe he drowned," Sable said. The words came out very small. "They let *me* keep his books. They indentured me against debts he never—" She stopped, because the arithmetic went further and she couldn't bear to watch it finish. If the fraud was Calloway's, then her indenture wasn't justice. It was *concealment*. She was not collateral on her father's debt; she was a hostage against a secret. The company hadn't taken her in grief. It had filed her.

Seven years. Every entry. Every quiet night keeping her head down. All of it, it turned out, had been somebody else's cover story, written in her handwriting.

"Sable." Ilse's hand closed over hers, warm and steady. "You're shaking."

"I'm angry. There's a difference. Angrier." She laughed, or something near it, and wiped her face with a salt-crusted sleeve. "Do you know what I did yesterday? I filed a shipment that was short eleven pounds and I said nothing, because saying nothing is how you survive here. And the whole time—" She held up the oilcloth packet. "The whole time the truth was sitting in the harbor. Forty kicks away. He hid it *here*, Ilse. In the one place he knew better than any auditor alive. He trusted the water with it and he didn't trust me."

"Maybe he was protecting you."

"Maybe he was protecting himself, and I was the safest place to leave it, because I never look at anything." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't take it back. Some bitterness is earned. Some is inherited, and you only discover whose it was when you dig it up.

Ilse said nothing for a long moment. Then she reached out and took the packet gently from Sable's hands and weighed it, the way you'd weigh a sleeping cat.

"This is worth a great deal," she said. "To Brandt. To the company. Against Calloway—it would ruin him."

"It would ruin everyone. That's what it's for. It's a weapon, and my father built it and sank it and left me to decide whether to swing it." Sable looked out at the water, which had resumed its innocent morning shimmer, gulls wheeling, tide beginning its long crawl back in over the flats. Somewhere across the harbor, the weighing house bell rang the hour, indifferent as ever. "Brandt is pulling threads right now. He'll get to my name within days. When he does, I want to know what I am holding before he tells me what I am."

"And what are you holding?"

Sable took the packet back. It fit in one hand, exactly as designed. Her father, whatever else he was, had been precise.

"A diver's daughter knows what the water takes and what it only borrows," she said. "This was borrowed. Time to bring it home."

She wrapped the oilcloth tight, tucked the packet inside her coat against her heart, next to the place where her father's letter had lived for two weeks, and started up the shingle toward town. Behind her, the tide slid in over the flats, filling the old beds, covering the rock, erasing everything except what people were stubborn enough to carry out.

She was halfway to the harbor road before she noticed Ilse had fallen silent—truly silent, in a way that Sable, who had spent two weeks learning the geography of this woman's silences, had not heard before.

Ilse was looking at the strongbox's empty shell, still lying open on the stones where they'd left it. Looking at it the way a person looks at a thing they expected to find something else inside of.

"Come on," Sable said. "Cold's getting into your lungs."

Ilse looked up quickly, and smiled, and the smile was warm and real and arrived perhaps half a second too late.

"Yes," Ilse said. "Coming."

And Sable walked on beside her toward town with the truth of Meridian warm against her ribs, telling herself that half a second meant nothing, that she was imagining things, that a woman who had lied for seven years by staying invisible could hardly complain now that invisibility was looking back at her from someone else's eyes.
