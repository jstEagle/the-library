## 11. A Letter Not Sent

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like patience.

I had been practicing it for eleven days.

Eleven days of hauling nets with the Greywater fishermen, of sitting at Sable's table eating her terrible barley soup—she cooked the way she kept ledgers, precisely and without love—and of saying nothing while the strongbox sat in my mind like a stone I'd swallowed. Eleven days of watching her trust me grow by small increments, the way ice grows on a harbor: slowly, invisibly, until one morning it will bear a man's weight, or drown him.

I knew where her father's strongbox was. I had known since March.

And every day I told myself: tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll tell her. Tomorrow there will be a moment when she isn't tired, when Tam's cough has quieted, when the weighing house is empty and the light is decent and the words won't come out sounding like what they were.

Tomorrow is a northern word. We use it the way southerners use *someday*, except our tomorrows actually run out. The ice sees to that.

It ran out on a Tuesday, because of a herring.

* * *

Let me explain the herring, because the herring matters, and because if I don't start with something small I will never manage the large thing at all.

The fishermen of Greywater paid their mooring fees at the company window, in coin, and the coin went into a locked box, and the box went into the company ledger, and somewhere between the box and the ledger a portion of it evaporated—a practice so old and so universal that even the men being robbed had stopped calling it theft and started calling it Tuesday. Sable Nystrom had spent seven years making sure none of that evaporation happened on her pages. She was very good at it. She was so good at it that when Auditor Brandt arrived with his grey gloves and his grey eyes and his grey little smile, he found nothing to hold, only threads to pull.

But that week, a fisherman named Oskar Lund came to the window with his fee and a grievance, and the grievance was this: he'd been shorted on his catch price three weeks running, and he wanted it written down. Officially. In ink.

"Written down where?" Sable asked him. She wasn't unkind. She was careful, which in Greywater passes for kindness most of the time.

"In your book. The real book."

"There is only one book."

Oskar Lund looked at her for a long moment, and then he looked at me, standing by the stove with my tea, and he said—and I will hear this sentence until I die—"Ask the northerner. Northerners know what happens to books."

He didn't mean anything by it. That's the worst part. He meant it as a compliment, almost. Up north we keep honest books because nobody's ever taught us better ways to be cheated.

Sable laughed. It was a small laugh, tired, but real, and she put her hand briefly on my arm—the first time she'd touched me without a reason, without passing me something, without steadying herself against the cold—and she said, "What do northerners know about books, then?"

And I said, before I could stop myself, before the cowardice could get its hands up in time:

"They know what happens to strongboxes."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the kettle to go from singing to screaming. Sable took it off the heat herself. She did everything herself; I have watched her carry things twice her size rather than ask. Then she turned around and looked at me, and her face had gone into its ledger-expression, the one where all the numbers are hidden behind the columns.

"What did you say?"

"You heard me." My voice came out flat. Northern voices do that when they're frightened; they flatten like sea ice. "Sit down, Sable."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit down anyway. What I have to tell you is better taken sitting. It's how we deliver bad news in the camps. Standing up, people fall down. Sitting, they just go quiet, and quiet you can work with."

She sat. She sat the way she did everything, straight-backed, hands folded, as though posture were a form of accounting. And I told her.

I told her that in March, in the camp called Vaskr, her father had sat across from me in a hut that smelled of fish oil and camphor, coughing the wet rattle I now knew too well, and had given me two things. A bundle of letters. And an errand.

"The letters were for you," I said. "That part was true. Every word of them true—he wrote them over four years, some of them, rewriting when his hand got bad. He loves you. I believe that. I've had occasion lately to learn what his love costs other people, but I believe it's real."

"And the errand." Her voice was very level. Leveler than mine.

"He sank a strongbox," I said. "Before he left Greywater. Seven years ago, the night the *Vigilant* burned. He dove the old pearl beds off Gull Rock and weighted it down among the dead beds where no diver bothers anymore, because the beds are played out—or everyone believes they are. Inside are documents. Shipping manifests. Correspondence. Proof that the fraudulent shipments weren't his idea alone, that Meridian's own director—Harrow, the one with the portrait in the weighing house—signed off on the underweight pearls and let your father take the blame, and the debt, and the drowning."

I made myself look at her while I said it. It seemed owed to her, eye contact, like a signature on a document.

"He sent me to retrieve it. For himself. To hold over Harrow. To buy his way back—not to Greywater, he doesn't want Greywater—to passage south, money, a life. The letters were the bait. Not deliberately—I think he convinced himself they were separate errands, the loving father and the debtor. But they came in the same boat. They came in *me*."

* * *

Here is what I expected: fury. Thrown crockery. The door.

Here is what I got, which was worse: Sable Nystrom going still. Completely still, the way water goes still just before it freezes, and asking questions. Small, precise, terrible questions.

"When did he tell you about the box?"

"March."

"When did you find out who I was? That I was his daughter?"

I hesitated. She caught the hesitation the way she caught a misfiled entry—one glance, no mercy.

"Before I left Vaskr," I admitted. "He described you. Pearl diver's daughter, indentured clerk, keeps her head down. He said you'd be the easiest person in Greywater to find because you'd be the hardest person in Greywater to notice."

Something moved behind her face at that. Something old and hurt and seven years deep.

"And the night I met you at the weighing house," she said. "When you asked about the Nystrom family with your unsettling precision. When I denied knowing anything and you left the letter anyway. You already knew everything."

"Yes."

"You watched me deny my own father."

"Yes."

"Why?" And here her voice finally cracked, just along one seam, a hairline fracture in good porcelain. "Why not say it? Why not say *your father lives, and also, incidentally, I'm here to rob him of his leverage*? Why sit at my table and eat my soup and haul nets with me and let me—"

She stopped. She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to. Let me—trust you, was the end of it. Let me believe that someone came to Greywater for me and not for what I could fetch out of the water.

I am dying—that's a fact I carry around like a second set of lungs, the bad ones, the ones full of the camp's lung-rot—and I have thought a great deal about what I want to be true when the truth gets final. So I gave her the true thing, unpolished.

"Because I'm a coward," I said. "Because the errand was simple and you were not. Because he paid my passage and I owed him and debts are the one scripture northerners keep faithfully. Because every day I meant to tell you and every day you handed me tea, or laughed at my accent, or fell asleep at the table over Tam's fever charts, and telling you would have ended it—ended *this*—and I wanted this more than I wanted to be honest." I spread my hands. "There's the whole ugly ledger. Read it however you like."

* * *

She read it for a long time.

I made more tea, because my hands needed employment and because tea is what you do in the north when words have failed; we brew through grief the way southerners pray through it. Sable stood at the window with her back to me, looking out at the harbor, where the last ship lay at anchor looking smug about its single berth.

"Does Brandt know?" she asked finally. "About the box."

"Not from me."

"Does my father know where I live? Exactly?"

"He knows the weighing house. He knows you're indentured. He doesn't know where you sleep."

"Small mercies." She turned around. Her eyes were dry, which surprised me; I'd braced for tears and gotten something harder. "Ilse. Answer me honestly, since honesty is apparently a new policy in this room. If I hadn't dived the beds myself—if I hadn't found the box on my own—would you have taken it? Would you have rowed out to Gull Rock one night and brought it north to him and been gone before I knew?"

I considered lying. I want that recorded. I considered it seriously, the way a drowning woman considers air.

"No," I said. "Not anymore. Ask me in April and the answer might have been yes. But not anymore."

"But in April—"

"In April you were a description in a letter. In November you're—" I stopped. Started again. "You're the reason I stayed past the errand. He gave me six weeks to deliver the letters and come home. It's been nearly three months. He'll have noticed."

"Then he'll send someone else."

"He can't afford anyone else. That's rather the tragedy of my father-in-errand. He spent everything getting me south. I'm the entire expedition." I tried a smile. It came out crooked, the way my smiles do these days; the lung-rot takes the symmetry first. "Congratulations. You have the only agent Meridian's ex-clerk's ex-partner could field, and she's compromised."

"Don't call yourself compromised like it's funny."

"It's either funny or unbearable, and I've found funny keeps the coughing quieter."

"That," said Sable Nystrom, "is the worst medical advice I have ever heard, and Tam's doctor once prescribed him a poultice of boiled socks."

And there—God help me—there it was. The corner of her mouth. Not forgiveness. Forgiveness is a document that takes longer to draft. But a stay of proceedings. A note in the margin: *case continued.*

* * *

We talked until the lamp burned low. Not everything—there are ledgers neither of us opened that night—but the important columns. She told me what the harbormaster had confessed: that her indenture was collateral, that her whole invisible careful life had been interest payments on her father's flight. I told her about the camps, the rot, the way the company bought northern fish at famine prices and sold southern salt at ransom prices and called it commerce. Two women comparing wounds, discovering they'd been bleeding into the same account book for years.

Near the end, she asked the question I'd been dreading.

"The documents in the box. Harrow's signatures. If they came to light—truly came to light, in front of the right people—what would happen?"

"To Harrow? Ruin. Prison, possibly, if the southern courts care about fraud committed against their own trading companies, which historically they care about exactly as much as it inconveniences them." I wrapped my hands around the cooling cup. "To your father? Nothing. He's dead, officially. Dead men can't be prosecuted. It's the one advantage of letting your children mourn you."

"To me?"

I looked at her. She looked back, steady, and I understood she'd already done the arithmetic herself and was only checking my figures.

"To you," I said carefully, "it depends who holds them. In your hands, they're armor. In Brandt's hands, they're a leash. In your father's hands, they're a weapon pointed at everyone including you. And burned—" I watched her flinch, very slightly, at the word, and filed that away for later, the way I file everything away, it's my one talent—"burned, they're ash, and ash protects no one and imprisons no one, and everyone walks free into whatever weather comes."

"Everyone," she repeated softly. "Except whoever's holding the pen when the story gets written."

"That's why," I said, "you should write it yourself."

* * *

She walked me back to my room above the chandlery, because that's what she does—escorts people, guards perimeters, manages risk—and at the door she paused.

"Ilse. The letters he wrote me. Four years of them. Did he write anything else? Anything he didn't send?"

I should have said no.

Here is the thing about dying: it makes you spend truth like a drunk spends wages. I reached into my coat, into the inner pocket where I keep the papers that matter, and I handed her a letter. Folded twice, sealed, worn soft at the edges from four years of being carried and never posted. His slanted hand on the front. Her name.

"He wrote this one in September," I said. "After the cough got bad. He told me to burn it. I told him I would." I shrugged, which hurt, and smiled, which hurt more. "Northerners are liars about exactly one thing each. This is mine."

She held it without opening it. "What's in it?"

"If I knew, I'd have read it. I don't open other people's mail. I just fail to deliver it." I pushed the door open. "Read it tonight or don't. But Sable—whatever's in it, remember it was written by a man who was drowning in his own debts and reaching for the only solid things left. People write badly when they're drowning. Judge the swimmer, not the stroke."

She stood in the lamplight with the unsent letter in both hands, and for a moment she looked so much like her father as he'd been twenty years ago—before the debts, before the drowning, when he was just a diver with a laugh like a gull—that my chest did something unrelated to the lung-rot.

Then the moment passed, because moments do, and she said goodnight, and I closed the door, and I stood listening to her boots go down the stairs, slow, stopping twice—once, I guessed, to tuck Tam's blanket, once to simply stand still and decide something.

I hoped she'd decide to forgive me. I hoped she wouldn't decide too fast. Forgiveness issued quickly is a promissory note, and I've seen enough notes come due.

* * *

I slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn to the sound that has governed my whole life: wind changing.

It came out of the north-northeast with a smell under it that any camp child knows—the smell of the ice pack moving, of the season putting its hand on the harbor's shoulder. By full light the gulls were acting wrong, riding the air in tight anxious circles instead of scavenging the docks, and old Marta at the fish stall was selling off her stock at panic prices, and the harbormaster's flag was running up signals I couldn't read but understood anyway.

Down at the weighing house, a notice had been nailed to the post before Sable even arrived. Fresh ink. Company seal.

**BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY: OWING TO THE EARLY MOVEMENT OF THE PACK ICE, THE ANNUAL SUPPLY VESSEL *MERIDIAN STAR* WILL DEPART GREYWATER HARBOR WITHIN THE WEEK, WEATHER PERMITTING. ALL CLAIMS TO PASSENGER BERTHS MUST BE SETTLED AT THE COMPANY OFFICE BY FRIDAY.**

Within the week. Friday. Seven days, maybe five, maybe fewer, and the ice already breathing down the strait.

I found Sable inside, reading the notice she'd copied into her own hand, her face doing its ledger-expression, and behind her, from the loft stairs, came the sound I'd been dreading for days: Tam, coughing. Not the dry winter cough of before. The wet, deep, freighted kind, the kind I knew intimately, the kind that means the body has started keeping its own books and the entries are all going the wrong direction.

Sable looked at the notice. Then at the stairs. Then at me.

"The ice," she said, "isn't waiting for anyone's arithmetic."

And somewhere north of us, in a hut smelling of fish oil and camphor, a man who believed his daughter was coming to him with a strongbox full of leverage was running out of tomorrows too.
