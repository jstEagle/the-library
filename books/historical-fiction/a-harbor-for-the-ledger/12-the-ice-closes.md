## 12. The Ice Closes

The weather turned the way bad news always does in Greywater: not all at once, but by degrees you could pretend not to notice until pretending stopped being an option.

It started with the gulls. They left. All of them, in a single gray morning, wheeling out over the harbor and southward like a congregation that had heard something from the pulpit it didn't like. Old Marta at the fish stall said she'd seen it twice before in her life, and both times the ice had come down the sound "like a landlord."

Then the wind changed. It had blown from the west for as long as I could remember—soft, wet, smelling of kelp and rain—and overnight it swung north and went hard and dry, the kind of wind that came off the icefields carrying nothing, giving nothing, taking the warmth out of your bones through your coat, through your skin, through whatever you kept underneath.

By Thursday the puddles in the yard outside the weighing house wore skins of ice thin enough to crack under a boot heel, which Tam, being eleven going on twelve and therefore constitutionally incapable of walking around a thing that cracked satisfyingly, made it his business to do.

"Stop that," I said, without much conviction, because he'd been doing it for twenty minutes and my arithmetic was wrong in three places.

"It's the last time," he said, cracking another one. "Before they freeze all the way through. You have to get them while they're fresh."

"That is not how ice works."

"That is exactly how ice works. Papa said—" He stopped.

He did that now. Started sentences about our father and stopped them, the way you stop yourself on a stair that isn't there anymore. Seven years of practice at not saying his name, undone in two weeks by a letter and a stranger, and Tam hadn't found his footing yet. Neither had I, but I was older, so I'd gotten good at hiding it, which is the only real skill adulthood confers.

"Papa said," Tam tried again, more carefully, testing the weight of it, "that the ice comes when it comes, and everything else is just people arguing with it."

I put down my pen. That was the trouble with a father who talked like that—seven years dead or seven years hiding, the sayings outlived him, and every one of them arrived now with a hook in it.

"Well," I said, "Papa's not here to argue with it either way, so we'll take the weather as we find it."

Tam looked at me with those eyes of his—our mother's eyes, dark and level, the ones that had watched me file an underweight shipment of pearls and say nothing, though thank God he didn't know that—and nodded slowly, the way children nod when they understand perfectly well that the grown-up has dodged.

* * *

The notice went up Friday morning, nailed to the weighing house door at an angle that suggested the harbormaster's clerk had been drinking since dawn, which suggested the harbormaster had been drinking since Wednesday.

BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY, it read. OWING TO THE EARLY SEASON AND THE APPROACH OF FIELD ICE TO THE OUTER SOUND, THE SUPPLY VESSEL *CORIOLIS* WILL DEPART GREYWATER HARBOR ON THE TWENTY-SIXTH DAY OF THIS MONTH, ONE WEEK AHEAD OF SCHEDULED SAILING. NO EXCEPTIONS WILL BE ENTERTAINED. THE COMPANY THANKS YOU FOR YOUR UNDERSTANDING.

"The company thanks you for your understanding," Ilse read over my shoulder. Her voice was flat as slate, but I'd learned by then to hear what moved underneath it, the way you learn to hear water under ice. "That's generous of them."

"A week." I stared at the notice until the letters swam. "Seven days. The berth committee meets Monday. And Brandt—"

"Brandt knows the schedule better than the captain does." Ilse pulled her collar up against the north wind. She was thinner than when she'd come off the packet boat, and grayer, and she pretended I didn't notice, and I let her pretend, because there are mercies you extend to the dying and to the proud, and she qualified for both. "If he means to move against the berth, he moves this week. So if you're going to do anything foolish, Sable Nystrom, do it before Monday."

"You say that like a woman encouraging foolishness."

"I say it like a woman who has done a great deal of foolishness herself and found most of it worth the price." She looked at me then, sideways, and something almost like humor crossed her face—the ghost of a smile, the way the ghost of a fire still warms a room. "Besides. You were going to be foolish regardless. I'm simply scheduling it."

* * *

Tam took a turn Saturday night.

It began as it always began—a cough deep in the chest like something turning over in its sleep—but it didn't stop where it usually stopped. By midnight he was sitting up in bed because lying down drowned him, propped against the wall with his arms wrapped around a pillow, breathing in little ragged sips, and his lips had gone the color of the inside of a mussel shell.

Dr. Lenz came through the snow at two in the morning, smelling of brandy and camphor, and listened to Tam's chest with the stethoscope pressed so long and so hard against my brother's ribs that I wanted to pull it away myself.

When we stepped into the kitchen, Lenz didn't perform any bedside gentleness. He never did; it was one of the things I'd come, grudgingly, to respect about him.

"The left lung is filling again. Faster than in autumn." He rubbed his eyes with a thumb and forefinger. "You know what I'm going to say."

"Warm climate. Dry air. South of the ice."

"I've said it since he was nine. Every winter I say it, and every winter Greywater keeps him, and every spring he's a little less boy than he was." Lenz put on his hat. "There is a ship leaving on the twenty-sixth. There is, as I understand it, one berth available to residents. I don't know how these things are decided and I don't want to know. But I will write whatever letter needs writing, sign whatever paper needs signing, and swear before God and Auditor Brandt simultaneously if it helps. Get that child on the *Coriolis*, Miss Nystrom, or next winter I won't be listening to his chest. I'll be measuring him for a box."

He said it kindly, the way men in Greywater say terrible things, and let himself out into the wind.

I stood in the kitchen a long while after, hands flat on the table, doing the arithmetic I hated. One berth. One brother dying by inches of cold. One father alive and hunted, waiting in a hut in the north, believing—I had promised nothing, but believing—that his daughter would bring him a strongbox and maybe a way out.

Three claimants. One seat. And the ice closing down the sound like a hand closing around the whole town.

* * *

Sunday evening, I told Tam.

I'd meant to wait. I'd told myself I'd wait until the berth was settled, until I knew how the story ended, so I could give him the version with the good ending stitched in. But he caught me packing.

I keep a sea chest under my bed—our mother's, iron-banded, holding everything of hers that survived: two shawls, a diving knife, a photograph gone silver with age. I had it open on the floor and was folding my second-best sweater into it when Tam appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, gray-faced and furious about being gray-faced, and looked at the chest, and looked at me, and understood in the way of children, instantly and completely, that the world was rearranging itself behind their backs.

"You're leaving."

"No." I sat back on my heels. "Come sit down."

"You're leaving and you weren't going to tell me." His voice cracked in the middle, half illness and half outrage. "You're going to put me on a ship. That's why Dr. Lenz wrote a letter. I saw the envelope, it had the company seal, I'm sick, Sable, I'm not stupid."

"I have never once thought you stupid. Come sit down."

He sat on the edge of my bed with the rigid dignity of the very ill, arms crossed, and waited, and I understood that the moment for the stitched-together version with the good ending was gone. He deserved the truth, or as much truth as I had, which was a tangled net with holes in it.

So I told him. About Ilse, and the letters in Papa's handwriting. About the northern camps. About the debts, and Meridian, and the fact that our indenture wasn't charity—it was collateral, collected. I told him the drowning story was a story, that Papa had let us believe it for seven years because a dead man owes nothing and a living debtor owes everything, and I watched my brother's face while I said it, and I would rather have dived the black beds in January than watch it again.

Tam was quiet for a long time. The wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere down the street a dog was making its opinion of the weather known.

"He's alive," Tam said finally, in a small, careful voice, like a man walking on the ice before it's ready.

"Yes."

"Our papa. He's alive. In a hut. Up north." Each sentence landed separately, like crates dropped off a cart.

"Yes."

"And he let us think he was dead." The careful voice was gone. What replaced it was something I hadn't heard from him before—something old-sounding, something with our mother's steel in it. "He let you work off his debt. He let me cough my lungs out in this town for seven years thinking he was at the bottom of the sound. He let Mama die—" Tam's voice broke and he pushed through it, furious at the breaking, "—he let Mama die thinking she was a widow!"

"I know."

"You *know*?" He was on his feet now, swaying, too sick to stand and too angry to care. "You sit there with your ledgers and your calm face and you *know*, and you're not even—Sable, are you going to give him the berth?"

The question hit the center of the target so precisely I flinched. Because that was the arithmetic, laid bare by a twelve-year-old in a nightshirt: three claimants, one seat, and the man who'd caused all of it asking for his share.

"Tam—"

"Because I don't want it." He said it fast, hot, before he could reconsider, and I saw even then—he'd already reconsidered, already regretted the shape of it, but the truth was out and truth doesn't go back in the bottle. "I don't want anything from him. Not the berth, not the money, not one thing. He's dead to me. He got to be dead for seven years, he can stay dead." He ran out of breath mid-fury and folded onto the bed, coughing, that awful wet tearing sound, and I was beside him in a second with the basin and the camphor cloth, hating the sickness, hating the north wind, hating a man in a hut four days' hard travel away with a completeness that frightened me.

When the coughing eased, Tam lay back, exhausted, eyes closed, tears cutting clean tracks through the fever-flush on his face.

"He taught me to crack ice," he whispered. "First winter after Mama. He said you have to get it while it's fresh." His mouth twisted. "I forgot he taught me. I remembered it this week and I was happy about it, and he was alive the whole time, letting me be happy about a dead man's habits."

I sat on the bed and held his hand—his poor thin hand, all knuckles and heat—and I didn't tell him it would be all right, because I was no longer certain what *all right* meant, and he'd earned better than a lie.

"Get some sleep," I said instead.

"Don't give him the berth, Sable." His eyes opened, fever-bright and absolutely clear. "Please. Whatever else you do. Don't give that man our mother's ship ticket."

* * *

Ilse found me an hour later on the back step, wrapped in a blanket, watching the harbor. The water in the sound had gone the color of pewter, and far out, where the channel lights blinked, there was a white line on the horizon that hadn't been there yesterday. Field ice, coming down like a landlord.

She sat down beside me without a word. She was good at that—filling silence without spending it.

"He hates him," I said finally.

"Of course he does. Hatred is just love with nowhere to go." She pulled the blanket tighter over both our shoulders, hers and mine together, and her shoulder fit against mine with the ease of long acquaintance, though we'd known each other a month. "My father died when I was nine. Fever. I hated the fever, then I hated God, then I hated my father a little, for dying, for leaving the arithmetic of survival to a girl. It passes. Or it doesn't. Both are survivable."

"I promised him nothing about the berth."

"I know. But you're dividing yourself into thirds trying to solve it, and a person can't live divided into thirds, Sable. I've watched men try. They end up being nobody anywhere."

The white line on the horizon was closer. Or I imagined it was. With ice you can never be sure until it's in the harbor, and by then it's sure for you.

"There's another way," I said. It had been forming in me all evening, in the wordless place below the ledgers, and saying it aloud made it solid, made it dangerous. "North. Before the twenty-sixth. If I go up to the camps—if I see him, if I settle this face to face—then whoever gets the berth gets it chosen, not defaulted. And Brandt expects me to run south or stay and hide. Nobody expects the clerk to row into the ice."

Ilse was quiet a long moment. When I turned, she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read—fear, maybe, or its close cousin, love.

"The camps are five days' travel in good weather," she said.

"The weather isn't good."

"The weather is *awful*. And Brandt has company men watching the north road since Thursday. I've seen them. Two of them, at the ferry landing, wearing coats too good for fishermen." She exhaled, and her breath hung between us and froze. "Which means someone has already told him where your father is. Which means, my love, that whatever window we thought we had—"

Down at the dock, a bell began ringing. Not the harbor bell's lazy evening count, but the fast, urgent pattern—three, pause, three—that meant a boat coming in unscheduled, after dark, in field-ice season.

We stood together on the step and watched lanterns bob along the pier toward the slip, and among them, tall and unhurried, coat collar turned up against the wind, walked a figure I knew by silhouette alone, the way you know a knife by the shape of its shadow.

Auditor Brandt had come down to greet the night boat personally.

And the night boat, I would learn within the hour, had come from the north.
