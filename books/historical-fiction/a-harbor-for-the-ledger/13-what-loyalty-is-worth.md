## 13. What Loyalty Is Worth

Auditor Elias Brandt had a theory about numbers that he had never once found occasion to revise in nineteen years of service to the Meridian Trading Company. It was this: every lie told by human beings was, at bottom, an arithmetic problem. A man who stole did not steal *a thing*, he stole a *difference* — the gap between what was and what was recorded. And differences, unlike men, could not run. They could only wait, patiently, in the margins of the world, for someone with clean spectacles and sufficient stubbornness to come and collect them.

Elias Brandt had come to Greywater to collect.

He woke before dawn in the company guest room above the counting house, as he woke everywhere, because dawn was when the day's errors were still soft and unguarded. He shaved in cold water, dressed in his second-best grey suit — the best being reserved for dismissals and funerals, of which his career had offered him a regrettable abundance of both — and went down to the office he had commandeered, where the ledgers of the Meridian Company waited for him like suspects who had been left alone overnight with nothing to do but confess.

The discrepancy was waiting too. It had been waiting since Tuesday, and it had grown no smaller, which discrepancies rarely did unless someone fed them.

He laid it out on the desk again, the way another man might lay out a photograph of a missing person. Shipment 114-C, pearls, gross weight recorded at forty-one pounds, six ounces. Net weight at final sale, thirty-eight pounds even. A loss of two pounds, six ounces of product somewhere between Greywater and the southern markets. Not enormous. Not the kind of theft that made headlines or ruined directors. But wrong, and wrong in a particular way — wrong in a way that had been *papered over*. The receiving documents bore the countersignature of a diver's agent long dead. The tally sheets were initialed in ink that did not match the ink used three lines above them. Someone had reached back seven years into the books and tried to make the past behave.

And someone else — this was the part Brandt found genuinely interesting, the part he turned over in his mind the way other men turned a coin — someone else had recently *filed* around it. Had entered the shipment into the current reconciliation ledger with a small, neat notation: *weight variance noted; source documentation pending.* No alarm raised. No correction made. Just a flag planted in the margin, quiet as a snowdrop, saying *I know this is here.*

That was not the work of a thief. Thieves smoothed things flat. That was the work of someone standing very still beside a hole in the ice, hoping nobody would look down.

Brandt had looked down. He had looked down for four days now, and the name that kept surfacing, patient as a drowned thing, was Nystrom.

* * *

At half past eight, Harbormaster Gudrun Peale arrived at the counting house carrying two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who had spent the night arguing with herself and lost.

"Mr. Brandt." She set one cup on his desk with the care of someone defusing ordnance. "You wanted the berth registry."

"I did." He drew it toward him but did not open it, which was a technique. People talked to fill silences the way water filled cracks, and Gudrun Peale, he had observed, had more cracks than most. "Thank you. I understand there is exactly one passenger berth available on the supply ship when she runs south."

"That's right. Annual. Always has been." She sat across from him without being asked, which meant she wanted something, which meant the conversation would be efficient whether either of them enjoyed it or not. "Company policy says it goes by seniority and need. In practice—" she spread her hands, "—in practice it goes to whoever wants it loudest and longest."

"And who wants it loudest this year?"

Peale looked at the window. At the fog. At her own coffee. Finally, with the reluctance of a woman handing over evidence against a friend: "There's a petition in. Tam Nystrom. Sick boy, sixteen. Doctor Kessler's written that the climate here will kill him inside two years if he doesn't get south. His sister's put in the claim on his behalf — Sable Nystrom. Your clerk."

"*My* clerk," Brandt said pleasantly, "is the company's clerk. As is her indenture." He opened the registry at last and ran one finger down the entries. Sable Nystrom, for Tam Nystrom. The handwriting was small, exact, and — he permitted himself the smallest private smile — beautifully ruled. Even her desperation kept its margins tidy. "Tell me, Harbormaster. If the claim were revoked, would there be another applicant?"

"There's always another applicant. There's a fisherman whose wife's expecting twins, there's old Marta Voss who swears the damp's getting into her bones, there's—"

"Anyone with money?"

Peale's mouth thinned. "Everyone in this town has money the way a stone has blood, Mr. Brandt. But yes. Oskar Lund wants it bad enough that he's asked me twice, and Oskar doesn't ask twice about anything he isn't prepared to pay for."

"Mm." Brandt closed the registry. Outside, the harbor horn sounded its low, mournful note, the sound Greywater made instead of conversation. "Harbormaster, I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to consider it carefully before you answer, because your answer will determine how much paperwork you do this winter. Has anyone approached you regarding the berth using improper influence?"

"You're asking if somebody's bribed me."

"I'm asking if somebody has *attempted* to bribe you. Whether they succeeded is between you and your conscience, which I understand is a local organ of variable reliability."

Gudrun Peale stared at him for a long moment, and then she laughed — a short, bark-like sound, entirely without humor, aimed at him rather than with him. "You know what you are, Mr. Brandt? You're a man who's never once in his life been cold, wet, or hungry, telling people who've been all three what they owe."

"On the contrary," Brandt said, sipping his coffee, which was terrible and therefore authentic, "I am precisely the man who tells people what they owe. It is my entire profession, and I am extremely good at it." He set the cup down. "Here is what I know, Harbormaster, and then I'll let you go. Seven years ago, a shipment of pearls left this weighing house under documents signed by a man named Anders Nystrom. Those documents are forged — I have compared them against eleven genuine samples of his hand, and I'd stake my pension on it, which is considerable, because I have never once paid for lunch. The same week those documents were signed, Anders Nystrom's boat sank in the outer channel, and Anders Nystrom was presumed dead, and his children were taken into indenture against his debts. Which means the company absorbed a loss, and then recovered it from a girl of nine years old, and everyone in Greywater agreed this was simply weather."

Peale said nothing. Her knuckles had gone white around her cup.

"Now," Brandt continued, in the tone of a man reading aloud from a pleasant pamphlet, "last month, a stranger arrives in town asking after the Nystrom family. Within days, my own clerk — the daughter of the dead man, the keeper of the ledgers where the forged shipment lives — files a curious little annotation around exactly that shipment. And within the fortnight, that same clerk claims the single berth south, for a brother whose illness, while I don't doubt it, provides a motive so sympathetic that no harbormaster in the world would think to question it." He folded his hands. "I am not saying any of these things are connected. I am an auditor. I don't say things. I *note* things. But I note that when a family that has spent seven years being perfectly invisible suddenly becomes visible in three places at once, the most likely explanation is that something moved underneath them."

"You think the father's alive," Peale said flatly.

"I think," said Brandt, "that the sea around Greywater has swallowed a great many men, and given back a great many empty boats, and that drowning is the only crime in this territory with a hundred percent acquittal rate." He rose, buttoned his coat, and smiled the smile that nineteen years of auditing had polished to the smoothness of a river stone. "Revoking a berth claim requires cause. So let us find some. I'll want a copy of the claim petition, the doctor's certificate, and — purely as a matter of routine — a list of every document Sable Nystrom has touched in the last sixty days."

"And if I say no?"

Brandt considered her with something adjacent to respect. "Then I write a letter to the regional office describing this conversation, and you spend February explaining yourself to a man named Hobb, who makes me look like a birthday party. You may take until noon to decide. Most people take less."

She took eleven minutes.

* * *

By ten o'clock, Brandt had what he needed, and by eleven he had done with it what he always did with raw material: nothing dramatic. Drama was for people who lacked patience. Instead he sat in the counting house among the smell of lamp oil and cold tea and built his case the way a careful mason builds a wall — one indistinguishable brick at a time, each one load-bearing, none of them interesting.

Sable Nystrom had touched, in sixty days: the general reconciliation ledger (expected); the payroll abstracts (expected); the diving-grounds lease renewals (expected); and the archived shipping manifests for the years 1890 through 1893 (not expected). Nobody consulted seven-year-old manifests out of curiosity. Curiosity did not survive clerical work; he knew this from personal experience, having killed his own at approximately age twelve. Somebody had gone looking into those archives for a *reason*, and the reason had arrived in town wearing travel-stained boots and asking questions with unsettling precision.

The stranger. Ilse Varric. Brandt had her lodging register entry copied already — the Seagull's Rest, room four, paid weekly, occupation listed as "*companion*" with no indication of companion to whom, which was either evasion or honesty so complete it circled around to suspicion. He had watched her, once, from the window of the chandlery: a tall woman moving through the fog like a ship that had forgotten its destination, pausing at the weighing house door for a full minute before going in. People did not pause a full minute before entering buildings unless the building contained something they feared or something they wanted. Preferably both.

And now there was the matter of the strongbox. This had come to him yesterday evening, in the bath, in the manner of his best ideas, which preferred to arrive while he was defenseless. Old Halvard Skarre — retired diver, seventy-one, deaf in one ear and voluble in neither — had been seen at low tide walking the old pearl beds with a grappling line, which was odd, because Halvard Skarre had not dived since the McKinley administration and had sworn publicly and repeatedly that the beds were fished out and his knees were finished. When Brandt had mentioned this to the harbormaster this morning, casually, in passing, the way one mentions weather — Peale had gone a particular shade of grey and said, "Halvard does odd jobs for people," and then had looked at her own hands.

Odd jobs. For people. At the exact stretch of seabed where a fleeing debtor might reasonably be expected to sink anything he couldn't carry.

Brandt did not yet know what was in the strongbox. He knew only that it existed, that it had been underwater for seven years, that it was now — judging by the fresh scrape-marks he intended to confirm on the sea floor himself, or rather by sending young Mr. Quill of the company boat crew down to confirm, Brandt did not dive, Brandt had read accounts of diving and found them excessive — that it was now *above* water. And that whatever it contained was important enough to make a retired man lie about his knees.

Documents, almost certainly. Documents were the cargo of choice for men in Anders Nystrom's position. Leverage, insurance, a pistol made of paper. Perhaps proof of the forged shipments. Perhaps — and here Brandt allowed himself, briefly, luxuriously, to imagine it — perhaps something worse for Meridian than forgery. Something with a director's signature on it. Something that would make the regional office very grateful to whichever diligent auditor secured it first.

He did not want to ruin Meridian. He wanted to be *indispensable* to Meridian, which was the same ambition wearing better clothes.

But first: the berth. Because the berth was the hinge on which everything swung. If Sable Nystrom got that berth for her brother, she gained leverage — a sick child aboard the last ship south was a hostage no auditor in the world could politely retrieve. And if she intended to run herself, or to pass something along, or to meet someone at the northern camps before the sailing date — and the northern fishing camps were, he noted in the margin of his mind, exactly where a resurrected dead man would live — then the berth was also her timetable. Take away the berth and you did not merely take away an escape. You took away the clock, and forced her to improvise, and improvisation was where careful people became legible.

He wrote the revocation order himself, in his beautiful, merciless hand:

*Whereas the claim lodged by Sable Nystrom on behalf of Tam Nystrom to the single southbound passenger berth has been brought to the attention of this office; and whereas questions have arisen concerning the standing of the Nystrom indenture with the Meridian Trading Company, said indenture being collateral against outstanding debts of record; and whereas no claimant may dispose of or benefit from company-held property while such debts remain unresolved; Now therefore the said claim is SUSPENDED pending audit, effective immediately, by order of E. Brandt, Auditor, Meridian Trading Company.*

*Suspended*. Not revoked. Revocation invited appeal, tears, sympathy, the whole unruly circus of public feeling. Suspension invited only waiting — and waiting, done correctly, was a form of pressure that left no bruises and therefore no evidence. Let her sit in the fog and watch the calendar shorten. Let her feel the ice close in from the north and the audit close in from the south, and discover, as all his subjects eventually discovered, that the walls of a trap need not move at all. They need only exist, and be patient, and let the trapped do the moving.

* * *

He delivered the order to the harbormaster's office personally, because a man should witness the faces of the people whose lives he rearranged; it was the only honest currency most of them ever paid him.

Peale read it standing, though there was a chair available. "This will break that boy's mother's heart," she said. "If she were alive. It broke it once already, they say, when the father drowned."

"The father," Brandt said mildly, "may prove to be the least drowned man on this coast. But that is speculation, Harbormaster, and I deal in records." He tapped the paper. "You'll post this where the claimant will see it. Today."

"She works fifty feet from your office."

"All the better. Proximity breeds compliance." He paused at the door, struck by a thought that was not quite professional and not quite otherwise, and turned back. "A question, off the record, which I will deny having asked. The daughter. Seven years in these ledgers. What sort of clerk is she?"

Peale's jaw worked. Whatever she was deciding, she decided it slowly, and it cost her. "Best I've ever seen," she said finally. "Catches things the rest of us miss. Never puts a foot wrong. Never asks for anything. Seven years, and I couldn't tell you one true thing about her that isn't written in a book somewhere."

"Yes," said Brandt softly. "I'd noticed that too." And he had — it was the thing about Sable Nystrom that had snagged his attention before the discrepancy ever had, the way a single unmoving figure snags the eye in a crowd. Everyone in Greywater leaked. They leaked grievances, gossip, debts, secrets, soup recipes. The whole town was a colander of loose talk, and through the middle of it walked this woman who shed *nothing*. Who filed her annotations and kept her head down and made herself, year upon year, a perfect nullity in the record. He had audited embezzlers with louder shoes, smugglers with shakier hands, and one genuinely memorable arsonist who cried at hymns. None of them frightened him. But a person built entirely of silence — a silence maintained for seven years at the cost of everything, including apparently her entire personality — that was a silence with architecture behind it. That was a silence with a *purpose*.

People assumed he hunted thieves. He didn't, particularly. Thieves were common as gulls. What Elias Brandt hunted, what he had crossed three territories and endured the coffee of four towns to hunt, was the exception — the one entry in ten thousand that did not resolve, the one account that balanced too well, the one quiet life that was quiet *on purpose*. Somewhere beneath the grey water of this town was a truth with teeth in it, and a drowned man who might not be drowned, and a strongbox scraped clean of barnacles, and a girl who had spent her whole inheritance of invisibility in the space of a fortnight because something had finally mattered enough to spend it on.

Loyalty, he thought, turning up his collar against the fog as he stepped out onto the quay. That was the word for it, probably. Loyalty was what it cost. In his experience loyalty was the only commodity on earth that appreciated with use — spend it and it grew, hoard it and it rotted — which was why it ruined more careful plans than greed ever had. Greed he could model to the penny. Loyalty made people do arithmetic with their hearts, and heart-arithmetic never balanced, and watching it fail was, if he were honest with himself, the closest thing to pleasure his profession afforded.

Below him, on the grey swell, the supply ship *Providence* rode at anchor, her one passenger berth empty as a held breath. Eleven days to sailing, weather permitting, and the barometer — he had checked it twice — was falling like a confession.

Behind him, in the weighing house, a bell rang: the noon mark, divers coming in with their baskets, the daily tide of small honest numbers. And threading through it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a page being torn from a ledger — sharp, deliberate, final — followed by a silence so total that even the gulls seemed to lean in to listen.

Brandt stood very still on the quay, listening to that silence the way other men listened to music, and smiled.

"Well," he murmured to the fog, to the harbor, to the patient arithmetic of the world. "Now we're getting somewhere."
