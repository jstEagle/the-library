## 14. The Harbor at Night

The rowboat was not hers. That was the first lie Sable told herself that evening—that she had simply borrowed it, the way one borrows a ladder or a cup of sugar, with every intention of returning it in good order.

In truth she had stolen it from Kessel's boatyard, slipping the oarlocks free of their chain with a length of wire and a prayer to no particular saint. The boat was a dory, tarred and patched, named *Patience* in flaking paint on the stern, which Sable felt was either an instruction or an accusation.

She rowed out past the breakwater because she could not bear to be inside. The weighing house smelled of ink and cold tea and Ilse's absence—Ilse had taken a room at the Salt House inn two nights ago, after the confession, after Sable had stood in her own doorway with her arms crossed like a customs barrier and said, *You knew. All along, you knew what he wanted from me, and you let me hand it to you.*

*Yes,* Ilse had said. Not sorry enough. Not sorry at all, really, if honesty were measured in tone. And then she had gone, taking the fog with her, or so it seemed, so complete was the grayness left behind.

Now Sable pulled at the oars and let Greywater shrink behind her into its usual smear of lamplight and chimney smoke. The harbor at night was a different country. The company warehouses went soft-edged and harmless, mere suggestions. The Meridian flag on the counting house—the one Brandt had ordered rehung, fresh bunting for the audit season—hung dead still in the windless air, and Sable took a small, private satisfaction in imagining it as a drowned thing, something fished up too late.

Her hands hurt. She looked down at them in the dark, at the rope burns from hauling up the strongbox, at the blisters from the oars. Her father's hands had looked like that once, when she was small—split at the knuckles, salt-cured—and she used to think those hands were the most honest things about him.

That was the trouble with fathers. They taught you everything they knew, and some of it was how to be lied to well.

A sound came across the water. Oars—not hers. She stopped rowing and let *Patience* drift, listening, heart doing arithmetic again, that hateful habit. Then out of the fog bank off Gull Rock came a second boat, low and steady, and sitting in it, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big for her frame, rowing with the unhurried competence of someone who had spent years pulling nets through northern water—

"Ilse," Sable said. Her voice came out smaller than intended.

"You're stealing a boat," Ilse observed. She let her own craft glide alongside, gunwale to gunwale, close enough that Sable could see the fog damp in her pale hair. "Kessel's, by the patches. He'll notice by morning. He notices everything except his own accounts."

"Go away."

"I've been going away for two days." Ilse shipped her oars. "It's very restful. I recommend it."

"I mean it. I don't—" Sable gripped the oars tighter. "I can't look at you right now. Every time I do, I see him writing letters through you. *Bring me the strongbox.* You were his errand. The whole time, you were—"

"The whole time," Ilse said quietly, "I was dying. And then I met you, and I was dying *here*, which is different."

Sable opened her mouth and found nothing in it. This was Ilse's particular cruelty and particular gift: she never argued the point you were making. She simply set down a truer one beside it and waited for you to pick it up.

"That's not fair," Sable managed.

"No," Ilse agreed. "Most of this isn't. Your father isn't fair. Meridian isn't fair. The lung-rot certainly isn't fair—it's killed half the camp at Sker Point and it's working on me at its leisure. Fairness is a luxury good, Sable. Like pearls. We both know who harvests those and who profits."

The fog closed around them. Somewhere behind, a bell buoy clanked its slow idiot rhythm. Sable became aware that she was crying, and that Ilse was watching her cry, and that being watched by Ilse Varric did not feel like being seen through, as she'd feared all these weeks, but seen *into*—which was worse and better in roughly equal measure.

"Why are you here?" Sable asked finally. "Truly. Not the errand. Not his letters. Why row out into the fog after a woman who told you to go away?"

Ilse was quiet long enough that Sable counted six passes of the bell buoy. When she spoke, she spoke the way she did everything—with unsettling precision.

"When your father asked me to come south, he gave me three tasks. Deliver the letter. Find the strongbox if I could. And take the berth on the last ship back north, because he doesn't trust anyone else's hands." She held up fingers, thin as winter twigs. "I delivered the letter. The strongbox—you took that out from under both our noses, which I confess I admired even while it was ruining my errand. And the berth." She lowered her hand. "I could have sailed north ten days ago. There was room on the cod boat. Captain Ostergaard owed me and would have said nothing. I stood on the dock with my ticket and my cough and I thought: *Ilse, you foolish woman, go home and die where the light is the kind you know.*"

"And?"

"And I bought a candle instead. At the chandler's on Rope Walk. A tallow one, bad quality, overpriced—I intend to complain to whoever keeps that shop's books, since apparently she's very thorough." A ghost of a smile. "And I carried it home to the Salt House and I lit it and I sat there and I understood that I had not stayed in Greywater for Eirik Nystrom. I don't owe him that much. I may not even like him that much; seven years is a long time to watch a man drink himself brave. I stayed for a clerk who files false weights rather than make waves and then steals a boat in the dark because she cannot stand being honest even with herself indoors."

"That's not—" Sable started, and stopped, because there was no end to that sentence that survived inspection.

"It is," Ilse said. "And I am telling you now, in a stolen boat, in the fog, because if I wait for a better moment I will run out of moments. My lungs keep their own ledger, and it does not balance in my favor." She leaned forward, forearms on her knees, and her voice dropped to something almost shy, which on Ilse was more startling than shouting. "I stayed for you, Sable Nystrom. Not for the errand. For the way you bite your lip before you write a number you hate. For the fact that you dove the beds alone rather than ask anyone's permission. I have crossed ice fields that frightened me less than saying this."

The bell buoy clanked. A gull somewhere complained about the economy.

"Oh," said Sable.

"*Oh,*" Ilse repeated. "Seven words of courage and I get *oh*."

"No, I—give me a moment, I'm not practiced at this. History happens to other people, remember? Including—" Sable gestured vaguely at the space between them, at the fog, at the whole unbearable tender mess of it. "*This.*"

"Well," said Ilse, settling back with the air of a woman prepared to wait out a tide, "practice."

Sable looked at her—at the coat too big for her, which Sable now recognized with a lurch as Tam's old oilskin, the one from before he grew; Ilse must have charmed it out of him during one of her visits to the sickroom. She looked at the hands that had carried a dead man's letters seven hundred miles. She thought about the arithmetic she hated—two claimants, one berth—and realized with something like vertigo that she had been doing sums with the wrong numbers entirely. Everyone kept asking her to divide. Nobody had suggested adding.

"I stayed invisible for twenty-three years," Sable said slowly, "because I believed that was safety. And then you walked into the weighing house and asked about my family with that terrible precision of yours, and I lied to you, and you saw straight through it, and instead of reporting me or leaving you just... put a letter on the counter and came back the next day. And the day after that." She swallowed. "I'm angry at you. I want to be clear about that. I'm going to stay angry at you for some time. Possibly weeks."

"Noted."

"But I don't want you on a boat going north without me in it. Or—" she corrected herself, hearing the sentence as it left her, "I don't want to be in Greywater while you're anywhere else. Which is a different sentence, and harder to say, and I've said it now, so."

Ilse was silent. In the fog-light her face did something complicated and unguarded, the mask of dry precision cracking along a seam Sable suspected very few people had ever been shown.

"There it is," Ilse said softly. "There's the diver's daughter."

"Don't make it strange."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

They laughed—or Sable did, a wet, cracked laugh that turned halfway into something else, and then Ilse's boat bumped against *Patience*'s hull and Sable reached across the gap without deciding to, and Ilse's hand closed around hers, cold fingers, strong grip, steady as a mooring line. Neither of them said anything grand. It wasn't a grand sort of moment. It was a fog-bound harbor at midnight, two women in stolen and borrowed boats, holding on in the smallest possible way, which somehow held more than anything grand could have.

* * *

They rafted the boats together off Gull Rock, sharing a blanket Ilse had had the foresight to bring, and passed a flask of coffee gone lukewarm and a heel of bread, and made plans the way people make plans in the dark: plainly, and with more courage than daylight would allow.

"He's at Sker Point," Ilse said. "The camp, not the village proper—your father wouldn't go where there's a company man. There's a shack above the drying racks. He'll be there until the ice closes the strait, and after that nobody goes in or out till thaw."

"The supply ship sails in nine days. Six, now—they moved it up, you heard."

"I heard. Which means we have five days to reach Sker Point overland, if the weather holds, and the weather will not hold." Ilse said it without drama, the way she'd say *the tide comes in twice daily*. "There's the mail trail to Fenn's Landing, then the coast path. Two days' hard walking if we're lucky. Three if the fog follows us, and fog has been following you all year, from what I can tell."

"We?" Sable raised an eyebrow. "You can barely climb the stairs at the Salt House without stopping twice."

"I can row, I can walk, and I can be disagreeable at great length, which historically wears down obstacles." Ilse's voice flattened. "Sable. Listen to me. Your father sent me to retrieve the strongbox *for him*. Whatever's in it, he wants it in his hands before any ship sails, because it's the only thing standing between him and Brandt. If we go north and simply tell him where we've hidden it, we gain nothing—we've just moved his leverage closer to him. But if we go north and we're standing between him and the berth..."

"Then he has to choose," Sable said. "He hates choosing. He's spent seven years avoiding it."

"He's spent seven years letting other people pay for his choices. Which is nearly the same thing, but worse." Ilse pulled the blanket higher against the damp. "Brandt knows the strongbox existed. He doesn't know it's empty of value now—or nearly empty. He thinks your father holds the proof that ruins Meridian, and he'll trade anything for it. A berth. An indenture. Silence. So the question isn't whether your father wants passage. The question is what we want from *him*, for once."

Sable stared out at the black water. Somewhere under it lay the pearl beds her father had taught her to read, the ridges and shadows, the patience of it. *Let the sea give you the pearl,* he'd say. *Never grab. Grabbing breaks them.*

"What do we want," she repeated. She turned it over like a stone in her palm. "I want Tam on that ship. That's first and last and everything in between. I want Brandt to have nothing left to hold over any of us. And I want—" her jaw tightened, "—I want my father to look at me and say it. Not to you, not to God, not to the fog. To me. That he let us believe he was dead. That he let them bind me to his debt. I want him to say it out loud, and then I want to decide, freely, what he gets. That's not forgiveness. That's just... an honest entry."

"Honest entries," Ilse murmured. "Is that what we're calling it now?"

"It's what I'm calling it. You may call it whatever you like; you called stealing my boat 'borrowing with ambition.'"

"I called it no such thing. Though it's a fine phrase. Keep it for the book you'll write someday."

"I don't write books. I write columns of figures."

"Mm," said Ilse, in the tone of a woman filing a prediction away for later. Then, more seriously: "Five days. We leave at first light, before the harbormaster's office opens and before Brandt's clerks start their rounds. Can you get word to Tam?"

"I'll leave him a note. He'll be furious."

"He's fourteen. Fury is his native language lately. Besides—" gently, "—he already knows you'd move mountains for him. Let him know you'd also walk around them."

Sable looked at their boats, tied gunwale to gunwale, drifting together on the swell. *Patience*, and Ilse's little skiff whose name she couldn't read in the dark.

"There's a problem," she said. "Two women, one boat apiece, forty miles of coast path, and a fugitive at the end of it. Even if everything goes perfectly—which, I should note for the record, nothing in this entire year has gone remotely near perfectly—what exactly do we do when we find him? He won't come quietly. He'll bargain. He always bargains."

"Then we out-bargain him," Ilse said. "We are, between us, the two best bookkeepers in Greywater. We know precisely what he owes."

The fog was thinning toward the east, though dawn was hours off yet. Sable found she was shivering, and found further that Ilse had noticed, and that the blanket had migrated, without discussion, to cover them both. She let it.

"One condition," Sable said. "If we do this—if we walk north and face him—when it's done, when the ship sails and Brandt leaves and the ice closes, you and I are having a conversation about the future. A real one. With plans in it. I've spent my whole life reacting to other people's ledgers. I'm done. From here forward, I write my own."

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then she said, "First entry?"

"First entry."

"Then write this one down," Ilse said, and leaned her shoulder against Sable's, and the fog rolled in around them like a page closing, "and date it tonight."

Out beyond Gull Rock, faint through the thinning gray, came the deep horn of a ship at anchor—the supply vessel, early, impatient, its whistle carrying over the water like a countdown. Five days. Sable tightened her grip on the oars, and on Ilse's hand, and began, quietly, to row for shore.
