## 15. Northward

The boat Ilse had promised turned out to be a fishing smack named *Patience*, which Sable took as either a joke or a warning. Her money was on both.

"She leaks a little," the owner said, scratching under his oilskin hood, "but only below the waterline."

"That's where boats do most of their leaking," Ilse observed.

"See? She understands." He handed over the tiller like a man surrendering a difficult child, and then, because Greywater was Greywater and everyone knew everyone's business by breakfast, he pressed a sack of dried cod into Sable's hands and refused payment. "For your brother," he said, in the voice people used for Tam now—the soft, careful voice that made her want to scream into the harbor fog. "Tell him the harbor's thinking of him."

The first two days were merely miserable. The third was brutal.

A storm came down off the northern ice like something with a grudge, and Sable spent it bailing with a bucket while Ilse lashed herself to the wheel and shouted course corrections over the wind. Sable's hands went numb past feeling, then numb past pain, then to some third country she had no name for. At some point—she would never be sure when—a wave came aboard and took the bucket with it.

"We've lost the bucket!" she screamed.

Ilse, plastered gray with spray, grinned at her like this was excellent news. "Then bail with your hat!"

"My hat is on my head!"

"Then your priorities are sound! Keep them!"

Sable laughed—actually laughed, a wild cracked sound torn out of her—and thought: *this is what I was invisible for seven years to protect? This.* And then she thought: *no. This is what I was hiding from. There's a difference, and I finally know which side of it I'm standing on.*

* * *

They made the last stretch of coast under a sky the color of old pewter. The fishing camps appeared the way everything up here appeared: reluctantly, out of weather, as if the land itself had agreed to exist only under protest. A scatter of huts on stilts above a black shingle beach, smoke rising thin and defeated from stovepipes, racks of split fish hanging like laundry left out too long.

The smell hit first. Salt and fish and tar and beneath it all something sweeter and worse—the smell Sable had learned to dread in Tam's room, the smell of lungs not doing their work.

"The rot," Ilse said quietly. She had gone still at the rail, watching the camp come closer with an expression Sable couldn't read. "Half these men have it. The company knows. They send new boats every year because new divers are cheaper than clean water."

Sable looked at her—at the hollows under Ilse's cheekbones that she had been telling herself were just the wind, just the journey—and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't break something open on a rocking deck forty miles from anywhere.

Ilse caught the look anyway. She always caught the look. "Later," she said. "Meet your father first. He's earned a proper audience."

There it was again—that edge under the tenderness, that small cold blade Ilse carried even now. Sable filed it away with all the other things she was keeping for later. Later was getting crowded.

* * *

She found him mending nets outside the third hut from the end.

She knew him by his hands before his face. Seven years, and the hands were unchanged—broad-knuckled, quick, the hands that had taught her to tie a bowline before she could write her own name, the hands that had held her up in five feet of black water and told her to breathe through her fear the way you breathe through a regulator: slow, deliberate, trusting the equipment more than the sea.

The face attached to those hands was another matter.

He was thinner. Gray through the beard. He coughed between knots—not Tam's wet, drowning cough but a dry rattle, the sound of a man spending himself in installments. When he looked up and saw her standing there, his face did something complicated: joy arriving, then being audited, then being approved for release in carefully measured amounts.

"Sable." He said it like a word in a language he was rusty at.

"Father."

He set down the net. He didn't come to her. That was the thing she noticed, later, when she replayed it in the dark of the hut they gave her—he didn't cross the distance. He waited for her to cross it, and she did, because she was his daughter, because seven years of grief does not dissolve in one moment no matter how much you want it to, and she let him hold her, and he felt like a coat she'd outgrown: familiar, and wrong-sized, and smelling of someone else's life.

"You got my letters," he said into her hair.

"Ilse brought them."

"And she came back with you." He pulled away and looked past her shoulder, toward the beach, where Ilse stood talking to a fisherman, and something moved across his face that Sable catalogued without understanding yet. "Good. Good girl. You always were."

"I'm not a girl."

"No." He studied her, and for one moment something almost honest surfaced in him—something that looked like regret, or its cousin. "No, you're not. Your mother would—" He stopped. He always stopped there. Even before the drowning-that-wasn't, he had never been able to finish sentences about her mother.

"Tam is dying," Sable said, because she had crossed an ocean of cold water and she was not going to spend it on pleasantries.

His face closed. Not all the way—just enough. Just the shutters coming down over the windows, quick and practiced, and Sable watched it happen and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water filling her ears, that she had seen him do this before. All her childhood. Every time the ledgers came home short. Every time her mother asked about money.

"The doctor says warm climate or—" She made herself finish. "Or a winter like this one kills him."

"So we get him south." His eyes had already gone bright with it, already working the problem, and for one treacherous heartbeat Sable felt relief—*he'll help, we'll manage it together*—before she heard what was underneath. "You heard about the berth, then. On the supply ship. One berth, and Brandt's men crawling over every dock in Greywater deciding who's connected enough." He laughed, a dry scrape. "Your auditor friend. He's thorough. I'll say that for Meridian—they don't hire lazy vultures."

"You know about Brandt?"

"He wrote to me." Her father smiled, and there was pride in it, actual pride, and Sable felt the ground tilt under her all over again, the way it had tilted in the weighing house with a dead man's handwriting folded on the counter. "Offered me terms. Passage south, debts forgiven, in exchange for certain documents in my possession. The man negotiates like a gentleman and robs you like a professional." He waved a hand. "I told him nothing, naturally."

*Naturally.* As if honesty were a strategy rather than a value. As if the question of whether to tell the truth had simply never occurred to him as separate from the question of what it cost.

"Father." She kept her voice level. Diver's breathing. Slow, deliberate. "Where is the strongbox?"

And there it was. The whole conversation, revealed at last as having been about that from the moment she stepped onto the shingle—maybe from the moment he wrote the first letter seven years ago. Maybe from before. Maybe her whole life had been conducted at a slight angle to some transaction she wasn't shown the books for.

"It's safe," he said.

"It's mine. I dove for it. In the old beds. In November water." She let him look at her hands, still scabbed at the knuckles, still remembering the cold. "You sent a sick woman across the strait to deliver your letters and fetch your box, and you sent your daughter down after it, and you're sitting here mending nets like a man who's owed something."

"I am owed something." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Seven years I've paid, Sable. Seven years in these camps eating fish heads and breathing other men's rot, all because I saw a chance to get clear of Meridian and took it. Do you know what they are? What they've always been? They owned your mother's grave plot. Did you know that? They own the church bell, the school slate, the very fog, near enough. I took one thing back from them—one box—and if it costs me my children's opinion of me, then that's a price I can pay, because unlike them I pay my debts."

"You didn't pay your debts," Sable said quietly. "I paid them. My contract. My indenture. Tam's whole childhood spent coughing in company housing because you sank a box in the bay and vanished." She heard her own voice go strange and calm, the way it went over numbers that wouldn't balance. "You let us mourn you. Do you understand that part? Tam lit a candle for you every year. Every year, Father. He's fourteen now and he found out last week his father let him grieve for nothing, and he didn't cry. He got angry. He's still angry. I don't know how to fix that, and neither do you, and no berth south is going to carry it away."

Her father stood very still among his nets. For a moment—she would remember this moment, too, and examine it later, looking for what she'd missed—his face was entirely unguarded, and what was underneath the guards was not remorse. It was calculation wearing remorse's clothing, badly.

"There's a ship," he said. "One berth. I need to be on it, and I need that box, because the box is the only leverage any of us has against Brandt. Give me both and I'll get us all south, I swear it—"

"On the ship's single berth," Sable said. "All of us. Father. It carries one."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind the hut a man coughed the long rattling cough, and another answered it, the camp calling back and forth to itself like sick birds.

Her father looked at her, and Sable watched him do the arithmetic. She recognized the look because she'd worn it herself a hundred times over a ledger line that wouldn't close—except his figures were people, and hers had only ever been pearls and freight, and the difference between those two kinds of arithmetic was, she understood suddenly, the entire difference between them.

He reached the answer. She watched him reach it.

"You," he began, in the reasonable voice, the negotiating voice, the voice that had signed contracts with her life before she was old enough to hold a pen—

"Don't," Sable said.

"—don't be hasty. If it's the boy, fine, the boy, but the boy needs someone with him, someone who can negotiate treatment, someone who knows the company doctors—"

"I said *don't.*"

She hadn't raised her voice. That was the remarkable thing. Seven years of keeping her head down and her ledgers clean, and when the moment came she didn't shout at all; she spoke the way she spoke to the harbormaster, the way she'd speak to a discrepancy: clearly, precisely, with the full weight of the figure behind it.

"You will not use my brother as your argument. You will not stand there and perform concern for him as a way of getting yourself onto that ship. I have spent seven years learning to read books, Father. Real ones, and the kind Meridian keeps. And I have just spent four days learning to read you. You want the berth. You want the box. Everything else—the grief, the family, the *good girl*—is ballast you'll jettison the moment the water gets rough. I know exactly what that looks like. I grew up inside it."

Her father's mouth worked. Whatever he saw in her face made him take half a step back, and Sable realized she had advanced without noticing, and that she was taller than him now, and had been for years, and he had never once looked up at her until this minute.

"You sound like your mother," he said finally, and it was meant to wound, and it did, and she let it, because wounds were honest in a way he apparently could not afford to be.

"Good," Sable said. "Someone in this family should."

She turned and walked down the shingle toward the waterline, where Ilse stood waiting against a sky going bruise-dark, her silhouette thin and upright and steady as a mooring post. Behind Sable, her father called her name once—just once, and not in anger. In something smaller than anger. In the voice of a man discovering that the debt he most feared had already been called, and that he was the one who owed.

Sable did not turn around.

Some entries, she thought, you make in the book and some entries you make in the body, and this one she was making in both: *Seen clearly. Written down. Closed.*

Ahead of her, Ilse pushed off from the post she leaned against and started toward her, and Sable saw that she was carrying a lantern against the early dark, and that her breath was coming shorter than the walk justified, and that she was smiling anyway.

Behind them both, up the beach, a light kindled in the third hut from the end, and a figure stood in the doorway watching them go—and beside it, half-hidden in the shadow of the woodpile, a second figure that had not been there an hour ago, wearing a coat cut far too well for a fishing camp, and holding a letter sealed with the Meridian crest.

Sable saw none of it. But Ilse, glancing back once, went very still—and did not say a word.
