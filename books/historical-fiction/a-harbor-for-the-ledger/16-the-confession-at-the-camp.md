## 16. The Confession at the Camp

Erik Nystrom had learned, in seven years of exile, to measure the day by small dishonesties.

The first lie came at dawn, when he told his lungs the air was good. The second at noon, when he told the camp cook that yes, the herring had come in fine this season, when in truth they had come in thin as communion wafers and twice as bitter. The third came every evening, when he told himself that tomorrow he would feel better. He had told himself that one for two thousand five hundred and some-odd days, and it had never once been true, which by any honest accounting made it the most successful lie of his life.

He was sitting on an overturned crate outside his hut—the last structure in Camp Verren still standing straight enough to be called a hut—when the girl came up the shingle path through the fog.

He knew her before he knew her. That was the strange part. Seven years, and a body changes: Sable had been all elbows and salt-stiff braids the last time he'd seen her, thirteen years old and furious with him for dying. The woman who walked toward him now wore his own careful posture, shoulders drawn in like a man passing through a room where something valuable might break. She carried herself the way he used to carry himself across the Meridian weighing floor, back when the ledgers were clean and so, he'd believed, was he.

Beside her walked Ilse Varric, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big, moving with the deliberate economy of someone spending each step from a purse that would not refill.

Erik stood. His knees objected. Everything objected; his body had become a committee of objections chaired by his chest.

"Sable," he said.

Her face did what his face was doing, he suspected—which was nothing. Two people trained in the same school of stillness, facing each other across thirty feet of gravel and seven years of salt water.

"You're shorter than I remembered," she said. "Or I'm taller."

"You're taller." His voice cracked on the second word. He covered it with a cough, which was easy, because coughing had become his native language. "You've got your mother's way of stating facts like accusations."

"She's dead, Da."

"I know." He had known it for four years, carried north to him by a fish buyer with a talent for delivering bad news the way other men delivered cod—gutted, packed, no ceremony. "I know."

* * *

The hut smelled of paraffin, wet wool, and the particular sweetness of a man who has stopped believing in fresh air. There was one chair, which Erik offered, and Sable took, and Ilse declined with a shake of her head, settling instead onto the bunk's edge near the little stove, where she could watch them both without appearing to. Erik recognized that too. He'd taught that trick to no one, and yet here it was, sitting in his daughter like a pearl in an oyster.

"You got my letters," he said.

"Ilse brought them. Eventually." Sable set a canvas bag on the floor between her feet and did not explain it. "Seven years, Da. You couldn't have sent word before now?"

"I sent word." He heard how it sounded even before she flinched. "No. That's not—I wrote letters. Eleven of them. Twelve. I lost count. None of them said anything worth the stamp, if we'd had stamps, or the boat, since we didn't. What was I going to write? *Dear Sable, I am not drowned, I am merely a coward in a northern fishing camp, please send regards to everyone I ruined.*" He laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough went on long enough that Ilse rose halfway off the bunk, and he waved her down with two fingers. "I'm fine," he lied. Fourth lie of the day. He was ahead of schedule.

"You let us believe you were dead," Sable said. When she said it, her voice didn't rise at all. It went flat and level as a frozen pond, and Erik thought: *she walks on things like that. Carefully. That's how she survived you.*

"Yes," he said.

"For seven years."

"Yes."

"Tam was nine."

"I know how old Tam was." The words came out harder than he intended, and he watched her take the hardness and file it away somewhere, the way she must have filed everything for years—every debt, every slight, every underweight crate of pearls she'd signed her name beneath. He had heard about that. Ilse had told him, on the long road south, about the clerk at the Meridian weighing house with the spotless books and the invisible life, and he had felt a pride so enormous and so shameful that he hadn't been able to look at her for half a day.

"I want to tell it properly," he said. "Not the version in the letters. Not the version I'd tell a magistrate. The true one, which is worse than either, and shorter, which is the only mercy in it."

Sable folded her hands in her lap. Waiting. God, she was good at waiting. Where had she learned that? From him, probably. From watching him wait out customers and creditors and her mother's silences.

"The shipment," he began. "The one in the spring of 'ninety. You'll have seen the records, or you will have. Four hundred weight of pearls entered as six hundred and ten."

"Two hundred and ten short," Sable said. "I filed the intake myself, the year after. The numbers never reconciled."

"No. They never would have. Because I took the difference." He held up a hand before she could speak—not to stop her but to stop himself, to keep the confession moving downhill now that it had finally started rolling. "Before you build me into something grander than I was, understand: I wasn't robbing Meridian to feed you. We ate. We always ate. I robbed them because Halvard Mersen, the director, asked me to—and paid me a tenth of the skim to keep the books that made it look like weather, or bad beds, or the usual losses the sea charges everyone. Mersen was selling pearls south in double shipments and writing the surplus off against the Greywater station, and he needed a weigher who could make two hundred and ten pounds of missing pearl look like an act of God."

"And you said yes."

"He offered, and I hesitated exactly as long as it takes a man to think about a new roof." Erik looked at his hands. Diver's hands once, scarred and strong; now they trembled unless he pressed them flat. "That was the whole length of my virtue, Sable. One breath long. And then I said yes, because the money was good and because Mersen frightened me, though I dressed the fear up as loyalty for years afterward. And for two years it worked. Two years of clean-looking books and a roof that didn't leak and Tam with new boots. And then Brandt—or men like Brandt, Brandt's predecessors, the company grows auditors like a ship grows barnacles—started pulling threads in the southern office. Mersen panicked. And Mersen's solution to panic, you'll be shocked to learn, was to arrange for the weigher to drown."

The stove ticked. Outside, somewhere down the beach, a gull made its idiot complaint.

"He didn't mean for me to actually drown," Erik said. "I don't think. I think the plan was a wrecked boat and a grieving family and a dead man who could sign anything posthumously. But I found out—a note passed to me at the tavern, a friend on the harbor watch who owed me—and I had one night to choose. Stay and hang for Mersen's arithmetic, with you all watching. Or go."

"And you went."

"And I went." He said it plainly. He owed her plainness; it was the only currency he had left that wasn't counterfeit. "I rowed out past the point in fog so thick I navigated by memory and prayer, in roughly that order, and I let the boat wash up broken on the rocks below the cliff road so they'd find it. And then I walked north. And your mother buried an empty coffin"—he stopped, corrected himself—"no. She didn't. They never gave her a body, so there was nothing to bury, just a stone in the churchyard with my name on it and a lie carved underneath in the parish register. *Lost at sea.* Three words. Cheapest words I ever spent."

"They cost us everything," Sable said.

"I know."

"Do you?" For the first time her voice moved—not louder, but deeper, like water finding a channel. "Because I've done the sums, Da. It's what I do. Seven years. Ma took in washing until her hands split, and then she took in mending, and then she took in whatever would sit still. Tam went hungry in a town where our name meant *the diver who drowned owing money*, except nobody even knew the owing part—they just knew we were the family the sea cheated, and people are kinder to the sea's victims than to a coward's, so maybe you chose right. Maybe drowning was the best career decision you ever made." She said it without cruelty. That was the terrible part. She said it like a line item.

"I put you in Meridian's hands," Erik said. "I know that too. My debt didn't die with me—it transferred. Mersen saw to it. Your indenture is collateral on what I stole, or what I was said to steal, the accounting being flexible in that house. I learned that from a fish buyer in 'ninety-three and I sat in this hut for a week afterward doing nothing but drinking and coughing, and then I got up and kept living, because living is the one habit I've never managed to break."

"Why now?" Sable asked. "Why the letters? Why Ilse? Why any of it after seven years of comfortable death?"

And here Erik did the thing he had been circling for two days, since Ilse had told him his daughter was coming, since he'd begun rehearsing this conversation the way he'd once rehearsed false weights—over and over, until the truth itself started to sound like a performance.

"Because I'm dying," he said. "Properly, this time. No boat required. The lung-rot's had me three years; the camps are full of it, we breathe the fish-oil smoke and the frost-fog and it chews the inside of your chest like rust chews iron. Ilse has it too. Hers is further along, though she'd gut me for saying so." Ilse, on the bunk, said nothing, which from her was agreement. "And a dying man gets honest, Sable. Not good. Honest. There's a difference, and it matters, and I want you to hear it: I'm not telling you this because I've become a better man. I'm telling you because I'm running out of time to be a worse one."

He pushed himself up from the chair, slowly, letting the committee of objections overrule him only twice, and crossed to the loose plank beneath the bunk where he kept the sum of his earthly estate: a tin cup, a folding knife, a photograph gone silver with age, and a bundle of letters tied with net twine. He brought back everything except the strongbox key—that hung around his neck on a cord, under his shirt, and had for seven years, a small cold anchor riding above his heart.

"These are yours," he said, setting the letters in her lap. "All of them. Every cowardly draft I ever wrote and didn't send. Read them or burn them, I don't care which, though I'll confess I hope you read them, because a man likes to be understood even when he doesn't deserve it."

Sable looked down at the bundle. Her thumb moved along the twine the way, he realized, it must move along ledger columns. Counting. Weighing. Her mother's jaw set against his own restless hands.

"What do you want, Da?" she asked finally. "Truly. Not the letter version."

And Erik Nystrom, who had spent seven years practicing this answer in the fog outside this hut, addressing gulls and empty water and a God he'd stopped invoicing, found that the practiced answer dissolved the moment he needed it, leaving only the raw material underneath.

"Passage," he said. "There's a berth. On the last ship south. Ilse told me—you know about it, I can see you do. I'm not asking you to give it to me. I want that understood. If you give it to Tam, it should go to Tam; the boy's lungs are worse than mine and he hasn't had seven years to make peace with dying, and besides, he's innocent, which puts him ahead of both of us in any fair queue." He knelt—his knees screamed, he ignored them, kneeling was a debt he could pay in full—and looked up at his daughter from below, which was where he belonged. "I'm asking you to help me get off this coast before the ice closes and before the company's men find me, because they will find me, Sable. Mersen knows I'm alive—I've had proof of that since summer—and a dead man who turns up breathing is a loose thread to a man like Mersen, and Brandt pulls threads. I'm asking you to help me run. Again. Which is the only thing I have ever been excellent at."

The stove ticked. Ilse's breathing had gone shallow and careful in the corner, the sound of a woman conserving herself for whatever came next.

"And if I say no?" Sable asked.

"Then I'll walk south along the shore until the ice takes me or a company man does, and I'll consider it fair pricing." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I've done the arithmetic too, love. It's what we do. It's the only inheritance I gave you that was ever worth anything."

"It isn't the only thing." Sable stood, and the letters stayed in her lap a moment before she tucked them, deliberately, into her coat—an acceptance of delivery, if nothing else. "You gave Tam your laugh. He laughs exactly like you, and it's the worst thing about him, and I'd burn down Meridian itself to hear it again."

She turned for the door, then stopped, hand on the latch, and Erik saw her decide something—saw the small internal transaction complete behind her eyes, the clerk approving a figure she didn't like but couldn't disprove.

"The strongbox," she said quietly. "Ilse says you sank it off the old beds before you ran. I dove for it. I have it, Da. I've had it since before I left Greywater."

The cold that went through Erik then had nothing to do with the fog outside. He gripped the back of the chair. *Mersen's documents. The order letters. The proof that would hang a director and save a dead man—or hang a dead man twice over.* Seven years guarding a key for a box that had already been opened.

"You—" His voice failed, restarted. "You've read them."

"Every page."

Outside, down the shingle path, through the thinning fog, came the crunch of boots on gravel—many boots, unhurried, in the rhythm of men who were paid by the day and fully expected to be paid tomorrow as well.

Ilse was on her feet. Sable's hand came off the latch.

"Well," said Erik Nystrom to his daughter, with the gallows cheerfulness that was, at last, one thing in him that had never been a lie, "it seems someone else has done their arithmetic too."
