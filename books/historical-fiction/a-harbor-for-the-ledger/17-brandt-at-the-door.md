## 17. Brandt at the Door

The trouble with the north, Auditor Emeric Brandt decided, was that it had no respect for paperwork.

He had said as much to the two company men he'd brought along—Ostler, a broad silent fellow who could carry a man under each arm like parcels, and young Pell, whose enthusiasm for the assignment had curdled somewhere around the second day of sleet. Neither of them had laughed. Brandt had not expected them to. Humor was a currency he kept in reserve, spent only when it bought something.

They had tracked the Nystrom girl the way he tracked everything: through arithmetic. A woman traveling north in November needed supplies, and supplies needed coin, and coin left traces the way blood leaves traces on snow—unmistakable if you knew what color to look for. She had paid for passage on a mail skiff out of Greywater using company scrip, which was either carelessness or confidence, and in Brandt's experience confidence was merely carelessness wearing better boots.

Three days by boat, half a day on foot through scrub pine with the wind doing its level best to peel his ears off, and there it was: Camp Verren, eleven shacks huddled against the shoreline like men around a dying fire, smelling overwhelmingly of fish, tar, and despair. The lung-rot camps. He had read about them in Meridian's medical disbursements—a line item so small it was nearly invisible, which was how the company preferred its suffering arranged.

"Charming," said Pell, pulling his collar up.

"It isn't," Brandt agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

He did not announce himself at the first shack or the fifth. He walked the length of the camp first, hands folded behind him like a clergyman inspecting his parish, taking inventory. Forty-one residents by his count, though several were more cough than person. A drying rack collapsed in the wind. A dog of no discernible breed watched him with the frank appraisal of an animal that had already decided he was not worth biting.

In the third shack from the water, a woman sat mending a net with fingers wrapped in rags, coughing in a rhythm so regular it might have been set to a metronome. Ilse Varric. He recognized her from the harbor records—the stranger who had arrived in Greywater in autumn asking questions, and then, inexplicably, stopped asking them. He had assumed she'd found her answers. It appeared she had also found something worth staying for, which was a complication he intended to address.

She looked up when he ducked through the doorway, and her eyes went flat and knowing.

"You're the auditor," she said.

"I'm flattered. My reputation precedes me even this far from a filing cabinet."

"Sable described you." She tied off a knot. "She said you had the face of a man reading a menu over someone's shoulder."

Brandt considered this. "Your friend is perceptive. Where is she?"

"Fishing," Ilse said, which they both understood to mean *not here, and I will not tell you when she'll be back*. "Her father's shack is the last one. Blue door. Well—it was blue. Before."

"Thank you for your cooperation, Miss Varric. I find that rare and refreshing."

"I'm not cooperating. I'm conserving my strength." Another knot. Her hands were steady but her breathing wasn't; he heard the wet whistle beneath it and revised his estimate of how long she had. Months, perhaps. Not years. People in these camps were spending down their lives like sailors ashore, and the tragedy—or the efficiency, depending on one's ledger—was that they all seemed to know the exchange rate.

Brandt inclined his head and withdrew.

* * *

The blue door had faded to the gray of old dishwater. Brandt knocked twice, precisely, because a man's knock should tell people exactly who he was before they opened anything.

Aldric Nystrom opened it himself, which told Brandt something too. A guilty man would have made them wait, would have fumbled, would have been "just getting dressed." Nystrom stood in the doorway in a coat too thin for the season, gray-bearded, hollowed out, and looked at the three of them with the expression of a man who had been expecting this particular visitor for seven years and was almost relieved the wait was over.

"Auditor," he said. "You have the look of the company about you."

"The look is accurate. May we come in? Your hospitality needn't be sincere; it need only be extended."

Nystrom stepped aside.

Inside was poverty arranged with a certain bitter tidiness—one cot, one stool, one stove burning driftwood that popped and spat salt. On the wall hung a single photograph, curling: two children on a dock, a girl squinting fiercely at the camera, a boy laughing at whatever the girl was doing. Brandt noted the photograph the way he noted everything—as evidence, though of what, precisely, remained to be determined.

"You know why I've come," Brandt said.

"I imagine you've come for my daughter."

"In a sense." Brandt took the room's single stool uninvited, leaving Ostler and Pell by the door, and produced from his breast pocket a leather folio. He did not open it yet. An unopened folio was worth ten opened ones; anticipation did most of an auditor's work for him. "Seven years ago, Aldric Nystrom, senior weighmaster of the Meridian Trading Company, Greywater office, falsified shipment manifests covering some four hundred marks' worth of pearls and vanished. His boat was recovered. His body never was. His children were informed he drowned, and his daughter's labor was applied against his debts, per contract clause fourteen, which I drafted myself and am, despite current circumstances, still proud of."

"Clause fourteen," Nystrom said. "I remember it. I signed it drunk."

"Most binding documents are improved by sobriety on only one side of the table." Brandt let that sit a moment, then continued, mild as milk. "Here is what interests me. For seven years you have lived forty miles from your children and allowed them to believe you were at the bottom of the harbor. That is not the act of a man fleeing debtors. Debts can be outrun quietly, comfortably, even honorably, if one is flexible about the word. Allowing your daughter to serve indenture in your place—that requires either monstrous selfishness or a reason. And I have spent my career learning that everyone has a reason."

Nystrom's jaw worked. "You talk like a bill collector."

"I *am* a bill collector. I simply collect other things as well." Brandt opened the folio at last and removed a single sheet, laying it on the cot between them. It was a copy of the manifest discrepancies—Sable's own careful annotations included, which had cost him a small pang to reproduce, since they were genuinely elegant work. "But you are not the reason I've come, Mr. Nystrom. You're the lever. What I want is a strongbox."

Something crossed the old man's face—not surprise, which interested Brandt greatly. Fear, yes, but fear of a specific flavor: the fear of a man watching someone else approach his buried treasure from a direction he hadn't fortified.

"Don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." Brandt smiled without warmth. "Then you won't mind hearing my offer anyway, since it costs nothing. The annual ship sails south in—" he consulted no notes, having memorized the schedule weeks ago, "—four days now, weather permitting, and weather is not permitting, so likely sooner. There is one berth. I control who fills it. I am prepared to offer that berth to you, Aldric Nystrom: safe passage south, out of my jurisdiction, out of the company's reach, a new name if you want one. Freedom. The thing you've been hiding up here among the rotting lungs and the fish guts for seven years."

Silence. The stove popped. Outside, wind worried at the eaves.

"And in exchange," Nystrom said slowly, "you take my daughter."

"I take nothing from your daughter. I require two things, both trivially easy. First: the contents of the strongbox—which, since it does not exist, you may hand over whenever convenient. Second: your daughter's silence regarding those same nonexistent contents. She keeps her nose in her ledgers. I keep my pen out of her file. Everyone prospers."

"And if she doesn't stay quiet?"

Brandt rose, brushing wood smoke from his coat. "Mr. Nystrom, do you know what I admire about your daughter? Everything in her handwriting suggests a woman who believes that history happens to other people. That if she keeps her numbers clean and her head down, the world will pass her by without incident. It is a charming philosophy. It has even worked for her, mostly." He paused at the door. "But history has found her now, sir. It found her the day a stranger put a letter on her counter. The question is no longer whether she'll be part of events. The question is whether she'll be a footnote or a casualty. I'm offering you the chance to keep her a footnote."

"She's a better bookkeeper than you'll ever be," Nystrom said, with sudden fierce pride that seemed to surprise even himself.

"I don't doubt it. Good bookkeepers survive. Great ones get blamed." Brandt settled his hat. "You have until the ship docks at Greywater. Three days, possibly fewer. When you've located your strongbox—and again, my condolences on its nonexistence—you can reach me aboard, or through Miss Varric, who I trust will pass along any message with the discretion of the dying."

He was halfway out the door when Nystrom spoke again, quieter.

"Why the berth? You could just arrest us both. Company courts, company gallows. Why buy silence you could compel?"

Brandt turned back and studied him for a moment—the ruined man, the photograph on the wall, the whole sad architecture of a life spent running from a sum on a page.

"Because compelled testimony is unreliable, and burned ledgers are worse than useless—they're suspicious," Brandt said. "And because, Mr. Nystrom, there are names in those documents that are not yours and not mine, and one of them signs my pay. If that strongbox surfaces anywhere—any court, any newspaper, any drunken diver's tale—dozens of careers end, mine included, and the company sails on unchanged with new faces in the same chairs. I have no interest in martyring myself to change nothing. I'd far rather close the account quietly." He smiled again, thin as ledger paper. "You of all people should appreciate that. It's how you'd have done it."

He left the old man standing in the doorway of the last shack, looking smaller than the doorframe, and crunched away across the frozen mud toward the water.

* * *

Pell caught up with him at the boat, breathless, cheeks raw. "Sir—the woman. Varric. She was watching from her window the whole time. Should we—"

"No."

"But if she warns them—"

"Pell." Brandt paused, one boot in the boat, and regarded his junior with something adjacent to patience. "Of course she'll warn them. I counted on it. Do you know why?" He waited, but Pell only blinked, so he answered himself, as he usually did. "Because a warning makes them feel clever, and clever people stop thinking. Because it tells the girl exactly what's on offer, so she spends three sleepless nights weighing her father's life against her brother's conscience instead of devising some fourth option I haven't anticipated. And because—" he stepped down into the boat and took the tiller, "—because I want her to come to me believing she's chosen freely. People defend choices. They only resent gifts."

Ostler rowed. The camp fell away behind them, smoke rising crooked from eleven chimneys into a sky the color of unwashed wool.

It was on the return crossing, watching the grey water slide past, that Brandt permitted himself the day's single honest thought: the Nystrom girl was the finest mind he had encountered in that harbor, and it was genuinely a pity that she was about to waste it keeping quiet, or ruin herself refusing to. He had offered her father the clean way out. Most men took it. He estimated the odds at four to one in his favor.

But he had read her marginalia.

Brandt pulled his collar tighter against the wind and began, in his head, drafting the report he hoped he'd never have to file—and the contingency plans for every version of her answer except the one he wanted.

Four days, possibly fewer. Weather permitting, and the weather, he suspected, was about to permit a great deal less than anyone planned for.
