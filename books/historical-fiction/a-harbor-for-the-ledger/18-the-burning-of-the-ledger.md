## 18. The Burning of the Ledger

The company men left the camp the way weather leaves—a slow withdrawal, a grayness that lingered after the cause was gone. Brandt had shaken my father's hand at the door of his own shack, which I think was the cruelest thing I saw him do all winter, and then he'd turned to me with that small, patient smile and said, "You have until the tide turns, Miss Nystrom. The strongbox contents for your father's passage. Or I take both men—the one who forged the documents and the daughter who hid them—and let the ice sort out the rest."

Then he'd tipped his hat to Ilse as though she were a lady he'd met at church, and gone down through the fish racks with his two hired shoulders, and the camp exhaled behind him.

My father sat on his bunk with his hands flat on his knees like a man waiting for a verdict. He'd been doing that, I realized, for seven years. Waiting. Letting other people carry the weight while he kept his palms open and empty.

"Well," he said. "You heard him."

I heard him.

I had carried the strongbox documents north in an oilskin wrap against my ribs, and every mile of the journey they had felt less like evidence and more like organs—something vital I'd cut out of the sea myself, something that would bleed if I handled it wrong. In the lamplight of my father's shack I unwrapped them now, and there they were: the shipping manifests in Meridian's own director's hand, the orders to underweigh the pearl lots and pocket the difference, the letters instructing a junior clerk named Nystrom to falsify the entries or lose his position. My father's crime, laid out beside the man who'd taught him how to commit it. Two signatures. One gallows between them, if you believed the law cared whose hand held the knife and whose merely steadied it.

Brandt wanted these pages. Meridian's director wanted them sunk. And I—

I stood in a room that smelled of fish oil and sickness and looked at the only power I had ever held in my life, and understood that it was not mine at all. It was theirs. It had always been theirs. Every scrap of paper in Greywater belonged to somebody, and the somebody was never me.

"Sable." My father's voice, careful as a man crossing rotten ice. "You know what has to happen."

"Do I?" I said.

He leaned forward. Seven years in the north had hollowed him; the lamp showed every groove the wind had carved, and yet his eyes still had that quick, hungry cleverness I remembered from childhood, the look he'd worn when he found a good pearl, when he found a way around a rule. "You burn them. That's all. Burn them and Brandt has nothing, the director sleeps easy, and nobody hangs—not even me. You've saved us both. You've always been the clever one."

*You've always been the clever one.* He'd said that when I was nine and balancing his books because his eyes had gone blurry from drink. He'd said it when I signed my indenture papers at fourteen so he could borrow against my wages. Clever, useful, mine. His clever girl, doing his arithmetic for him since before I could write my own name without help.

"And Tam?" I said.

He blinked. "What about him?"

"The berth. The ship sails tomorrow with one berth, and Brandt is offering it to you—for the documents and my silence. If I burn them, you get nothing. No passage. No escape. You stay here until the camps finish what the lung-rot started." I watched his face do its arithmetic—it was fast, I'd give him that, faster than any ledger I ever kept. "Say it plainly, Father. What am I supposed to do about Tam?"

Something moved behind his eyes and was put away again, quick as a coin into a pocket. "Tam is young. Young people survive. I won't."

"No," I agreed quietly. "You won't. Not up here. Not without money. Not without someone else carrying you." I looked down at the papers in my hands. "You know what I figured out on the boat coming north? It took me twenty-three years, which I'm told is slow for someone so clever. I figured out that drowning."

"What?"

"Your drowning. The one we buried you from. I used to think it was the worst thing that ever happened to our family." I smiled at him, and I felt my mouth make the same polite shape I wore across the weighing house counter, the one that says *I am invisible, I am nothing, move along.* "It wasn't. It was the most honest entry you ever made."

He came off the bunk then, reaching for the papers—"Sable, don't be foolish"—and Ilse was suddenly between us.

I hadn't seen her cross the room. She did things like that now, moving quiet as her cough allowed, and she stood with her back half-turned to me, facing him, thin as a winter reed and absolutely immovable. "Sit down, Anders," she said. "You've done enough sitting in safety while children paid your debts. Sit down some more."

"You're dying anyway," he snarled at her. "What do you care—"

"I care," Ilse said, "because I carried your letters seven hundred miles thinking I was delivering them to a family worth grieving for. Don't make me a liar twice."

* * *

We went out onto the shingle.

The camp at night was a scatter of dim windows and drying nets, the sea working at the stones with that patient grinding sound, like a clerk adding a column that never ends. Cold enough that breath hung. Cold enough, I thought, that paper would catch fast and burn clean if you gave it flame.

Ilse had brought the lantern and, unasked, a box of matches. She set the lantern on a driftlog and stood back, arms folded into her coat, watching me with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave more than they should have.

"You could still trade it," she said. "Passage for your father. It's not nothing. He's your blood."

"So is Tam." I turned the oilskin over in my hands. "Do you want to know the arithmetic? Because I've done it. God knows I've done nothing but arithmetic since I was fourteen. Here's the column." I ticked it off against the cold air. "If I give Brandt the papers, my father lives another few years somewhere warm, spending money that isn't his, and Tam dies of a fever a southern doctor could cure in a month. If I keep the papers, I hold a sword over Meridian's director's neck—which sounds fine until you notice that Brandt holds *me*, and a sword in someone else's grip is just a blade pointed at your own throat. Either way, the papers win. Not me. Not anyone I love." I laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged. "Every path leads through their ledger. So."

"So," Ilse said softly.

"I'm taking the ledger out of the world."

She nodded slowly. "There'll be a cost. There always is."

"There's already been a cost. I've been paying it since the day I filed an underweight pearl shipment and said nothing. Since the day I learned my whole life was collateral." I crouched by the lantern. "You know what Brandt never understood about me? All winter he thought I was careful because I was afraid. I was careful because I was *waiting*. A diver learns patience, Ilse. You go down, you hold your breath, you wait for the water to tell you what's true. And then—" I struck the match, "—you come up."

The first page caught reluctantly, the way old paper does, curling brown at the edges before the flame took hold properly. I fed it into the second. The director's signature bloomed orange and vanished. The order to falsify—*or lose your position, Nystrom, and consider what happens to your children*—went up in a whisper of sparks that the wind pulled apart like thread.

I burned them one at a time. I will not pretend otherwise. Some part of me wanted to memorize each line as it died—to keep the words somewhere fire couldn't reach, so that someday, if I ever grew cruel or clever in my father's direction, I might write them down again. But the sea taught me something else too: a pearl you refuse to sell is just a stone with pretensions. Knowledge you can't use without destroying everyone is just a stone.

The last page was the oldest, the original agreement, and it fought the flame longest. My father's name on it, young-sounding even in ink. Mine, appended below in a child's careful hand: *witnessed, Sable Nystrom, aged 14.* I watched my own small signature curl black and lift away on the wind over the water, and I felt—I want to say grief, but it wasn't, quite. It was lighter than grief. It was the feeling of setting down a load so old your bones had grown around it, so that putting it down feels briefly like losing part of yourself.

Scorched earth, Ilse called it later. I say it was the first honest disposal of records ever conducted in the Nystrom family.

When the last ash stopped glowing, I stirred it with a stick, ground it into the stones, and let the tide begin its work. Then I sat back on the driftlog next to Ilse and discovered I was shaking—from cold, I told myself, though the cold had stopped bothering me somewhere in the middle of it.

"Well," I said. "Now nobody wins."

"Now everybody loses equally," Ilse corrected. "That's different. That's almost fair." She glanced sideways at me. "Brandt is going to be furious."

"Brandt is going to be *ruinous*," I said. "But he's going to be ruinous with empty hands. There's a difference between a man with a knife and a man holding a sheath."

Ilse laughed—that low, cracked laugh of hers that the lung-rot had roughened but couldn't spoil—and then she did something she had never done in front of anyone: she reached over and took my hand. Her fingers were cold and bony and steady. "Anders is raging in there," she said. "He'll come at you tomorrow with new arguments. He'll cry, maybe. He's practiced."

"He can rage at the sea. It's the only creditor he's got left." I looked north, where the dark hills hid the harbor road, the long way back to Greywater, the ship, the berth, the morning. "Ilse. The ship sails at dawn and I have no papers, no proof, no leverage, and a brother who needs that berth more than anyone alive needs anything. I have exactly nothing."

"That's not true," she said. "You have me. And you have the fact that Brandt's offer died with those papers—he can't buy your silence anymore, because there's nothing left to sell. Which means the only thing standing between Tam and that berth is paperwork." Her gray eyes caught the lantern light, and I swear to you there was mischief in them, actual mischief, in a woman three hundred miles from her grave. "And paperwork, Miss Nystrom, happens to be the one weapon they handed us ourselves. You've been forging honest entries for the Meridian Company for nine years. How hard can one dishonest one be?"

I stared at her. Then I started laughing—helpless, freezing, ash-fingered laughter on a beach at the end of the world—and she joined in, wheezing, until the cough took her and I held her shoulder and we stayed like that, two criminals against the dark, while the tide erased the last of the ashes.

Behind us, in the shack, a lamp moved past the window. My father, pacing, counting losses. Let him count. I had closed his account.

Dawn was four hours off, the ship six, and the road home was frozen mud and bad intentions. I picked up the lantern.

"Come on," I said to Ilse. "Let's go commit some bookkeeping."
