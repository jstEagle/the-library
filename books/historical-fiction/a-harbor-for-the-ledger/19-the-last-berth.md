## 19. The Last Berth

The fog came off the water that morning like a debt collector—patient, inevitable, and utterly indifferent to what you had planned for the day.

Sable stood at the foot of the dock with Tam's coat over one arm and a carpetbag that weighed almost nothing, because almost nothing was all they owned that could be carried. The *Meridian Star* sat low in the gray water, her hold stuffed with the season's last cargo: salted fish, bundled pelts, crates of pearls that Sable herself had weighed and logged and, for the first time in seven years, entered honestly into the ledger. Every gram accounted for. It had taken her most of the night, and Ilse had kept the lamp trimmed and made terrible tea and said nothing at all about how Sable's hand shook on every third figure.

"Your hands are shaking," Ilse had said, on the third cup.

"They're cold."

"It's October in Greywater. Everyone's hands are cold." Ilse had reached across the table and steadied the pen with two fingers, warm from the cup. "But only yours are shaking."

That was the thing about Ilse Varric. She would not let you lie to her even when the truth was inconvenient, and she did it so gently you almost thanked her.

Now, at the dock, the whole town seemed to have turned out, which was to say perhaps forty souls in oilskins and wool, breath steaming, everyone pretending they weren't staring at the woman who'd bought—or won, or stolen, depending on which rumor you preferred—the last berth south before the ice closed the harbor until spring. The harbormaster stood at his little podium with his manifest, looking like a man who had been told by Auditor Brandt himself exactly how this morning was to go, and was relieved beyond all measure to have instructions.

Tam leaned against her shoulder. That was new, and it broke her heart a little. Her brother had spent seventeen years being too proud to lean on anyone; now he weighed against her like driftwood, light and damp and hollow-sounding when he coughed.

"You're doing that face," Tam said.

"What face?"

"The arithmetic face. You do it with your eyebrows." He coughed into his sleeve, then managed a grin that cost him something. "You've been doing it since Tuesday. I'm not an idiot, Sable. I can count. There's one berth and there are three of us who need it, and I'm the only one whose lungs sound like a accordion falling down stairs."

"That is not how berths are allocated."

"No, they're allocated by money or connections or whoever Brandt decides deserves to breathe warmer air." He looked out at the ship. "I heard Papa was at the weighing house yesterday."

The words landed like cold water down her collar. "Who told you that?"

"Piet. Piet tells me everything because he thinks I'm dying and dying people should get good gossip." Tam shrugged. "Is it true? Is he really—"

"He's really alive." She said it flatly, because if she didn't say it flatly she wasn't sure what would come out. "Hiding up north. Sick. Out of money, out of luck, and out of anyone willing to look at him."

"And he wants the berth."

"He hasn't asked."

"Sable." Tam turned to look at her fully, and his eyes were their father's eyes, which had always been the cruelest thing about him. "Everyone wants the berth. The asking is just paperwork."

* * *

They had worked through the night, she and Ilse, at the table in the back of the weighing house with its scarred wood and its smell of brine and ink. The papers were Ilse's craft more than hers—a bill of passage in the company's own format, a medical writ signed with a doctor's name that belonged to a man who had died two winters ago and therefore could not object, a letter of sponsorship bearing the seal of a fishmongers' guild in the south that Ilse swore existed because she had once drunk its founder under a table in another lifetime.

"You forge beautifully," Sable had said, watching her work.

"I was married to a customs officer for four years." Ilse hadn't looked up. "You learn a great deal about documents, living with a man who worships them. Mostly you learn that they're all just paper wearing uniforms."

"And after the customs officer?"

"After the customs officer there was a fishing boat, and after the fishing boat there was your father, and after your father—" Here she had paused, ink drying under her nail, "—there is a weighing house in Greywater where a woman makes honest entries and lets me sit near the stove. So far that is my favorite posting."

Sable had wanted to say something then. Something true and large. But the pen was shaking again, and Ilse had put her hand over Sable's, and somehow that said it better than anything either of them could have drafted.

Now, at the dock, the papers were folded inside Tam's coat, next to his chest, where the warmth would keep the ink from cracking. Ilse stood beside Sable with her hands buried in her pockets, watching the crowd with the flat, cataloguing attention of someone who had spent years learning which faces meant trouble. Sable followed her gaze once and found him.

Her father stood at the edge of the crowd, half behind a stack of herring barrels, as though a man might hide from his children behind forty barrels of fish. Seven years had done what seven years do. He was thinner than the sea had left room for, gray to the roots, wrapped in a coat that had been good once in a country she'd never seen. His hands were in his pockets. His eyes were on Tam.

He did not come forward.

She found, examining herself the way she examined a column of figures, that she was not surprised. That was the whole of it, really—the discovery she'd rowed north through freezing spray to make. Her father was a man who waited at the edges of things. He had waited while Meridian's debts mounted. He had waited while the story of his drowning hardened into fact. He had let two children grow up on a dead man's legend rather than walk into town and pay what he owed. And now he would wait here, behind the barrels, and see whether his daughter would carry him to the ship on her back, and he would call it love, and some small part of it might even be.

She hated him. She loved him. Both entries went in the same ledger, and neither canceled the other out. She had learned that much from the books: some accounts simply run at a loss forever, and the trick is deciding which ones you keep open.

* * *

"Passengers for the southern berth!" The harbormaster's voice cracked across the dock. He cleared his throat. "One berth. Manifest says—" he squinted at the paper with theatrical care, "—one T. Nystrom, sponsored passage, medical writ attached. Papers?"

Sable walked Tam forward. Each step felt measured out like rope. Behind them she heard the crowd shift, murmur—the Nystrom name still had teeth in this town, even after seven years, even drowned. Especially drowned.

The harbormaster took the papers. He read them. Sable watched his eyes move down the forged lines and knew, with the certainty of a woman who had caught a hundred fraudulent shipments, that he saw it—that the writ was too clean, the guild seal a hair too crisp, the sponsorship letter addressed to a berth number that wouldn't be assigned until boarding. He looked up at her. She looked back, level, and thought: *you owe me*. Thought: *your director signed the order that sank my family, and you know it, and we both know I know it.* The harbormaster was many things, but he was not brave enough for that arithmetic.

"Everything appears in order," he said, and stamped the manifest, and the sound of it went through Sable like a bell through ice.

Tam turned to her.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Around them the dockhands shouted, the gulls screamed their eternal grievances, the *Meridian Star*'s engines grumbled awake below decks like an old dog hearing the word *walk*. Fog threaded between the mooring lines. Somewhere in the crowd her father stood very still behind his barrels.

"You wrote the list?" Tam asked.

"In the bag. Left pocket. Dr. Halloran's tincture first, then the iron tonic, don't let them give you the syrup, it's laudanum with delusions of grandeur. The landlady in Corvenport owes Ilse a favor, tell her Ilse sent you and she'll feed you whether you can pay or not. Write to the weighing house, Greywater harbor, mark it private—"

"Sable."

"—and if anyone asks about your father, you say he drowned, and you cry a little, you're good at crying—"

"*Sable.*" Tam took her face in both hands. His palms were fever-hot. "I'm going to be fine. That's the whole point of this. You did the sums right."

"I hate the sums."

"I know." He smiled, and it was their father's smile, and for once she let it be a gift instead of a wound. "Do you want to hear something funny? All these years I thought you were the careful one and I was the reckless one. And then you burn down an auditor's case and forge a berth for your dying brother and dive for sunken strongboxes, and I realize—" he coughed, laughed, coughed again, "—I realize I never once kept up with you. I was just loud."

She pulled him in. He smelled of camphor and salt and boy, though he was hardly that anymore. Over his shoulder she saw her father step out from behind the barrels—one step, no more—and stop, hands still pocketed, mouth working around words that had been seven years late and were now, apparently, going to be eight.

He did not ask.

She waited, one breath, two, giving him the space the way you leave a gap in a ledger for an entry you know will never come. He looked at her. She looked at him. Between them lay everything: the forged drowning, the indenture sold like a sack of meal, the nights she'd sat up with Tam's cough believing grief was the worst thing the sea could do to a family, when in truth the sea had been the merciful party all along.

Her father raised one hand—not a wave, not quite, more the gesture of a man hailing a boat that has decided not to come in.

Sable turned away first. It was the only inheritance she wanted from him: knowing when to stop waiting.

* * *

The gangway went up. The lines came off. The *Meridian Star* backed water, swung her blunt nose toward the harbor mouth, and began the long laborious business of leaving Greywater behind, and Sable stood on the dock with Ilse's arm solid around her waist and watched the rail until she found Tam's face among the few passengers leaning there. He wasn't waving. He was doing his arithmetic face, eyebrows working, and when he caught her looking he pointed south, firmly, twice, as if to say: *that way. Go on. Live.*

Then the fog took him, politely, the way Greywater takes everything eventually.

"Well," said Ilse.

"Don't," said Sable. "If you're kind to me right now I'll come apart on this dock in front of forty fishermen and the harbormaster will enter it in the log."

Ilse considered this. "Would that be so bad?"

"Yes. It would take three men and a block and tackle to get me home, and the company would charge me for the tackle."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through her chest, raw-edged but real, and she leaned into Ilse's shoulder and let the fog wet her face so that no one could testify to anything else. They stood like that while the dock emptied, while the gulls resettled, while the harbor settled back into its long gray breathing. Somewhere behind them a hatch cover banged. The world, indifferent as ever, resumed its bookkeeping.

It was only when they turned to go that she saw him again. Her father had come out from behind the barrels at last—as the ship faded, as the berth sailed south without him—and now he stood alone on the emptying dock, a gaunt man in a dead man's coat, watching the water where his son had been. He didn't approach her. He didn't speak. He simply looked at her with an expression she recognized from the ledgers: the look of a man reading a final balance and finding that the account has been closed, and that the closing entry was written in someone else's hand.

Sable looked back at him for a long moment. Then she took Ilse's hand, and they walked up the dock together, out of the fog, toward the weighing house and the stove and the fresh page waiting in the ledger—

—and found Auditor Brandt standing at the door, hat in hand, waiting for her with the patience of a man who has just read the morning manifest and knows precisely what was stamped upon it.
