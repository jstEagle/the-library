## 20. What the Company Cannot Own

Auditor Elias Brandt had been awake since four o'clock, which was not unusual, and had been staring at the ceiling of his room at the Meridian guesthouse since four-oh-five, which was.

The ash was the problem. He kept seeing it: the curl of paper going up in the brazier at the northern camp, the way Sable Nystrom had stood there with soot on her knuckles and her chin up, watching eleven years of evidence become weather. He had seen men burn ledgers before—twice, in fact, once in Kettering and once aboard a ship whose captain had then discovered that fire at sea is a poor place to hide anything—and in both cases the burners had wept or raged or begged. She had done none of those things. She had simply fed the pages to the flame one at a time, like a woman banking a stove for the night, and then she had said, "Now nobody owns anybody," in a voice as level as a well-set scale.

He had not slept well since. This annoyed him more than the loss of the case did.

Brandt rose, dressed, and went down to the common room, where the landlady—a broad, cheerful woman named Mrs. Pell who treated all Meridian employees as though they were underfed nephews—had laid out porridge, smoked fish, and a pot of coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in.

"You look peaky," Mrs. Pell announced, depositing a second fish on his plate without being asked.

"I am perfectly well."

"That's what my late husband used to say, right up until he wasn't." She patted his shoulder. "Eat the fish."

Brandt ate the fish. It was, he conceded privately, excellent fish, and this was part of what had gone wrong in Greywater. The town had refused to be disagreeable. He had arrived expecting sullen dockhands, evasive clerks, a harbormaster who would grovel on schedule. Instead he'd found a town that fed its auditor too much and asked after his health, and a clerk who had outmaneuvered him with a matchbox and a brazier while he stood there holding a warrant that was now worth exactly the paper it wasn't printed on anymore.

He finished his coffee, thanked Mrs. Pell in a way that made her beam, and set out for the weighing house through fog so thick it seemed to have opinions about where he was allowed to walk.

* * *

The facts of the matter, as Brandt had reconstructed them over three sleepless nights, were these:

One. Director Corvane of the Meridian Trading Company had, seven years ago, ordered fraudulent shipment documents to conceal a shortfall of his own making—the pearls skimmed, the books balanced with paper instead of stone. Two. Josiah Nystrom, the company's own diver-clerk, had known, because Nystrom had kept the real numbers in his head and eventually in a strongbox at the bottom of the pearl beds. Three. Nystrom had fled rather than be hanged for it, letting the sea take the blame, and his children had paid the price: an indenture signed against a dead man's debts.

Four. And here was the item that had kept Brandt awake—the strongbox had contained proof enough to ruin Corvane, and Sable Nystrom had burned it. All of it. Every page that could have bought her freedom ten times over, every page that could have seen her father cleared or condemned, every page that could have made Brandt's career.

She had burned the weapon pointed at her enemy and the shield held over herself in the same fire.

He had spent two days trying to find the angle. There was always an angle; he had built twenty years of a career on the certainty that there was always an angle. Blackmail? No—she hadn't approached anyone. A copy? He'd had the camp searched twice, discreetly; nothing but fish scales and despair. Sentiment, then? That was the answer that disturbed him most, because it couldn't be audited. You could follow money anywhere. You could not follow a woman who looked at a burning page and saw, apparently, only her brother's fever breaking and her father's name coming clean of the mire, and decided that was worth more than justice or revenge or freedom.

Freedom, he thought now, walking the fog-bound quay. She had traded away her own freedom to keep it out of everyone's hands. And I am the man who is supposed to decide whether she has any.

* * *

The weighing house smelled of brine and lamp oil, as always, and Sable Nystrom was behind the counter, as always, entering figures into a ledger with that unnerving precision of hers. Ilse Varric sat by the window, sorting pearls into grades with a diver's quick fingers, coughing occasionally in a way that made Brandt's own chest tighten in sympathy—he had a cousin who'd died of the lung-rot, up in the herring camps, and the sound never stopped sounding like that cousin.

"Miss Nystrom," he said.

She did not look up from her ledger. "Mr. Brandt."

"Mrs. Varric."

Ilse looked up, gave him a nod that managed to be both polite and entirely unimpressed, and went back to her pearls.

"I've come to settle accounts," Brandt said.

That got her attention. She set down her pen—not slammed, merely set down, but he noticed her hand stayed flat on the cover of the ledger, the way a person rests a hand on a door they may need to shut quickly.

"Which accounts?"

"Yours." He took the folded document from inside his coat and laid it on the counter between them. Her contract of indenture, the original, with the company seal and his own countersignature already inked beneath the release clause. "The Meridian Trading Company hereby releases Sable Nystrom from all obligations arising from the debt of Josiah Nystrom, deceased—or rather," he corrected himself, "not deceased, which is a separate irregularity I have elected not to pursue, on the grounds that pursuing it would require documentation that no longer exists."

Sable looked at the paper. Then at him. Her face did something complicated and then settled into careful blankness, which was, he had learned, her version of shouting.

"Why?"

"Because you are no longer worth the trouble."

It was the truth, and he watched her weigh it, turning it over the way she turned over figures, looking for the hidden column. He respected that. He had spent his career among men who accepted good news without checking it, and every one of them had eventually ruined somebody.

"Explain," she said.

"Very well." He folded his hands on the counter. "An audit exists to establish what is owed and to whom. I came to Greywater to reconcile a discrepancy of some four hundred marks in pearl shipments across seven years. I now know precisely where those discrepancies originated, who ordered them, and how they were concealed. I also know that the sole documentary evidence was destroyed by fire on the nineteenth, in the presence of myself, three company men, and a very unimpressed gull. Without evidence, there is no case. Without a case, there is no leverage. Without leverage—" he tapped the contract "—this is not an instrument of debt. It is an instrument of spite. And I do not work for spite. Spite doesn't balance."

"And Corvane?" Her voice was quiet. "The man who actually ordered it. He just—stays?"

"He stays," Brandt said. "Corvane sits on the board in Kettering behind four layers of lawyers, and the man who might have touched him burned the touching along with everything else." He paused. "You knew that when you struck the match. I assume you considered it."

"I considered it." She met his eyes. "I decided some fires are worth more than the light they give."

"Yes," Brandt said. "That is exactly the sort of sentence that has been keeping me awake, if you must know."

From the window, Ilse Varric made a sound that was almost certainly a laugh disguised as a cough.

Sable picked up the contract. Read it through—all of it, every clause, which he approved of enormously—and then read it again. When she reached the end she looked up with an expression he suspected few people ever saw on her face, because it seemed to surprise even her.

"It's real," she said.

"It's real."

"No conditions. No testimony. No silence sworn."

"The silence is already yours to give or sell as you please. That's rather the point of having burned your side of the ledger. You hold nothing I can charge you for, and I hold nothing you can't survive losing." He allowed himself the smallest pause. "You played it well, Miss Nystrom. I want that noted somewhere, even if it can't be in a report. In twenty years of auditing, I have caught everyone. Everyone. Men smarter than you, richer than you, better connected than you. You are the first person to defeat me by making the game not worth playing. I don't know whether to arrest you or write you a letter of recommendation."

"Could you do both?" Ilse suggested from the window. "The letter would look lovely framed next to the arrest warrant."

"I don't frame things," said Brandt. "Framing implies sentiment."

"Of course it does," said Ilse, and grinned at him, and Brandt found—to his considerable alarm—that he was fighting the corner of his own mouth.

Sable signed the release line. Her pen moved steadily. When she finished, she blew on the ink, and then, instead of handing the paper back, she held it a moment longer, reading her own name at the bottom—her name, standing alone, owing nothing.

"Seven years," she said softly. "I used to add up what I cost them. Bread, coal, lamp oil. I kept a private ledger of my own debt, in my head, so I'd always know exactly how much of me belonged to somebody else." She slid the paper across the counter. "I never once added up what they owed me."

"They owed you nothing," Brandt said. "That's what contracts are for." He took the paper, blotted it, folded it, and then—because the moment seemed to ask it of him, and because he was, despite all evidence, a man and not a machine—said: "For what it is worth, which is nothing, legally: your father's debt was never yours. The indenture was Corvane's invention, built on a drowning that didn't happen, signed by a frightened girl. I have audited many unjust things. Yours was among the cleaner injustices I've seen executed. Cleanliness is not the same as correctness. I thought you should hear someone say so."

Sable looked at him for a long moment.

"Thank you," she said finally, and it sounded like it cost her something, and he understood that it did, and he respected that too.

* * *

The last ship south was loading at the east quay, and Brandt walked down to watch, because watching ships load was one of the few pleasures his profession permitted him. Cargo was cargo, but a ship leaving harbor was arithmetic in motion—tonnage and tide and manifest, all agreeing, for once, to go somewhere.

The berth situation had resolved itself, as berth situations did, through money and paperwork, and Brandt had reviewed the final passenger list that morning with his coffee. One name had interested him: Tam Nystrom, sixteen, bound south for the warmer air the company doctor had prescribed, papers in order, fare paid by an unnamed benefactor whose handwriting on the payment note was, Brandt observed, distinctly not Sable Nystrom's—it was broader, loopier, with a certain theatrical flourish on the capital T. The Varric woman's hand, unless he missed his guess. And he did not miss his guesses; missing guesses was for other people.

The boy himself was on the dock, wrapped in two coats and arguing cheerfully with a stevedore about the correct angle at which a crate should be loaded, an argument the boy appeared to be winning through sheer persistence. He had his sister's dark hair and none of her stillness; he moved like a boy who had never once considered the possibility of being overlooked, which in Greywater, Brandt reflected, was either ignorance or genius.

Tam spotted him and—this was the remarkable part—came over.

"You're the auditor," the boy said. "The one who chased Sable north."

"I am the one who went north on company business, yes."

"Sable says you're like a compass that only points at other people's mistakes."

Brandt considered this. "Your sister," he said, "has a gift for description that I suspect she wastes on bookkeeping."

"She says the same about me. Except about the bookkeeping." Tam stuck out a hand. "I'm Tam. I'm going south. The doctor says if I stay here another winter I'll die, and I said good, one less thing for the company to own, and Sable hit me with a dish towel."

"A proportionate response."

"So you're really letting us go? All of us? Sable too?"

"All of you. The company's claim on your family ended this morning."

Tam studied him with a frankness that would have gotten most people slapped. "You don't seem happy about it."

"I am neither happy nor unhappy. I am complete. There is a difference, and it is the difference between my profession and everyone else's."

"That sounds lonely," said Tam.

Brandt opened his mouth to explain that loneliness was an inefficiency, and found, to his mild horror, that no words came out. The boy waited politely, hands in his pockets, fog beading on his eyebrows, and Brandt closed his mouth again.

"It can be," he said at last.

"Yeah," said Tam, with the enormous authority of sixteen. "That's what I figured."

A whistle blew from the ship. Tam hoisted his bag—one bag, Brandt noted, containing an entire life, which was either poverty or wisdom—and then paused.

"She'll write to me," Tam said. It wasn't a question. "Every week. She's been practicing the letters for days. Ilse keeps telling her the first one doesn't have to be perfect and Sable keeps saying that's exactly why it does." He grinned suddenly, and for a second Brandt saw the whole family in the boy's face—the stubbornness, the precision, the love worn openly where the others kept theirs locked in strongboxes. "Take care of yourself, auditor. Try pointing the compass at your own mistakes sometime. Sable says everybody's got at least one."

"I assure you I have several," Brandt said. "They are simply well-organized."

Tam laughed, all the way up the gangplank, and Brandt stood on the quay and listened to the laugh get smaller until the fog ate it.

* * *

He sailed on the same ship. There had never been any question of staying; Greywater's audit was concluded, its files closed, its mysteries reduced to ash and rumor. He took a small cabin, arranged his papers with geometric precision, and stood at the rail as the tug took the line and the harbor began, degree by degree, to let go of them.

Greywater receded in the fog: the weighing house, the crooked church spire, the long gray arm of the breakwater. Somewhere behind that fog was a woman who had beaten him without cheating, a dying woman who sorted pearls like a queen sorting jewels, a boy laughing his way south toward breathing, and a father watching from a crowd, not asking. Brandt catalogued them all, out of habit, and then—for the first time in his professional life—closed the file without writing a summary.

There was nothing to summarize. That was the whole point of what she'd done. She had taken a story with villains and victims and debts, and burned it down to its honest remainder: people, choosing.

He found that he did not disapprove.

The fog thickened. Greywater went out like a lamp. Brandt turned from the rail, went below, and began—as he always did, as he would always do—to open the files for the next account.

But in his coat pocket, unlogged, unfiled, and unaccounted for, he carried a single sheet of paper he had written the night before and had not yet decided what to do with. It was brief. It read:

*To whom it may concern: The bearer, Sable Nystrom, is the finest keeper of accounts I have encountered in twenty years of practice. Any firm would be fortunate to employ her. Any firm that cheats her will answer to me.*

He had addressed it to no one. He had signed it anyway.

Some entries, he supposed, you wrote simply because they were true—and true things, unlike ledgers, did not require a recipient.

Behind him, Greywater was gone. Ahead, the sea ran south, and somewhere on it, in a cabin two decks down, a boy was already drafting his first letter home.
