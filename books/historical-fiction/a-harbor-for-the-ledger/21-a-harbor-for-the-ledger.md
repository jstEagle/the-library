## 21. A Harbor for the Ledger

The weighing house smelled of brine and old cedar and, for the first week, of Ilse's medicinal smoke—a bitter tangle of dried lungwort and pine pitch that she burned in a tin pail by the door because, she said, "the walls should learn what's killing me before they learn what's saving me."

Sable had not argued with this. One did not argue with Ilse Varric about much of anything, a lesson she'd learned in a rowboat in the dark, somewhere between betrayal and forgiveness, when the distance between those two things turned out to be about four feet of water and one woman saying *I stayed because of you* in a voice that made arithmetic irrelevant.

The deed to the weighing house had cost them nearly everything Sable had left after Tam's berth: her mother's pearl clasp, two years of hoarded wages Meridian had grudgingly released along with her indenture papers, and a promise to the widow Harkness—who owned the building and half the waterfront grudges in Greywater—that they would keep her late husband's brass scale polished "so he can see himself in it and remember what an honest man looked like."

"He was a smuggler," Ilse had said.

"He was an *inaccurate* smuggler," Sable corrected. "There's a difference, and I intend to make my living explaining it."

---

The first morning, they swept out the old company ledgers—Meridian's books, abandoned in the move south like furniture too heavy to love—and Sable stacked them in the corner to be sold as scrap paper. Then she opened a fresh ledger, bound in grey cloth, its pages blank as new ice.

Ilse watched her from the doorway, arms crossed against the cold, breath fogging. "Well? What does it feel like?"

Sable considered the question seriously, because Ilse asked questions seriously. She thought of seven years of entries in other people's ink. Of underweight pearls filed without comment. Of her own name written into columns that treated her as a number with legs.

"It feels like standing on a boat deck at night," she said at last, "when you finally stop gripping the rail."

"Is that good?"

"You find out whether you can swim."

Ilse snorted—which from her was practically applause—and went to light the stove.

---

Word traveled through Greywater the way everything did: slowly in winter, then all at once.

*The Nystrom girl is keeping books at the old weighing house.* *Honest ones, they say.* *Can't be. Nobody's honest for less than Meridian pays.*

Old Pietr the diver came first, hat in his hands, which was unusual; Pietr's hands were usually full of either oysters or grievances. He laid a crumpled tally-slip on the counter—the company's count of his season's haul—and said, "Tell me if I'm mad."

Sable smoothed it flat, read it twice, and felt the familiar cold settle behind her ribs. The slip shorted him eleven pounds of shell. Not enough to fight over. Exactly enough to keep him grateful for whatever they paid.

"You're not mad," she said. "You're eleven pounds poorer, and you've been eleven pounds poorer every season since before I could write."

Pietr stared at her. Then at the fresh ledger. "And what'll it cost me to have it written down proper?"

"Nothing." She dipped her pen. "That's the entire business model. Ilse thinks I'm mad."

"I think you're mad," Ilse confirmed cheerfully from the stove. "But I also think Meridian's auditor once told me that honest books are the most expensive thing in any harbor. We're going to find out if he was right or just dramatic."

"Brandt," Sable said, and the name sat between them a moment—not a wound anymore, exactly. More like a scar that ached before rain. "He wasn't being dramatic. He was being precise. It's worse."

---

By midwinter they had nineteen clients. By deep winter, forty-three.

Not all of them paid in coin. Sable kept a second ledger for barter: smoked fish, mended nets, a jar of honey from the Lindqvist sisters that Sable suspected had been traded twice already and was mostly wax. Ilse teased her about the precision of it all—"You recorded three herring as 'three herring, one of which was optimistic'"—and Sable defended herself with dignity: "An honest ledger records what *is*, not what we wish were there. That herring was aspirational."

Her favorite client was Marta Osei, who ran the fish-gutting shed down the pier and had never once been paid what the company owed her, because the company claimed her tallies were "irregular"—meaning she counted in her own shorthand, which no clerk could be bothered to learn.

Sable learned it in an afternoon.

Marta watched her transcribe the first page, arms folded, eyes narrow as a gull's. "Why?"

"Because your numbers are better organized than mine," Sable said honestly, "and it offends me that nobody noticed."

Something in Marta's face loosened, like ice going out of a harbor. "Seven years," she said quietly. "Seven years they told me I couldn't count."

"They were wrong about the counting," Sable said. "They may have been right about other things. But the counting, no."

---

Some nights, when the stove burned low and Ilse dozed in the chair by the window—her cough quieter now, though neither of them said aloud how quiet, or why that frightened more than comforted—Sable sat with paper and pen and wrote nothing.

She wrote to Tam easily enough. That was the strange part; she'd expected grief to pool around his name, and instead every time she took up the pen, warmth came out instead. She told him about the weighing house, about Pietr's eleven pounds, about Marta's shorthand, about the honey (mostly wax). She told him about Ilse in the way you tell someone something enormous by describing it as ordinary: *She burns that awful lungwort smoke and claims it keeps the walls honest. She has opinions about everything and shares roughly half of them. The half she shares are usually right.*

She mailed the first letter with the coastal packet boat and got a reply in five weeks, Tam's handwriting grown suddenly taller, like the boy writing it:

*Dear Sable — The doctor here says my lungs are "responding," which I have decided is doctor for "you're not dying, stop asking." I got a job carrying parcels. My hands work fine, tell everyone. Tell Ilse I'm holding her to teaching me cards when I'm home. Tell Father nothing yet. I'm not ready and I don't think he's earned it, and both of those things are true at once, which nobody warned me about.*

Sable read that last line four times. *Both true at once.* She copied it onto a scrap and pinned it above the counter, where clients assumed it was some kind of accounting maxim.

In a way, it was.

---

The letter to her father was another matter entirely.

She started it eleven times. She had the false starts to prove it, folded into the stove drawer where Ilse pretended not to look, though Ilse looked at everything—it was her defining trait, along with dying inconveniently and loving stubbornly.

The problem was that every draft began with a question, and every question was a knife wearing a polite coat.

*Do you know what it cost us, believing you dead?*

*Did you ever once, in seven years, write our names in the air and wish them well?*

*Tam is alive because I gave him your berth. Do you understand what that means? Have you done the arithmetic? You, of all people, who taught me that everything balances eventually—did you balance us anywhere in your books?*

On the twelfth attempt, in the blue hour before dawn, with the harbor fog pressing soft against the windows and Ilse asleep in her chair looking younger than her lungs allowed, Sable stopped trying to say the large things.

She wrote:

*Father —

I am keeping the weighing house now. Honest books. The divers come to me and I write down what their shells actually weigh, and you cannot imagine what a revolution it is, or perhaps you can, which is rather the point.

Tam is in the south. His lungs are responding. He says to tell you nothing yet, so consider this letter a violation of instructions, which will surprise you not at all.

I am not writing to forgive you. I don't know yet what I'm writing for. Perhaps just to put your name in a ledger of mine, where it belongs to me now instead of to your debts.

The scale by the window is polished every morning. Widow Harkness insists her husband liked to see himself in it. Some mornings I catch myself doing the same thing — checking that the face looking back is one I recognize, one I chose.

Come spring, the ice goes out. If you write, I will read it. That is all I can enter in this column today. Balances carry forward. Even unfinished ones.

— Sable*

She read it over twice, wept briefly and without drama—one of the skills her father had accidentally taught her, getting through hard things efficiently—and folded it into an envelope.

Then she set it on the shelf by the door, unsealed, and left it there for six days while she decided.

Ilse never mentioned it. On the sixth day, coming back from the packet boat landing with the mail, Ilse simply said, "Cold walk," and handed Sable a cup of coffee, and Sable understood that the letter was gone, sent, and that Ilse had carried it down in her coat pocket against her own heart for six days waiting to be asked—and hadn't asked, because that was Ilse's whole theology: you did not decide other people's entries for them.

"Thank you," Sable said.

"For the coffee."

"For the six days."

Ilse looked at her over the rim of her cup, gray eyes steady, and shrugged. "Balances carry forward," she said. "Even ours."

---

Winter deepened. The business grew teeth.

Meridian noticed, of course—they always noticed; it was the one thing they did reliably. A junior clerk came by in February with a smile like a stapled receipt and suggested, delicately, that the weighing house might prefer to operate *under* company license, for everyone's protection.

Sable served him tea, showed him the license from the harbormaster's office—which Brandt, in his final act of bureaucratic spite-turned-respect, had signed and stamped before sailing, apparently unable to resist closing one last entry properly—and thanked him for his concern.

"And give my regards to the southern office," she added pleasantly. "Tell them the Nystrom girl remembers what an auditor's reference letter is worth. And tell them forty-three clients know exactly what their shells weigh now."

The clerk left quickly. Ilse laughed until she coughed, then laughed again anyway, which Sable had learned was Ilse's policy regarding joy: spend it while the account was open.

---

One evening in late February, snow falling fat and slow past the windows, they closed early and sat together in the lamplight—Sable balancing the day's book, Ilse mending a net she had no intention of using, purely for something to do with her hands.

"Forty-six clients," Ilse said.

"Forty-seven. Marta brought her sister in."

"A dynasty of accurate gutters. Your father must be proud."

The words landed gently, which was how Ilse aimed them now; she'd learned where the bruises were and touched around them. Sable didn't look up from the ledger.

"I don't know if he knows," she said. "About any of it. Whether I'm proud, whether I'm alive in any way that counts. I sent the letter into the dark and the dark hasn't written back."

"The dark writes slow," Ilse said. "It's got the worst postal service in the world. Everything arrives a season late and smelling of salt."

"That's just Greywater."

"That's just *everything*, love."

Sable finished the day's totals—balanced to the penny, naturally, some creeds survived everything—and drew the line beneath them. Outside, the harbor lay black and patient under the snow, and somewhere north of it, past the ice, a diminished man might or might not be holding a letter with her handwriting on it.

She blew out the lamp, and in the dark, Ilse found her hand without looking, the way you find the rail of a boat.

Three days later, the packet boat came in early, fighting the tail of a storm, and the boy who carried the mail sack up the pier was running, which mail boys did not do, and he was shouting her name across the ice-glazed cobbles, which they really did not do—

"Sable! Sable Nystrom! There's a letter—two letters—from the north!"
