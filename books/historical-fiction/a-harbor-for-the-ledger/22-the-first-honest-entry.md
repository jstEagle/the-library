## 22. The First Honest Entry

Spring came to Greywater the way it always did—apologizing.

The ice went out of the harbor on a Tuesday, groaning and cracking like an old man rising from a chair he'd sat in too long, and by Thursday the whole town was pretending winter had been someone else's idea. The fog thinned to gauze. Gulls returned with their opinions. And the weighing house—the old Meridian weighing house, with its crooked scale and its window that had never once in forty years opened all the way—had a new name stenciled above the door in paint Ilse had mixed herself:

**NYSTROM & VARRIC, WEIGHTS AND ACCOUNTS. HONESTLY.**

"Honestly," Sable said, squinting up at it that first morning of April, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"It's a promise and a dare," Ilse said. "I've decided it's both."

"The *Honesty* was also a ship that sank."

"That was the *Honest Emma*, and she sank because her captain was a drunk, not because of her name." Ilse wiped her hands on her apron, leaving two gray crescents. She was thinner than when she'd come off the northern boat, her breath shorter on stairs, but the lung-rot had quieted through the winter the way the doctor in Southport had promised it might, given warmth and dry air and, as Ilse put it, "a reason to be annoyed about dying." She had plenty of reasons now. Most of them were customers.

Sable unlocked the door and breathed in the smell she'd spent seven years breathing in—ink, paper, salt, lamp oil—but it smelled different now. It smelled like a room she owned rather than a cell she occupied. The difference was invisible and enormous, like the difference between water you swim in and water you drown in.

* * *

They had exactly four customers before noon, which for a first day was either a triumph or an indictment depending on how you counted.

Old Marta Hedström brought in her winter catch records, three pages of fish tallies kept on the backs of tar-paper scraps, and watched with open suspicion while Sable entered them into a proper ledger.

"You're charging me for this?" Marta asked.

"A fair rate. Posted on the wall."

"Meridian never charged us to weigh."

"Meridian weighed your catch at eight ounces light on every hundred pounds and called it dampness." Sable didn't look up from the page. "You paid them plenty. You just never saw the bill."

Marta chewed on that like a piece of salt pork. Then she said, "Huh," which in Marta's currency was a standing ovation, and left a smoked herring on the counter that nobody had asked for and that Ilse ate half of before lunch.

The third customer made Sable's hands go still on the pen.

He stood in the doorway a long moment—a broad man in a diver's coat, pearl-roughened hands hanging at his sides—and Sable knew him before she knew his name. Joren Bakker. Her father's oldest diving partner. The man who had rowed out with the search boats the night the *Lark* was reported down, who had carried Tam on his shoulders through that whole terrible winter so a boy could see over the crowd at the harbor.

"Sable Nystrom," he said.

"Bakker." She set down the pen carefully, as if it might go off.

"I heard you'd taken the weighing house." He looked around at it—at the clean ledgers stacked square, the scale freshly trued, the posted rates in Ilse's careful hand—and something moved across his face that wasn't quite a smile and wasn't quite grief. "Your father used to say you'd end up keeping books for somebody. He said it like it was a small thing." He shook his head. "It was never a small thing. None of us could read our own accounts. That's how they had us."

"That's how they had everyone," Sable said.

"He wrote to me. From the north." Bakker said it plainly, no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "Last autumn. I didn't answer. I'm not sure yet what I'd have said to him."

"What would you say now?"

Bakker considered this with the same patience he'd once brought to reading weather off the water. "That I'm glad his daughter's got honest scales. The rest can wait for whatever comes after waiting." He put his catch book on the counter. "Weigh me true, girl. Your father taught you the beds; seems right you should weigh what comes out of them."

She did. It took ten minutes. When he left, he touched his cap to Ilse too, and Ilse said, "Well," in the tone of a woman filing something away, and Sable understood that Ilse would be thinking about that letter for days.

* * *

Tam's letter came on the second Saturday of April, and Sable knew it was from him before she broke the seal, because he still crossed his sevens the way their father did, a habit neither of them could kill.

She read it standing in the doorway of the weighing house with the spring sun warm on the back of her neck, and she read it twice, and then she laughed out loud, alone, in front of God and the harbor, which was not a thing the old Sable had ever done.

*Sister,* it began,

*I am writing this from a chair on an actual veranda, which I have learned is a thing houses can simply have. The doctor here says my lungs sound like "a young man's lungs," and I said they ought to, considering what they cost, and he laughed, though I don't think he knows what I meant.*

*Southport is absurd. There are trees. Actual trees, everywhere, like the town couldn't decide where to put buildings so it gave up halfway. I have gained eleven pounds. I eat oranges like a man with a grudge against scurvy. Mrs. Calloway, my landlady, has a dog named Admiral who has decided I am his personal project and follows me to the pier every morning as if I might forget how to walk without naval supervision.*

*The work is good. I know you'll want specifics, so: I keep the accounts for Harrow & Sons, chandlers, and Mr. Harrow the younger says I am the first clerk he's ever had who found an error before the auditors did. I told him I had excellent training. He thought I meant school. I let him think it.*

*Here is the part you will skip if I don't put it plainly, so I am putting it plainly. I am not angry with you anymore. I want that in ink. You gave me the berth and I spent most of the voyage south being furious about it, and then somewhere past the cape I looked at the water and understood that you looked at that same water your whole life and never once asked it for anything, and I stopped being furious. Mostly. I reserve the right to be furious again at Christmas.*

*Father writes to me. Did you know that? Short letters. He doesn't apologize—he tells me about the nets and the weather and once, memorably, about a fight he won with a walrus, which I do not believe, or believe too much. I write back. I don't know what we are to each other yet. Maybe nothing. Maybe something that takes years, like a bed of pearls—you don't dive it, you tend it, and mostly it gives you nothing, and then one day.*

*I'm mixing my metaphors. Yours, actually. Stealing your metaphors is another thing you can be furious about at Christmas.*

*Come south when you can. Both of you. Ilse especially—I owe her a debt I intend to pay in oranges.*

*Tam*

P.S. *Admiral sends regards, by which I mean he is currently eating my boot.*

Sable read the postscript aloud to Ilse that evening over supper, and Ilse laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that cost her a little cough afterward and was worth it anyway—and said, "Tell him the oranges are accepted as legal tender."

"There's a letter I haven't answered," Sable said, after a while.

Ilse didn't ask which one. She just refilled both their cups and waited, the way she did, with the particular patience of a woman who had delivered letters for a living and knew that some of them traveled slower than others.

Her father's letter had come a week after Tam's. It was short. It was not an apology—he was constitutionally incapable of it, or perhaps just unrehearsed—but it said: *I heard you took the weighing house. Your mother would have liked that. The walrus story is true. — E.N.* And underneath, smaller, as if added after long consideration: *The ledgers were always safe with you. That was the one true thing I built.*

She had read it perhaps forty times. She had not written back. She told herself there was nothing to say yet, which was true, and also that there was everything to say, which was also true, and that the two truths could sit in the same drawer for a while longer, the way pearls sat in a bed, unhurried.

* * *

May came in loud. The herring runs brought the fleet home thick, and the weighing house smelled permanently of brine and ambition. They hired a boy, Piet, fourteen, gap-toothed, quick with figures, whose father had lost a season's wages to Meridian's old arithmetic and who had therefore taken to honesty the way other boys took to piracy.

"Miss Varric," Piet said one afternoon, appearing at Ilse's elbow with the gravity of a much older man, "the Kessler brothers want to know if you'll arbitrate. They're fighting over a share split."

"Are their numbers wrong?"

"One of theirs is. I think it's Henrik's. Henrik always rounds in his own favor."

"Then tell them we'll look at it tomorrow, and charge them the arbitration rate, and Henrik can round *down* for the privilege."

Piet went off glowing. Ilse watched him go with an expression Sable had learned to read as deep satisfaction wearing work clothes.

"You're enjoying this," Sable said.

"Immensely." Ilse leaned against the counter. In the afternoon light her face had color in it that hadn't been there in January. "Do you know what I did, before? Before your father, before the camps?"

"Delivered letters."

"I hid. Same as you, different geography. I was good at being overlooked—it's a skill, and I was proud of it, and it nearly killed me twice." She turned her cup slowly on the counter. "And then a gaunt fool of a man in a fishing camp said, *my daughter keeps perfect books, she'll know what to do with these*, and handed me a bundle of letters, and I thought: perfect books. What a strange inheritance. What a useless-sounding, magnificent thing to give someone." She looked up. "He was right, though. About you. Not about much else, but about that."

"Don't make me sentimental during business hours."

"We closed an hour ago."

"Then make it quick, before I recover."

Ilse laughed again and kissed her, brief and certain, tasting faintly of tea and herring smoke, and outside the window the harbor went on with its evening business—nets coming in, lamps lighting, the fog rolling back in off the water like a tenant who paid no rent but kept the place cool.

* * *

June. The longest day. Sable stayed late after Ilse went up to bed, the way she sometimes did, not from duty anymore but from a pleasure she was still getting used to: the pleasure of a room that was hers, of numbers that told the truth, of a lamp she could burn as late as she liked because the oil was bought with honest money.

On the shelf behind the counter sat the ledgers. A full year of them now—Nystrom & Varric, weights and accounts, honestly. Marta's fish. Bakker's pearls. The Kessler arbitration, settled. Piet's wages, paid on time. Every entry in her own hand, slanted slightly, unmistakably, the hand of a woman who was very much alive.

And beneath the counter, in the drawer where she kept things that were not yet finished, two letters. One from Tam, answered, framed in memory by a chair on a veranda and a dog named Admiral. And one from her father—short, unrepentant, true in exactly one line—which she had not burned and had not answered and did not intend to force. Some letters, she had learned, were not questions requiring answers. They were seeds requiring seasons. She could wait. Waiting, it turned out, was different when you chose it.

She took down a fresh ledger—new cloth cover, uncracked spine, the smell of possibility—and laid it open on the counter under the lamp.

Every ledger she had ever kept had begun the same way, with someone else's name at the top of the page. Meridian Trading Company. Account of: Sable Nystrom, indentured, collateral, held. Her whole life had been entries in other people's books, debits against a debt she hadn't run up, history happening to her in columns she hadn't ruled.

She dipped her pen. Wiped the nib. Thought, briefly, of her father teaching her to hold it—*light grip, girl, the pen isn't a knife*—and of how strange it was that a man could be so wrong about everything and still teach you the only grip that mattered.

At the top of the first page, in her own slanted, unmistakable hand, she wrote:

*Nystrom & Varric. Weights and Accounts.*
*Account of: Sable Nystrom — owner.*

She looked at it a long moment. Owner. Such a small word. It weighed almost nothing, and it held everything.

From upstairs came the creak of Ilse turning over in bed, the familiar rhythm of the house settling around them both. Outside, the tide was coming in, patient and certain, filling the harbor the way it always filled it—not because anyone ordered it, but because that was what harbors were for.

Sable turned the page and began the first entry of the first day of the rest of her accounts.

The pen moved lightly. The numbers came out true.
