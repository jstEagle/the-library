## 1. The Weight of Paper

The city woke to cannon fire the way an old man wakes to rain—without surprise, without much interest, and with a certain resentment about having to get up at all.

Isandro Joubert heard the first boom from the window of a rooming house on the Rue Tissandre, where they had slept four hours with their boots on, their coat folded over a satchel that never left touching distance, and one eye, in some professional sense, open. They lay still and counted. A second report came eleven seconds later. A third, nine seconds after that.

Closer, then. Not close enough to worry about yet. Isandro had a mental map of the siege lines the way other people had mental maps of their own families: detailed, current, and consulted mostly in moments of stress. The guns were two districts east. The Ministry checkpoints had moved in the night—that was worth worrying about—and the bread line outside the bakers' on Tissandre had already formed, which meant it was past five, which meant Isandro was late by their own standards and nobody else's, because nobody else knew what time it was supposed to be.

They got up, washed face and hands from the basin with water gone grey overnight, ate half a heel of bread saved from yesterday, and weighed the satchel in their hand before slinging it across their chest.

This last was habit, but it was also doctrine. Isandro Joubert had three rules, learned over nine years of carrying other people's secrets through a republic in its final illness, and they repeated them each morning like prayers, though Isandro did not believe in prayer and trusted rules instead, on the grounds that rules could be tested.

*Carry nothing personal.*

*Know no one.*

*Weigh everything.*

The third rule was literal as well as philosophical. Every commission had a weight. A sealed letter ran between forty and ninety grams depending on paper stock and wax. A package of documents might reach three hundred. If the satchel ever felt wrong—if it gained weight without explanation or lost it—then something had been added or removed, and Isandro needed to know before anyone else did. Nine years ago, in their first month of work, they had carried a letter that arrived forty grams heavier than when it left. The extra weight had been a second sheet, inserted by a clerk who wanted a message slipped past a censor's eyes without paying courier's rates. The message had been a love note. The recipient had been the wife of a colonel. Isandro had delivered it anyway—it was not their business—but they had noted the clerk, noted the colonel's wife, and noted, above all, the feeling of being used by people who assumed a courier was furniture.

Furniture did not forget. That was the whole trick of being no one.

* * *

The first dispatch of the day went to the Legation of Merelande, on the far side of the river, which meant crossing at the Pont aux Chats because the bridge at Marchette had been barricaded since Tuesday and the ferryman wanted payment in salt.

Isandro moved through streets still blue with early dark, past shuttered shops and the occasional door cracked just wide enough for a face to look out and withdraw. Everyone in the city was choosing sides these days—the Committee against the Assembly, the Assembly against the General, the General against whoever was left after that—and the choosing showed in small ways. In chalked marks on doors. In which neighbors stopped speaking to which. In the way a woman selling chestnuts watched Isandro pass and then, deciding they were nobody, looked away again.

Isandro found this comforting rather than insulting. To be seen as no one, in a city where being someone could get you dragged out of your bed at night, was a kind of armor. It had taken years to build. You built it by walking at an ordinary pace, wearing an ordinary coat, answering questions with the flat politeness of a man perpetually on an errand for his landlord. You built it by never once, in nine years, telling anyone what you did for a living—not even the rooming-house widow, who believed Isandro was a clerk for an insurance concern and pitied them gently for the dullness of it.

At the checkpoint on the Pont aux Chats there were four soldiers and a corporal with a list. The soldiers were young. One of them had his helmet strap undone, which would get him killed someday, though probably not today, and probably not by anything to do with Isandro.

"Papers."

Isandro produced them. Residence permit, work card, a travel chit stamped by a ministry office that technically existed. All genuine, all boring, all bearing the name *Joubert* spelled correctly, because the fastest way to be remembered was to have something wrong in your papers.

"Business?"

"Copies of insurance schedules for delivery," Isandro said, in the tone of a man who found his own life tiresome. "The Merelande legation insures warehouses. Somebody has to carry the schedules."

The corporal glanced up with the expression of a man who has heard the word *insurance* and immediately wants to be doing something else. He waved Isandro through.

On the far side of the bridge, Isandro permitted themselves a small private satisfaction. Not triumph—triumph was memorable—but the quiet pleasure of a lock opening smoothly, of a rule holding under pressure. The legation doorman took the dispatch, signed a ledger without reading it, and gave Isandro a receipt chit. Forty grams. Delivered at 6:20. Two more to go.

The second dispatch was the difficult one.

* * *

It went to a safehouse in the Tanner's Quarter—a narrow house behind a tannery, where the smell alone discouraged visitors, which was presumably the point. The recipient was a woman Isandro knew only as Madame Lys, and *knew* was generous; Isandro had met her twice, exchanged perhaps thirty words total, and intended to keep the account balanced at exactly that figure.

Rule two: *know no one.* This was the rule civilians always found hardest. Surely, they said, you must know your contacts. How else can you trust them? But trust was precisely why couriers must not know. A courier who knew nothing could be caught, searched, beaten, and released, because there was nothing in them to take. A courier who knew faces and names and addresses was a ledger walking around on legs, and ledgers got confiscated.

So Isandro carried to Madame Lys without knowing whether Madame Lys was her name, and received from her without asking what she sent, and both parties were safer for the mutual ignorance, the way two men on opposite ends of a rope are safer if neither knows how high the drop is.

She opened the door before Isandro knocked, which meant she'd been watching the street, which meant she was nervous. She was a small woman with ink stains on her fingers—printer's ink, Isandro noted automatically, and filed it away without curiosity, because curiosity was also weight.

"You're late," she said.

"The bridge at Marchette is barricaded."

"It was barricaded Tuesday."

"And I only learned today." Isandro held out the dispatch. She took it, turned it over, checked the seal with a thumbnail, and frowned.

"This seal is wrong," she said.

Isandro's stomach did not drop, because Isandro's stomach had been trained out of dropping. But attention narrowed, the way it did. "In what way?"

"The wax is the right color but the impression is shallow. Look." She held it toward the light. "Someone pressed it quickly, or with an old matrix. Or—" she paused, "—it's a copy."

"If the seal fails your inspection," Isandro said, "return it to me and I'll return it to my principal with a report. That's the arrangement."

Madame Lys looked at Isandro for a long moment. There was something almost like sympathy in it, which alarmed Isandro more than the seal had. "No," she said finally. "It'll do. Whoever forged it wanted it to pass a casual glance, and mine is casual enough. Take the receipt."

She signed the chit. Isandro took it, inclined their head, and was halfway down the steps when she spoke again.

"Courier."

Isandro stopped. Rule two vibrated faintly, like a plucked string.

"People are saying things will end soon," she said. "The city. The government. All of it. What will you do, when there's nothing left to carry?"

It was, Isandro thought later, the most personal question anyone had asked them in four years, and they resented it thoroughly.

"I expect I'll carry something else," they said. "Good morning."

* * *

The third dispatch was the strange one, and Isandro didn't know it was strange until they were standing in the anteroom of a private house on the Rue de la Fontaine, holding a sealed letter addressed in a hand they recognized.

Minister Corvel. Third time Isandro had carried for him. Corvel paid well, tipped better, and never once asked a courier's name, which in Isandro's private taxonomy made him nearly perfect as a client. His letters were always heavy-ish, always sealed properly, always addressed to people Isandro did not know.

But this one had come through a different channel. Not Corvel's usual secretary—a boy Isandro had dealt with a dozen times—but through old Perrin, Isandro's handler of six years, who had appeared at dawn looking grey as ash and coughing in a way that Isandro, who noticed everything and remarked on little, did not like at all.

"Take this to the Ministry of Order," Perrin had wheezed, pressing the letter into Isandro's hands along with a purse noticeably heavier than the standard rate. "Within nine days. Before the evacuation. Nine days, no later. And Joubert—" he had gripped Isandro's wrist, hard, with fingers that felt like bird bones, "—weigh it first."

Isandro had weighed it. Eighty grams. Correct for its size.

And then Isandro had done the thing they never did: they had looked at the seal. Closely. Under the lamp in Perrin's room, tilting it so the light raked across the wax.

The impression was shallow. The wax color was right, the device correct, the edges crisp—but the depth was wrong, the way a coin struck in haste is wrong, and Isandro had spent nine years handling seals the way musicians handle instruments, and their fingers knew.

A forgery. On a letter ordering delivery to the Ministry itself, within nine days, with double pay.

Perrin had watched them examine it and said nothing, which was its own message. Then he had coughed, sat back against his pillows—he had been in bed, which was new, Perrin was never in bed—and said, "Payment buys passage, Joubert. Out of the city, wherever you want to go, when this all comes down. That's what the extra is for. Don't ask me anything else, because I don't know anything else, and if I did I'd be too tired to tell you."

He had looked, Isandro thought now, walking toward the Rue de la Fontaine, like a man apologizing in advance.

Now here was the problem, and Isandro turned it over as they walked, the way they turned everything over, patiently, weighing it. A forged seal on a ministerial letter was not necessarily sinister. Half the functioning documents in this dying republic were forged by somebody or other; seals were copied the way recipes were copied. Perhaps Corvel's secretary had pressed the matrix hastily. Perhaps the matrix itself was worn. Perhaps—

Perhaps none of that, said a colder voice, the voice that kept Isandro alive. Perhaps a dying handler had been made to hand off a false order, with double pay attached, to the one courier reliable enough to deliver it without question.

And the destination wasn't a person this time. It was an institution. *The Ministry of Order.* Deliveries to institutions could be intercepted, read, rerouted by anyone inside them. Deliveries to institutions were how you made a piece of paper disappear into a machine, or made a machine move on the strength of a piece of paper.

Eighty grams. Isandro flexed their fingers around the satchel strap. Weigh everything. Everything included motives.

The sensible course was to return to Perrin and refuse the commission. But Perrin was in bed and grey, and might be dead tomorrow, and if he died while Isandro held an undelivered commission, the purse would be reclaimed by whoever inherited Perrin's ledger, along with questions Isandro very much did not want asked. Questions were weight too. Questions accumulated.

And underneath the calculation, quieter, was the thing Isandro admitted only in the privacy of their own skull: passage out. Real papers, real exit, a life beyond the siege lines before the city finished falling. Nine years of being no one had bought safety but not a future. The purse in the satchel was the first future anyone had offered Isandro Joubert in the entire span of their working life.

They walked past the bakery, past the chestnut seller, past the barricade going up on the corner with much shouting and little competence. The city was choosing sides all around them, loudly, fatally, and Isandro Joubert walked through the middle of it belonging to none of them, carrying eighty grams of probable treachery, and told themselves—as they had told themselves every morning for nine years—that this was comfort. That invisibility was peace. That the loneliest place in a burning city was also the safest, and safety was enough, and had been enough, and would continue to be enough.

The lie sat comfortably. It had practice.

They turned onto the Street of Presses because it was the fastest route to the Ministry quarter—only that, only geography—and stopped without meaning to.

The Vasseur workshop stood where it had always stood, third door past the corner, its windows fogged with steam from the drying sheets, the old wooden sign with the press-and-dove carved into it gone soft-edged with weather. Light burned behind the frosted glass. Somewhere inside, faintly, the rhythmic knock of a hand press at work—*clunk, squeak, clunk*—a sound Isandro hadn't heard in fourteen years and recognized the way one recognizes a mother's heartbeat: instantly, uselessly, somewhere below the level of decision.

In the doorway stood a young woman arguing with a ministry inspector. Dark hair pinned up carelessly, sleeves rolled, ink to the elbows, gesturing at the inspector with a composing stick as though daring him to make her put it down. The inspector's back was to Isandro. The woman's face was in profile, and fourteen years fell off Isandro's life like a coat dropped from the shoulders.

Odile. Odile Vasseur, who had been nine years old when Isandro left, who had followed them to the end of the street shouting a word Isandro had spent fourteen years pretending not to hear.

Isandro stepped back into the shadow of a doorway, heart performing entirely unprofessional behavior, and stood there in the cold with eighty grams of treachery against their ribs, watching a lit window they had sworn never to look at again.

The press knocked on inside, steady as a pulse. The inspector raised his voice. Odile raised hers higher.

Nine days, Perrin had said.

Isandro pulled their collar up, made themselves walk past the workshop at an ordinary pace, at an ordinary stride, wearing an ordinary face—and did not look back, except once, which was once too many.
