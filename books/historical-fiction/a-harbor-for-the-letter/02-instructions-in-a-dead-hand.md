## 2. Instructions in a Dead Hand

The rain had been falling on the capital for eleven days, and Isandro Joubert had learned to read it the way other people read newspapers. A thin drizzle meant the checkpoints would be bored and thorough. A proper downpour meant the soldiers huddled under their awnings and waved you through with a shout. Today's rain was the second kind — generous, almost friendly — and so Isandro stood under the eaves of the fishmonger's on Rue Delacour and waited for it to slacken, because the third rule of carrying was that you never arrived at a door wet.

Not that anyone had written down the rules. There was no guild, no ledger, no school for what Isandro did. The rules had accumulated over nine years the way silt accumulates in a harbor: one grain at a time, each deposited by some small disaster that had happened to somebody else.

Carry nothing personal. Know no one. Weigh everything.

And the fourth rule, which Isandro had added themselves after the winter of the bread riots: *never let them see your face long enough to describe it.*

Isandro had gotten very good at being forgettable. It was not modesty; it was craft. They wore a coat of that particular gray-brown that exists nowhere in nature and therefore nowhere in memory. They walked at a pace that was neither hurried nor leisurely, the pace of a person running an errand so dull that even they had forgotten it. When soldiers asked questions, Isandro answered in a flat provincial accent borrowed from a town they'd passed through once at nineteen, and they always gave answers slightly more boring than the question required.

*Where are you coming from?* The tanner's, with a delivery receipt. Here, look, ink-stained and dull.

*Who sent you?* My employer, who doesn't pay me enough to remember his name.

Soldiers did not want interesting answers. Interesting answers meant paperwork, and paperwork meant standing in the rain, and no man in a wet uniform has ever wanted paperwork. Boredom, Isandro had learned, was a kind of armor. Possibly the best kind. Cheaper than steel, certainly, and easier to maintain.

The rain thinned to a mist. Isandro pulled up their collar, checked that the three dispatches from yesterday's run were truly delivered and their pockets truly empty — an empty courier is an innocent courier — and set off through the flooded streets toward the Pont aux Herbes.

Marette lived above a shuttered chandlery on the far side of the canal, in a room Isandro had been visiting every month for two years without either of them ever acknowledging that the visits had a purpose beyond tea. Marette had been a handler back when handlers were called something else, back when the republic still pretended it would outlive everyone currently drawing breath inside it. She was seventy-one now, or seventy-three — she adjusted her age depending on whether it made a better story — and she had a cough that had stopped pretending to be a cough around midsummer.

Isandro climbed the stairs two at a time out of habit, then slowed on the last flight out of respect. There was no sense arriving breathless at the bedside of a dying woman. It struck a false note, like wearing your good coat to a funeral you intend to rob.

* * *

"You're late," Marette said, by way of good morning.

"I'm early," Isandro said. "You're dying ahead of schedule."

She laughed, which turned into the cough, which she rode out with the grim patience of a woman who had survived two purges, one bombardment, and forty years of men explaining things to her. She sat propped against pillows that had once been white and were now the color of old candle smoke. The room smelled of camphor and cold tea. On the table beside her: a pot, a single cup — she had stopped offering Isandro tea months ago, another thing neither of them acknowledged — and a letter.

The letter lay face-up on the table like a cat that knows it isn't supposed to be there.

Isandro looked at it for exactly as long as politeness allowed, which is to say they looked at everything else in the room first and only then let their eyes rest on it, casually, the way one glances at a stranger's newspaper on a train.

It was sealed. That alone was unusual. Most commissions came to Isandro as verbal instructions, memorized and repeated back twice, because paper could be found on a body and a body could be found in a canal. But Marette's commissions had always come with paper when the client insisted, and the client, whoever he was this time, had clearly insisted.

"Before you ask," Marette said, "yes, it pays."

"It usually pays."

"It pays well." She said it the way a physician says *the tumor appears benign*, meaning: I have checked, and I am telling you what you need to hear, and we both know the checking was imperfect.

Isandro sat down on the one chair, the one with the broken cane seat that bit into you if you shifted weight, which Marette considered a feature. "Who's the client?"

"A clerk. Ministry-adjacent." She waved a hand that encompassed the entire rotten edifice of the government. "He came Tuesday. Nervous type. Sweated on my floorboards. Paid half in advance, which in my experience means either honesty or desperation, and this week in this city, the difference hardly matters."

"And the delivery?"

"Ministry of Order. Ninth Arrondissement. Within nine days." She held up her fingers, all of them, then four more, an obscene little fan. "After that, the evacuation starts and the Ministry seals itself, and whatever's in that letter stops mattering to anyone but the people who wanted it delivered. So: nine days. Then you're free."

Free. Isandro turned the word over. Free to do what? The city was a pot coming slowly to boil, and everyone who could leave was leaving, and leaving cost money, and money was the one thing a courier never kept, because keeping money meant having money meant being worth robbing. But passage out — real passage, a berth on a ship or a seat in the last convoy south — cost more than Isandro had seen in a year of carrying. And Marette knew it. That was why the letter paid well. She was buying Isandro's exit with someone else's errand, and dressing it up as generosity, and both of them understood that too.

"Marette," Isandro said carefully. "How sick are you?"

"Sick enough that I'm not going to tell you." She reached for the letter, and her hand — Isandro noticed, and hated noticing — trembled on the way across the table. Not much. Just enough. "Take it. Read nothing. Ask nothing. You know the trade."

"I know the trade," Isandro agreed, and took it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about handling sealed letters for a living: your hands learn things your eyes are forbidden to know.

Weight first. Isandro weighed the letter in their palm before it left Marette's table — a courier's habit, pure reflex, the same way a farrier taps a hoof. This one was heavy for its size. Two sheets, maybe three, folded into quarters, plus something small and stiff that slid when tilted. A card. An invitation, perhaps, or a name.

Then texture. Good paper. Better than good — laid stock, the kind with faint parallel lines running through it that caught the light like watermarks of watermarks. Nobody in the Ministry of Order wrote on laid stock. Ministry clerks wrote on the gray municipal paper by the ream, paper so cheap it went soft in damp weather, and half the time they wrote on both sides to save it. This letter had been bought at a stationer's, deliberately, expensively, by someone who cared how it looked.

Which meant it was meant to be seen. Which meant its appearance was doing work.

Isandro carried it down the stairs and out into the street and did not open it, because reading a commission's contents was the fastest way Isandro knew to stop being a courier and start being a conspirator, and conspirators got shot. But hands learn things, and eyes notice even when instructed not to, and by the time Isandro reached the corner of Rue Delacour again, the unease had settled in — quiet, professional, familiar. The feeling of a stair that gives slightly more than it should. Nothing broken yet. Just built wrong.

They stopped under the awning of the fishmonger's, ostensibly to watch the rain, actually to examine the seal.

Every ministry had its own wax. This was not vanity; it was bureaucracy, which is a kind of vanity with a budget. The Ministry of Order used a dark red wax with a fine grain, pressed with a matrix cut in the old style — deep lines, sharp shoulders, the eagle's wing feathers rendered individually because the original die-cutter had been a perfectionist and the Republic had never paid to have him replaced. Isandro had handled perhaps two hundred Ministry-sealed letters over nine years. Their thumbs knew that eagle the way a priest's lips know a psalm.

This seal was close. Very close. The color was right, the size was right, the eagle spread his wings at exactly the correct span.

But the wing feathers were smooth. Merged. The die that had pressed this seal was a copy made from a copy — cast, probably, off an impression rather than the original matrix, which is how every counterfeiter does it and why every counterfeit is slightly soft, slightly melted, slightly *wrong*, the way a face is wrong in a poor mirror.

Isandro stood under the awning and felt the rain-fresh air go cold in their lungs.

A forged seal. On a letter bound for the Ministry of Order itself — the one building in the city where forged seals went to die, where clerks whose entire careers consisted of examining wax would hold this letter up to the light and see, within a minute, exactly what Isandro's thumb had seen in ten seconds.

Either the sender didn't know the difference. Or the sender didn't care. Or—

Or the letter was never meant to arrive. Or it was meant to arrive and be questioned. Or it was meant to pass through a dozen intermediate hands, none of whom examined seals, before it reached the one desk that did, at which point the confusion would be someone else's problem entirely.

There were too many *ors*. Isandro disliked letters with too many *ors*. Honest correspondence was a straight road. This was a maze with the exit drawn in pencil.

They thought about taking it back upstairs.

They thought about Marette's trembling hand, and the pot of tea poured for one, and the way she had said *sick enough that I'm not going to tell you*, which was Marette for *I will be dead before this letter matters, so whatever happens, it cannot touch me, and I have arranged it so that it cannot quite touch you either — probably.*

And they thought about the passage. Nine days' pay plus the balance Marette had described — enough for a berth south, out of the city before the boiling point, out of the story entirely. Isandro had spent nine years being no one in a city where everyone was choosing sides, and the whole trick of it, the whole *art* of it, depended on being gone before the sides finished choosing. You could not be invisible in a city that had burned. Ash remembered everyone.

The fourth rule: *never let them see your face.* The fifth rule, unwritten until now, forming itself in the rain-smell air: *know when the city is about to make remembering mandatory.*

"Damn it, Marette," Isandro said softly, to no one, and tucked the letter into the inner pocket where it lay against their chest like a small warm animal with unclear intentions.

Professionalism, after all, was mostly just fear wearing a neat coat. Isandro buttoned theirs up to the chin and walked.

* * *

That evening, in the rented room that was not home but was at least reliably empty, Isandro did the arithmetic.

The advance was already spent — half to Marette's landlady, who had quietly become her nurse and deserved it, and half to the harbormaster's cousin, who sold berths the way priests sold absolution, on credit against the soul. The balance on delivery would cover the rest. Nine days. Deliver on day eight, collect, be at the docks by dawn on day nine, and be a rumor on the tide before the evacuation sealed the gates.

It was a clean plan. Isandro turned it over looking for flaws and found only the letter itself, sitting on the table under the lamp, radiating its expensive paper smell like a guest who has overstayed.

Two sheets and a card, they estimated. Heavy wax. Laid stock. Forged eagle.

"You're a lie," Isandro told it. "Somebody dressed you up to look like truth, and you're going to the one house in this city that checks costumes at the door."

The letter said nothing, being paper, but Isandro had carried enough paper to know that silence from a letter is never really silence. Every document is a message from someone who couldn't be there — and the ones that travel sealed are messages from someone who very much does not want to be there when it's opened.

Isandro banked the lamp low, lay down clothed, as always, one boot off and one boot on, as always, and listened to the city. Distant on the wind: a crowd, somewhere, chanting something with a rhythm too steady to be spontaneous. A church bell ringing the hour and then, unaccountably, ringing it again — some sexton's error, or some signal. The rain starting again against the shutters, thinning toward drizzle. Tomorrow's checkpoints would be bored and thorough.

Nine days. Eight of work and one of escape. Carry it, collect, vanish. Simple.

Isandro fell asleep rehearsing the route — north bridge avoided, the quay road after dark, the ninth arrondissement approached from the east where the guard rotated at dusk — and if the route, as it happened to do, ran along the southern edge of the printing quarter, past a certain street where the air still smelled faintly of ink and oil and a childhood Isandro had spent nine years not thinking about, well. Routes were routes. Geography did not consult your feelings. The shortest line between a dying woman's room and the Ministry of Order happened to pass within fifty paces of a workshop with green shutters and a sign shaped like a compositor's stick, and no power on earth could change that, and Isandro told themselves it changed nothing at all.

They dreamed, briefly, of a girl with ink to her elbows laughing at them over a composing stone, and woke before the dream could say anything, and got up, and made tea in the dark, and weighed the letter one last time in their palm.

Heavy. Two sheets and a card.

On the morning of the first day, with eight remaining and the city holding its breath, Isandro Joubert put the forged seal next to their heart and went out to deliver it — walking, as always, at the pace of a person running an errand so dull they'd forgotten it.

Behind them, up the hill, the presses of the printing quarter had begun, somewhere in the night, to turn.
