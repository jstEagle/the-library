## 3. The Street of Presses

There are two ways to cross the city from the river quarter to the Ministry of Order, and Isandro had walked both so many times that they thought of them not as streets but as sentences—one long, one short, both ending in the same punctuation.

They took the long one. The short one ran through the Place des Martyrs, where a crowd had gathered around a man selling roasted chestnuts and revolution in equal measure, and Isandro had learned years ago that crowds were weather: you did not argue with them, you simply got wet. The long way curved along the canal, past the tanneries, through the quarter where the air always smelled faintly of ink and lye, and it was emptier. Emptiness was a currency Isandro understood.

The letter rode against their chest in its oilcloth sleeve, sewn into the lining of a coat that had been chosen precisely because it looked like every other coat on the street. That was the whole art of it, really. A courier's genius was not speed or nerve or memory—any fool could run fast, any drunk could be brave, and memory was a liability you paid to have removed. No. A courier's genius was *unremarkableness*. To walk like a man going nowhere important. To sweat like a man who sweats anyway. To be, in the eyes of every checkpoint clerk and street boy and bored sergeant, a person whose name would slide off the mind like rain off waxed canvas.

Isandro had been practicing unremarkable for eleven years. They were very good at it. Sometimes, alone at night in whatever rented room they could afford, they wondered if it was the only thing they were good at—and then they stopped wondering, because wondering led to other questions, like *who are you when no one is asking you to carry anything*, and those questions had never once bought them dinner.

The city was worse than last week. That was saying something, because last week the bread queues had stretched past the second fountain and a woman had thrown a turnip at a tax collector with what witnesses described as remarkable accuracy. This week there were soldiers at the canal bridge—not garrison soldiers, who were merely bored, but Ministry men in gray coats with the new armbands, checking papers with the particular slowness of men who had recently discovered they were allowed to say no.

Isandro joined the line and became furniture. They studied the water. They thought about nothing, magnificently. A graycoat worked down the queue, and when he reached Isandro he asked the usual question—"Business?"—and Isandro gave the usual answer, which was true in shape even when false in fact: "Errands."

The soldier glanced at the coat, at the hands, at the face that offered itself the way an empty room offers itself. "Move along."

*Errands.* It was beautiful, really, how much of civilization could be crossed with one dull word.

Past the bridge, the canal bent north, and Isandro felt it before they saw it—the way you feel a scar ache before rain. The smell changed first. Lye gave way to lampblack, lampblack to the sweetish reek of hot lead and fresh paper, and then the street opened up and there it was: the Rue des Imprimeurs, the Street of Presses, where half the buildings still bore the ghosted names of shops dead thirty years and the other half rattled and thumped like great iron hearts, printing everything from prayer books to pamphlets to, this season, evacuation schedules for people who could afford to evacuate.

Isandro should have taken the turning before it. There *was* a turning before it—a little alley called the Passage Saint-Aubin that shaved four minutes off the route and smelled considerably worse. Eleven years of discipline said: take the alley.

Isandro's feet kept walking straight.

It is a strange thing, the body's treachery. You can train it to lie to soldiers, to starve politely, to sleep sitting upright against a wall in three different capitals—but you cannot train it to forget the street where you learned to read by pulling letters out of type cases, tasting each one, because Grandfather Vasseur said a compositor must know his alphabet in his fingers before his eyes, and small children learn with their mouths.

There it was. Number nine, Rue des Imprimeurs. The Vasseur workshop.

The sign had been repainted—the old gold leaf worn to a whisper, new black letters over it, slightly crooked, which would have made Grandfather grind his teeth: *VASSEUR & FILS, IMPRIMEURS*. Except there was no *fils*, had never properly been a *fils*, since Odile ran the press these days and Odile was most certainly not a fils, and Isandro found a small, absurd comfort in the crookedness. Someone in that house still did things slightly wrong on purpose.

Eleven years since Isandro had stood on this cobbles. Fourteen years old, one canvas bag, and the words still hanging in the air behind them like the smell of burnt rag paper: *You are no son of this house.* Not that Isandro had ever been a son of anything, strictly speaking, but precision had never been the family's strong suit when they were angry. Father had done the disowning. Mother had cried in the kitchen where she thought no one could hear, and Grandfather had said nothing at all, which at fourteen Isandro had taken for agreement and at twenty-five had begun, in the bad hours, to suspect was something else entirely.

And then Isandro had done what abandoned things do. They had become excellent at not being seen. They had found the couriers' guild—or rather, the guild's loose, deniable edge of it—and discovered that a person with no name, no faction, and no face was worth more than a person with all three. Carry nothing personal. Know no one. Weigh everything. Three rules, learned young, kept absolutely.

Except today, apparently, when their feet had decided to commit treason against all three at once.

A knot of people stood outside number nine. Isandro's professional instincts woke instantly, cataloguing: two neighbors pretending to sweep already-swept steps; a boy of maybe ten, chewing a nail; and at the workshop door, in the gray-and-black of the Ministry of Order, an inspector—middle-aged, well-fed, holding a document case the way priests hold reliquaries.

And facing him, arms folded, chin up, stood a woman Isandro recognized before recognition finished being painful.

Odile.

She had been eleven when Isandro left. She was—what now? Twenty-two? Twenty-three? She had her mother's stubborn jaw and her father's way of standing as though the ground owed her rent. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow and her forearms were black to the wrist with ink, and there was a smear of it across one cheekbone like a dueling scar, and she was talking to the inspector in the tone of a woman explaining arithmetic to a particularly slow child.

"—and I'm telling you," Isandro heard her say as they drifted close enough, hood up, eyes down, one more unremarkable body among six, "that the inventory list your office requested was delivered on the fourth. Delivered. By hand. Signed for. If your office has lost it, that is a tragedy for your office, but I fail to see how it becomes a tragedy for mine."

"The ministry," the inspector said, with the patience of a man quoting scripture to a heretic, "has reason to believe certain materials are being produced at this address without license."

"Certain materials." Odile smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of someone about to make a point with a hammer. "Inspector, we print almanacs. We print funeral notices—we're very busy with funeral notices lately, everyone's dying to use us. And we print the *Canal District Gazette*, four pages, circulation six hundred, of which perhaps four hundred people can read. If the republic's enemies are hiding somewhere in our columns, they are hiding between 'Widow Perrin mourns her husband' and the price of tallow, and honestly, they deserve whatever happens to them."

A neighbor made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole.

"Nevertheless." The inspector held out his hand. "I'll have a look at your presses."

"You'll have a look at my *ledger*," Odile said. "My presses are working. Every hour you spend poking at them is an hour of the Gazette late, and then six hundred people will know exactly whom to blame, because I will print your name, Inspector. In full. With your title. I have lovely big type for names."

Isandro felt something move in their chest—an old muscle waking up, rusty with disuse. Pride, probably. Or terror. With the Vasseurs the two had always been printed on the same page.

The inspector looked at her for a long moment. Then, visibly deciding that a widow's almanac was not worth a report that might reflect back on himself, he snapped the document case shut. "This isn't finished, madame."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "Nothing is, these days."

He strode off toward the canal. The neighbors resumed sweeping with the guilty energy of people returning to a play after intermission. The boy spat out his nail.

And Odile Vasseur stood alone on her own doorstep, and Isandro watched her shoulders come down—not much, a centimeter, maybe two—and watched her press the backs of her ink-black wrists against her eyes, just for a moment, just until her face was safe again, and then she squared herself, pushed open the door, and went inside to her presses.

Isandro stood across the street feeling as though someone had pulled a single thread and the whole coat of their life was quietly unraveling.

She didn't know them. Of course she didn't. She'd been eleven; Isandro had been gone within the week; and besides, Isandro had spent eleven years becoming nobody. That had been the point. That had been the *achievement*. They had built a life out of not being looked at, and here they stood, looking—hungry, furious, grieving all over again for a house they told themselves daily they did not want.

*You are no son of this house.*

Then why, Isandro asked the cobbles, does the house still know exactly how to hurt me?

Because that was the thing about the Street of Presses. Everything here got set in type eventually. Grief, love, betrayal—you fed it into the stick, the compositor locked it up tight, and it came out the other side permanent, multiplied, readable by strangers two hundred years from now. Isandro had fled thinking distance would blur the letters. It hadn't. Eleven years of checkpoints and forged seals and sleeping in rooms rented by the week, and the words were still sharp-edged, still inked deep, still legible every time Isandro closed their eyes.

They made themself walk. One step. Another. Past number nine without turning their head, which took more discipline than crossing any checkpoint in the republic.

But at the corner of the Passage Saint-Aubin, Isandro stopped. Because the letter lay against their chest—heavy, sealed, addressed to the Ministry of Order, due within nine days—and because the seal, though Isandro had not yet dared examine it closely, had struck them wrong from the first moment, wrong in some small way they couldn't yet name, the way a compositor knows a letter is set upside-down before knowing which one.

And because the Ministry of Order lay ten minutes further along this same street, and the Vasseur workshop lay behind them, and for eleven years Isandro had arranged their entire existence so that the two things would never share a map.

They stood at the corner a long moment, one foot pointed toward the alley, one heart still parked outside number nine.

Then Isandro pulled their collar up, became once more the person whose name slides off minds like rain off canvas—and turned, instead of toward the alley, back down the Rue des Imprimeurs, walking slowly, walking nowhere, walking past the crooked black letters of *VASSEUR & FILS* one more time.

Just to look. Just to weigh it. That was all. Weighing was allowed. Weighing was rule three.

Inside the workshop, muffled by stone and door, the great press began to thump—steady, patient, alive—like a heartbeat Isandro had spent eleven years pretending they couldn't hear.
