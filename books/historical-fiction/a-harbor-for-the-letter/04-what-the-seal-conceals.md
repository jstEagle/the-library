## 4. What the Seal Conceals

The inspector's boots were the first thing Odile heard—too polished for the street, too confident for the times. Everything else in the city had begun to scuff and shuffle and apologize for itself, but these boots came down the Street of Presses as if the cobblestones had been personally arranged for their benefit.

She was at the front counter, arguing with a delivery boy about a bill for glue that had never been delivered, when the boots stopped outside the Vasseur workshop door. She knew the sound the way sailors know weather.

"Papa," she said, without raising her voice, the way one might remark that it had begun to rain. "Company."

Her father, Rémy Vasseur, looked up from the composing stone where he had been pretending, for the benefit of any passerby, to set a wedding announcement. He was in fact setting a wedding announcement—there was a genuine wedding on Thursday, the Ferreol girl to a fishmonger's son—but beneath the tray of wedding type, in the shallow drawer built into the stone itself, lay four pages that would have gotten the whole household hanged if the inspector read them standing up.

"Company," Rémy repeated. "How many?"

"One. Ministry gray coat. The tall one with the mustache like a dead moth."

"Berthier." Her father sighed with the weariness of a man greeting an old debt. "He's been promoted. Last month he could only count to two."

The knock came anyway, because inspectors are not men who wait to be imagined. Odile opened the door with her best expression—the one she called her flour face, blank and helpful and faintly dim, learned from her mother, who had used it on tax collectors, bailiffs, and one memorable archbishop.

"Monsieur Berthier. What an honor. We were just closing."

"At midday?" Berthier peered past her into the workshop, where the great press stood with its iron arm raised like a patient ox.

"The glue," Odile said. "There's been a shortage. We can't bind, so we can't print, so we close early and pray." She let her eyes go wide and pious. "We pray a great deal these days."

Berthier's mustache twitched. He did not believe a word of it, but belief was not strictly his department. His department was inventory, and he stepped inside with the air of a man doing everyone a favor by breathing their air.

* * *

The workshop smelled the way it had smelled for sixty years—ink and oil and the ghost of a thousand arguments conducted in type. Odile's grandfather had built the press before the republic was a republic, back when printing was still half magic and half contraband, and the family had been arguing ever since about whether the press or the republic was older and therefore owed what to whom. Odile had always held that the press came first, which made the republic a kind of child, and children, whatever else you owed them, could not be permitted to burn down the house.

She kept up the flour face while Berthier wandered. Her father drifted to the composing stone and stood in front of it with the studied casualness of a man guarding a drawer full of treason.

"You've been busy," Berthier observed, tapping a stack of finished work. Funeral notices. Bread ration announcements. A playbill for a theater that had burned down in the spring and was, in the opinion of most of the city, the only honest institution left.

"Funerals mostly," Odile said. "Business is very good in funerals. Would you like to place an order? We give a discount to the Ministry."

Her grandfather, Ancel Vasseur, eighty-one years old and built like the typecase he'd slept beside for six decades, chose that moment to come down the stairs. He did this deliberately. Odile could see the deliberation in it—the slow hand on the rail, the theatrical pause on the fourth step. Ancel had survived three governments and one genuine firing squad by the simple method of being too old to threaten and too stubborn to die, and he deployed his age the way other men deployed artillery.

"Berthier," he said. "You've gotten tall. Or the government has gotten short. It's hard to tell which, from down here."

"Master Vasseur." Berthier inclined his head with genuine respect. Everyone respected Ancel; it was a citywide affliction. "I'm conducting a survey of printing establishments. New regulations. All materials to be logged. All editions to be submitted for review before distribution."

"Review," Ancel said, tasting the word. "In my day we called that censorship, and we printed it on the broadsheets to complain about it."

"In your day, Master Vasseur, you also printed a pamphlet calling the governor a turnip."

"A slander. It was a squash. The engraver took liberties."

Odile bit the inside of her cheek. This was the family disease: they could not be afraid without making jokes, and they could not make jokes without making danger, and the whole household went around and around the wheel like that, laughing and sweating, until the danger got bored or the jokes ran out.

Berthier was not bored yet. He moved along the shelves, reading spines, and Odile's stomach performed a slow, cold rotation, because she knew exactly what was in the back room. Her father's work. Four pages, set in the small hours over the past week, run off in the dark on paper they could ill afford: an account of the granary shipments that had left the city in the night while the bread lines grew, with dates, with weights, with the names of the warehouses and the men who signed for them. No accusations. Just numbers, which were worse than accusations, because numbers could not be shouted down.

Three hundred copies. Hidden, at present, in the one place in the city no inspector would ever search with any seriousness: the paper chest in the back room, beneath a layer of the wedding announcements, because no one in the history of governments had ever been curious about a wedding.

"Back room," Berthier said. Not a question.

"Storage," Odile said. "Paper, glue, the mold for the ornaments, my grandmother's chair. She died before the republic. Even the chair predates the regulations."

Berthier smiled at that, which was the worst thing yet. He walked toward the back room, and Odile's father moved with him, talking—about the glue shortage, about the price of ink, about the lamentable state of the roads—and Odile understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that she had perhaps ninety seconds and one distracted inspector to become a different kind of person than she had been at breakfast.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it is mostly logistics.

Odile had imagined, in the nights since her father first showed her the granary pages, that if the moment came it would arrive with trumpets. She had imagined herself standing firm, chin up, some fine sentence ready. Instead the moment arrived in the form of a gray coat walking toward a paper chest, and what she actually did was knock over a shelf.

She did it with her hip, which was the only part of her that could be relied upon in a crisis, and she did it thoroughly. A cascade of ornament molds—doves, laurels, the little printer's fist that had been the shop's mark since before her grandfather could walk—went across the floor with a crash like the end of the world in miniature.

"Grandmother's chair!" she cried, with genuine feeling, because the chair had in fact gone over, and because a woman who mourns a chair is a woman nobody suspects of anything. She dropped to her knees among the wreckage and began gathering molds at a speed that would have shamed her at any other time.

Berthier stopped. He was, Odile observed from the floor, at heart a tidy man, and tidy men cannot walk past disorder. It is their one great weakness and the reason they are given uniforms.

"Clumsy," he said.

"Grief," Odile said, holding up the printer's fist. "It makes the hands stupid. You'll understand when the government falls."

"Odile," her father said, in the voice he saved for her worst jokes.

But it worked. Berthier crouched—actually crouched, gray coat and all—and handed her the laurel mold, and for a moment they were just two people kneeling in a mess, and in that moment Odile's father slid the drawer of wedding type across the stone with a sound like a whisper, and the back room stood between Berthier and the chest with nothing but a chair and a dead woman's reputation to defend it.

Berthier looked at the back room doorway. He looked at it for a long time. Odile counted his breaths, because she had nothing else to count.

Then Ancel said, from the front, "Boy, if you're going to inventory us, inventory us. I'm eighty-one and I'd like to get my nap in before the next government."

And Berthier, who respected Ancel like everyone else, and who was in any case not paid enough to die of curiosity, stood up, brushed off his coat, and produced his ledger. "Wedding announcements, funeral notices, ration postings, one playbill. That's the lot?"

"That's the lot," said Rémy.

"Sign here." He held out the pen, and Odile signed her father's name with a steadiness that surprised her, and Berthier put on his hat, and at the door he paused and said, "Master Vasseur. The squash joke. I've always thought it underrated."

Then the boots went away down the Street of Presses, polished and confident, and the family stood in the wreckage of the ornament shelf and did not move until the sound of them was gone entirely.

* * *

They held the argument that evening, in the kitchen above the shop, which was where the family had held every important argument since the republic was young. Odile's mother had died in that kitchen; her grandmother had died in it; the table had been repaired so many times it was mostly memory held together with pegs.

"Three hundred copies," said Odile's aunt Marguerite, who had married into the family and never once stopped being sensible about it. "Three hundred copies, in a city of two hundred thousand. What do three hundred papers do?"

"They get read," said Odile.

"By three hundred people."

"By three hundred people who tell six hundred people. That's how printing works, Aunt. That's the whole invention. That's why they're frightened of us and not of the criers."

"They're not frightened of us," Marguerite said. "That's my whole point. We are a shop. We are glue debts and wedding announcements and a chair. The moment we print those pages, we are traitors, and traitors get their shops burned, and I have one coat that fits the cold weather."

"The city is going to burn anyway," Odile said. "Have you looked out the window? The granaries are empty and the soldiers are leaving and the man who emptied the granaries is about to be the government, and everyone will call it order because it will arrive in uniform." She heard her own voice getting high and made herself stop, made herself sit down, made herself say the rest quietly. "Papa set those pages at night. For a week. I watched him do it. He's sixty-three and he can't see the type without the lamp anymore, and he did it anyway. I'm not going to be the one who tells him it was for nothing."

Rémy, who had been silent at the head of the table, said, "It wouldn't be for nothing. It would be for something. That's what frightens me. Nothing is safe. Something gets you killed."

"Everything gets you killed," said Ancel. "I've outlived four governments and every single one of them promised to protect me. The trick is not to be protected. The trick is to be useful to the people standing next to you when it all comes down." He tapped the table with one knuckle, a printer's gesture, a man setting a line. "We have paper for one more run. One. After that, nothing. So the question isn't whether we're brave. The question is what we spend the last of it on."

"Wedding announcements," said Marguerite, weakly.

"The Ferreol girl would appreciate it," Odile conceded.

And then, because the family disease would out, they all laughed—tired, cracked laughter, the kind that lives next door to weeping and borrows its sugar—and Marguerite threw up her hands and said that if she was to be hanged she would prefer it be for something with proper margins, and that settled it, the way everything in the family was settled: not by agreement but by exhaustion.

"One edition," Rémy said at last. "The granary pages, and the names, and nothing else. We print it small so it travels well. We print it at night. And when it's done—" he looked around the table at all of them, and Odile saw that her father, the steadiest man in the quarter, had ink still in the creases of his knuckles from work that had officially never happened, "—when it's done, we decide what we are."

"What we are," said Ancel, "is a print shop. We've been deciding that for sixty years and it always comes out the same."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up, Odile stood at the workshop window with the lamp turned low, doing what she did every night now: watching the street. The city had a new sound underneath its old sounds, a low continuous murmur like a press being inked somewhere below the world, and she had learned to read it the way her grandfather read type, upside down and backward, by long practice.

The street was empty except for one figure.

A person stood across the way, in the shadow of the cooper's doorway, and had stood there, she realized, for some time. Not a beggar—beggars shifted their weight, begged with their whole bodies. This one was still. Still the way couriers are still, the way people are still when their whole trade is being overlooked. There was something in the set of the shoulders that snagged at her memory like a burr, some old angle she knew from somewhere, from a lifetime ago, from a boy who used to sleep in the loft above the typecases and who had left at fourteen without a backward look and without, in all these years, a single letter.

The figure saw the lamp move. For a moment, across the dark street, two people who used to be family looked at each other through glass and shadow, and neither of them breathed.

Then the figure turned and was gone into the murmur of the city, and Odile stood with her hand flat against the cold pane, and thought: *I know you. I have always known you. Come inside, or stop watching, but do not stand in doorways like a debt we haven't paid.*

She did not sleep. Down in the shop, the press waited with its arm raised, patient as an ox, and somewhere in the paper chest beneath three hundred wedding announcements lay the truest thing the Vasseurs had ever set in type, waiting for its ink.
