## 5. A Letter Against the Family

The inn at the Rue des Teinturiers was called the Broken Compass, which Isandro had always taken to be an honest name. Every compass in the city was broken now; the only directions left were toward the harbor or away from it, and both roads were clogged with carts and rumor. The innkeeper, a broad woman named Madame Sable with flour on her forearms and a soldier's bluntness softened by twenty years of feeding travelers, looked Isandro up and down exactly once.

"One room?"

"One room."

"You'll want it at the back. The front ones face the street, and the street faces the Ministry quarter, and everything facing the Ministry quarter is being watched by somebody." She slid a brass key across the counter. "Soup's until nine. After that you can have bread and my opinion, but the bread's better."

Isandro paid in coin that had been good for longer than most people expected it to remain so, and climbed the stairs.

The room was small and clean and smelled of lavender doing valiant work against mildew. A bed, a washstand, a chair, a window shuttered against the street. Isandro set down the courier's satchel with the care of a person setting down a sleeping animal that might yet bite, and then sat on the chair and did not move for a long time.

This was the rule, and Isandro had kept it for eleven years without exception: *never open what you carry.* Not because of loyalty—loyalty was a word handlers used the way bakers used "fresh," meaning whatever they needed it to mean—but because opened letters were dangerous twice over. Dangerous to the sender, if discovered. Dangerous to the carrier, always. A sealed letter was a stone thrown by someone else's hand; open it, and your own fingerprints were on the rock.

But the seal had been wrong. Isandro had turned that fact over all afternoon like a coin of doubtful weight. The Vasseur blue wax had been right—the color, even the faint smell of the workshop's particular dye, a smell Isandro knew from childhood, from watching Odile's father melt sticks of sealing wax over a candle while complaining about the price of pigment. But the impression itself had been pressed too hard, the way a forger presses when he is thinking about the seal rather than the letter beneath it. Real seals are casual. Real seals are made by people whose minds are already on the next thing.

And there had been no second mark. Every genuine dispatch Isandro had ever carried bore a tiny counter-mark—a dot, a nick, some private signature of the sealing hand. This one was pristine. Pristine meant practiced. Practiced meant false.

Isandro had taken it anyway. Payment bought passage out of a dying city, and passage out of a dying city was worth more than suspicion. That was professionalism. That was what Isandro told themselves while checking into an inn two streets from the workshop they had sworn never to approach, in a city where every hour the checkpoints grew one question longer.

Professionalism had a taste, Isandro discovered, sitting alone in a rented room with the letter lying on the table between them like something dead. It tasted of iron and cold water.

* * *

Steaming a letter open is a patience trade, and Isandro had learned it young—not from the courier guild but from the workshop, absurdly enough. Old Grandfather Vasseur used to demonstrate it as a party trick when the apprentices were restless on winter evenings: hold the envelope over the kettle spout, let the steam find the glue, work a fine blade along the fold while the paper softens, and lift the flap whole. "Every seal is a promise," he'd said, winking at fourteen-year-old Isandro across the bench, "and every promise can be unmade by someone patient enough. Which is why we print things instead. Ink dries. Wax never really does."

He would be horrified to know where his trick had ended up. Or perhaps not. The Vasseurs had never been sentimental about tools.

Isandro lit the spirit lamp, set a shallow pan of water to simmer, and worked with hands that remembered. Steam curled. The blade—actually a bone folder lifted from the inn's writing desk, apologies to Madame Sable—slid along the fold. The wax had already been cracked once, carefully, at a seam chosen so the break could be re-fused with a warmed knife; whoever had sealed this letter had known it might need to be read by someone else first. That detail settled any last doubt. Genuine dispatches are not designed to be reopened.

The flap lifted.

Inside: a single sheet, folded in thirds, in a hand Isandro knew the way you know a voice through a wall—familiar, hated in a vague administrative way, never before feared.

*By order of the Ministry of Order, and under the emergency provisions of the Eleventh Article:*

*It has been established by reliable testimony that the premises known as the Vasseur printing house, Street of Presses, have served as a gathering place and instrument of persons conspiring against the lawful succession of government. The named principals—Vasseur, Étienne; Vasseur, Marguerite; Vasseur, Odile—are to be taken into custody upon receipt of this order. The premises, presses, type, and stock are forfeit to the Ministry.*

*Execution of this order may proceed without public announcement.*

*Signed,*

*A. Corvel, Minister of Order*

Isandro read it three times. The room got colder each time, though the lamp still burned.

Corvel. Aurélien Corvel, Minister of Order, a man whose correspondence Isandro had carried four times in two years—orders mostly, requisitions, one extraordinary long letter to a general in the south that Isandro had later understood, reading the newspapers in a doorway, had been the opening move of a purge nobody had a name for yet. Corvel wrote a beautiful hand. That was the thing people missed about him. He signed himself with a small flourish after the *l*, like a man signing his name on a dance card. He had once, when Isandro waited in his antechamber for a receipt, asked the courier whether the rain was likely to continue, and Isandro had answered that it usually did, and Corvel had smiled as if this were wit, and Isandro had walked out into the rain and thought: *there is a man who has never once been wet.*

And now that hand, that beautiful, dry hand, had written the name *Vasseur* three times.

Étienne, who had taught Isandro to read proof upside down because it was faster than teaching them to wait. Marguerite, who fed apprentices first and family second and claimed this was equality. Odile—

Odile, who at eleven had defended Isandro's right to learn composition against her father's objection that couriers didn't need letters, only legs. *"Then why do you keep making me learn mine?"* she had demanded, and won, and Isandro had spent one golden year setting type badly beside her before everything else happened.

Taken into custody. Premises forfeit. Execution without public announcement—which was bureaucratic language meaning *shot in a courtyard and buried under someone else's name,* and everyone who worked near ministries knew it, and pretended not to.

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, Madame Sable was arguing cheerfully with someone about the price of eggs, as though the world were a place where eggs had a stable value and tomorrow were a thing you could plan soup around.

Isandro sat very still and felt the shape of their own life change around them, the way a room changes when a door you thought was painted-on turns out to open.

Eleven years of carrying. Eleven years of rules: *carry nothing personal, know no one, weigh everything.* Eleven years of being a hinge, a tube, a pair of legs with a satchel—and telling themselves this was safety, this was wisdom, this was the smart animal surviving the forest. And all the while, it turned out, they had been something else entirely. They had been the hand. Letters don't travel by themselves. Orders don't arrive out of the air like weather. Somewhere, every time, a person had walked through rain and past checkpoints and up ministry steps, and that person had been the mechanism by which other people's lives were decided.

Invisibility, Isandro saw now, was not neutrality. It was service. The invisible serve whoever writes. And the writers, it appeared, wrote whatever they liked, in whatever beautiful hand they liked, and slept dry.

They had made Isandro into a pen. Pens don't get to read what they write.

Except this pen had read it.

* * *

There followed what Isandro would later think of as the argument, conducted in the privacy of their own skull, between the professional and the apprentice.

The professional spoke first, in the flat voice of the guild instructors: *You took a commission. You deliver the commission. You do not know what's in the letter; formally, you still don't—you broke a seal, that's a crime, but ignorance of contents remains technically available to you if the flap goes back down and the wax is re-warmed. Deliver it. Take your pay. Take your passage. Be in Brannoc's cart or some smuggler's hold or a fishing boat within nine days, alive, anonymous, gone. One family against one life. The arithmetic is ugly but it is arithmetic.*

The apprentice—that younger self, ink-stained, standing at the case in the Street of Presses with a composing stick held wrong—answered differently. The apprentice said: *Do you remember the smell? Do you remember the sound the press makes when the forme seats properly, that deep satisfied clunk, like a door closing on a house you're allowed to stay in? Do you remember Marguerite Vasseur putting the heel of the bread in your bowl because you were growing and she could see it? You were going to let a piece of paper kill them. You. The paper would just lie there. You would do the walking.*

"Neither of you gets a vote," Isandro said aloud, to the empty room, and then laughed—a short, cracked, terrible laugh, because talking to yourself in a rented room was the first symptom, everyone knew, of becoming a person with opinions, and opinions were how couriers died.

Because here was the rest of the arithmetic, the part the professional preferred not to mention: Isandro could not simply destroy the letter. Orders have twins. Ministries keep copies, or believe they do; Corvel above all believed in paperwork the way misers believe in gold. If the order went undelivered, questions would follow its absence like gulls follow a fishing boat. *Where is the courier. Where is the confirmation.* And a missing courier in a falling city was not a mystery, it was a target—hunted by the Ministry for treachery and by every faction for whatever the courier had been carrying. Destroy the letter, and Isandro didn't save the Vasseurs; Isandro merely scheduled two funerals instead of three, and attended neither, having been shot earlier in the proceedings.

Which left—what?

Isandro got up, paced the four steps of floor the room permitted, and came back to the chair. Thought about checkpoints. Checkpoints were the city's grammar now: every road a sentence, every sentry a punctuation mark, and nothing traveled without being parsed. But Isandro had spent eleven years learning the grammar's exceptions. There were routes that existed on paper and not on the ground—paper roads, orders routing couriers through posts that had burned in the spring, or been abandoned, or had never quite existed except in the Ministry's own ledgers, because the Ministry, like all dying institutions, had stopped checking its maps against its territory sometime around the second coup. An order routed through such a checkpoint would take days to discover its own error. Days in which the error would blame the checkpoint, not the courier.

A delay. Not a destruction. A letter, lost honestly, in the fog of a collapsing state.

It was treachery. Isandro examined the feeling of it the way one examines a new coin—turning it over, testing the edge, biting it half-expecting it to be soft lead. It should have felt like falling. Instead it felt like setting the first line of type in a fresh forme: the slight resistance of the metal, the click as the piece seated, the sense that an empty space was beginning, very slowly, to fill with something that might say something true.

Terrifying. Also—Isandro admitted it, sitting in the lavender-smelling dark—exhilarating. For eleven years they had been carried by the current. Tonight, for the first time since the age of fourteen, they had put a foot down and felt river stones under it.

* * *

Near midnight, footsteps on the stairs, and Isandro was across the room with the letter hidden and a hand on the shutters before the knock resolved into a voice: "Courier! Your lamp's smoking. If you burn my inn down I'll want payment in advance."

"Just reading," Isandro called back. "Late hours."

"Hm." A pause, and then, with the terrible kindness of innkeepers, "Whatever it is you're reading, eat something first. Bad decisions keep better on a full stomach. That's free advice; the soup costs extra."

Footsteps receded. Isandro stood in the middle of the room, heart knocking, and realized two things at once.

First: Madame Sable had seen the satchel. Of course she had. Couriers were recognizable the way priests and prostitutes were recognizable—by bearing, by the way they counted exits. Which meant that by morning, discreetly, for the price of a drink, the fact that a courier was lodging at the Broken Compass could belong to anyone. Invisibility, it turned out, did not survive indoors. It required motion. It required the street.

Second: the letter had to go back together perfectly, and Isandro's hands, for the first time in eleven years, were not steady.

They sat back down at the table and breathed, and worked. Warm knife along the wax crack; the blue fused invisibly, or nearly; the flap pressed home; the whole thing weighed in the palm—lighter than a loaf, heavier than anything Isandro had ever carried. Three names. Étienne. Marguerite. Odile. Nine days on the face of the order, and eight of them left, and somewhere between here and the Ministry of Order lay a fictional checkpoint where a fiction could be sold.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the river, something boomed—artillery, or a barricade coming down, or the city clearing its throat—and the windows shivered in their frames, and Madame Sable's dog began to bark and was hushed.

Isandro lay down on the narrow bed with the satchel strap wound twice around their wrist, an old habit, and stared at the ceiling, and did not sleep either. Sleep, apparently, ran in the family. The thought arrived uninvited, wearing its old grief like a coat that no longer fit: *family.*

Eight days. One letter. Three lives that had once set a place for them at the table, and one courier who had spent eleven years pretending the table had never existed.

Tomorrow, Isandro thought, as the boom sounded again and the dog started up again and the city murmured on, tomorrow I will need a map of the Ministry's own lies, and a clerk fool enough to stamp one.

Tomorrow I stop being a pen.

The lamp went out on its own, as lamps do when no one tends them, and the dark was total, and in the dark Isandro Vasseur—who had been no one at all for eleven years, and was about to become the most visible person in the Street of Presses—began, finally, to make plans.
