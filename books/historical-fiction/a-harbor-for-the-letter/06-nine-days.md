## 6. Nine Days

Isandro counted the days the way other people counted money—by touch, in the dark, without ever quite letting themselves look at the sum.

Nine. The letter had been sealed for two of them already, riding against their ribs inside a coat that suddenly felt less like clothing and more like a small, patient bomb.

They had taken a room above a candlemaker's shop on the Rue des Oubliés—a street whose name, *the forgotten ones*, had never seemed so much like an instruction. The room cost three sous a night and came with a bed that listed to port like a sinking ship and a window that looked out on a wall. Isandro had chosen it precisely because of the wall. Walls asked nothing of you. Walls did not have daughters who argued with inspectors in doorways, or presses behind them still warm from some midnight run.

They sat at the table now with the letter before them, unopened again—they had learned their lesson about resealing wax; the second attempt looked like a scab picked twice—and performed the arithmetic they had been avoiding since dawn.

*Option one.* Deliver the letter. Walk it through the Ministry of Order's doors as contracted, collect payment, buy passage out on the last evacuation barge, and live.

The family dies.

Not *might* die. Not *could*. Minister Corvel did not write letters that arrived as suggestions. Isandro had carried eleven of his dispatches over four years and had once watched, from across a courtyard, what happened to a customs house after one of them: men in grey coats, then smoke, then a new sign over the same door. Corvel's letters were not messages. They were weather.

*Option two.* Destroy the letter. Burn it in the candlemaker's own hearth—there was a certain poetry available there—and vanish into a city that was busy forgetting everything else.

Then both courier and family were hunted. Because Corvel kept copies. Of course he kept copies; a man who forged seals did not trust originals. The letter would arrive late or not at all, someone would ask questions, and the questions would travel backward along the route until they found the courier who'd been paid and produced nothing. Isandro knew how that inquiry went too. They had once helped carry the answer to such an inquiry, years ago, in a satchel they had not opened. They had told themselves, then, that ignorance was cleanliness.

They were done telling themselves things.

*Option three.*

Isandro got up, paced the four steps the room allowed, sat back down, and looked at the letter as if it might blink first.

*Option three* was the thought that had been circling them since morning like a moth around a flame: you cannot destroy a letter, but perhaps you can make it take the long way home.

Every courier knew the map of the city's checkpoints the way priests knew psalms. And every courier knew something better: which checkpoints existed only on paper. The republic, in its long illness, had grown a bureaucracy the way dying trees grow fungus—flourishing, elaborate, untethered from anything real. Half the inspection stations marked on the Ministry's routing charts had been abandoned months ago. Their garrisons reassigned, their ledgers shipped off, their buildings now occupied by squatters, pigeons, or in one memorable case a man selling roasted chestnuts out of what had been a customs office.

But the routing charts still showed them. And dispatches were routed by chart, because no one in a collapsing state had the time or nerve to admit that half its machinery was decorative.

If the letter were sent by way of the Ferrière checkpoint—

Isandro closed their eyes and pictured it. The Ferrière post had been decommissioned in the spring, quietly, when its garrison was pulled north. But it sat on a canal spur, and canal spurs flooded, and everyone knew the roads around it turned to soup in wet weather. A delay there would be *credible*. A delay there would be *documented*. Some clerk in the Ministry would stamp a form saying *held at Ferrière awaiting passable road*, and the form would be true in spirit if not in letter, which was the national condition anyway.

It would buy days. Three, maybe four. Enough time for—what? Isandro honestly didn't know. Enough time for the Vasseurs to run. Enough time for Odile Vasseur to stop arguing with inspectors long enough to load a cart. Enough time for Isandro to figure out why their hands shook whenever they thought of the workshop, and whether the shaking was fear or grief or something with a simpler name.

Enough time to become, for the first time in fourteen years, a person who chose.

Isandro laughed once, short and joyless, at the ceiling. *Treachery.* That was the word for it. There was no softer word that fit. You could call it mercy, but mercy was treachery wearing its good coat. You could call it conscience, but conscience didn't require forging a Ministry routing slip, which is what this would require, because a delayed dispatch needed paperwork the way a lie needed a straight face.

They took out their writing kit—the courier's kit, brass-cornered, anonymous, the one object they had kept from the workshop all those years ago, though they'd have bitten off their own tongue before admitting it—and began, very carefully, to practice the Ministry clerk's hand.

It was a round hand. Pompous, even. Isandro had seen ten thousand examples of it and hated every one.

By lamplight, they forged.

* * *

From her window on the third floor of the workshop, Odile could see the whole street of presses going about its business of pretending nothing was wrong.

Old Tissot was sweeping his step with the particular violence of a man sweeping away thoughts. The Marchand girls were hauling water, gossiping in the way they hauled everything—loudly, competitively, with elbows. And down the block, half-hidden by the awning of the shuttered tobacco shop, someone stood watching the Vasseur door.

Not standing *near* the door. Standing near enough to seem like a person waiting for a friend, far enough to be nobody. Odile had grown up in a printing quarter; she knew watchers the way farmers knew weather. This one had the stillness of it. Watchers fidgeted when they thought no one saw. This one didn't fidget at all.

"Odile." Her mother's voice floated up the stairwell. "Are you at the window again?"

"I'm airing the linens," Odile called back, having no linens anywhere on her person.

"You've been airing those linens for an hour."

"They're very stubborn linens."

She heard her mother's sigh travel up two flights, a sound of long practice. Below, the watcher shifted their weight, and for a moment the light moved across their face under the awning's shadow, and Odile's breath caught—not at recognition, exactly, but at the shape of a memory. Narrow face. Ink-stained cuticles, though the hands tried to hide in pockets. Someone who had stood in that exact posture once before, fourteen years ago, at the edge of the yard, the morning the cart left with everything they owned in it and one person stayed behind on the cobbles, refusing the seat they were offered.

No. It couldn't be. People didn't come back from that kind of leaving. They especially didn't come back to stand outside your door during a purge, looking like a bad conscience in a good coat.

"Odile!" Her father this time, from below. "Grandfather wants to know if we're setting the Gallican tract tonight or tomorrow."

"Tell him tomorrow," she called down, not moving. "Tell him I want to check the ink stock first."

Which was true, incidentally, and gave her an excuse, because the ink cellar's window also faced the street.

Down in the cool dark, among the smell of lampblack and linseed, Odile crouched at the little grate and watched the watcher watch her house. Her grandfather's voice was in her ear, though he was upstairs: *A press tells the truth twice, Odile. Once when you set it and once when they burn it. Make sure both printings are worth reading.*

What she couldn't work out was which kind this watcher was. Ministry? Corvel's people already? Or something stranger?

The watcher checked the street both ways, then did something odd: they reached up and touched the doorframe of the tobacco shop, briefly, the way you'd touch a grave marker or an old scar. Then they walked away, quick and quiet, toward the river.

Odile sat back on her heels in the dark among the ink pots, heart going hard.

She knew that gesture. She had seen it a thousand mornings, from a child's height, at the workshop door where the apprentices touched the frame for luck before touching type. An old workshop habit. A *Vasseur* habit.

"Well," she said aloud to the ink cellar, to nobody. "That's interesting."

* * *

Back in the room on the Rue des Oubliées, Isandro discovered that forgery, like most sins, was mostly tedium.

Four drafts lay crumpled by the bed. The fifth was nearly right—the round hand achieved, the official stamp approximated with a coin, a steady wrist, and a prayer to whatever saint handled idiots. The routing order read:

*Dispatch 447-C rerouted via Ferrière station. Road conditions reported impassable. Hold for escort or passable road, whichever first. By order, Office of the Underclerk, Ministry of Order.*

It was beautiful. It was monstrous. It was, Isandro reflected, the first thing they had ever made instead of carried, and they had made it out of lies.

Their hand wouldn't stop shaking, so they put it flat on the table and watched it shake, and waited for the shame to arrive.

It didn't. That was the terrifying part. What arrived instead was something lighter, something almost giddy—like the moment on a rooftop when you step off onto a gap and find your foot lands on solid tile after all. *I lied to the Ministry,* Isandro thought, *and the sun has not gone out.* Somewhere below, the candlemaker was singing to himself, badly, a song about a fisherman's wife. The world continued. Treachery, it turned out, fit inside an ordinary evening.

And underneath the giddiness, colder water: this was the first day of the rest of a much shorter life. Every forged paper was a thread, and Corvel's clerks pulled threads for sport.

Fine. Let them pull. Isandro had spent fourteen years being a thread nobody bothered to pull, invisible as dust, useful as dust, safe as dust. Dust did not save anyone. Dust just settled on the furniture while the house burned.

They sealed the false routing order, tucked the true letter—Corvel's letter, the murder letter—into the inner pocket with the forged slip riding beside it like a knife in a loaf of bread, and blew out the lamp.

Tomorrow they would present themselves at the Ministry's courier desk with the rerouting request, walk it past a clerk, and pray the clerk was bored rather than curious. Tomorrow they would begin spending days they could not earn back.

Tonight, sleep refused them entirely. So Isandro sat in the dark doing what couriers do best: memorizing routes. But the route they memorized this time was not on any chart. It was the distance between the Rue des Oubliées and the Street of Presses. Fourteen years long. Eleven minutes on foot.

They intended to walk it again tomorrow. Closer, this time.

* * *

In the workshop kitchen, the Vasseur family held its nightly parliament, which consisted of Grandfather Vasseur, Odile's father Luc, her mother Sabine, and a pot of chicory coffee stretched past all decency.

"The inspector will come back," Luc said. "He was polite. Polite inspectors are the worst kind."

"He was a boy," said Grandfather. "He blushed when I offered him a chair. Boys who blush can be managed."

"Grandfather, he wrote down our names."

"He writes down everyone's names. Writing down names is what the government does instead of governing." The old man sipped his coffee with the serenity of a bishop. "What matters is the forme under the floor, and whether Odile finished sorting the type."

"She finished," Sabine said. "I checked. She always finishes. She gets it from me."

"I get it from Papa," Odile said, coming in, shaking rain off her shawl. "You get your stubbornness from being right too often, Maman, which is different."

Her mother smiled into her cup. Outside, thunder muttered over the rooftops—the season turning, the roads going soft, the whole country dissolving gently into mud, as it had been doing for years in one way or another.

"We should decide," Luc said quietly, when the joking had spent itself. "About running. Brannoc says the river barges fill up by week's end. After that it's carts, and carts are searched."

Nobody answered at once. The fire ticked. Upstairs, faintly, the great press stood silent in the dark like a sleeping animal they were all pretending not to listen for.

"Run where?" Odile asked at last. "To do what?"

"To be alive somewhere else," her father said. "It's an underrated occupation."

"And print what there? Prayers? Almanacs?" She heard her own voice sharpen and didn't soften it. "Papa. If we run now, with the type set and the pages planned, then everything Corvel does, he does in the dark, and we spend the rest of our lives in some foreign kitchen telling each other we were brave. We weren't. We were *elsewhere*."

"It's easy to talk brave when you're twenty-three."

"It's easiest of all to talk careful when you're frightened. I learned that watching you argue with the inspector yesterday. You were magnificent and terrified and I couldn't tell which was driving."

Luc opened his mouth, and shut it, and looked at his own father for rescue, and received none. Grandfather Vasseur was gazing into his coffee as though the grounds had arranged themselves into an opinion.

"The boy at the door today," Odile said, changing the angle because she couldn't help herself. "The one watching the shop. I think—I think it might be the apprentice. The one who left."

The silence changed texture instantly. Fourteen years fell over the table like a dropped cloth.

"That child is dead to this family," Luc said, too fast.

"No one in this family ever actually said that word out loud," Odile said. "We just let you say it for us, for fourteen years."

"Odile."

"Papa, they were *touching the doorframe*. Nobody touches the frame but us. Whatever they are now—courier, spy, ministry ghost—they came back to look at this door. That's not nothing."

Sabine reached over and refilled her daughter's cup, which in this household was a parliamentary maneuver meaning *proceed, but gently*. "And if it is them? What then?"

"Then I want to know why they're watching us," Odile said. "Because people watch houses for two reasons. To hurt them, or—" she stopped, hearing her own sentence arrive somewhere unexpected. "Or because they used to belong to them."

Thunder again, closer. In the cellar beneath their feet, under the floorboards, the forbidden forme lay wrapped in oilcloth, waiting. Somewhere out in the rain, a figure with ink under their fingernails was forging papers in a rented room, and none of the Vasseurs would learn it for another day—but Odile felt the shape of it coming, the way printers feel a page approaching the press.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would go out into the street and find the watcher, and ask.

Grandfather set down his empty cup with a small decisive click.

"Whatever happens," he said, "someone should tell the boy the frame still works."
