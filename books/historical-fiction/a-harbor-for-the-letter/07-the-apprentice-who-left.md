## 7. The Apprentice Who Left

Odile Vasseur had a theory about the man across the street, and the theory had been developing for three days.

It went like this: no one loitered in the Rue des Presses without reason. Not anymore. The street had emptied of everyone who wasn't essential — the ink-sellers gone, the paper merchants shuttered, even Madame Ferrand's pie cart retired to wherever pie carts retired when a city began to eat itself. The only people left were printers, soldiers, and fools, and the man leaning against the rain barrel opposite the workshop was dressed too well to be a printer and too badly armed to be a soldier.

Which left fool, and Odile had learned that fools came in two varieties: the harmless kind and the dangerous kind wearing the first kind's coat.

This one wore his collar up against the drizzle and held a courier's satchel flat against his hip like it was part of his body. He stood in the same spot every morning at roughly the same hour, sometimes an hour, sometimes two, and he did nothing. He simply watched the workshop the way other people watched weather — patiently, from a safe distance, as though something in it might eventually come toward him.

On the third morning, Odile decided she was done being weather.

She pulled on her father's oilcloth apron, told Grandfather she was going out for glue — which was true, incidentally; they were nearly out of hide glue and a lie with truth inside it was sturdier than either alone — and crossed the street before her courage could file a formal objection.

Up close, the watcher was younger than he'd seemed. Thin, sharp-faced, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent years learning not to be looked at. His eyes flicked to her, then away, then back with the reluctant precision of a man confirming something he'd hoped not to confirm.

"You've been standing there three days," Odile said.

"Have I."

"Forty minutes this morning. An hour yesterday. Two hours the day before." She folded her arms. "In my experience, men who watch a building that long are either casing it or courting it, and you're holding your satchel wrong for courtship."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile — more like a smile's ghost, one that had died young and haunted the premises ever since. "What does casing look like?"

"Nervous feet," Odile said. "You haven't shifted your weight once. Either you're very calm or you're very practiced, and both worry me."

"I'm just passing through."

"No one is passing through anything anymore. The city has stopped letting people pass through it. It's started keeping them." She tilted her head. "So. Are you going to tell me what you want, or shall I fetch the inspector? He comes by Tuesdays. It's Tuesday."

That landed. She saw it land — a small tightening around the eyes, a fractional straightening of the spine, the involuntary arithmetic of a man calculating how much trouble an inspector would be. Which meant he had trouble to calculate. Which meant she'd guessed right about something, though she couldn't yet say what.

"There's no need," he said carefully, "for inspectors."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "There isn't. There's also no need for you to keep pretending you don't know this street. You look at the third-floor window every time you think no one's watching. That window belongs to the type room. Nobody watches a type room unless they know what a type room is." She stepped closer, lowering her voice. "So I'll ask again, friend, and this time I'll ask nicely, because I have a household full of frightened people behind me and a ministry inspector who visits like a bill collector. What do you want?"

The man looked at her then — really looked, the way you look at a lock you're trying to remember how to pick — and Odile felt something cold walk down her spine, because there was something in the looking that wasn't strangerly at all. It was almost familiar. Like a tune heard through a wall.

"I knew a girl who talked like that," he said quietly. "She used to argue with the compositor's stick. Said letters had feelings."

The street tilted slightly.

Odile's mouth opened. Closed. She stared at him — at the sharp cheekbones, the ink stain that never quite washed out of the crease of his right thumb, the way he stood with his weight on his heels like someone braced to run — and the years peeled back one at a time, each one leaving a mark.

"...No," she said.

"It's good to see you too, Odile."

"No." Louder now, and a woman passing with a bundle of sticks glanced over, so Odile grabbed his sleeve and hauled him around the side of the rain barrel where the shadows were thick. "No, absolutely not. Isandro Joubert is dead. Papa said— we all said— fourteen years ago you walked out of that door and Papa said *let them go*, and Mama cried for a week and then stopped saying your name at all, and I was nine years old and I waited at that window every evening for a month until Grandfather told me waiting was a kind of lying to yourself." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "So no. You're a mistake. You're a trick of the light. You're—"

"Odile."

"—a ghost, and ghosts don't get to stand outside my father's shop looking sorry for themselves for three days—"

"Your grandfather's shop," Isandro said. "Your father sold his share to Grandfather Vasseur the winter after I left. Don't you remember? He said printing was a dying trade and he wanted out while the dying was slow."

The words hit some old tender place, and Odile realized with a flash of anger that he remembered everything. That was the worst of it. She'd spent fourteen years half-forgetting him, sanding the memory down to something she could carry, and here he was with all of it intact — the arguments, the window, the winter Papa sold his share. Carrying it all around like luggage, while she'd traveled light.

"Why are you here?" she demanded.

Isandro's jaw worked. For a moment Odile thought they might lie — and she noticed, with a strange distant clarity, that she'd already begun thinking of them as *they*, the way the family always had, because Isandro had never fit inside any single word and the family had long ago stopped trying to force one. Then:

"I was passing through," Isandro said, "and then I wasn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can afford right now." They glanced up the street, down the street — the old courier's sweep, automatic as breathing — and when they spoke again their voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Odile. How many days do you think you have? Before the inspector stops visiting and starts arriving?"

The question sat between them like a dropped compositor's stick — small, metal, capable of breaking a foot if stepped on wrong.

"We have arrangements," Odile said stiffly.

"You have broadsheets in the cellar with Corvel's name on them. I've seen your papa's hand through that window twice this week, setting type by lamplight at hours honest shops are dark."

"You've been counting our lamp-hours?"

"I count everything. It's what I am." Isandro's hand drifted to the satchel — touched it, didn't open it, the way a man touches a wound he hasn't decided to show yet. "And counting is why I'm here. Or it's why I should leave. Both things are true at once, which is the whole problem with me."

* * *

They ended up in the alley behind the tannery, because Odile refused to bring a ghost indoors until it had explained itself, and because the tannery alley smelled bad enough that no one lingered in it, which these days counted as privacy.

Isandro unstrapped the satchel with hands that weren't quite steady, and Odile watched, arms crossed, still furious, still nine years old at a window somewhere inside herself.

"I was commissioned four days ago," Isandro said. "A sealed letter, Ministry of Order, nine-day deadline. Standard work. I don't open letters. It's the first rule — carry nothing personal, know no one, weigh everything." A short breath. "I broke the second rule fourteen years ago. Apparently I've moved on to breaking the first."

They drew out the letter. Held it in both palms like something venomous.

"The seal is forged. Good forgery — better than good — but forged. And last night, in a room above a wine shop on the Rue aux Chats, I steamed it open." Their voice stayed level, but Odile saw the effort of it, the way you see the effort of a press bearing down on a sheet. "It's an arrest order, Odile. For the Vasseur family. All of you. Conspiracy against the state, sedition, seizure of the works. It names your grandfather by name."

The alley went very quiet. Somewhere beyond the rooftops a church bell rang the hour, off-key, the way everything in the city had gone slightly off-key lately.

"Signed?" Odile managed.

"Corvel."

Minister Corvel. The name had been coming through the workshop like weather of its own — in Papa's muttering, in Grandfather's drafts, in the careful coded language the family used over supper ever since the inspections started. Odile had thought him a distant threat, a storm system on the horizon. Now he was a name in a courier's hands, four streets away from her family's beds.

"And you brought it *here*?" she said. "You carried the thing that kills us to our own door?"

"I carried it because I didn't know whose door it was until I read it." Isandro's composure finally cracked, just at the edge, just enough to show the person underneath. "I stood outside your window for three days trying to decide. Deliver it and you die. Destroy it and they'll know within a week — orders have twins, Odile, copies travel separate roads, and if mine doesn't arrive they'll send someone to find out why, and that someone will find me, and then find you anyway. Burn it, delay it, reroute it — every road ends at the same place unless—" They stopped.

"Unless what?"

"Unless the letter becomes irrelevant. Unless something happens that makes Corvel afraid to sign anything else." Isandro looked at her, and there was something raw in their face, something that had been buried under fourteen years of professional blankness. "Your grandfather is printing something. I've seen enough through that window to guess what. If it's what I think it is, then maybe—"

"You want us to save you."

"I want," Isandro said, with terrible precision, "to stop being the instrument of the thing that destroys you. Those are different requests, and I'd understand completely if you only granted the second one."

* * *

Odile made them wait in the alley while she went inside, and the waiting stretched, and Isandro later admitted they had genuinely considered running — feet on the cobbles, satchel tight, gone before the door opened again. But the door opened, and Odile stood in it with the lamplight behind her, and her face had changed. Not softened. Changed. The fury was still there, but it had been joined by something else, the way a storm front is joined by the pressure drop that means it means business.

"Papa wants to see you," she said.

"Odile—"

"He doesn't know it's you. I told him a courier needed sheltering from the checkpoints. He said yes before I finished the sentence, because that's the kind of man he is, and it's the kind of man you apparently betrayed, so you can spend the evening being fed soup by someone you abandoned and we'll see how you like it." She stepped aside to let them pass, and then, quieter, in the doorway, close enough that only Isandro could hear: "Why did you leave? Really. Not the story Papa tells. Not the story Mama tells. Yours."

Isandro stood in the threshold of the workshop they'd fled at fourteen — the smell of ink and hide glue and lamp oil rising up like a language they'd once been fluent in — and found they could not answer. Not here. Not yet. Some letters took steaming open, and this one wasn't ready.

"I'll tell you," they said. "When it won't cost anyone anything. That's a promise I can actually keep."

Odile studied them for a long moment, this thin sharp-faced ghost with ink in the crease of their thumb, and whatever she found there, she gave a single curt nod — the nod of a forewoman accepting a bid of unknown quality, on credit, against her better judgment.

"Soup's on the stove," she said. "Take your boots off. And Isandro—" They turned. "If this is a trap, if you're wearing my brother's face for the Ministry, I will set your type myself. Your name, your crimes, every edition in the city."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair. It's generous. Don't make me regret being generous."

Inside, Auguste Vasseur rose from the composing stone, wiping his hands on his apron, squinting at the stranger in his doorway with polite, tired courtesy. "A courier, Odile says? Well, sit, sit. Any friend of the roads is a friend of ours tonight. We're short on everything except work."

He offered his hand. Isandro took it.

And Auguste — who had set type for fifty years, who could read a page by touch in the dark — paused, frowning faintly at the grip, at something in the angle of the wrist, at some muscle-memory older than sense. He looked down at their ink-stained thumb. Then up at their face.

His hand tightened.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well."

From the corner of the room, Odile watched, arms crossed, ready — she told herself — for anything.
