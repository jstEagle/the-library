## 8. Corvel's Ledger

There is a particular hour in a dying city when the checkpoints grow bored. The soldiers stop searching your bags and start searching your face instead, looking for something to arrest besides paper. Isandro had learned to walk through that hour with the expression of a man mildly inconvenienced by weather.

They had also learned, over eleven years of carrying other people's secrets, that every secret has a price, and that price is almost always drinkable.

The Lamplighter's Rest had once been a respectable tavern. Now it was a respectable tavern with a hole in the roof and a clientele that included two deserters, a customs officer who had stopped collecting customs, and Madame Sef, who had once been the republic's most feared forger of transit seals and was now its most feared drinker of other people's wine. Isandro slid onto the bench across from her and set down a bottle of the good stuff—the kind with a wax seal, which in the current economy was worth roughly a horse.

Madame Sef looked at the bottle. Then at Isandro. Then at the bottle again, the way a woman looks at a long-lost son.

"You want something," she said.

"I want to admire your company."

"You want something *expensive*." She took the bottle anyway. "Sit. Talk. But talk quietly, because Pieter in the corner is a customs man and customs men repeat everything they hear at half price."

Isandro sat. The Lamplighter's Rest smelled of smoke, tallow, and the particular despair of a city that had begun counting its days on one hand. Somewhere beyond the walls, the artillery had gone quiet—not because the war was over, but because the enemy had realized the city was falling on its own, like fruit, and why waste powder on fruit?

"I need to know about seals," Isandro said. "Ministry seals. Specifically, who has been ordering them, and whether the orders are clean."

Madame Sef poured with the reverence of a priest. "Clean," she repeated. "You know how many clean seals have crossed this city in the last month? None. The Ministry's own seal-cutter sold his matrix to three different people and then left town. I know because I bought one of the three." She said this with the pride of a craftsman. "So whoever you're carrying for, they're either honest—and in this city, darling, honest means broke—or they're forging, and if they're forging, they didn't buy from me, which is frankly an insult."

"Minister Corvel's office."

The bottle paused halfway to her lips. Madame Sef had a face built for the theater—she'd been an actress, before, in the years when the city still had theaters—and she used it now, letting the surprise play across it in full, generous movements before settling into something more careful.

"Corvel," she said. "The patriot."

"The patriot."

She set the bottle down. She looked out the broken window, at the street, at nothing. When she spoke again it was quieter, and the actress had left her voice entirely.

"I did a job for his office. Six weeks ago. Before the evacuation orders, before any of this." She gestured at the general ruin of everything. "A man came—not a clerk, a *fixer*, the kind of man whose hands are soft because other hands do the work. He wanted a batch of internal routing seals. The kind that move paper between ministries without it ever touching a public registry. Paper that travels in the dark."

"That's not unusual."

"No," she agreed. "What was unusual was the quantity. Forty sheets. And the dates." She leaned in. "They were all backdated. Every one. Stamped as if they'd been issued months earlier, before the seal-cutter sold his matrix, before—well. Before the things those papers claimed to authorize had happened."

Isandro kept their face still. It was a courier's first skill: a face like an unmarked envelope.

"Backdated authorization," Isandro said. "So that an order given today can claim it was given in spring."

"So that a paper trail can be *built backwards*, darling." She picked up the bottle and drank, and her hand was not entirely steady, and Isandro noted that Madame Sef's hands were usually steadier than the city itself. "I've forged love letters, death certificates, a duchy's entire genealogy once, which was great fun. But backdating authority—" she tapped the table, once, twice— "that's not forgery. That's architecture. You only build backwards when the thing you're constructing needs to look older than it is. Like a fake ruin. Like a fake *conspiracy*."

The word landed between them on the sticky table, and neither of them picked it up.

"Who else has he bought from?" Isandro asked.

"Ah." She smiled, and there was something sad in it. "Now you're asking me to name colleagues, in a city where colleagues are disappearing. That costs more than wine." She turned the bottle, considering it. "But I'll tell you what I'll give you for free, because it keeps me up at night and I'd rather it kept someone else up too. The fixer paid in *scrip*. Ministry scrip. Which is fine, except the denominations he paid in—the ones he had *stacks* of—were treasury scrip. The kind that only gets issued against the vaults. The kind that's supposed to be locked in the vaults." She looked at Isandro. "Somebody has been paying for forgery with the republic's last money, and doing it casually, the way you'd pay a fishwife."

*

The second contact was a boy called Ferren who worked the Ministry's internal mail room, and who could be bribed not with money but with the one thing a boy in a starving city could not get anywhere else: his mother's approval, delivered in the form of a letter Isandro had once carried from a border town, in which the mother had written, among other things, that she had always known he would amount to something. Ferren had read it forty times. Isandro knew this because Ferren had told them, tearfully, twice.

The mail room of the Ministry of Order occupied what had once been a chapel, which struck Isandro as the kind of joke the century was making constantly now. Ferren met them in the vestry, among the defrocked vestments, and talked with the breathless efficiency of a boy who had a great deal to say and a very small window in which to say it.

"You want Corvel's correspondence. Everyone wants Corvel's correspondence. Do you know what I've learned, carrying his letters? I've learned that a minister's mail tells you nothing, because a minister's mail is what he *wants* known. It's the routing slips that tell the truth." He held up a finger. "Every letter gets a routing slip. Who sent it, who received it, what it authorized, what account it was charged against. The slips go to the ledger office. The ledger office burns its copies monthly. *Officially.*"

"And unofficially?"

Ferren grinned, and for a moment he was just a boy with a secret, and the war fell away from him like a coat. "Unofficially, old Master Delacroix keeps his own book, because Master Delacroix trusts no one, least of all the republic, and Master Delacroix is the only man in this building who has been right about everything."

"Can I see the book?"

"Can a fish swim?" Ferren paused. "No, wait, that's the wrong—yes. Yes, you can see the book. But not tonight. Tonight the ledger office has visitors. Tonight—" and here he dropped his voice to the register of a boy who has finally arrived at the good part— "tonight Corvel's fixer is *there himself*. With a strongbox. With Master Delacroix. And I am not supposed to know, and you are not supposed to know, and the strongbox is not supposed to exist."

"When?"

"Two nights. The fixer drinks with the garrison captain on the even nights. Delacroix works alone on the even nights. Even nights, vestry, I'll bring you through the archive corridor, and if anyone asks, you are a chimney inspector, because this building has never once been inspected for chimneys and no one will question it."

"A chimney inspector."

"You'd be amazed what people don't question," Ferren said, "when the question might get them assigned to the walls."

*

The two intervening days, Isandro did what couriers do when they are not carrying: they walked, they watched, they weighed.

They weighed the letter in their satchel—the letter, the forged-sealed letter naming the Vasseurs, the letter they had now carried for four days past every honest schedule. Nine days had been the commission. Five were left. The arithmetic sat in Isandro's chest like a swallowed stone.

They walked past the Ministry of the Treasury and noted the wagons. That was the thing about a city evacuating: everyone was loading carts, so one more cart meant nothing, and everything. But Isandro had spent eleven years learning to read carts the way priests read scripture. These wagons were not fleeing. They were *consolidating*. They came out of the treasury's side gate, heavy-axled, riding low, escorted not by the treasury's own guards but by garrison men—Corvel's garrison men—and they went not toward the evacuation docks, where the republic's records and relics were being shipped to safety, but toward the old customs warehouse on the river, which had no ship at its dock, and had not for a month.

Heavy wagons, going nowhere, guarded by the wrong soldiers.

Isandro stood across the street in the disguise of a man waiting for a friend, and did the sums. The treasury scrip Madame Sef had been paid in. The backdated seals. The wagons. And underneath it all, the letter in the satchel: *the Vasseur family, conspirators, arrest, seizure of premises.* A print workshop. A print workshop with presses, with type, with a cellar, with a location two streets from the customs warehouse, in the one quarter of the city that would be—Isandro realized, with the cold sliding sensation of a lock turning—*the last quarter evacuated*. Because the evacuation routes all began at the docks, and the printing quarter was the far side, and whoever controlled the printing quarter in the final days controlled the only presses left standing in the capital.

You could not forge a republic's final documents without a press. And you could not seize a press more cheaply than by naming its owners traitors.

Isandro had carried orders for executions before. They had carried them the way they carried everything—sealed, unweighed by conscience, because the rules were the rules and the rules were the only thing that had ever held. But those orders had always had the shape of passion: this man denounced that man, this faction ate that faction, the old ugly hunger of politics wearing its old ugly face. What was being built here had no passion in it at all. That was what made Isandro's hands cold on the warm afternoon. There was no belief to fight, no cause to argue with. There was only a man in a fine coat who had looked at a falling republic and seen, in its collapse, a *delivery window*.

*

The even night came gray and drizzling. Ferren led Isandro through the archive corridor, past shelves of dead ministries' papers—the Ministry of Roads, dissolved; the Ministry of the Interior's second office, purged; a whole shelf labeled, in someone's bitter hand, *PEOPLE WHO USED TO MATTER*—and into the ledger office, where old Master Delacroix sat behind a desk scarred by sixty years of ink, holding a lamp up to Isandro's face with the unimpressed squint of a man who has been bribed by professionals.

"A courier," he said. "Of course. Half the truth in this city travels in the pockets of people paid not to read it." He unlocked a drawer and drew out a book—plain, leather-bound, unmarked. "You have until the fixer finishes his brandy. He drinks fast when he's nervous, and he's been nervous for six weeks."

Isandro opened the book.

It was routing slips, copied in Delacroix's small, furious hand: date, sender, recipient, authorization, account charged. Isandro read the way they'd been taught to read—fast, pattern-first, letting the individual lines blur until the shape emerged. And the shape emerged quickly, because Corvel had not been careful. Why would he be careful? He was building his forgery for an audience of one: whatever government, junta, or foreign patron emerged from the rubble, an audience that would check nothing, because checking would mean admitting the republic's last minister was a thief, and new regimes never look in mirrors.

The slips told it plainly. Six weeks of them. Authorization after authorization, all backdated, all charging against the treasury reserve: *transport of archival materials. Emergency custody of specie. Protective relocation of state funds pending evacuation.* Each one signed with the counter-seal—the one Madame Sef had described, the one bought in batches of forty. And threaded through them, like dark thread through pale cloth, the other papers: the denunciations. A customs officer, denounced, his warehouse requisitioned—the customs warehouse on the river. Two treasury auditors, denounced, fled the city—fled, or were fled, Isandro thought, remembering that people who fled usually left in a hurry and sometimes left in a cart. And a print workshop on the Street of Presses, its family denounced, its premises marked for seizure by the Ministry of Order, authorization backdated to a spring day when no such order had ever been dreamed of.

The conspiracy was real. That was the elegant horror of it. There *was* a plot to seize what remained of the government. Corvel had simply written it himself, in other people's names, and then arranged to be the one who discovered it. The Vasseurs were not conspirators; they were *set dressing*—the respectable, beloved, half-legendary printers whose arrest would make the whole fiction believable. Everyone knew the Vasseurs. Everyone trusted the Vasseurs. If the Vasseurs were traitors, then the plot was real, and if the plot was real, then Corvel, who had heroically uncovered it, could do anything—suspend anything, seize anything, *move* anything—while the city burned behind him.

And the wagons on the river road weren't fleeing with the treasury. The treasury was being stolen *from* the evacuation, quietly, wagon by wagon, into a warehouse, to wait for the new order that Corvel's forged papers had already built.

"Delacroix," Isandro said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Do you know what happens to you when the fixer finishes his brandy?"

"I have considered it," the old man said dryly. "I have also considered that I am seventy-one years old, that my wife is already in Marseille, and that a man my age who lets a thief rob a country in front of him will have a great deal to explain to her." He closed the book. "Copy what you need. You have perhaps ten minutes. And courier—" he looked at Isandro over the lamp, and his eyes were tired and clear— "whatever you're carrying that brought you here. Put it down. Some letters are not meant to be delivered. I have spent forty years learning the difference, and I will tell you the rule for free: a letter that serves the man who wrote it, and no one else, is not correspondence. It is a weapon. And no one is obliged to be its courier."

Isandro copied the slips in a hand that did not shake, though Isandro watched it the whole time, waiting for it to.

*

They walked back through the drizzle with the pages sewn into the lining of their coat, past the darkened shopfronts, past a barricade being built by boys too young to remember what the republic had promised and old men who remembered perfectly. The city smelled of wet ash and baking bread, because the ovens still ran, because people still insisted on breakfast at the end of the world.

Cold clarity, Isandro thought. That was what Delacroix's book had given them. Not rage—rage was hot, and heat made mistakes. This was colder and more useful: the simple, level sight of a man's whole design, laid out like type in a case, every letter in its place. Corvel had built a conspiracy out of paper, and paper was Isandro's country, and Isandro knew every road in it.

Which meant Isandro also knew the one thing Corvel's design could not survive.

A forged conspiracy is only as strong as the silence around it. It needs no witnesses, no accomplices, no truth—only no *counter-statement*. Paper against paper. If the republic's last days produced a second document, a true one, printed and scattered past any hope of recall, then Corvel's beautiful architecture would stand in the open with no walls, and everyone would see it for what it was: a fake ruin, built backwards, by a thief.

But a counter-statement needed a press. And the only press that mattered sat on the Street of Presses, behind a door Isandro had sworn at fourteen never to open, in the hands of a family whose arrest Isandro was carrying in their satchel like a stone.

Five days left. Four, if you counted honestly, and Isandro had always counted honestly.

They stood at the corner of the Street of Presses in the rain, looking at the workshop's lit window, where a lamp still burned—where someone was still working, still setting type or banking the stove, still believing the night was ordinary. The light fell yellow on the wet cobbles, and Isandro's feet, which had carried orders across a republic for eleven years without once deciding anything, made the decision for them.

They crossed the street and raised their hand to the door of the Vasseur workshop—and the door opened before they could knock, and Odile Vasseur stood in the lamplight with a type stick in her hand like a dagger, and said:

"You. I've been watching you watch this house for a week. Come in and tell me why, before I decide you're a spy and fetch the neighbors."
