## 9. Ink Enough for One Edition

The paper was stored in the cellar behind a false wall of spoiled stock—reams my father had soaked in river water years ago so the inspectors would believe the workshop had nothing worth taking. It had worked. The ministry men had walked past those ruined-looking bales a dozen times over the years, and every time I had stood at the top of the stairs with my heart in my throat, listening to their boots scuff against what they believed was waste.

It was not waste. It had never been waste. My father had dried each sheet flat between boards, one by one, over four winters, and never once told me he was doing it. I found out the way I found out most things about Papa: by accident, and late.

"Count it," he said now, hauling the last bale up into the lamplight. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow and his forearms were dusted white with paper-fiber, so that he looked like a man who had been wrestling a ghost. "Every sheet. We should know exactly what we have before we decide anything."

"Decide what?" asked my mother, though her voice said she already knew. Maman always knew first. She simply preferred to let the rest of us arrive at our conclusions at our own pace, like slow students she was fond of anyway.

Papa looked at her. Then at Grandfather, who sat on an upturned type case near the stove with his hands folded on his knee, as patient as furniture. Then at me.

"We have paper enough," Papa said, "for one edition."

* * *

The word *edition* did something to the room. You would not think three syllables could change the temperature of a cellar, but this is a printing family, and we are a superstitious people about certain words. *Edition* meant a run. A run meant commitment. Commitment meant that whatever we printed next would be the thing the soldiers found when they kicked the door in—and everyone in that room understood that they would kick the door in eventually. The only question left was what they would find us doing when they did.

We carried the paper upstairs into the workshop proper, because no one wanted to make this decision underground, among the spiders and the false wall. There is a superstition in our trade that important choices must be made within sight of the press. My grandfather holds to it absolutely. He says a press can smell cowardice. I have never been able to determine whether he believes this literally or merely finds it useful, which is, in my experience, how most of Grandfather's beliefs operate.

So we stood in the workshop with the lamps lit and the shutters drawn, and the great press itself sat in the middle of the room like a black animal pretending to sleep, and Papa laid out the arithmetic of our situation the way he laid out everything: plainly, and with an air of mild disappointment at the universe's poor bookkeeping.

"One run," he said. "Two thousand sheets if we print both sides, twelve hundred if single-sided and larger type for tired eyes. Tired eyes matter. Half this city hasn't slept properly since the bridges closed." He tapped the stack of documents Isandro had brought us—the ledger pages, the correspondence, the whole rotten architecture of Corvel's scheme, copied out in a courier's careful hand. "This is enough to hang him. But only if enough people read it before the city falls, because after the city falls nobody will care about the crimes of a drowned republic. History has short arms. It can't reach back for you unless you put the evidence somewhere it can grab."

"That's not how history works, Alaric," said Maman.

"It's exactly how history works. It's a lazy giant. Someone always has to carry the proof uphill to it."

Grandfather cleared his throat. In our family, this is the equivalent of a trumpet fanfare. We all turned.

"I am eighty-one years old," he said. "I set my first type when I was seven, standing on a crate, and my father told me the letters were heavier than they looked because words weigh more than paper. I have printed wedding announcements and tax notices and, during the last bad year, forty thousand copies of a pamphlet explaining to frightened people how to boil water safely. I have outlived two governments and one wife who deserved better than both of them." He paused, and his eyes went around the room, resting on each of us a moment longer than comfort required. "I do not intend to die in a cellar behind a false wall. If we are going to spend this paper, let us spend it on the truth, and let us spend all of it, and let us not count the cost afterward, because counting the cost afterward is a habit for merchants and cowards, and I have never been either."

"You were briefly a merchant," said Maman. "In '41. The calendar business."

"The calendars were a public service."

"They lost money."

"They brought order to eleven households."

I laughed. I didn't mean to—it came out of me sideways, startled loose—but it broke something open in the room, the way laughter does in families, the way it lets fear in through a side door where it can be dealt with quietly instead of through the front where it makes demands.

Maman spoke next, and she spoke the way she always did at the end of arguments, which is to say as though the argument had already concluded and she was merely reading the minutes aloud. "Then we vote. All in favor of printing the edition—all of it, every sheet, Corvel's name large enough to read across a market square—say so now."

Papa raised his hand. I raised mine. Maman raised hers without looking up from the cloth she was folding, which was her way of voting while maintaining plausible deniability about having participated in any rebellion whatsoever.

We all looked at Grandfather.

He did not raise his hand. He stood instead—slowly, with the small honest sounds of an old body rising—and walked to the composing stone where the type cases waited in their long ranks, drawers of leaden letters, thousands upon thousands of them, each one cast in this room or bought by my great-grandfather before the war before the last war. He slid open the case marked with the capital C. He took out a C. He held it up to the lamp.

"This is how I vote," he said. "In metal."

And then, with hands that trembled only slightly and only at the very beginning, Auguste Vasseur set the first letter of the first line of the final edition of the Harbor Street Press: the C of CORVEL, the man's name, the accusation itself, placed on the stone like a stone.

Nobody clapped. That would have been wrong somehow. But Papa put his palm flat on the press bed, the old greeting, the one we give the machine before any run, and Maman blew out a breath she'd been holding since the bridges closed, and I felt something settle in my chest that I hadn't known was unsettled—a door closing gently, not locking. Just closing. The question of whether we would flee was finished. Now there was only work, and work was the thing our family knew how to love.

* * *

We worked until the candles burned down to their brass holders, and then we worked by lamplight, and then Maman declared that anyone who set another line of type without eating would faint into the forme and ruin it, which was her way of saying supper was ready.

We ate in the kitchen, off the good plates, which Maman only produced for occasions, and I suppose the end of the world qualified. There was bean soup and the heel of the rye loaf and a jar of pears from the cellar that Papa opened with the ceremony of a man defusing a bomb. For a little while we talked about nothing. The neighbor's dog. Whether the pear syrup could be stretched with hot water into a drink for tomorrow. Whether Grandfather's new spectacles made him look distinguished or merely surprised. He wore them tilted down his nose and reported that both effects were available depending on angle, which he demonstrated until Maman threatened him with the ladle.

It was the kind of evening we'd had a thousand times, and knowing it might be among the last made it neither sadder nor sweeter exactly, but denser. Like bread pressed tight. I caught myself memorizing it without meaning to: the steam on the window, the wobble in the table's third leg, Papa's elbow on the cloth. Memorizing is a strange inheritance for a printer's daughter. We spend our lives making copies, and here I was making one by hand, inside my own head, of a kitchen.

Afterward, Grandfather went back to the stone.

"You should sleep, Papa," said Maman.

"I will sleep when the edition sleeps." He was already standing at the case, and there was something in his posture I recognized from photographs of him as a young man—some straightness that age had bent but not broken. "Besides, your mother used to say I snore like a collapsing warehouse, and the city has enough collapsing in it tonight."

So he set type, and I stayed to assist, because someone had to hold the lamp and someone had to fetch letters from the far cases, and mostly because I could not bear to leave him alone with it. Setting type is slow work even for young hands. For his, it was glacial. He would compose a phrase in his mind, murmuring it, then pick the letters one at a time, upside down and backward as they must be, checking each against the light. Every few minutes he would stop and rest his hand on the edge of the stone and breathe.

"Tell me a story while I work," he said after a while. "Your grandmother used to. She said the type sets better if the setter is happy."

"She said that because you set faster when distracted and she could steal your best quads."

"A slanderous woman. God rest her." But he was smiling. "A story, Odile."

So I told him one. Not a real story—I invented it as I went, the way I used to when I was small and he was teaching me the alphabet from the case, telling me each letter was a little machine for holding a sound. I told him about a courier with no name who carried letters through a burning city, and how the courier discovered that the heaviest thing they ever carried was a piece of paper with their own family written on it, and how they set it down, finally, in a workshop that smelled of ink and lamp oil, and were fed soup.

Grandfather listened with his back half-turned, picking letters. When I got to the part about the soup he made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"And then what happens?" he asked.

"I don't know yet. I haven't set that far ahead."

"Neither have I," he said, nodding at the stone, at the growing stick of type, at the name CORVEL climbing line by line toward its sentence. "That's the whole of the trade, Odile. Nobody knows the ending. We just keep setting the next letter, and trust the sentence to mean something."

He worked another hour. Then his hand began to shake in earnest—not the small tremor of age but the deeper stutter of exhaustion—and I made him sit, and he protested with genuine artistry, and then he fell asleep in the chair by the stove almost mid-sentence, spectacles sliding, mouth open, dignified as a dropped marionette. I covered him with the wool blanket from the press room and he muttered something about quads and was gone.

I stood at the composing stone alone.

The forme was barely begun. At the current pace, with four of us working in shifts, we had perhaps three days of setting before we could lock the first chase, and then the printing itself, sheet by sheet, two thousand pulls of the bar, and the drying, and the folding—

Three days. Maybe less. The city outside had its own schedule, and it was not consulting ours.

I looked at the type my grandfather had set, those first lines, slightly uneven in the old way, the way that made everything he printed feel hand-made even when it came off the press by the thousand. And I thought about the paper downstairs, every sheet of it dried board by board through four secret winters by a quiet man who never told his daughter what he was saving it for, because he hadn't known himself. He had only known that truth might someday need somewhere to land.

I picked up a composing stick. I checked it against the light, the way he'd taught me when I was seven, standing on a crate.

And that was when we heard the knock—not at the street door, which would have been ordinary, but at the loading door in the alley, three soft raps and a pause, the rhythm couriers use, the rhythm Isandro Joubert had used two nights ago—and I went to see what the night had brought us, with ink still wet on my fingers.
