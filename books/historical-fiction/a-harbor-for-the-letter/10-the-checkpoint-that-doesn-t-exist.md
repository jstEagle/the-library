## 10. The Checkpoint That Doesn't Exist

The trouble with lying to a government is that governments keep records.

I had known this for years, in the way one knows water is wet—abstractly, professionally, without ever once expecting to drown in it. My whole trade rested on the fact that paper could be made to say anything. A seal was wax and a stamp; a signature was ink and a wrist. I had carried orders that moved regiments, emptied treasuries, ended friendships between ministers, and not one of those documents had been more true than a bedtime story. They had merely been believed.

But a forgery—that was different. A forgery was a story you told on your own behalf, and stories told on one's own behalf were the only ones anyone ever checked.

So on the morning of the fourth day, I sat at the back table of the Vasseur workshop with a dead man's confirmation papers spread before me like an autopsy, trying to make them tell a lie convincingly enough to save nine lives.

* * *

"You're holding the pen wrong," Grandfather Vasseur said.

"I'm holding the pen the way clerks hold pens."

"Clerks hold pens wrongly. That's how you know they're clerks." He leaned over my shoulder, smelling of pipe smoke and lamp oil, and adjusted my grip with two fingers as gentle as a compositor setting a fragile piece of type. "More from the wrist. A man who writes from the fingers is thinking about his letters. A man who writes from the wrist is thinking about his dinner. Which would you rather be suspected of?"

"Dinner," I said. "Definitely dinner."

"Then write like a hungry man."

It should be noted that I had not asked for his help. I had asked for the room—an hour of privacy with the workshop's stock of official blanks, which the Vasseurs kept for reasons I chose not to examine too closely, since examining them might have required me to arrest someone. And instead I had acquired a grandfather, a lamp, and a running commentary.

He had also acquired me, apparently. There was no other way to explain why he'd pulled up a stool.

"The seal is the hard part," he said, tapping the wax impression we were working from—a genuine impression lifted from a genuine dispatch, borrowed while its owner slept off a long evening at the Café Delorme. "Anyone can copy a hand. Hands are lazy. But wax remembers everything."

"Wax?"

"Wax has memory the way type has memory. Press it warm against the original matrix and it takes the whole truth of it—the pressure, the angle, the little crack in the stamp where Corvel's clerk dropped it in the winter of—" He stopped himself. "That is, if one had ever seen such a crack. Which no one here has."

"No one here has," I agreed solemnly.

There was a crack in the stamp. I'd found it myself, three years ago, on a dispatch I carried from Corvel's office to the Naval Ministry—a hairline fracture through the anchor of the Ministry of Order's arms, so fine that only a courier who weighed everything would ever notice. I had noticed. I always noticed. It was the entire content of my personality, some people said, though never to my face, because nobody knew my face.

Now that crack was going to buy the Vasseurs six days.

Here was the lie, laid out plainly: the letter naming them as conspirators was to travel from my hands to the Ministry of Order via the Saint-Aubin checkpoint, where a confirming officer would countersign its passage into the ministry ledger. That was procedure. Procedure was the republic's last living organ, and it still twitched. But the Saint-Aubin checkpoint had flooded—this much was even true; the river had been eating the embankment all week—and traffic had been rerouted to the Grenouille gate, which was slower, meaner, and staffed by men who took bribes in bread.

A letter delayed by flood conditions was no one's fault. A letter delayed by a courier was treason.

So I had built, over three nights, a small paper architecture: a rerouting order in the correct bureaucratic dialect, stamped with the correct cracked seal, dated correctly, signed by a confirming officer named Aubertin who had died of fever eleven days ago and therefore could not object. Dead men made excellent co-conspirators. They never wanted a share.

All of it hinged on the morning I now faced: delivering the packet to Corvel's outer office, where a clerk named Perrin would log it, weigh it, and send it onward—and where, if Perrin looked closely at anything, I would hang.

* * *

"You're going to be late," Odile said from the doorway of the composing room. She had ink on her cheek again—I was beginning to suspect she applied it deliberately, the way other women applied rouge—and she was holding two rolls wrapped in cloth. "You haven't eaten."

"I don't eat on delivery days."

"You didn't eat yesterday either."

"I was working yesterday."

"You were sitting at this table looking like a man composing his own funeral oration." She set the rolls down next to the forged papers, daring them to contaminate each other. "Eat. You can't forge anything on an empty stomach. Your hand shakes."

"My hand does not shake."

"It shook when you wrote *Aubertin*. The *b* leans like a drunk against a lamppost."

Grandfather Vasseur made a sound that in a younger man would have been called a snort. "She's right. I said nothing out of politeness, but the *b* is disgraceful."

"You said it was 'serviceable.'"

"That was politeness."

I ate the roll. It was good—dense, dark, the kind of bread that bakers made now because white flour had left the city along with everyone who could afford it—and I ate it standing up, fast, because I had decided years ago that kindness was more dangerous than checkpoints, and the Vasseur household dealt in kindness the way the mint dealt in coin.

They had also, all four of them plus the apprentice Léon, spent part of last night helping me. Nobody had discussed it. I had come down to fetch a fresh lamp and found Odile aging paper over the stove flame, Léon practicing the confirming officer's slanted hand on scrap, Madame Vasseur—Odile's mother, a woman whose approval I had spent my childhood failing to earn—mixing ink to match the ministry's particular gray-brown, which she identified by smell alone. Nobody had asked what the papers were for. Nobody had needed to. That was the thing about this family that I had forgotten for fourteen years and remembered in the space of one evening: they did not ask questions because questions implied you might answer no.

I tried not to think about what that generosity was going to cost if I failed today.

* * *

Corvel's office occupied the second floor of the old Customs House, which meant you reached it by a staircase designed to make visitors feel accused. The treads were marble worn into shallow bowls by forty years of petitioners, and the walls held portraits of ministers dead and disgraced, arranged so that whichever way you climbed, someone official seemed to be watching your hands.

My hands were clean. I had washed them twice and inspected them under daylight for ink. This sounds paranoid. It was paranoia, but paranoia is simply professionalism with the volume turned up.

Perrin sat behind the receiving desk, a narrow man with narrow eyes and a widow's peak he combed forward like a fortification. I had delivered to him perhaps thirty times over five years. He knew me the way a toll bridge knows a cart: by weight, by frequency, by the exact rattle of its wheels. He did not know my name. Couriers were furniture. Furniture was safe.

"Joubert," he said anyway.

Every sound in the room stopped existing for me. The clock on the mantel, the scratch of a pen somewhere behind the partition, my own heartbeat—all of it went silent, the way a forest goes silent when something predatory passes through.

Because Perrin had never used my name. Not once. Not in five years.

"Perrin." I let my voice do what couriers' voices do: nothing at all. Flat as a sealed envelope.

"You've heard, then." He gestured at the city beyond the window, where smoke stood up from the eastern quarter in gray columns. "Everyone knows everyone's name now. Last days of the republic—suddenly we're all neighbors." He put out his hand for the packet.

I gave it to him. My hand was steady. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: my hand was steady, and the roll of bread sat in my stomach like ballast, and I watched Perrin turn the packet over with the specific boredom of a man performing a ritual he no longer believed in.

He weighed it in his palm. Weighed it again.

"Lighter than usual," he said.

"It's a single letter."

"Single letters are usually heavier. Paper's gone thin, I know, but—" He slit the outer wrapping with a bone knife, checked the address, checked the inner seal. Checked the seal.

Checked the seal.

The crack was there, in the anchor, exactly where it lived on every genuine dispatch. I had pressed it warm, pressed it true, aged the wax with lamp smoke until it looked like something that had survived a pocket and a winter. Perrin tilted it toward the window light, and the little flaw caught the sun like a scar catching fire.

And then he said the sentence I have relived every day since, in one variation or another, usually at around three in the morning:

"Aubertin confirmed the routing, then."

"He did." Past tense. Let the grammar do the work.

"Flood at Saint-Aubin, reroute to Grenouille." He read from the confirmation slip without really reading it, the way men read things they've already decided about. "That'll add two days, maybe three."

"The river doesn't consult the ministry's schedule."

"No." He set the packet down. Picked it up. Set it down again. "No, it doesn't."

Behind the partition, the scratching pen had stopped.

I became aware, with the terrible slow clarity of a man noticing water rising around his ankles, that there was a second clerk in the back office—one I had never seen before, older, heavyset, wearing spectacles repaired with wire—and that this second clerk had stopped writing and was looking at me. Not at the packet. At me. With an expression I recognized because I had invented most of my personality out of it: the expression of a man who weighs things.

"Do I know you?" the second clerk asked.

"I don't believe so, sir."

"Hm." He came to the partition. He looked at me the way Grandfather Vasseur looked at a line of badly spaced type—with disappointment already settled in, waiting only for confirmation. "You have the look of someone. Maren's boy? No—you're too tall. You came up under Maren, though. The handler. Before he got sick."

"Maren trained half the couriers in this city," I said.

"He did, he did." The old clerk nodded slowly. "He used to bring them through here when they were green, show them the ledger, teach them the seals. 'Know the seals better than the men who stamp them,' he'd say. Terrible lesson to give children, when you think about it." He smiled. It was a kind smile, which was somehow worse. "You have Maren's stance. Weight on the back foot. Ready to leave."

The silence went on one beat too long, and I felt it stretch, and in that stretched beat I understood exactly what kind of trap this was—not a trap of evidence, because I had built my evidence well, but a trap of memory. Systems built on seals can be beaten with seals. Systems built on people remembering people cannot be beaten by any craft. Somewhere in this building was a ledger with my deliveries recorded, and beside the earliest entries, in a dead man's handwriting, possibly a note: *the quiet one, the one who watches.*

"You were fond of him," I said. "Maren."

"Fond of all of them." The old clerk sighed, and turned away, and picked up his pen, and the world resumed making noise. "Tell the new ones the same thing, whoever trains them after us. Know the seals." He waved a hand at the packet. "Log it, Perrin. Flood reroutes are God's business, not ours. Though mark the date clearly—Corvel will want to know why his correspondence is taking the scenic route."

"Yes, sir," Perrin said, and began to write.

I stood there while he logged it. I watched the nib move across the page—date, origin, seal verified, routing confirmed per attached slip—and each word went down like a stone into a wall I had built, mortaring the lie permanently into the ministry's own record. There is a strange intimacy in watching someone believe you. You stand very close to them while they do it. You could touch them. In a sense you already have.

"Three days, then," Perrin said, not looking up. "Give my regards to the river."

"I always do," I said, and walked down the accusing staircase on legs made of someone else's bones.

* * *

I did not go straight back. Rule one: never arrive anywhere directly from anywhere. I crossed the Pont aux Chats, bought apples I didn't want from a vendor who didn't look at me, doubled back through the fish market, and stood for ten full minutes studying a window display of mourning clothes, which the shopkeeper had inexplicably arranged in cheerful colors, the city having apparently decided that grief this year would be festive.

Only when I was certain—certain the way I was certain of nothing else in my life—did I take the alley to the loading door and give three soft raps and a pause.

Odile opened it before I finished the pause. She had been watching for me; I could see it in her face, the tension arranged behind the ordinary expression like type locked in a forme, waiting only for the press.

"Well?" she said.

"It's done. Logged. Sealed. Three-day delay, entered in the ministry ledger in Perrin's own hand, witnessed by a senior clerk."

Her breath went out of her. "Then—"

"There was a moment," I said, "when an old man who knew my first teacher looked at me across a desk and asked if he knew me, and I lied to his face, and he believed me, and I am going to need to sit down."

She sat me down. She was very practical about it—shoved me onto a crate of paper stock, put an apple in my hand, and crouched in front of me with her ink-stained chin level with mine, studying my face with the frank diagnostic attention printers give to smudged proofs.

"You're shaking," she said.

"I'm not."

"Your hand is shaking."

"That's the apple's fault. Apples imply a certain tremor."

"Isandro." She said my name—my real name, the one almost no one alive used—and it landed differently than Perrin's version had. Perrin's use of my name had been a searchlight. Hers was a lamp on a table. "It worked. Say it worked, and then fall apart, in that order."

"It worked." My voice did something embarrassing on the second word. I steadied it. "It worked, Odile. Six days. Maybe seven, if the river cooperates."

From across the workshop, where the press stood under its dust sheet like a sleeping animal, Grandfather Vasseur raised his mug of chicory in salute. Madame Vasseur, at the stone sink, did not turn around, but her shoulders lowered by a degree, which from her was applause. Léon, seventeen and incapable of subtlety, pumped his fist and then pretended he hadn't.

"We should celebrate," Odile said.

"We should print."

"After. Tonight we celebrate. Tomorrow we print." She took the apple back out of my hand, bit it herself, and handed it to me with the bite mark facing me, which was either carelessness or a joke, and with Odile it was never carelessness. "Six days, Joubert. Do you understand what we can do with six days?"

I did. I had done the arithmetic on the walk home, the same arithmetic she'd been doing at the composing stick: paper for one run, ink for one run, time for one edition of perhaps eight hundred sheets—eight hundred copies of Corvel's forged orders and the ledger proving them forged, scattered into the evacuation crowds like seeds into wind. Enough to poison his name in every mouth from the harbor to the gates. Not enough to save the republic. Nothing was going to save the republic. The republic had stopped accepting rescue some weeks ago and now lay in bed with its boots on, waiting.

But eight hundred sheets could save the truth from being buried with it. That was worth six days. That was worth the old clerk's kind smile and the stones in my stomach and every hour of the rest of this lie.

"One problem," I said, when I could speak steadily again.

"One?"

"One. The delay buys time only if Corvel believes it. And Corvel doesn't believe weather. He believes schedules. Sooner or later—probably sooner—he'll ask why his private correspondence is moving at the speed of mud." I looked at the press under its sheet. "Someone is going to have to be in that office when he asks. Someone with a face he trusts."

Odile followed my gaze to the press, and then brought it back to me, and I watched her understand, in real time, what I was proposing for chapter six of this disaster.

"You," she said.

"Me. I'm the only courier whose face means nothing and everything. I carry for him. I've carried for him for years. When the letter arrives late, he'll want an explanation, and the explanation has to come from the one person who couldn't have tampered with it."

"Because tampering would be suicide."

"Because tampering would be suicide," I agreed. "Invisibility is my alibi. It's the only one I've got."

She was quiet a moment, turning the apple over in her ink-dark fingers. Then she smiled, and it wasn't entirely a comforting smile.

"You know what my grandfather would say? He'd say you've just set yourself as the last line of a page, Joubert. Everything above you can be corrected. The last line goes to press whether it's right or wrong." She tossed me the apple core. "So don't be wrong."

Outside, down the street toward the river, a church bell began to ring—and then stopped mid-peal, raggedly, as if the ringer had simply walked away. In this city, lately, bells did not finish their sentences.

I had five days left to make sure I wasn't wrong, and I had just realized, sitting on a crate in the only home I'd ever fled, that I had no idea how to begin being right.
