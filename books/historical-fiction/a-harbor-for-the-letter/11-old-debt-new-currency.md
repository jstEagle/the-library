## 11. Old Debt, New Currency

The silver had lived in the cellar longer than Odile had been alive.

She knew this because her grandfather had told her so roughly four hundred times, usually while polishing it, usually while saying something like, *Your grandmother carried this spoon across two borders and a river in flood, so mind how you hold it.* The spoon in question was not remarkable. It was slightly bent at the neck, as if it had once been used to pry open something that should have stayed shut—a family history in miniature, her father liked to joke, though never within earshot of Grandfather Vasseur.

There were eleven pieces in all. A soup tureen with a lid that no longer sat straight. Six spoons of varying dignity. Two candlesticks gone dull as old coins, because they were old coins, more or less, melted and reshaped by some ancestor with more ambition than silversmithing skill. And a small dish shaped like a shell that had never held anything but buttons.

Odile laid them all out on the composing stone on Thursday morning, in the gray hour before the apprentices arrived, and looked at them the way one looks at a beloved animal one is about to send to market.

"You could sell your hair instead," said Perrin, the youngest apprentice, who had appeared at her elbow with the silent, unnerving skill he applied to everything. He was seventeen, all elbows and earnestness, with ink permanently ground into the creases of his knuckles like a stain from a previous life.

"My hair isn't worth a sack of flour."

"Your hair is *magnificent*."

"That's very kind and entirely useless." She wrapped the tureen in an apron. "Besides, if we're caught, they'll cut more than hair."

Perrin went quiet at that, which she regretted. Fear was a tool; you sharpened it when you needed it and put it away when you didn't, and she'd just left hers lying out where a boy could cut himself on it. She softened her voice. "We need three days, Perrin. Three days of press time without interruption—paper, grease, someone watching the street, someone bribing the watchman on the corner. Courage is free. Everything else costs."

"And the silver pays for it."

"The silver pays for it."

He nodded slowly, then, with the gravity of a man consigning a comrade to the earth: "Grandfather's going to cry about the spoons."

"Grandfather has been crying about the spoons since before you were born. It's practically a hobby."

* * *

Brannoc kept his office above a rope-maker's shop on the Rue du Treuil, which Odile considered a fitting address for a smuggler, since everything about the man suggested he was good at holding things together under strain. She had met him twice before—once when the workshop needed Dutch paper during the embargo, and once when her father needed to be extracted from a tavern argument with a tax collector, a task Brannoc had accomplished with what witnesses described as "surprising gentleness" and what her father described, for weeks afterward, as "a humiliation I intend to repay."

The stairs smelled of hemp and tar. At the top, a door stood open onto a room that contained almost nothing: a table, two chairs, a window overlooking the rope-yard, and Brannoc himself, a mountain of a man with a beard going to iron-gray and hands like a printer's nightmare—broad, scarred, and stained in ways ink would never manage.

"Mademoiselle Vasseur." He did not get up. He never got up; she suspected chairs were largely decorative to him. "Sit. You have the look of a woman about to ask me for something inconvenient."

"Do I?"

"You've got your mother's face and your father's posture. Your mother asked for things beautifully. Your father asks for things like he's filing a complaint with heaven." He pushed a chipped cup toward her. It contained something dark and hot that smelled faintly of chicory and faintly of arson. "Drink that. It's terrible. It builds character."

Odile drank it. It built character.

"I need three days," she said. "Quiet ones. Paper moved through the east gate without inspection. Grease for the press—I'm told even the black market has run short of tallow. And a man named Gaspard on the corner of our street, who watches doors for the Ministry and pretends he doesn't, needs to develop a sudden interest in the opposite direction."

"That's four things."

"Three days," she said. "I said three days of quiet. The quiet contains four things."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but a cousin of one. "And what are you offering? Because I'll tell you now, if it's the broadsheets themselves, the answer's no. I don't carry sedition. Sedition attracts soldiers, and soldiers attract questions, and questions are bad for the rope business."

She unwrapped the apron.

The tureen came out first, then the spoons in their rows, then the candlesticks, and finally the shell dish, which she set down last with a care that surprised her. In the flat gray light from the window, the silver looked less like treasure than like bones—the remains of some large, comfortable animal that had once been her family's life.

Brannoc picked up a spoon. Turned it over. Bent the neck between finger and thumb, gently, testing, and Odile felt the bend in her own chest.

"Old work," he said. "Good weight. This one's been used to open something it shouldn't have."

"It's a family tradition."

He set it down. "This buys your paper. Maybe the grease. It does not buy Gaspard. Watchmen cost more than silver these days—they know their trade is ending and they price accordingly, like every other vulture in this city."

"What does Gaspard cost?"

"A favor."

The word landed in the room and sat there.

"No," Odile said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I've heard it. Everyone in this city has heard it. It's always 'a favor,' and it's always later, and later always comes at the worst possible moment, wearing boots."

Brannoc leaned back until his chair creaked in protest. Through the window behind him, the rope-yard stretched out long and pale, men walking backward down its length, paying line out hand over hand. She found herself watching them. That was the trick of the place, she thought. You couldn't make rope standing still. You had to walk away from the thing you were making and trust the twist to hold.

"Mademoiselle," Brannoc said, "let me tell you what I know, free of charge, since you're clearly deciding whether to be insulted. I know your shop is printing something big. I know it isn't almanacs, because almanac printers don't need the east gate, they need the west, where the paper warehouses are. I know your father hasn't paid me a visit in a month, which means either he's dead or he's busy, and dead men don't leave daughters with good posture and empty pockets. And I know that in nine days—" he held up a hand, thick fingers splayed, "—this city evacuates, or burns, or both, depending on whose rumor you prefer. So whatever you're printing, you're printing against a clock, and clocks, unlike favors, cannot be negotiated with."

Odile said nothing. Her heart was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"So here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and then we will drink more of this coffee and speak of the weather like civilized people. I move your paper through the east gate tonight. I find your tallow. I lean on Gaspard—an old debt of his own, as it happens, which makes him cheap, which is fortunate, because he's also a coward, and cowards are expensive in courage but cheap in coin. And in exchange, you owe me one favor. One. Unspecified. Redeemable at a time of my choosing, before the year is out."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you keep your silver and lose your three days, and in nine days it won't matter what anyone owes anyone, because we'll all be standing in a road somewhere counting our shoes." He shrugged, vast and slow, like a hill changing its mind. "I'm not threatening you, girl. Threats are for men with more imagination than honesty. I'm telling you the weather."

She looked at the silver on the table. Eleven pieces. Her grandmother's border-crossing spoon. The buttons that had never left the shell dish, because there had always seemed to be time to sort them properly someday.

Every escape route has a price. Her father said that too, though he said it about taxes, mostly. But sitting in the tar-smelling room above the rope-walk, with the city ticking down around her like a cooling stove, Odile understood that he'd had the direction wrong. It wasn't that escape cost. It was that staying cost, and leaving cost, and printing cost, and silence cost, and the only question worth asking was which bill you were willing to sign your name to.

"One favor," she said. "Unspecified. Within the year. But I want it in writing, and I want the writing to say that the favor cannot require me to print lies, carry weapons, or inform on my family."

For the first time, Brannoc smiled fully, and it transformed his face from something forbidding into something almost jolly, like a bear that had decided the picnic was friendly. "Conditions! Your mother negotiated exactly like this. She once made me promise, in writing, that a crate of contraband lace would arrive *uncreased*." He hauled open a drawer, produced paper, a pen, and an inkwell of surprising elegance—smugglers, she reflected, were connoisseurs of other people's stationery—and wrote while she watched over his shoulder:

*Received of Odile Vasseur: silver goods, eleven pieces, in fair condition, one bent. Owed in return: passage of goods, procurement of materials, and the redirection of one (1) watchman, all within three days. Owed by Odile Vasseur to Brannoc: one (1) favor, unspecified, redeemable within the year, said favor not to include falsehoods in print, bearing of arms, or betrayal of family.*

He signed it with a mark rather than a name—a coiled length of rope—and pushed it across the table along with the pen.

"Sign," he said. "And then take the shell dish back."

She blinked. "What?"

"The dish. Take it home. It's not silver, really—it's pewter with pretensions, and it weighs nothing, and I'd only throw it in a drawer." He was already wrapping the rest in sailcloth, his huge hands moving with unexpected delicacy. "Keep the buttons. A household should keep one thing it doesn't need."

Odile signed her name—her full name, *Vasseur* and all—and took the little dish, and discovered that her eyes had gone hot, which was ridiculous, because it was pewter, it weighed nothing, and she had just sold her grandmother's spoon to a man who bent silver to test it.

But Brannoc, wrapping candlesticks, pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of courtesy.

* * *

The walk home took longer than usual, because she kept stopping.

At the corner by the fishmonger's, a crowd had gathered around a man selling what he claimed were government evacuation schedules and what everyone privately understood to be fiction of a high order—dates and gates and guarantees, printed on paper so thin you could read a lie through it. Odile bought one anyway, for a sou, because she wanted to feel its weight. It was lighter than a broadsheet. It was lighter than nothing. Somewhere in the workshop, under the floor, beneath the forme her grandfather had set with his own failing hands, lay pages that would weigh something. Pages people would fold and hide and pass hand to hand like coins.

That was the difference, she thought, walking on. Lies printed thin. Truth printed heavy. You could feel it in the palm.

Gaspard the watchman stood at his post near the workshop street, collar up against the wind, radiating the studied boredom of a man being paid to see nothing. As she passed, he glanced at her—at her, specifically—and then quickly away, and there was something in the glance that hadn't been there yesterday. Something nervous. Someone had already spoken to him. Brannoc worked fast, or Gaspard's own debts were catching up with him, or both.

Three days. She let herself feel it for half a block—the giddy, terrified lift of it, like the first pull of a press lever, all that weight gathering to come down clean on blank paper. Three days of quiet. Paper coming through the east gate tonight. Tallow by morning. The run beginning tomorrow if the forme held, if the type survived its hiding, if her hands remembered everything her grandfather had taught them.

Then she turned onto the Rue des Presses and stopped again, because there was smoke in the air.

Not their smoke. Not chimney smoke, not the sweet resinous smell of the glue pot. This was sharper, uglier, with a chemical edge underneath, and it was coming from the direction of the Chardon et Fils bindery, two streets over, where old Chardon had bound ledgers for the Ministry for thirty years and told anyone who listened that the Ministry was run by thieves.

A bell was ringing somewhere. People were running—not away, which was the strange part, but *toward*, buckets in their arms, the whole quarter suddenly tilting like a ship.

Odile stood in the middle of the street with a pewter dish full of buttons clutched to her chest, and understood that the nine days had just gotten shorter.

She ran.
