## 12. The City Begins to Burn

The smoke came before the bells.

Isandro noticed it first as a taste—char and something sweeter under it, like sugar left too long on a stove—and only then looked up from the alley mouth where they'd been waiting out a patrol. The sky above the printing quarter had gone the color of old pewter, except in the east, where a bruise of orange was spreading behind the rooftops as though the sun had decided to rise four hours early and do it angrily.

They counted the bells. Three strokes, then silence, then three more. Not the curfew bell. Not the alarm that meant soldiers in the streets, which every child in the quarter could recite along with their multiplication tables. This was the fire bell, rung by hand, ragged and desperate, the way you'd ring it if your hands were shaking or if whoever was supposed to ring it had already run.

Isandro stood very still in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery and did what they always did: took inventory. One satchel, currently empty of everything but a change of shirt and a forged confirmation paper they were rather proud of. Two coppers. One knife, one bootlace, one name—not their own—that would get them past two of the city's remaining checkpoints. The rules held steady even now, especially now: carry nothing personal, know no one, weigh everything.

The trouble with rules, Isandro thought, watching the orange bruise spread, was that they assumed the world would stay organized enough to check them.

A woman ran past with a washbasin full of nothing at all. A boy followed her carrying a stool, which was not useful for firefighting but seemed to be giving him courage. Somewhere down the Street of Presses, glass broke—a whole window's worth, the bright crunch of it—and then voices, many voices, layered over each other like type set by a drunk compositor.

Isandro should have gone north. The safehouse on the Rue des Trois Chandeliers was forty minutes away if the crowds were kind, and the crowds were not going to be kind. There was a dispatch due at dusk. There was a route mapped in their head with the neatness of a hymnal, and every line of it went around the burning thing, not toward it.

Their feet, which had served them faithfully through six years of checkpoints and never once developed opinions, turned east.

* * *

The fire had started at the Duret shop, the bindery two doors down from the Vasseurs' workshop, and by the time Isandro reached the corner it had climbed into the eaves and was working through the tarred beams with the appetite of a creditor. The Durets had bound books for forty years—ledgers, psalters, once, scandalously, a run of romances with covers so gilded the ministry had confiscated half the edition—and now all of it was feeding the flames page by page, and the heat came off the building in waves you could lean against.

The bucket line stretched from the corner pump to the shop door, maybe thirty people long, most of them printers' folk in leather aprons, passing pails hand to hand with the grim rhythm of people who already knew how the evening would end. Water went up. Steam and black rain came down. Between the two, the fire ate.

"They're saying it started with a torch," said a voice beside Isandro, "but between us, I've seen how the Durets store their lamp oil. That man has been courting combustion for a decade."

It was Madame Ferrand from the paper stall, sixty years old, built like a flour barrel, holding a bucket in both arms as if it were a sleeping cat she disapproved of. She squinted at Isandro. Isandro braced for the question—who are you, whose are you, which side—and began assembling the answer out of borrowed names.

"You," Madame Ferrand said instead, thrusting her bucket at them. "You have young shoulders and an unoccupied look about you. Corner position. Move."

And here was the thing Isandro would think about later, turning it over in the dark like a coin found in a coat pocket: they moved. No alias offered, no weighing of risk, no careful calculation of exits. A stranger had shouted at them and their body had simply obeyed, the way a body obeys when something long-unused finally gets used. They took the bucket, took the corner position, and became, without ceremony, the fourth link in a chain of strangers.

The work was simple and enormous. Pump, fill, pass, empty, pass back. The buckets were mismatched—one had FERRAND & FILS stenciled on the side, another had clearly begun life as a soup pot, and a third was a hatbox lined with pitch that leaked exactly enough to keep everyone humble. The line swayed and cursed and passed. Smoke got into everyone's lungs and made the cursing creative.

"Left! Your left!" someone yelled at Isandro ten minutes in, and Isandro, who had been passing right-handed out of habit, corrected, and the man who'd yelled—a journeyman binder with forearms like hams—nodded as though a great contract had been executed. "Good. You'll do."

Will do what? Isandro didn't ask. It didn't matter. That was the strange mercy of a bucket line: it asked nothing of you but the next bucket, and gave nothing back but the next bucket, and somewhere inside that exchange the whole question of who you were quietly dissolved. For six years Isandro had worked to be no one. Here, being no one was the entire job description, and everyone qualified.

Then the wind shifted.

It came off the river in a gust that smelled of coming rain that wasn't going to come, and it pushed the fire sideways, toward the gap between the Duret shop and its neighbor—which was to say, toward the workshop with the green door and the brass plate reading VASSEUR & FILS, LETTRES ET IMAGES, which Isandro had spent three days pretending not to look at.

The line felt it before anyone said anything. The rhythm stuttered. Faces turned east.

"Not tonight," said Madame Ferrand, with the tone of a woman informing the weather of her opinion of it. "I have known that family since before some of you were typeset. Nobody is burning the Vasseurs today. Widen the line! Wet the roofs!"

What followed had, Isandro realized afterward, the shape of something rehearsed—not by these particular people, perhaps, but by the quarter itself, by a street of presses that had survived two wars and three changes of government largely by agreeing, collectively, to be more trouble than it was worth. The bucket line split. Half stayed on the Duret shop, officially containing it, actually conceding it. The other half formed a second chain to the Vasseur roof, hauling water up ladders and over the party wall, soaking the shingles until they gleamed black in the firelight.

Isandro ended up on the ladder.

They hadn't decided to be on the ladder. There had been a moment when the binder with the ham forearms—he was called Perrin, apparently, though he answered to "you, again"—had needed someone light and unafraid of heights, and had looked down the line, and his gaze had landed on Isandro the way a finger lands on a name in a ledger. "You. Up. Take the pail to the ridge and pour slow, we haven't water to waste."

"I'm not—" Isandro started, meaning to say not yours, not anyone's, and then caught sight of the green door below, and whatever they meant to say came out as nothing, and then they were climbing.

From the ridge of the Vasseur roof, the whole burning world arranged itself legibly. The Duret shop roared and sagged. The crowd below looked small and stubborn, a spill of dark figures around the pump. And beyond the printing quarter, across the rooftops, other fires—smaller ones, meaner ones—were blooming in the direction of the ministries, and there were sounds reaching this far: shouting, the crackle of barricades being built or broken, once a long rolling boom that Perrin, hauling up the next pail, identified without looking up as "the arsenal, probably. Or a bakery. Hard to say till morning."

"Pour slow," Perrin reminded them.

Isandro poured slow. The water went down the tiles in dark sheets, and the steam rose, and the fire, meeting the wet wall of the Vasseur roofline, snarled and spat and found nowhere further to go. Twice embers landed on the shingles near Isandro's knees, glowing like furious punctuation, and twice Isandro drowned them with the heel of a boot and a splash from the pail, feeling an anger they couldn't remember having felt in years—clean anger, pointed at a specific target, a luxury their old life had never allowed. You cannot be angry at a checkpoint. You can be angry at a fire.

Somewhere below, a shutter banged open and a voice Isandro knew—knew the way you know a scar—called up: "Who's on my roof?"

Odile. Even muffled by smoke and distance, the voice carried its old architecture: every sentence a door closing firmly on its own argument. Isandro pressed themselves flat against the chimney stack like a courier hiding a document, which was, they reflected, the single least effective concealment available, given that they were silhouetted against a burning skyline.

"The neighbor lad," Perrin called back, cheerfully lying on Isandro's behalf for reasons Isandro could not fathom. "Good worker. Doesn't talk much."

"Doesn't talk much," Odile repeated, in the tone of a woman filing the phrase away for later prosecution. "Fine. Tell him the gutter on the north side drains onto my father's type storage, and if he lets one spark down that drain I will personally—"

"We hear you, mademoiselle!"

"—there will be consequences," Odile finished anyway, because she was Odile, and doors closed fully in her house or not at all. The shutter banged shut.

Perrin grinned up at Isandro, teeth white in his smoke-black face. "She's like that in a flood too, they say. You know her, then?"

"No," said Isandro, and handed him the pail, and did not examine why saying it felt like setting a letter in type wrong-side-up.

* * *

The Duret shop came down at half past nine with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and after that it was mostly mop-up—soaking smoldering timbers, standing guard over the gap where the fire might have jumped, passing around a bottle of something that Madame Ferrand produced from her apron with the solemnity of a sacrament. Isandro drank from it when it reached them, coughed, and was laughed at by three separate strangers, none of whom asked their name, all of whom somehow now knew them.

That was the part that unsettled. Visibility, in Isandro's old arithmetic, was a debt that came due at checkpoints. But here in the ash-light, being seen appeared to function differently. The baker's wife pressed a heel of bread into their hand and said, "You were on the roof, weren't you, with the north gutter?" A boy of maybe eight looked up at them and announced, "You poured water on our street," with the gravity of a magistrate reading a verdict. Perrin clapped them on the shoulder hard enough to reset their spine and said, "Same time next riot, eh?" and everyone within earshot laughed, and Isandro heard themself laugh too—a short, rusty sound, like a press engaging after years idle.

They were, Isandro understood slowly, being filed. Not into any ministry's ledger—into the quarter's. Into the loose, unofficial registry of people-who-showed-up, which had no seals and no forgeries and, in Isandro's professional assessment, considerably better security than anything Corvel's clerks maintained.

Madame Ferrand settled onto the pump step beside them near midnight, offered the bottle again, and studied them with eyes that had appraised reams of paper for flaws for forty years.

"Ferrand & Fils," Isandro said, nodding at the bucket still sitting nearby. "Your husband runs the stall on the Rue aux Oies?"

"My late husband invented that stall. My son neglects it adequately. And you're the one who's been haunting the corner by the green door for three days." She said it mildly, the way you'd note a change in the weather. "Don't bother denying it. I sell paper; I notice what stands still."

Isandro's pulse did something unprofessional. "I'm waiting on a delivery."

"Mm." Madame Ferrand drank. "The Vasseurs are good people," she said, to the fire, to the street, to nobody in particular. "Stubborn as set type and twice as hard to move, but good. The grandfather taught half this quarter to read for free, back when free cost you. The daughter argues with inspectors the way other women argue with weather—uselessly and magnificently." She paused. "Whatever letter you're carrying, courier, I'd weigh it twice."

The word courier landed like a dropped forme. Isandro turned, and Madame Ferrand met their eyes with perfect calm, entirely unafraid, a woman who had watched governments come and go from behind a paper stall and expected to watch several more.

"I don't know what you—"

"Of course not." She stood, groaning in the manner of her generation, and retrieved her bucket. "You carry nothing personal, you know no one, you weigh everything. My husband used to say the same about money." At the edge of the firelight she stopped, silhouetted against the smoking ruin of the Duret shop. "For what it's worth—you were on the ladder fast for someone who doesn't know anybody. Fastest I've seen. Almost like you'd been practicing for years."

She went off into the dark with her bucket, and Isandro sat on the pump step holding bread they'd been given by a stranger, laughing at a joke told by a stranger, named—if not by name, then by deed—in the memory of a whole street.

The rules had not broken, exactly. Isandro checked them carefully, one by one, the way you check your pockets after a fall. Carry nothing personal: they were still carrying the letter, still carrying the lie, still carrying six years of practiced blankness. Know no one: technically intact, though the technicality was beginning to creak. Weigh everything—

Weigh everything. And what weighed heaviest, now, in the balance? A sealed order that would bring soldiers to the green door. A gutter that drained onto a father's type storage. A woman who argued with inspectors magnificently. An old man who had taught a quarter to read.

The bells rang again—two strokes now, weary, the all-clear or nearly. The crowd thinned. Ash drifted down the Street of Presses like grey snow, settling on the wet cobbles, on the green door, on the brass plate that the fire had spared by the width of a gutter.

Isandro stood, brushed the ash from their coat, and found they were walking toward the workshop before deciding to walk toward the workshop. There was a figure in the doorway—arms crossed, silhouette unmistakable, a woman keeping watch over her own roof in the middle of a falling city—and she had seen them coming, and she did not close the door.

Isandro had a speech prepared, some alias, some errand. It evaporated somewhere around the third step.

"You're the one who haunted my corner," Odile Vasseur said. "And you're the one Perrin lied for, which he does for no one. So." Her eyes, lit from behind by the banked glow of the dying fire, narrowed. "Before you say a single word—whoever you are—you should know that tomorrow we start setting the most dangerous pages this quarter has ever printed, and I am short exactly one pair of hands I can trust."

She stepped back from the doorway, leaving it open behind her—an opening, an invitation, an accusation, all three.

"Well?" she said. "Are you coming in, or do you plan to haunt the corner until the city finishes burning?"
