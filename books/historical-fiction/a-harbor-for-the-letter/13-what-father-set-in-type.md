## 13. What Father Set in Type

The grandfather clock in the corner of the workshop had not told the correct time in eleven years, and Grandfather Vasseur refused to have it repaired on the grounds that a pressroom should keep its own hours anyway. So when Odile looked up from the stone where she'd been grinding ink and saw the light through the south windows had gone the color of weak cider, she knew only that it was late, and that her grandfather was still standing at the composing stick, and that he had been standing there too long.

"Papi," she said. "You've set that line three times."

"I'm checking it," he said, without turning around.

"You've checked it three times."

"Then I'm checking it a fourth time, so we're all certain."

He was eighty-one years old and he stood at the case the way other men stood at altars, and Odile loved him for it, and she also knew—had known for two days now, watching him squint at the type and hold each galley up to the window as though the light itself might be persuaded to testify—that something was wrong with his eyes. Not wrong in the ordinary way of eighty-one. Wrong in the way of a man whose hands still remembered everything but whose eyes were quietly resigning their post.

She said nothing. There was a run to finish. There were nine days on the courier's count—or eight now, or seven; Isandro had left before dawn to move the false checkpoint papers along their fictitious route, and Odile had stood in the doorway watching them go and thought: *I am trusting my family's lives to someone who used to steal plums off our drying rack.* The plums, apparently, had been forgiven. She was working on the rest.

The workshop smelled the way it always had—ink and oil and the particular dusty sweetness of lead type, which Odile had been breathing since before she could walk and which she fully intended to be breathing on her deathbed, wherever and whenever the republic allowed such a thing. Outside, the city grumbled. Two streets over, the bakeries had been looted that morning, and the smoke from somewhere east of the canal kept changing its mind about which direction the wind blew. Inside, the presses waited like patient animals, and the forme for the first pages sat locked in its chase, and her father's evidence against Minister Corvel lay in a stack of drafts on the proof table, written in a hand that shook only when he wrote the man's name.

"Papi," she tried again. "Sit down. Eat something. There's bread."

"Bread is for after the first four pages are set."

"Bread is for staying alive long enough to set them."

He turned then, and gave her the look—the one that had cowed apprentices, inspectors, two bishops, and one memorable tax collector—and opened his mouth to deliver whatever proverb about bread and duty he had ready, and instead his knees simply resigned their commission all at once, the way soldiers do when nobody is looking.

Odile caught him mostly. Mostly was enough to keep his head off the floor of the type case, which was not enough to keep her heart out of her throat. She lowered him down along the cabinet until he was sitting, absurdly, among the lower-case drawers like a king enthroned in vowels, and his face had gone the color of unprimed paper.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You are not fine."

"I am *temporarily* fine. It's a different thing." He waved a hand at her, feeble but imperious. "Water. And don't tell your mother yet. Let me find the sentence first."

"There is no sentence, Papi. Your eyes are going and your legs followed them out."

"My eyes are *conserving*," he said with dignity, and then, because he had never in his life let a true thing pass by unexamined, he added, quieter: "They're tired, little one. They've read a great deal."

She brought water. She did not tell her mother yet. She sat beside him among the drawers while he drank, and his hand found hers, ink-blackened both of them, indistinguishable in the dusk.

"The pages," he said. "The last two. Corvel's treasury order, the dates, the routing of the funds through the evacuation levies. Nobody else knows where everything sits in the case. You do, but you'll be slow, and slow is—" He stopped himself. He never finished sentences that were cruel. "Slow is honest work," he amended. "But we haven't got honest time."

"How long has it been bad?"

"A year." He said it lightly, the way you'd confess to having eaten the last of the jam. "Perhaps two. I memorized the drafts. That's why I keep setting the same line over and over—I'm not checking it, I'm saying goodbye to it."

Odile laughed, because if she didn't she was going to cry, and there was no time for crying either, and her grandfather squeezed her hand as if he'd heard the whole argument happen inside her and approved of her verdict.

Then he taught her. Right there on the floor, propped against the jobbing case, he walked her through where he'd hidden the special sorts—the long s they used for the forged documents facsimiles, the row of asterisks he'd cast himself, the drawer where the thin brass rules lived that would box Corvel's own signature reproduction tight as a coffin. His voice stayed steady. His memory was a cathedral; his eyes had merely lost the keys.

"Go on," he said finally. "Set it. I'll listen. I've spent sixty years listening to this room. I can hear a wrong sort from across the street."

* * *

Setting type alone was a different religion.

When Odile was small, the workshop had hummed with nine compositors, and the sound of it—metal whispering into sticks, galleys sliding, someone always singing badly—had been the sound of safety itself. Now it was her, alone at midnight, by lamp, working the case her grandfather had worked since before her father was born, and the silence had a texture to it. Every piece of type clicked into place like a word spoken into an empty church.

Her mother and father had taken Papi upstairs over her loud objections and his louder ones. Her brother Tomas was out at the canal district seeing whether Brannoc's cart could come around the back earlier than planned. Isandro was somewhere in the dark doing whatever couriers did to make a checkpoint exist retroactively. So it was Odile and the case and the drafts.

She read them as she set them, because you must read what you set—it was the first law of the shop—and reading them made the hair on her arms stand up even in the warmth of the lamp.

Corvel had not stumbled into treason. He had *composed* it, the way a compositor builds a page: piece by piece, line by line, with an eye for spacing. The Vasseurs' supposed conspiracy existed in a file he had assembled himself—witnesses paid, letters forged in a hand Odile recognized with a sick lurch as resembling her own father's. The evacuation was the justification; the treasury was the prize; the "emergency custody" of the state's remaining gold was to pass, by a decree already drafted and awaiting only the right moment of panic, into a ministry account with Corvel's cousin's name on it. And the arrests—the arrest orders naming the Vasseur workshop, naming *her*, naming Papi—were dated five days *before* the alleged conspiracy was discovered.

Her grandfather had caught it the way he caught everything: by reading closely. A date out of order. A signature whose ink was newer than its paper. Sixty years of listening for the wrong sort, applied to a government.

"He built it in a stick," she murmured to the empty room, "and never proofed it." Somewhere, distantly, she felt sure her grandfather heard her and was pleased.

She worked. Her fingers bled a little at the knuckle where a ligature bit her, the old familiar sting, and she wrapped it in a rag without stopping, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the image of Papi sitting among the drawers like a deposed king, and she couldn't afford that image yet. Later. There would be later, or there wouldn't, and either way the pages came first.

It was near the bottom of the draft stack, hunting for the sheet with the treasury figures, that she found the loose pages that didn't belong.

They were older paper. Warmer, softer, yellowed at the edges—the good stock the shop hadn't bought in years. And the hand on them was Papi's, but younger, steadier, the hand from her childhood, the one that had written her name in the margin of her first proof sheet with a flourish she'd spent weeks trying to copy.

The topmost page bore a single line:

*For Isandro, who will not take a letter from us, so I shall write one anyway.*

Odile went very still.

* * *

There are moments when a room changes temperature without any physical cause. The lamp seemed to dim politely, giving her privacy. She sat down on the composing stool with the pages in her lap like contraband, which in a sense they were—the most carefully smuggled document ever to sit in a Vasseur workshop, smuggled across fourteen years without moving an inch.

She knew the story the way everyone in the family knew it, the official version, worn smooth from handling: Isandro had been taken in as a child, some distant connection, an orphan of the border troubles; they'd grown up in the shop, quick-fingered, quiet, half-wild; and at fourteen, after the quarrel with Papi over the stolen money—no one ever said exactly what money—they had left in the night and never come back, and Papi had raged, and then gone silent on the subject forever, and the silence had calcified into policy. *We do not speak of the apprentice who left.* Even the phrase had a formal, heraldic quality, like the name of a battle everyone agreed to stop commemorating.

Odile had been ten when Isandro went. She remembered a figure on the stairs with a bundle, backlit, and she remembered being told afterward not to cry, that people who leave choose the road over the family, and that love doesn't follow those who refuse to be found. She had obeyed. She had built her whole understanding of Isandro out of that obedience, brick by brick, and here in her lap was a document suggesting the entire structure rested on a foundation of—of what? She unfolded the second page with hands that were suddenly not a printer's steady hands at all.

*They think I am angry,* Papi had written, in that young strong hand. *Let them. Anger is a coat that keeps a secret warm.*

*The purge lists came again today. Our name is on them—not for printing, worse luck, for sheltering. And Isandro's name is beneath ours, entered in the clerk's book as kin. As OURS. Someone in the ministry decided long ago that the child we took in belongs to us on paper, and paper is the one truth this republic believes.*

*I cannot get the boy's name off the list. I can only get the boy off the street. So we will drive him out—we will make it unbearable, I will play the tyrant, your mother will weep beautifully—and he will vanish the way only Isandro can vanish, and in four years, when the lists burn or the ministers do, we will find him. I have already begun saving for his journey. He must never know. If he knows, he stays, and if he stays, they hang us all side by side, and I will not have my stubbornness hanged onto that child's neck.*

*Tell me this is wisdom. Some nights I am not certain. Some nights I lie awake and think: he will believe it. He will believe we cast him off, and he will carry that stone in his chest his whole life, and I am choosing to put it there. But the alternative is a rope, and I am a coward about ropes, and perhaps that too is wisdom.*

*If you are reading this, Isandro, then either I have finally found my courage, or the world has finally become safe enough for the truth, or—most likely—you have found it yourself, because you were always the one who found things in this shop that we hid, including the plums, yes, we knew about the plums, we counted them, we let you think you were clever.*

Odile pressed the page flat against her knee. She was crying now, quietly, uselessly, the tears landing near the ink, and she pulled it away fast, horrified—a printer's first instinct, protect the sheet—and laughed at herself through it, a wet broken sound in the empty shop.

There were more pages. Dates. Accounts—a ledger of sums saved under a false heading, *Misc. Repairs*, growing year by year, never spent. Notes in the margins tracking rumors: *seen at the eastern checkpoints? carrying dispatches? our Isandro?* Her grandfather had hunted for Isandro the way he'd once hunted a mislabeled font through forty drawers—patiently, systematically, with total confidence that the thing sought was merely misplaced, not lost.

And every few pages, the refrain, the sentence he'd rewritten across years like a compositor chasing a line that wouldn't justify:

*I was wrong to let you believe it. Come home. The case misses your hands. So do I, worse.*

The last page was recent. The paper matched the stack she'd been setting from. The hand shook only a little.

*The new trouble is Corvel, and I am out of cleverness. If these pages reach you, Isandro—if Odile finds you, if anyone finds you—know that the disownment was the greatest act of love this clumsy household ever committed, and the greatest lie, and I have repented of it daily for fourteen years while continuing it weekly, which I believe is the definition of a parent.*

*Come home. Whatever happens with the presses, come home. We will find the words. We were always better with type than with talk, but we can learn.*

Odile folded the pages along their original creases, precisely, the way you handle another person's proof. Then she sat in the lamplight of her family's shop, in a city that was burning street by street, holding fourteen years of misdelivered mail.

A courier, she thought. All this time. He had become a courier—someone who carried what others could not deliver themselves—and none of them had ever seen the joke, or maybe all of them had, and Papi had seen it best of all, and that was why the old man watched Isandro across the workshop these past days with an expression Odile had mistaken for suspicion of a stranger's hands near his type.

It had never been suspicion. It had been a man reading his own unsent letter in the flesh.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a faint gray smear, appropriate somehow. Then she went back to the case, because the pages had to be set, all of them now—Corvel's theft and Papi's confession alike, the whole truth in one edition, the shop's final testament. Her fingers found their rhythm. Metal whispered into stick.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stair—the soft, deliberate tread of someone trained to make no noise and not quite succeeding—and Odile looked up to find Isandro in the doorway, road-dusted, checkpoint-weary, asking with their whole guarded face whether the run still lived.

"It lives," Odile said. Her voice cracked on the second word. She took the folded pages from the proof table and held them out, and found she could not manage anything clever or gentle or prepared, so she simply said:

"Papi wrote you a letter. Fourteen years ago. He never stopped writing it."

Isandro looked at the pages the way people look at a door they swore never to open again.

Then they crossed the room and took them.
