## 15. Betrayal at the Door

### Odile

The apprentice's name was Perrin, and Odile had taught him to tie his own apron strings when he was nine years old, because his fingers were all thumbs and he cried about it once, quietly, in the corner behind the paper store where he thought no one could see.

She remembered this on the morning the soldiers came, and she would remember it for a long time after, in the way you remember a splinter: not the pain of it going in, but the knowledge that it is there, under the skin, working its way somewhere worse.

But that was later. That morning, before any of it, there was only work.

"Your grandfather wants more spacing on the third column," Perrin said, setting a fresh galley beside her. "He says the page looks like a man standing too close to a wall."

"He always says that."

"He has said it every day for six days. I'm beginning to think it's less about typography and more about having something to say to me that isn't *faster, boy*."

Odile laughed despite herself. The workshop at dawn had become a strange kind of church—everyone whispering, everyone moving with purpose, ink on every elbow and chin. Grandfather Vasseur had taken to wearing two pairs of spectacles on a string around his neck, swapping between them depending on whether he was reading or scolding, which meant he was never caught without the correct instrument for the job.

The forme for the crucial pages lay locked in the press bed, covered with a sheet against dust. Six hundred copies' worth of type, Corvel's name set in letters tall as accusations. They had perhaps two days left. Maybe less. Nobody said *less* out loud, but everybody thought it, and the thinking of it made the whole room move a little quicker, like a family tidying a house before an unwelcome guest arrives.

"Perrin," she said, "run down to Brannoc's man and see if the second lot of paper's come. Don't take the alley by the tannery—the militia's been stopping people there to ask questions they have no right to ask."

"What sort of questions?"

"The sort where the question isn't really the point."

He nodded, pulled his cap low, and went out into the gray light. Odile watched him from the doorway until he turned the corner, a small figure with a small errand, and felt the particular tenderness one feels toward someone young in a time that has stopped being kind to youth.

She did not see the way he paused at the corner. She did not see him look back at the workshop door, or the way his hand went briefly to his pocket, or the coin-shaped weight of what sat inside it.

There are betrayals that announce themselves like thunder. And there are betrayals that look exactly like a boy tying his apron strings, which someone once had to teach him how to do.

* * *

### Isandro

Isandro came to the workshop an hour past noon carrying bread, a false bottom basket, and the increasingly familiar sensation of being a person who had opinions about other people's safety—which was, for a courier, roughly equivalent to catching a fever.

"You're hovering again," Odile said without looking up from her stone. "You hover like a pigeon outside a bakery. It's very unprofessional. I thought couriers were supposed to be invisible."

"I am invisible," Isandro said. "I'm invisible *with bread*."

Grandfather Vasseur looked up over both pairs of spectacles. "The boy sets a good page today," he announced, apropos of nothing. "Perrin. Show them the Corvel line."

Perrin held up the galley, and Isandro had to admit it: the name stood straight as a fence post, evenly spaced, not a letter leaning. "It looks like a man standing at a proper distance from the wall," they said gravely, and Perrin went pink with pleasure, and Odile rolled her eyes at both of them, and for one moment the workshop smelled only of ink and bread and not at all of the ending of things.

That was the last easy hour any of them got.

It was Perrin himself who came running back first—not with paper, but with the wrongness arriving ahead of him like weather. He burst through the door so hard it banged the wall.

"They're coming," he gasped. "Soldiers—at the end of the street—a whole file of them—and a wagon for—" He stopped. His face did something complicated and terrible. "I didn't—I never said anything, I swear it, I only—"

"How many?" Isandro was already moving, because a body that has spent fifteen years reading danger in doorways does not wait for the full sentence.

"Twelve. Maybe more. An officer. They're checking doors."

Later, Odile would try to reconstruct those minutes and find them missing, the way a printed page misses a line when the compositor's hand slips. What remained were fragments:

Grandfather Vasseur saying, quite calmly, "The forme. Floor. Now." As though he'd been waiting his whole life to say exactly that.

Isandro wrenching open the loose boards beneath the paper store—the same boards, though no one knew it yet, that had hidden a child from a purge twenty years before—while Odile killed the lamp and threw sacking over the press.

Perrin standing frozen in the middle of the room, still trying to finish his sentence, still holding nothing, confessing to a crime nobody had yet accused him of.

And the knock. Not a knock, really. Soldiers do not knock. It was more the sound of a door being informed of its own opinion.

"In the name of the Ministry of Order." A voice through wood, flat as a ledger line.

They hid the forme in ninety seconds. It took eleven men twenty minutes to fail to find it, and Isandro counted every one of those minutes from inside the dark of the paper store, pressed between reams of rag paper that smelled of their childhood, listening to boots.

The officer was thorough in the way of men who enjoy their work. He read the warrant aloud. Sedition. Printing of false documents. Conspiracy against the lawful evacuation of the government. Words chosen by someone who had never seen a press, describing things he did not understand.

Then he saw Perrin.

Or rather, Perrin saw the soldiers and made the mistake the young make, which is to be honest at the worst possible moment. He stepped backward. Only a step. But guilt has a signature the way handwriting does, and the officer read it fluently.

"This one. He knows the machine. Take him."

"No—" Odile was moving, and Isandro caught her arm, and the strength it took to hold her was the hardest labor of their life. "He's a child, he doesn't know anything, take me—"

"The list says printers," the officer said, with the mild patience of a man quoting scripture to the deaf. "It doesn't say daughters. Count your blessings."

They took Perrin out between two soldiers, his thumbs bound with wire, his cap knocked askew. At the door he turned—not to Odile, not to Grandfather, but to Isandro, whose face he had known for three days of shared bread and shared work—and said, clear enough for the whole street:

"I didn't tell them. Whatever happens, I didn't tell them."

And then he was gone into the wagon, and the door closed, and the street was quiet in the way a mouth is quiet when it is deciding what to say next.

* * *

### Odile

They lifted the boards again only after the boots had faded, and stood looking down at the forme in the dark, six hundred copies of the truth lying face-up in the floor of the world like a body laid out.

Nobody spoke. Grandfather Vasseur took off both pairs of spectacles, hung them around his neck, and rubbed the bridge of his nose for a long time.

"It was Perrin," Odile said finally. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I sent him out. I watched him go. He stopped at the corner and I thought—he was just being careful—"

"Odile."

"—and someone bought him. Someone found a boy who cries behind the paper store and put silver in his pocket and—"

"*Odile.*" Her grandfather's voice had the old case-room authority, the tone that had squared ten thousand crooked lines. "You will not write this boy's betrayal before you know it happened. I have set type my whole life. I know the difference between a lie and a truth that hasn't been printed yet."

"But the soldiers—"

"Came because someone talked. Yes. Someone talked." He looked at her, and his eyes behind neither pair of spectacles were terribly tired. "But I watched that child's face when they took him. I have watched liars for sixty years, girl. Liars look relieved. He looked like a page torn out of a book."

Isandro, who had been silent by the window, watching the empty street with courier's eyes, said quietly: "Even if he talked, they'd have come anyway. Corvel knew we were printing. The raid wasn't a surprise to him—it was scheduled early. Someone moved up the timetable." They turned from the window. "Which means we're out of time. All of it. Every hour we thought we had is spent."

"Then we print tonight," Odile said.

"With what hands?" Her grandfather gestured at the room—at the scattered type, at the space where Perrin's apron still hung on its peg, tied in the clumsy knot she herself had taught him. "We are three. We were five."

"We are three," Odile agreed. Her voice steadied as she said it, the way a hand steadies on a stone. "And we have ink enough, and paper enough, and a press that has outlived three governments. We'll manage. We always managed. That's what this family does."

She said *family*, and meant all of it—the old man, the runaway at the window, the boy in the wagon, even the traitor if he was one. Especially the traitor, maybe, because family is not a thing you get to edit.

Grandfather Vasseur put his spectacles back on, the reading pair, and looked down into the dark at the forme.

"Help me lift her out," he said. "We'll pull the first proof by midnight."

* * *

### Isandro

While the Vasseurs raised the forme and began the sacred, furious work of setting the press, Isandro slipped out the back and stood in the yard among the ink barrels, doing what a courier does best: calculating routes.

The holding depot was the old customs house by the river gate. Isandro had carried dispatches through its yard twice; knew the rotation of the guards, the hour the officer's brandy arrived, the gap between the last patrol of evening and the first of night. Knew, too, that prisoners taken on a ministry warrant were inventoried, catalogued, and—in a city preparing to evacuate—very often simply forgotten, or worse, loaded onto barges as evidence and sunk with the evidence.

Perrin was fourteen. He cried behind the paper store. He tied terrible knots.

Isandro pressed their palms against their eyes until the dark went red, and understood, with total clarity, that the exit papers sewn into their coat lining—the one possession that had ever mattered, the guaranteed way out of the falling city, the whole reason they had taken the poisoned commission in the first place—were already spent in their mind. Already promised. They had been promised since the wagon door closed.

There was a smuggler in this city who owed the Vasseurs a favor. There was a debt with teeth in it, and Brannoc had said, in his gravel voice, *a favor later, mind, and don't come asking small.*

Isandro looked up at the workshop window, where lamplight now burned and the great press breathed its slow rhythm—clunk, sigh, clunk, sigh—like a heart that refused to be talked out of beating. Somewhere in that rhythm was the sound of Corvel's name being set into the world's memory, one copy at a time.

"All right," Isandro said to the empty yard, to the smoke-stained sky, to whatever kept the ledgers of such things. "Not small, then."

They went back inside to tell Odile they were leaving, and to ask her, in the politest possible terms, how quickly a smuggler could be woken.
