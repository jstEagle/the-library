## 16. The Favor Owed

The holding depot had been a granary once, back when the republic still imagined it would have winters worth storing against. Now its windows were bricked to slits and its doors were iron, and the sacks of grain that remained in the corner had gone soft with damp, so the whole building smelled like bread that had given up on itself.

Isandro stood across the canal from it for most of an hour, learning it.

This was the first rule of their trade, older than any seal or cipher: *before you carry anything through a door, stand outside it until the door tells you what it is.* Doors lied. Doors wore friendly faces and posted cheerful signs about processing times. But a door could not help how many boots crossed it, or whether the guards leaned left or right when they got bored, or where the light failed at dusk. A door was just a mouth, and mouths always showed you what they'd swallowed if you watched long enough.

What this door showed: two sentries at the front, relieved on no schedule Isandro could detect, which meant no schedule at all, which meant men who had stopped believing anyone would bother attacking a granary full of printers and tax clerks. A cart gate at the rear, chained but with a chain new enough to suggest the old one had rusted through. And along the canal side, a loading window set three feet above the waterline, barred — but the bars were original to the granary, installed by merchants who had feared thieves more than they had understood iron.

Merchants always overpaid for iron and underpaid for mortar. Isandro had carried enough smuggled ledgers to know.

*You are stalling,* said the voice in their head that sounded unpleasantly like their old handler, Madame Serre, may she rest badly. *You have counted every bar twice and you haven't decided anything.*

They hadn't. That was true. Because deciding meant going to Brannoc, and going to Brannoc meant spending the only coin they had left, and it wasn't even a coin anyone could see. It was a favor. It was four words spoken in a smoke-filled cellar nine days ago while Odile counted out her grandmother's silver: *a favor, later, courier.*

Brannoc had said it the way other men said *interesting weather we're having.* Isandro had nodded the way other people nod at weather. Both of them had known exactly what the words weighed, because favors were the one currency that never inflated and never forgave.

And now Perrin was behind that door.

Perrin, who had set type beside Grandfather Vasseur for thirty years and who had once, when Isandro was eleven and still allowed in the workshop, shown them how to hold a composing stick so the letters didn't spill — "like carrying soup, little ghost, like carrying soup" — and who had been taken in the raid with the forme half-hidden under his apron and had said nothing, as far as anyone knew, though everyone knew that saying nothing in a depot like that had a price, and the price was collected nightly.

Odile hadn't asked Isandro to do anything. That was the worst of it. She had stood in the ruined workshop with soot on her cheekbones and said, in the flat voice of someone rationing herself, "He wouldn't want anyone traded for him. He'd say the edition matters more." And then she had looked at Isandro with eyes that were her father's eyes and her grandfather's eyes and entirely her own, and had added, "But you're the one who knows how to get into buildings."

Which was not asking. Which was worse than asking.

* * *

Brannoc kept his office above a chandler's shop near the salt docks, which meant the whole stairwell smelled of tallow and the whole man smelled of tallow, and Isandro had long suspected this was deliberate — a smuggler who smelled memorable was a smuggler who couldn't be quietly replaced by an impostor. He was enormous in the way of furniture rather than men, all of him built low and load-bearing, with a beard that appeared to be negotiated separately from the rest of his face.

"Well," he said, before Isandro had finished climbing the last stair. "The ghost comes up my stairs. Sit. No — don't sit. Sitting means talking and talking means you're here about the favor, and I'd like another minute of pretending you came for the tallow."

"I came for the tallow," Isandro said.

"You did not."

"I'm considering a purchase."

"You are considering," Brannoc said, pouring two cups of something dark from a pot that had clearly once held tar and now legally did, "the rescue of a printer out of the granary depot, because I have ears in four districts and the fifth district owes me money. Sit down, courier. Favors go better seated."

Isandro sat. The chair complained. Everything in Brannoc's orbit complained; it was how things showed respect.

"The depot," Isandro began.

"Holds forty-one souls, give or take the ones the sergeant sells out the back for drinking money, which he does, which is useful, because it means there's already a hole in the wall and the hole has a price." Brannoc drank. Made a face suggesting the tar had won. "The price is papers. Movement papers, exit class, signed and sealed. The sergeant can't read well but he can read a seal, and your republic's seals are the last thing in this city still functioning properly. Tragic, really. The government dies and its stamps live on."

Isandro's hand went, without permission, to the inner pocket of their coat.

Brannoc watched the hand the way a cat watches a bird that thinks it's hidden. "Ah," he said softly. "There it is. The thing you've been keeping."

Everyone knew couriers kept something. It was shop talk, tavern talk, the kind of lore soldiers swapped between sieges: every courier has one thing they never spend. For some it was a bribe, for some a forged identity, for some a letter they should have delivered and didn't. Isandro's was a set of exit papers — genuine, sealed, purchased years ago with three years of dangerous work, made out to a name that had never existed and therefore could never be traced, good for passage out of the city through any checkpoint, any cordon, any collapse. The one guaranteed door. The whole architecture of their invisibility had been built under it, the way a family builds a house over a well.

"I have papers," Isandro said.

"I know you have papers. I've known for six years. You think a man in my trade doesn't notice the one courier who never sweats at checkpoints?" Brannoc set down his cup. His face, which usually performed jolliness the way a puppet performs grief, went still and serious. "Here's what happens now, and I'll say it plainly because I like you and plainness is the only inheritance I'll ever leave anyone. You give me those papers. I give them to Sergeant Vellac, who will look at them, pocket them, and open his back hole for one body between the second and third bell tonight. Your printer walks out smelling of grain and fear, and by morning Vellac will be drunk enough to forget which body it was. In exchange, I consider our account settled — the silver, the favor, all of it, closed book."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Perrin rots, the edition prints without him, and someday — maybe soon, maybe in ten years — I come collect the favor anyway, for something worse, and you'll wish you'd spent it on something kind." He shrugged, an avalanche in miniature. "I don't make the arithmetic, courier. I just keep the ledger."

Isandro looked at the cup of tar in their own hands. They thought about the papers. Not about giving them up — about what they were. A name that never existed. A door that opened onto nowhere, onto no one, onto the long gray freedom of being nobody at all. They had carried it through seven years of war the way other people carried a saint's medal. Every checkpoint they'd walked through unarmed and unafraid, they'd walked through *because* of that pocket. Take it away and what remained? A person. Visible. Weighable. Someone the city could grab.

"You're doing the thing," Brannoc said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you weigh everything. It's very impressive and it makes me tired. Courier — listen. I've moved a lot of people out of this city, and I'll tell you the ones who survive the refugee roads. It's not the ones with papers. Papers burn, papers get stolen, papers get eaten by goats, don't ask. It's the ones somebody's waiting for." He tapped the table with one finger like a schoolmaster. "You gave up being invisible the night you stood in a bucket line with your name in your mouth. The papers were for the person you used to be. Let the dead bury their dead, as your priests say, probably, before they also fled."

"That's a terrible reading of scripture."

"I'm a smuggler, not a theologian." He held out his palm. Enormous. Patient. A dock waiting for cargo.

Isandro took out the papers.

It should have been harder, they thought later. There should have been some tearing, some ceremony, the sound of a life's keel scraping bottom. Instead it was four sheets of good rag paper with a seal in green wax, and their hands — steady hands, famous-steady hands among people who would never know they existed — passed them across the table, and the world declined to end.

"There's a condition," Isandro said.

Brannoc sighed hugely. "There's always a condition. That's why I deal with couriers and not with sensible criminals."

"When Perrin comes out — he doesn't come to me, and he doesn't come to the workshop. You take him to the chandler's cellar yourself, feed him, keep him till dawn. If the soldiers come back for the rest of us tomorrow, I don't want him recaptured standing next to a press."

"And after dawn?"

"After dawn he goes wherever Odile says. That part isn't mine to arrange."

Brannoc studied them for a long moment, and then something happened to his beard that in a smaller man might have been called a smile. "You know what you just did? You planned past tonight. Couriers don't plan past tonight. Couriers plan to the next door and then evaporate." He tucked the papers into a leather wallet somewhere inside the geography of his coat. "You're becoming a person, Isandro Joubert. Disgusting. Second bell. Be at the east canal steps with a boat hook and pretend to fish. The fish are all dead, so no one will question the lack of success."

* * *

The fish were indeed all dead. The canal had been gray and sullen for days, and whatever lived in it had either fled or unionized. Isandro stood on the steps with a boat hook borrowed from a neighbor who asked no questions because the neighbor was currently using Isandro's courtyard to hide two barrels of contraband candle wax, which is the kind of economy a dying city runs on.

Second bell came. Third bell nearly did.

Then the loading window on the canal side — the granary's old window, the merchants' over-mortared window — shifted, ground, and swung outward on hinges that had waited thirty years for exactly one night of work, and a shape oozed through it headfirst, arms first, entirely undignified, and dropped the last three feet into the boat Isandro had not technically been offered but had requisitioned through the same barrel-wax arrangement.

Perrin was thinner. That was the first thing. The second thing was that his hands, even in the dark, even shaking, were ink-black to the second knuckle, and when Isandro grabbed his wrists to steady him, the old man laughed — a cracked, wheezing laugh, but a laugh.

"Little ghost," he whispered. "Still carrying soup?"

"Still carrying soup," Isandro said, and found, to their considerable embarrassment, that their voice did not cooperate fully on the second word.

They poled away from the wall, low and slow, past the sentry who was asleep sitting up with the beautiful confidence of a man whose sergeant had been paid. Perrin lay in the bilge among the fish-lessness and breathed the free air like a man tasting a vintage. After a hundred yards he spoke, barely above the water's lap.

"They asked me names. Every night, names. I gave them the names of typefaces." A pause. "Baskerville. Garamond. Caslon. Told them it was a conspiracy of foreign agents." Another pause, and the laugh again, weaker. "The fool wrote them all down. I saw him write them down. Somewhere in that building there's an official report on the Baskerville cell."

Isandro laughed too, and it came out wet, and neither of them mentioned it, because that is what family does — the found kind and the born kind alike.

At the chandler's stairs, Brannoc himself was waiting with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and he hauled Perrin out of the boat with the tenderness of a man lifting a crate marked FRAGILE, THIS SIDE UP, DO NOT READ THE CONTENTS. Before Isandro pushed off, the smuggler bent close.

"Account's closed," he rumbled. "But between us — the sergeant will find those papers on his desk tomorrow morning and realize he's been robbed, because Vellac is greedy but not stupid, just slow. When he realizes it, he'll start asking who in his depot had visitors. So whatever you're printing in that workshop of yours—" he tapped his nose, "—print it fast."

The boat slid out toward the printing quarter, where the sky to the east was beginning, very faintly, insultingly, to turn the color of dawn.

One night. Maybe less. Isandro dug the pole in and rowed as if the light were something that could be outrun, knowing, with the cold clarity of a courier reading a deadline, that it could not — that Corvel's men would come with the full morning, and everything now depended on what a press, a bleeding-handed girl, and a streetful of strangers could set in type before the sun finished rising.
