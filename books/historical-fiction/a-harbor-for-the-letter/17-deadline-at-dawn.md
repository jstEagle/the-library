## 17. Deadline at Dawn

The press was older than Odile. It was older than her father. By the smell of it — iron and linseed and something faintly animal, like a very old horse that had been well loved and never once thanked — it might have been older than the republic itself, which was not saying much anymore, since the republic had lately taken to behaving like something that wanted to die.

"Talk to it," her grandfather had always said. "A press is like a mule. It knows when you're afraid of it."

Odile talked to it. She told it about the night ahead, about Corvel's soldiers coming with the full morning, about the two hundred sheets of paper stacked by the door like the last loaves in a starving city. The press creaked. She chose to believe this was encouragement.

Her hands were already ruined. That was the thing nobody warned you about printing — everyone imagined ink on the fingers, charming, artistic, the sort of smudge you'd wipe on an apron with a laugh. Nobody mentioned that setting type for six hours turned your fingertips into something like the underside of a boat: soft, waterlogged, and permanently black in the creases. Nobody mentioned the cuts from the brass reglets, or the way a compositor's thumb eventually developed its own callus shaped exactly like the piece of type it held, so that even when your hands were clean, you were still carrying the alphabet around on your body somewhere.

She pulled the lever. The platen came down. The first sheet of the last edition came up with *THE TRUE ACCOUNT OF MINISTER CORVEL'S TREASON* printed across its face in letters so black they looked wet, and would look wet forever, because she had mixed the ink herself and had perhaps been overgenerous.

"It's beautiful," said Marta from the folding table, without looking up.

"You haven't read it."

"I don't need to. I've been listening to you read it aloud to the press for an hour."

That was fair. Odile had been reading it aloud. Her grandfather's draft had gone through four versions before he collapsed at the case, and each version had gotten shorter and angrier, the way his sermons used to when he was feeling healthy. The final text was nine hundred words long and every one of them was a nail. Somewhere in the middle was the ledger page Isandro had traced — the treasury transfers, the forged orders, the evacuation routes reserved for one man and his chosen friends while ten thousand people fought over the harbor gates — and at the bottom, where a loyal servant of the state would put his seal, Odile had set a line in smaller type, the kind of line you set when you wanted to be quoted:

*Let him deny it. We will print his denial too, and let the city judge which hand shakes.*

* * *

By midnight the workshop smelled like a tannery that had lost an argument with a bakery. Someone — Odile never learned who — had brought bread. Real bread, white bread, bread from before, the kind of bread that made people go quiet when they tore it. It appeared on the folding table next to the stack of damp sheets and vanished at roughly the speed of rumor.

The neighbors had begun arriving around eleven.

They did not knock. That was the remarkable part. Odile had spent three years learning that doors in this quarter opened only after careful knocking, coded rapping, long silences — the whole liturgy of fear. But tonight the door simply opened, again and again, and people came through it the way water comes through a cracked wall: the widow Perrin from upstairs with her three sons; the cobblers' apprentice whose name Odile only knew as "the boy who whistles"; Madame Ferrand from the dye house, still in her apron, her forearms stained blue to the elbow; two dockworkers who smelled of tar and river and stood shyly by the press until Odile handed them stacks of paper and pointed at the folding table, and then became machines of industry, folding as though their lives depended on the crease being sharp, which, in a way they could not have articulated, they did.

Nobody asked what the broadsheet said. Everybody knew. That was the other remarkable part — that in a city where knowing things out loud had become fatal, knowledge moved anyway, silently, house to house, the way heat moves through a wall.

"You'll want these squared better," said the eldest Perrin boy, who was fourteen and had the solemnity of a bishop. He showed her how he folded: one motion, thumbnail down the spine, crisp as a knife. "My mother does laundry for the ministry clerks. She says documents are folded carelessly now. Everyone important has stopped caring how things look." He said it without judgment, the way children report weather.

"Then we'll be the last careless-free household in the capital," Odile said, and he almost smiled, which from him was apparently an event.

The press ran. She learned its moods across the hours. At sheet forty it wanted the screws tightened a half-turn; at sheet ninety it sulked and printed gray until she coaxed it with more ink and, yes, a word spoken aloud, low, apologetic, the way you'd speak to a horse on ice. Marta worked the tympan, laying each sheet true. Her father — who should have been resting, who had been ordered to rest, whose orders had all the enforcement power of a request made to weather — sat at the stone sharpening the knives for tomorrow's none, keeping his hands busy, keeping his back to no one, because a man who had been betrayed by an apprentice did not turn his back on doors anymore, and everyone understood this and no one said it.

At some point Odile realized she was crying, and that she had been crying for some time, and that it had not interfered with the work even slightly. Tears fell on a sheet and she wiped them off with her sleeve before the platen came down, and the sheet printed fine, and she kept going. There was no time to feel things properly. Feeling properly was a luxury for people whose city wasn't ending. She cried the way the press printed — steadily, mechanically, without stopping the run.

* * *

Around two in the morning the whistling boy arrived with news: soldiers had been seen at the far end of the street, checking doors. Not many. A patrol, bored, early. But early was the wrong word for anything Corvel did; early was the word for a man who knew exactly what time it was.

"Half an hour of running left," her father said quietly, doing arithmetic on his fingers. "Then we pull the forme, hide it, scatter the loose type into the flour barrels again—"

"No," said Grandfather.

He had come awake on his cot near the case, and his voice, thin as it was, went through the room like a wire. He sat up. He looked terrible. He looked magnificent. He was wearing his old working coat with the burned sleeve, the one he'd sworn to be buried in, and he pointed one trembling finger at the forme locked in the chase — the whole edition, every letter, standing in its bed of wood like a sleeping army.

"That forme comes apart over my dead body," he said. "We didn't set nine hundred words to hide them under flour. If they take the shop, they take the shop. The type goes into the fire with us if it must. But it prints first, and it prints *all* the sheets, do you hear me? All of them. A partial truth is just another lie with good posture."

There was a silence. Then the widow Perrin, who was seventy-one years old and had buried a husband and two sons and had therefore earned the right to say anything she liked, picked up her stack of folded papers and said, "Well. You heard the old man. Fold faster."

They folded faster. They folded like the fate of nations depended on their thumbnails, which was not entirely a joke. The dockworkers took turns pulling the lever so Odile's arms wouldn't fail — they were strong pulls, amateur pulls, and twice the impression came down too hard and punched through the paper, and both times the whistling boy caught the spoiled sheet midair and whisked it away to be torn for packing, so smoothly that the run never broke rhythm. The workshop had become a single organism with twenty hands and no head, or rather with every person its own head, and Odile understood suddenly why her grandfather had never once, in her whole childhood, described printing as work. He called it *the trade*. As in a vocation. As in something you were inside of rather than something you did.

Ink got everywhere. It got on the widow's shawl, and she laughed — actually laughed, high and unguarded, a sound Odile had never heard from her in three years of sharing a stairwell — and said her husband would have said the shawl finally had an honest color. It got on the dockworkers, who wore it like medals. It got on the boy's mouth somehow, entirely, comprehensively, so that he looked like he'd been eating the night sky, and Marta laughed so hard she misfolded three sheets and had to start them over, and blamed him, and he whistled at her, insolently, joyfully, with his whole black mouth.

And Odile stood at the press with her bleeding thumb wrapped in a strip of her own petticoat, pulling the lever in the rhythm her grandfather had taught her when she was small enough to need a stool — *pull like you mean it, child, the paper can tell* — and she thought: *this is what belonging is.* Not a declaration. Not a name on a door. This. Twenty strangers in an ink-clouded room at three in the morning, folding treason with beautiful creases, passing bread, laughing with black mouths, because someone they barely knew needed the work done and done well, and they had come without being asked, and they would leave without thanks, and they would all be hunted together by morning and none of them cared, because for one night the shop was not a hiding place. It was a harbor. It was what the city had been pretending to be all along.

* * *

The light through the shutters went from black to charcoal to the color of a bruise deciding whether to become a wound.

Four hundred sheets. Five hundred. Her father counted aloud in a whisper, and the count became the heartbeat of the room. Six hundred. The widow Perrin's sons had fallen asleep sitting up against the wall, in a row, like little apostles, and no one woke them because there was nothing they could do that sleep wasn't also doing — namely, staying out of the way and reminding everyone what the work was *for*.

Seven hundred sheets lay folded in the grain sacks by the back window, ready for Brannoc's cart, whenever Brannoc's cart came, which was a prayer none of them voiced because prayers had gotten expensive lately.

Odile pulled the lever. Eight hundred.

It was then that the whistling boy — stationed at the door, having appointed himself to it hours ago with the gravity of a customs officer — stopped whistling.

"Boots," he said. "Lots. Coming up the street. And—" he pressed his ear to the door crack, and his face changed, and he turned around slowly, "and they're not hurrying."

Not hurrying. Odile's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, into the earth. Soldiers in a hurry were soldiers responding to a report. Soldiers not hurrying were soldiers who already knew everything and were taking their time about it, the way a man takes his time approaching a trap he has personally built.

Her father blew out the lamp nearest the door. Grandfather rose from his cot without help, which frightened Odile more than the boots did. The neighbors melted toward the back wall, toward the sacks, toward the window that had swallowed four hundred copies of the truth and was waiting, patiently, for the rest.

Eight hundred and forty sheets. She needed maybe sixty more for a full run.

The boots stopped outside the door.

And then — not knocking, not breaking, but something worse — a voice, calm, unhurried, familiar from a dozen intercepted dispatches, from a hundred whispered warnings, from every nightmare she'd had since the day her father first said *Corvel* the way you say a diagnosis:

"Mademoiselle Vasseur," said Minister Corvel, through the door, pleasantly. "I believe you have something of mine."

Odile looked at the forme. At the press. At the sixty sheets still owed.

Her hand found the lever.
