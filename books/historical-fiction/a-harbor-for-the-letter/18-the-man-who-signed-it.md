## 18. The Man Who Signed It

Corvel came to the workshop himself.

I should have expected it. Powerful men, in my experience, send clerks for everything—summons, arrests, apologies—but when they want to look a person in the eye and lie to them, they come in person. It is a courtesy of their class. They believe their faces are instruments worth playing.

He arrived an hour past dusk, in an unmarked carriage that stopped too far down the Street of Presses to be inconspicuous, with two guards who waited by the door like furniture that had learned to stand. He wore a gray coat cut close and expensive, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has never once had to hurry.

Odile saw him first from the upstairs window and came down the stairs two at a time, which is how I knew it was bad before she said a word.

"He's here," she whispered. "In person."

"Then we've stopped being paperwork," I said. "We've become an errand."

Grandfather Vasseur, who had been dozing at the stone sink where we washed the rollers, opened one eye and said, "Do not let him sit in the good chair," and went back to sleep, which was either great wisdom or great exhaustion, and with Grandfather it was always both.

* * *

Corvel stepped into the workshop the way a man steps into a church he does not believe in but respects as real estate. His eyes went over the presses—the old Albion, the hand press, the composing stones swept clean—and I watched him price everything in the room without touching any of it. That was his gift, I think. Corvel could look at a thing and see only what it would cost to take.

"You are the courier," he said. It was not quite a question. "Joubert."

"I am no one," I said, "on most days."

"And today?"

Today, my hands were black to the wrist. We had been setting type since dawn, Odile and I, and the ink gets into the creases and stays there; you can scrub with lye and pumice and it only goes deeper, until your fingerprints read like small print. I held them at my sides and let them be seen. Let him see them and decide what they meant.

"Today I'm finishing a print run," I said.

"So I am told." He removed his gloves, finger by finger, a man settling in. "You have been carrying letters for eleven years. You carried mine twice. Do you remember?"

"The second time," I said. "Winter before last. A sealed packet to Admiral Lescot's townhouse. You tipped in scrip, which was generous, since everyone knew the scrip was about to become wallpaper."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not a smile, exactly, more the place where a smile is stored. "A professional notices things. That is why I am here. The republic is dying, Joubert. In nine days—perhaps fewer now—there will be no ministries, no checkpoints, no seals worth the wax they're pressed in. There will be a new order, and men like me will be writing its first pages." He turned a slow circle, taking in the workshop again, the smell of oil and ink and lampblack. "And men like these—" a small gesture that gathered up the sleeping grandfather, the absent father, the daughter standing rigid by the stair, "—will be footnotes. Or ashes. It depends very much on cooperation."

"You came all this way," I said, "to offer us a footnote."

"I came to offer *you* passage."

And there it was. He said it plainly, because plainness was part of the performance: pardon papers, signed and sealed under whatever government rose next. A berth on an evacuation ship—not the crowded ones, he specified, not the ones where people fought over deck space like gulls over fish heads, but a proper cabin. And money, real coin, and a name in the new regime. Not a made-up name. A *title*. "You have spent your whole life being no one," Corvel said, and his voice softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet. "I am offering to make you someone. All it costs is the plates. The forme beneath your floorboards—I know it's there, don't insult us both—the plates, and the names of everyone who touched them."

Behind me, Odile made a sound like she was swallowing a shout whole. I did not turn around. Turning around would have been a confession that I needed her steady, and she was steadier than I was.

"You know about the floorboards," I said.

"I know about everything. That is the entire profession." He examined his fingernails. "The apprentice who sold you out also sold me out, if it helps. Loyalty is a currency, and he spends wherever the rate is best. I respect it, in its way."

I thought about the boy—Perrin, seventeen, chapped hands, forever burning the first pull of every run because he never believed the ink was ready. I thought about him sitting somewhere in a holding depot right now because he'd been bought cheap, cheaper than he was worth, and something in my chest went hard and quiet.

"May I ask you a question?" I said.

"You may ask."

"The letter I was commissioned to carry. Nine days ago. Naming this family as conspirators." I watched his face with the same attention he'd given the presses. "Your seal was forged. Badly. The courier who taught me would have caught it in her sleep. Did you know that? Did you choose me *because* I was careful, or despite it?"

For just a moment—one breath, maybe less—the instrument of his face slipped, and underneath it there was a man doing arithmetic. Then it settled again, smooth as fresh-set type.

"These are chaotic times," he said. "Seals get copied."

"They get copied well or badly," I said. "Yours was badly. Which means whoever wrote that order wanted it deniable. Wanted a courier who couldn't prove provenance. Wanted, if I may be so bold, exactly what happened: a letter delivered, a family seized, a workshop emptied, and no thread leading back to the hand that signed it." I took a step closer. He did not step back; powerful men never do; retreating costs them more than danger does. "Except the hand that signed it signs everything. I've traced it. Eleven years of carrying paper teaches you a man's hand the way a wife knows a snore. The slant on the *r*. The way you cross your *t*s like you're striking them through. You sign orders against people the same way you sign receipts, Minister. As if they were the same thing."

The lamp between us guttered. Somewhere outside, far off toward the river, a crowd was making a sound that was not yet a riot but was practicing.

"Careful," Corvel said quietly.

"No," I said. "That's rather the point. Nobody has been careful with you. Everybody in this city has been so busy being afraid of you that nobody has simply stood here and *looked* at you." My heart was going like a press drum, fast and even. "You manufactured a conspiracy. You wrote the plot yourself and then ordered its arrest. You are stealing the treasury of a dying country while its own soldiers hold the doors open for you, and you are doing it under cover of loyalty, which is the cheapest disguise ever invented, because no one checks a patriot's papers."

Odile had come to stand beside me. I don't know when. Her hands were inked too—blacker than mine, if such a contest can be won—and when Corvel's eyes flicked to her she held them up, palms out, like a blessing. Or an accusation. With ink-stained hands it amounts to the same thing.

"The plates," Corvel said. His voice had cooled several degrees. "This sentiment is very moving. It will also burn. Last chance, courier. Passage. Pardon. A name."

"You keep offering me a name," I said. "That's the strange part. I already have one. It took me twenty-six years to pick it out, and it isn't for sale, and it isn't yours to give." I looked at him the way you look at a finished page—checking for errors. "No."

He exhaled through his nose, slow, the sigh of a man closing a ledger. "Then you understand what happens next. Tomorrow, perhaps the day after. Soldiers. Fire, if the soldiers are lazy, which they usually are by this stage. Your grandfather dies in a chair. The girl runs, and is run down, because the gates are closed to people without papers, and hers—" he smiled thinly, "—will be revoked by morning. And the broadsheet you're killing yourselves over will be ash before a hundred people read it. History, Joubert, is written by whoever pays the printer. I have paid for every printer in this city. I checked."

"Not this one," Odile said.

It was the first thing she'd said aloud, and it landed harder than anything I'd managed, because it wasn't clever. It was just true, and true things said simply have a way of hanging in the air like the smell of ink.

Corvel looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. And I saw him make his decision—the small relaxation of the shoulders, the clerk's finality—and I understood that he had come not to bargain but to confirm the address.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I want to tell it accurately, because afterward people will ask, and because I want to remember it myself as it actually happened rather than as it will get retold.

He turned to go. He drew on his gloves again, finger by finger, unhurried even now—that terrible patience of his—and he paused at the door with his back half to me, and he said, "Eleven years invisible, and this is what visibility buys you. A burned building. Think on it tonight. My offer stands until morning."

And I crossed the room and I took his hand.

Not shook it. Took it. Pulled the glove off—I expect I surprised him; I surprise myself even now—and pressed his bare palm flat against my own ink-blackened one, and then, before he could wrench free, I gripped his wrist and dragged his hand across the front of his beautiful gray coat, a long deliberate stroke from shoulder to hem, the way you'd ink a brayer. Lampblack and linseed, the good Vasseur ink, the batch Odile's father mixed himself—it went into the wool like a signature. It would not wash out. I know ink. It does not wash out. It sets.

He stared down at himself. The smear ran diagonally across his chest, black and glossy in the lamplight, unmistakable, unexplainable, the mark of a composing stone or a press bed or a printer's hand—and his coat, that immaculate gray flag of his new regime, now declared him a man who had stood in a seditious print shop and left with its evidence on his body.

"What," he said softly, "do you imagine that accomplishes?"

"Witness," I said.

My voice didn't shake, which surprised me. "Every man who sees that coat tonight will wonder where you got it. Your clerks will wonder. Your guards will wonder—they're wondering *now*, look at them, they're trying not to. The officers at the ministry will wonder, and some of them have cousins in the printing quarter, and cousins talk. You will spend the last days of your conspiracy explaining a stain, Minister. And explanations are just another kind of printing—you of all people should know that whoever controls the story owns the man. For eleven years I carried other people's stories. This one's mine. You're wearing it."

For a moment—for one long, glittering moment—I honestly believed he might strike me. His jaw worked. His hand, bare still, curled. Behind him his guards shifted, and one of them made a small involuntary noise, and I understood suddenly that the guards had heard everything, that guards always hear everything, and that Corvel's power had just acquired its first leak.

But he was a patient man. That was always his horror. He straightened his cuffs around his ink-marked wrists, looked at me with an expression I will be turning over for the rest of my life—something between contempt and a strange, unwilling curiosity, the look of a man discovering an unfamiliar species—and he said:

"When the building burns, I'll know it was necessary."

"It was never necessary," I said. "It was always just profitable. Those aren't the same word. You count on people confusing them."

He put the glove back on over the stained hand. It struck me then—he would wear the mark hidden inside the glove, all evening, all night, feeling it against his skin. Good. Let him feel it. Evidence works best when the accused carries it on his person.

The door shut behind him. The carriage pulled away, and through the window I saw him holding his stained hand slightly away from his body, like something dead.

* * *

Odile locked the door and put her back against it and slid down it until she was sitting on the floor, laughing—a wild, breathless, slightly hysterical laughter that I recognized, because I was swallowing the identical thing in my own chest.

"You inked a minister," she said. "You grabbed a minister of the republic by the wrist and used him as a brayer."

"He offered me a title."

"You should have taken it. 'Brayer-General of the Provisional Government.'"

"We'd need a sash." I sat down next to her, on the cold boards, our shoulders touching. Upstairs, faintly, I could hear Grandfather snoring, and the press waiting under the floorboards above our heads like a heart under a ribcage. "They come tomorrow," I said. "Maybe tonight. He came to confirm the address, Odile. We have hours, not days."

Her laughter died by degrees, the way a lamp gutters. She looked up at the floor, at the place where the forme lay hidden, ten thousand pieces of lead holding one true sentence.

"Then we run the press tonight," she said. "All night. Wake Brannoc. Wake the neighbors. Wake anyone with hands." She was already getting up, already pulling her sleeves back, and there was ink to her elbows and lamplight on her face and she had never once in her life looked less like someone to be pitied. "If they're coming at dawn, then dawn finds us mid-run."

I went to the window and looked down the dark street toward the river, where the crowd-sound had grown teeth. Somewhere out there Corvel's carriage rolled through the city wearing its secret, and somewhere out there Perrin sat in a depot, and somewhere out there were strangers who would become family by morning, though none of us knew it yet.

I flexed my fingers. Black to the bone.

"Hazel," I said to myself, testing nothing, meaning everything, "fetch the ink."

And we went to wake the house.
