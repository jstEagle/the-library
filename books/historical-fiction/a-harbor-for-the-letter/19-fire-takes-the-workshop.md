## 19. Fire Takes the Workshop

### Isandro

The knock came at half past three in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a fist deciding a door had opinions.

Isandro was on the roof with the last bundle of broadsheets, passing them down through the skylight to Odile's waiting arms, when they heard it. Below, in the street, torchlight moved like spilled honey across the cobbles. Too many boots for a patrol. Boots that had stopped pretending to be anything but what they were.

"They're early," Odile said from below, her voice flat as a composing stone.

"Corvel never did believe in keeping appointments," Isandro said. "It's his one flaw as a correspondent."

The cart was already at the back window. Brannoc stood in it, enormous against the grey, holding the horse's bridle with one hand and reaching up with the other like a man harvesting impossible fruit. "Throw them," he said. "Don't fold them, don't kiss them, throw them."

So Isandro threw them. Bundle after bundle, forty copies at a time, each one still smelling of ink and lamp oil and the particular exhaustion of people who had worked through the night. The pages tumbled into the cart and Brannoc buried them under sacks of onions, which struck Isandro as exactly the right fate for the truth: hidden among ordinary things, destined for kitchens.

Downstairs, the front door groaned under the second blow.

* * *

### Odile

Odile had imagined this moment — she supposed everyone in the workshop had, these last weeks, the way you imagine your own funeral out of some grim professional courtesy — but she had imagined it with more shouting. In practice there was very little noise at all. Grandfather Vasseur stood by the press with his hand flat on its frame, the way you'd steady an old horse. Her mother was counting heads: Papa, Tante Mireille, the two apprentices who hadn't been the one to betray them, little Cosette from next door who had simply refused to go home. Seven. Eight with Odile. Nine with the courier.

"The back," her father said. "Now. Take nothing."

Her mother took the ledger anyway. Nobody argued. There are things a house keeps that are not furniture.

They went out through the drying room, single file, past the racks where the final edition hung in rows like laundry, like flags of a country that existed for exactly one night. Odile touched one page as she passed — just the corner, just enough to feel the ink — and then she was through the window and into the yard and the cold air hit her face and she realized she was laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, the sound you make when your body doesn't know yet what has happened.

Behind them, the front door gave way.

* * *

### Isandro

They made it four houses down before the smell reached them. Then the light.

Soldiers did not bother with lanterns here; they simply put fire to the place, quick and businesslike, oil first and flame after, the way Corvel signed things — no flourish, no hesitation, a signature that had clearly been practiced until it cost him nothing. From the mouth of the alley Isandro watched the workshop's windows turn orange, and thought, absurdly, of all the mornings they had spent inside that building as a child, warming their hands at the stove while Grandfather Vasseur explained why you never set type angry, because the letters remember.

The letters would be ash now. But the words wouldn't. The words were in a cart under onions, rolling toward the harbor gates in the pockets of strangers.

Beside them, Odile made a small sound. Not a cry. Something more like a stitch being pulled tight.

"They got out," Isandro said. "All of them. Every copy."

"I know."

"The press—"

"I know." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if the tears were a smudge she could correct. "I'm allowed one minute. I'm taking it."

Isandro said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't arithmetic, and arithmetic could wait. They stood in the alley with the whole Vasseur family pressed around them — her father's arm heavy across Isandro's shoulders, a weight they had not felt since they were fourteen and had believed themselves finished with weights — and watched the house burn.

It burned the way a workshop burns, which is to say magnificently and without dignity. The paper went first, a soft roar like applause. Then the ink stores, blue-green flames climbing the walls. And then, when the fire reached the press itself, there was a sound none of them were prepared for: the great iron frame, heated unevenly, gave a long metallic groan, almost musical, almost a note held — and Grandfather Vasseur, ninety-one years old and wrapped in two coats, said clearly:

"E-flat. She always was flat in the upper register."

And Odile laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked and wet, but it was a laugh, and her mother grabbed her, and her father grabbed both of them, and Tante Mireille grabbed whoever was nearest, which happened to be Isandro, and so it was that a courier who had spent twenty years touching nothing and no one stood in a burning street held by four people at once, and found that they did not, in any particular, mind.

* * *

### Odile

Grief, Odile discovered, has a sense of occasion. It waited until the roof beam fell — waited, actually, with a certain theatrical patience, until the whole family had drawn breath together — and then it arrived all at once, and she was crying into her mother's shoulder while her father held grandfather upright and pretended his own eyes were watering from smoke.

"It's only iron," her mother kept saying, into her hair. "Iron and wood. We are not iron and wood."

"We're not?" Odile managed.

"We're what comes off the press, darling. That can't be burned. Well." A pause. "It can, obviously. But it takes longer, and it's much harder to find us."

Somewhere behind them a soldier shouted — the fire brigade of the Ministry of Order had arrived, which was to say men with pikes making sure the fire stayed where it had been put. Nobody in the alley turned around. There was a specific courage in not turning around, and Odile took her full ration of it.

Grandfather Vasseur had gone quiet in the way that frightened her more than shouting would have. He was watching the fire with his head tilted, the way he watched a new apprentice pull the first proof.

"Look at the smoke," he said.

She looked. The column rose straight up into the pre-dawn dark, black against blacker, and at its top the wind caught it and bent it eastward, toward the harbor, toward the gates, toward the crowds already gathering for the evacuation.

"That's the distribution network," he said. "Best one we ever had. Free of charge."

"Papa," her mother said, "you cannot make jokes about our house."

"I'm not making jokes about the house. The house is doing quite well. Look at it. It's finally getting read."

* * *

### Isandro

Later — much later, when the fire had settled into sullen orange bones and the soldiers had grown bored and marched off to burn something less literary — Brannoc found them in the cellar of the cooper's shop three streets over, where the family sat in a circle around a single candle like the world's most melancholy dinner party.

He filled the doorway entirely. He looked at the nine of them, at the candle, at the ash in Odile's hair, and he said, "Cart's away. Onions and all. My cousin's boys take the north gate, my sister's take the river, and a woman I've never met and don't want to has the harbor stairs. By noon there'll be five hundred of those pages in five hundred hands, and by evening five thousand, because that's how paper works once you let go of it."

Nobody spoke. Brannoc shifted, uncomfortable, a man built for cargo holds confronted with feelings.

"You knew," Isandro said quietly. "When you took the silver. You knew we'd end up standing in an alley watching it burn."

Brannoc considered this. "I knew the price of printing," he said. "Same as I know the price of everything. Doesn't mean I don't think it was worth paying." He dug in his coat and produced, of all things, a wheel of hard cheese, and set it in the middle of the candlelit circle like an offering. "Also I brought this. Grief is worse hungry. I've tested it."

Tante Mireille, who had not spoken in an hour, looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes and said, "You enormous sentimental smuggler," and cut the cheese with the only knife anyone had thought to save, and that, somehow, was the moment the night turned. Not the fire, not the escape — the cheese. The family ate in silence, shoulder to shoulder in a stranger's cellar, and Isandro ate too, because Odile put a piece in their hand and closed their fingers around it and said, "You're in this now. Eat."

* * *

### Odile

Near dawn, Odile climbed the cooper's outside stair to the roof and found Isandro already there, sitting with their back against the chimney, watching the smoke still rising over the printing quarter. She sat down beside them without asking. That was new. A month ago she'd have asked. A month ago she'd have thrown them off the roof.

"Do you remember," she said, "the summer you left?"

"No," Isandro said. "I remember the winter before it. After that it goes soft."

"I used to hate you for it. For years. Fourteen years old and gone, no note, and Mama crying over your empty cot, and Grandfather saying nothing, which was worst of all." She pulled her knees up. "I practiced hating you like an instrument. Got quite good at it."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen what you did with the hatred. You became a courier. You carried other people's words for twenty years because nobody would carry yours." She shook her head. "That's not leaving, Isandro. That's just leaving *late*, and by the longest road."

Isandro was quiet a long moment. Down in the street, the city was waking to its last morning — shutters banging open, carts loading, somewhere a child singing a song about the republic that had been outdated for a year. The smoke over the quarter thinned against the brightening sky.

"When the presses started last night," Isandro said finally, "pulling the last copies — I counted them going out the window. Forty, eighty, a hundred and sixty. And I thought: every one of those is a letter I'm delivering. First ones I've ever carried that were mine." They turned to look at her, and in the grey light their face was open in a way Odile had never seen it, unguarded as a page before the type is set. "The house is gone. The press is gone. But I keep thinking about what your grandfather said about the smoke."

"He was joking."

"He was always joking. That's how you tell when he means it."

Below them, the cellar door opened and Brannoc's voice rolled up the stairwell, urgent but not panicked: "Courier! Odile! Get down here — there's a man at the cooper's front door asking after you both, and he's wearing Corvel's colors, and he's *smiling*, which in my experience is the worst thing a man in uniform can do."

Odile stood. Her legs ached; her hands were still ink-black; her home was a column of smoke on the eastern wind. She felt, beneath all of it, something that was not despair — something with edges, something awake.

"Well," she said, holding out her hand to Isandro. "Shall we go see what the minister wants?"

Isandro took her hand, and stood, and said, "After you, Vasseur."

"Not yet," Odile said, pulling them toward the stairs. "But ask me again by noon."
