## 20. Distribution

The gates opened at dawn, and the city began to pour itself out.

Isandro had seen evacuations before — the fall of Sarn-le-Haut, the retreat from the river provinces — but never anything like this. This was not an army withdrawing in good order. This was a whole capital deciding, street by street, household by household, that it would rather be a refugee than a witness. The roads north were rivers of carts and children and grandmothers carrying portraits of ancestors who had fled worse things and been wrong about it.

Into that river, Odile Vasseur dropped four hundred sheets of paper like a miller dropping dye into water.

"Start at the edges," she said. She had ash in her hair and ink to her elbows and the particular brightness in her eyes that Isandro had learned, over nine days, to read as either joy or catastrophe. "Crowds take what's handed to them. They don't take what's shouted. So we hand."

"And when someone asks who wrote it?"

Odile smiled. It was not a kind smile. "It's signed, isn't it?"

It was signed. Grandfather Vasseur's last setting of type, the letters he'd placed with hands that shook only a little: *Compiled from the ledgers, seals, and sworn testimony herein reproduced. Minister Corvel, by his own hand.* Below that, no publisher's mark, no printer's device. Just the ghost of the workshop's old colophon, burned now along with everything else — a small anchor, set crooked, because the grandfather had insisted on setting it himself even after the doctor said he should be lying down, and his hands had not been what they were.

Isandro took a bundle — fifty sheets, tied with the same twine Brannoc used on his cart harness — and walked into the current of the fleeing city.

They had done this their whole life, after all. Carried paper into crowds. The difference was only this: for twenty years, the paper they carried had belonged to other people. This was theirs. The thought sat strangely in the chest, like a coat cut for someone else that turned out, once worn, to fit perfectly.

* * *

The first sheet went to a baker's boy pushing a handcart of loaves he could not sell, because everyone leaving had stopped buying bread they couldn't carry.

"You'll want this," Isandro said.

"I want money," the boy said.

"It's worth more than money." Isandro held it out flat, the way one offers a hand to a horse. "Read the second page. There's a list of names. Check whether yours is on it."

That was Odile's instruction too, and it was a good one: *don't tell them why it matters; let them find themselves in it.* The boy scowled, tucked a loaf under his arm, unfolded the sheet one-handed, and scanned. His face did something complicated.

"This says Corvel requisitioned the granary reserves two months ago. For the garrison." He looked up. "There's *no bread* in the garrison. My uncle drives for the garrison. They've been eating—"

"Now you know where your flour went," Isandro said, and moved on, because the rules still held even now: don't linger, don't argue, let the paper do its work. Though it must be admitted that the old rules sat differently on the shoulders these days. Lighter. Or perhaps just worn differently, the way a tool wears smooth in the hand that finally uses it for its proper purpose.

By midmorning, the first bundle was gone. Isandro stood on the fountain steps in the Place aux Herbes and watched the mechanism work.

A woman read aloud to a knot of porters. A seminarian argued with a soldier about the seals reproduced in facsimile — "that's the ministry countersign, look at the flourish, I've *seen* that flourish" — until the soldier went pale and stopped arguing. Two old men who had spent forty years complaining about the republic read that Corvel planned to steal what remained of its treasury and were, briefly, united in outrage, embracing like brothers before remembering themselves and stepping apart.

And then Isandro heard it, drifting up through the noise of wheels and weeping and livestock:

"*Corvel.*"

Said the way one says the name of a disease. Said by strangers, to strangers, as explanation and accusation and warning all at once. A mother telling her child to keep close or Corvel's men would take him. A carter blaming Corvel for the road tolls. Someone had already made up a rhyme — refugees make up rhymes the way rivers make up silt, constantly and without effort — something about the minister and the money-boxes, crude and catchy, and by noon it was being sung by boys who could not have explained a single clause of the broadsheet if you paid them.

"That fast?" Odile said, arriving with empty arms and shining eyes.

"People have been waiting for a name," Isandro said. "You can't curse a rumor. You can't spit at a suspicion. Give them a name with letters after it, and—" they gestured at the square, where the rhyme was now being sung in two keys, neither correct.

"He sounds like a villain in a puppet show."

"He sounds like a villain in a puppet show because he *is* one. Puppet shows are honest that way."

Odile laughed, then pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth, because laughing still hurt, would hurt for a long time, and Isandro understood that and did not pretend otherwise. They stood together a moment, watching the crowd flow past them like water around two stones.

"Three hundred left," Odile said. "Brannoc's taking a hundred south with him, for the harbor towns. That leaves two hundred for us."

"For us," Isandro repeated. It still caught sometimes, that word, like a stair that isn't where the foot expects it.

"Well," Odile said. "Unless you'd rather work alone."

"I've tried that," Isandro said. "The hours are terrible."

* * *

They worked the gate road all afternoon.

It was courier's work, really, and Isandro fell into it with the ease of long habit — reading crowds, choosing hands, knowing which cart driver would actually read and which would use the broadsheet to wrap fish. Deliver truth to strangers: it was the job they'd always done, inverted. Instead of carrying secrets from power to power, they carried proof from the powerless to anyone who would hold it.

Some of it was simple. Sheets into open hands, a sentence of context, move on.

Some of it required more craft. There was a checkpoint at the North Gate — one of the last functioning ones, run by garrison soldiers who had not yet deserted and did not entirely understand why everyone else had. Soldiers confiscated papers. That was the trouble. You could not simply hand a man at a checkpoint a document accusing his own minister; it would vanish into a sergeant's pocket, or worse, into a report.

So Isandro did what couriers do. They adapted.

"The trick," they told Odile, crouched behind a wine merchant's cart, "is that soldiers can't confiscate what they think they're confiscating for the other side."

"I don't follow."

"You will. Watch." Isandro straightened their collar into something official-adjacent, put on the face of a person mildly annoyed by paperwork, and walked up to the checkpoint carrying ten broadsheets folded inside a leather dispatch case — the old case, scorched at one corner, smelling faintly of smoke. They saluted the corporal with exactly the amount of precision that says *I outrank you slightly and resent having to prove it.*

"Dispatches for Captain Ferrant. He's at the customs house?"

"Ferrant pulled out yesterday," the corporal said. "Left no forwarding."

"Of course he did." Isandro sighed the sigh of middle management. "Then these sit in my custody another night. Wonderful. Unless—" a pause, calculated, the glance at the corporal's tired face, "—unless there's an officer here authorized to receipt for sealed material?"

The corporal looked at the case. The case looked expensive. Somewhere behind his eyes, the ancient arithmetic of army life performed itself: *if I sign for it, it becomes my problem; if I don't, it stays his.*

"No officer here," the corporal said, with the relief of a man handed an excuse.

"Pity." Isandro turned to go, then paused, frowning, as if struck by a bureaucratic afterthought. "Actually — these are duplicates anyway. The originals went out this morning. If you've got men rotating off watch, they may as well have copies for their own files. Saves me the walk tomorrow."

And Isandro handed the corporal three broadsheets, folded, official-looking, and the corporal — because a thing that comes free inside a leather case cannot possibly be dangerous — took them, thanked them, and distributed them to his sentries as reading material for the slow hours.

Within the hour, Isandro reflected, watching from a distance as one of those sentries read the second page twice and then showed it to his neighbor with an expression like a man finding a knife in his own bed, the republic's last functioning checkpoint was distributing anti-Corvel literature to its own garrison.

"There is something deeply funny about this," Odile said, "and I'm too tired to find it."

"It's called irony. It keeps."

* * *

Not every hand was easy.

An old woman in mourning gray refused the sheet flatly. "Paper burned my son. They printed his name in one of these things during the purges, called him a traitor, and he wasn't, and the mob believed the paper because paper always looks like it knows more than you do."

Isandro stopped. This was the moment, the old self would have moved on — know no one, carry nothing personal — but the old self had burned with the workshop, or perhaps had finally come home.

"Then you know better than anyone what a printed lie costs," Isandro said. "Which means you're the one person on this road qualified to check whether this one is true. The seals are reproduced. The testimony is sworn. If it's a lie, burn it and tell everyone you did. If it isn't—"

The woman took the sheet. She read standing there in the road while carts detoured around her skirt, and she read it thoroughly, the way people read when they have lost someone to a lie and will never again skim.

"The seal at the bottom," she said finally. "The crooked little anchor. What house is that?"

"A print workshop on the Rue des Presses," Isandro said. "It doesn't exist anymore."

She folded the broadsheet with care, put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was where she presumably kept the things that mattered. "Then I'll say it existed," she told Isandro, "and I'll say so loudly," and she walked on into the crowd like a small gray ship under way.

Odile had watched the whole exchange from the cart. She didn't say anything. But that night, counting the remaining sheets by candlelight in Brannoc's barn outside the walls, she said, "The woman with the anchor. You told her the truth about us. About the workshop."

"It seemed owed."

"To a stranger."

"She wasn't a stranger by the end of it. None of them are, if you stand still long enough." Isandro smoothed a curled corner of a broadsheet. "I spent twenty years believing strangers were the safest people to deal with precisely because you owe them nothing. Turns out I had it backwards. They're the best ones precisely because you can."

Odile looked at them for a long moment, firelight on her ink-stained fingers.

"Grandfather would have liked hearing that," she said quietly. "He used to say a press was just a machine for making strangers into neighbors." She cleared her throat. "One hundred and twelve left. We finish the gate road tomorrow, then the harbor quarter before the last boats."

"Before Corvel finishes falling," Isandro said. "Or the city does. Whichever's faster."

* * *

They saw Corvel himself the next day, though he did not see them.

His carriage came down the boulevard at speed, escorted by riders, bound — by the direction of it — for the river gate and whatever ship was waiting there. Through the window, brief as a struck match, they saw the minister's face: composed, pale, correct. A man arriving punctually at his own escape.

What Isandro noticed was the crowd.

The carriage did not pass through the evacuation so much as through a gauntlet of it. No one threw stones. No one cried treason — not at first. They did something far worse. They went quiet, street by street, the way a room goes quiet, and turned, hundreds of faces, and watched him pass, and the silence had the broadsheet in it, every copy, every hand, every fireside where the pages had been read aloud. Corvel's escort rode closer to the carriage. Corvel drew the curtain.

But not before Isandro, professional observer of faces, saw the exact moment the man understood that the city already knew — that he was not escaping *with* his secret but *from* it, and that the secret now traveled faster than any ship.

"He knows," Isandro said.

"Good," said Odile.

"He'll move faster now. And desperate men at gates are dangerous men."

"Yes," Odile said, watching the carriage shrink down the boulevard. "So let's be quick." She hoisted the last bundle onto her shoulder and held one sheet out to Isandro, the way they'd handed papers to each other across a press for nine days. "Last hundred and twelve. Hand to hand?"

Isandro took it. "Hand to hand."

* * *

They finished at dusk on the harbor road, giving the final sheets to sailors and stevedores and a fishing family loading a boat by lantern-light, and when the bundle was empty Isandro stood with nothing in their hands for the first time in twenty years and found the sensation not frightening but strange, the way silence is strange to a man who has lived beside a river.

Down at the quay, Brannoc's cart waited, and beside it Odile, talking with her grandfather wrapped in two blankets, and beyond them the dark water and the boats and the road north where the refugee columns stretched out of sight.

Corvel's name was a curse in three languages on that quay already. The republic was not dead yet, but its death had acquired an author, and the author's name was known, and there was — Isandro searched for the word and found it, and found it insufficient and true — *comfort* in that. Not victory. The workshop was ash and Marot the apprentice was still recovering and the city was burning behind them. Nothing had been won. But the truth had been delivered, every copy, signed for by strangers.

Vindication without victory. It would do. It would have to.

Odile turned and saw them standing empty-handed and smiled.

"Well, courier," she said. "Your last delivery's made. The columns leave at first light." She tilted her head, and Isandro recognized the look — the same one from the stairs of a burning day, the question gathering behind her eyes like weather. "Which means tonight somebody has to decide what name goes on the travel papers. Brannoc's asking."

The harbor wind came up the road, smelling of salt and tar and, faintly, impossibly, of smoke.

"Ask me again by morning," Isandro said.
