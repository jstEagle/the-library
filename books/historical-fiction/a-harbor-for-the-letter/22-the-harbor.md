## 22. The Harbor

The gulls woke me before the bell did.

That was new. In the capital, the bells had been the first voice of every morning—the quarter-hour chimes of the Ministry towers, the alarm bells when a checkpoint burned, the great cracked bell of Saint-Aubin that rang only for funerals and had rung so often in the last winter that they'd finally silenced it out of mercy or embarrassment. Here in Sermont-les-Bains there was one bell, small and cheerful, hung above the fish market, and it rang only when the boats came in. The gulls objected to this arrangement loudly and at length, and I had learned to love them for it.

I lay a moment in the narrow bed above the shop, listening to the town assemble itself beneath my window: cart wheels on cobbles still wet with sea fog, a woman calling to someone named Perrin to *come back here this instant*, the groan of the harbor crane lifting its first catch of the day. A town that announced itself without shame. After a city where everyone had learned to whisper, it sounded like health.

Then I got up, because the type would not set itself, and because Odile had made it very clear that if she came downstairs and found the forme unfinished again, she would say something cutting about couriers and their famous discipline, and she would be right, which was worse.

* * *

The press stood where the hearth used to be.

This required explanation to visitors, of whom we had few but who always asked. The room had been a fishmonger's stall once, then a chandler's, then nothing at all for two years—a square stone room off the Rue des Filets with a good floor, a better roof, and a door wide enough to admit a disassembled press carried through it piece by piece on a handcart over three cold nights in March. We had put the press against the old hearth wall because the wall was thickest there, and because Grandfather Vasseur, when we told him about the room, had said: "A press wants to stand where the fire was. Everything else is furniture."

Grandfather did not set type anymore. His hands had never fully recovered from the night at the case, and his eyes tired quickly now, though he claimed—loudly, whenever anyone suggested rest—that his eyes were the finest in the household and that the rest of us squinted like moles. What he did instead was sit by the south window in a chair Odile had dragged from three streets away, and read proofs aloud in a voice trained by sixty years of reading other people's words, catching errors the way a heron catches fish: motionless, patient, and then all at once.

"Comma," he said this morning, before I had even finished hanging up my coat. "Fourth line."

I looked at the galley. There was no comma missing in the fourth line. There was no fourth line yet; I had not inked it.

"Fourth line of the *second* column," he said, with the mild triumph of a man who has been waiting since dawn to be right.

I checked. He was right. He was always right, and he knew precisely how much I disliked this, and he rationed it carefully, one correction per morning, like medicine.

"Thank you, Grandfather."

"Mm." He turned back to his newspaper—the *Sermont Advertiser*, which was four pages of shipping news and one page of gossip, and which he read with the critical attention he had once given seditious broadsheets. "Your sister says the paper shipment comes Thursday."

"She's not my—" I began, and stopped, because this joke had a shelf life of exactly one telling and we had both worn it smooth months ago. "Thursday," I said. "Good. We'll need it."

"We'll need more than that, if you're going to print what I think you're going to print."

I said nothing. I had learned from Odile that silence, properly deployed, is a full sentence.

Grandfather smiled into his newspaper. "Your letter," he said. "The one you keep setting and breaking and setting again. Fourth time this month, by my count."

"Fifth."

"Fifth." He nodded as though this were reasonable, which, from him, it almost was. "A man who sets the same page five times is either a perfectionist or a coward, and you're too careful with your commas to be a perfectionist."

* * *

The broadsheet was called *The Harbor*, and it was one page long, and it was not, strictly speaking, a newspaper.

It was Odile's idea originally, back in the refugee columns outside the walls, when the city was still a smudge of smoke behind us and Brannoc's cart was still carrying the last copies of the Corvel edition toward towns that didn't yet know they needed them. "People are going to arrive places," she'd said, walking beside the cart with her hands blistered from the press and her face gray with exhaustion and something underneath the exhaustion that I recognized later as purpose. "Arriving people. People with no papers, no names anyone knows, no idea which of their neighbors informed and which hid them. Someone has to write to those people. Not *about* them. *To* them."

"To them saying what?"

"I don't know yet." She'd shrugged. "You're the one who's good at carrying things. Carry that question for a while and see what it turns into."

So I had carried it. Through the border town where the Vasseurs had been stopped, searched, and then—because the Corvel broadsheet had reached even the border guards, crumpled and passed hand to hand like contraband bread—waved through with something dangerously close to respect. Through the weeks in a borrowed loft while Odile negotiated with a Sermont printer for the loan of his spare type. Through the long spring of rebuilding: the press reassembled, the license applied for under the name Vasseur & Vasseur (the clerk had asked if we were brothers; Odile had said yes with such a straight face that I nearly believed it myself), the first trial sheets pulled and found crooked and pulled again until they were true.

And somewhere in the carrying, the question had turned into an answer, the way questions do if you hold them long enough.

*The Harbor* was a single sheet, printed monthly, sold for whatever the buyer could pay—including nothing—and it contained, in no particular order and under no particular logic that any of us could defend to the licensing office: lists of names. Names of the disappeared, submitted by families, printed so that someone in another port might recognize a brother, a daughter, a debt unpaid. Names of the dead, so that the living could stop waiting. Notices of work offered and shelter available, written in plain language because half our readers had never trusted official documents enough to learn to parse them. And letters—this had been Odile's addition, and it was the part the town had taken to fastest—short letters from strangers to strangers, a hundred words or fewer, signed with whatever name the writer wished to be known by, because we had learned, all of us, that a name is not a fact but a choice, and that some people need a season or two before they can make it honestly.

We called the letters "landings." Odile invented the term and defended it against all comers. "Every letter is somebody arriving somewhere," she said. "Even if the somewhere is just being understood."

* * *

By midmorning the shop smelled the way I had decided heaven must smell, if heaven existed and had any sense: ink and oil and coffee, the particular iron-and-linseed tang of a working press. Odile was at the stone, cleaning the rollers with a rag and a stream of commentary directed at the roller itself, which she addressed as "you great useless cylinder" in the affectionate tone other people reserved for beloved horses. She worked with her sleeves rolled and her forearms black to the elbow, and when she caught me watching she raised one ink-dark hand in a wave that left a smear on the air, or would have, if air took ink.

"Don't look at me like that," she said.

"Like what?"

"Like the first week you were here. Like everything's a miracle and you're afraid to touch it in case it notices you."

"I don't do that anymore."

"You absolutely do. Less. But you do." She set the roller down and leaned against the stone, studying me with the frankness that had terrified me at fourteen and sustained me at twenty-something, and would sustain me, I suspected, for the rest of my life. "Letter's due Friday. Have you finished it?"

"It's finished."

"You've set it five times."

"It's finished *now*."

She wiped her hands on her apron, which was a gesture with no function whatsoever, the apron having passed beyond salvage sometime in April, and came around the stone to stand beside me at the case. Together we looked down at the forme: the masthead, the little woodcut of the harbor that a fisherman named Perrin—who turned out to be the son of the woman who shouted at him mornings, and who drew better than he mended nets—had carved for us from a block of pearwood. Below it, the landings, thirty-one of them this month. And at the bottom, in a space I had left deliberately blank for five drafts, the final item, unattributed, unsigned:

*A letter from the typesetter.*

Odile read it. I watched her read it, which was a mistake, because her face did the thing it always did—went completely still, completely unreadable, the workshop face, the face she'd worn hiding broadsheets from inspectors—and the waiting was unbearable, and I opened my mouth to fill the silence with some deflating remark, and she said:

"Oh, *good*."

"*Good?*"

"It's good. It's—" She pointed at the third paragraph. "This. This right here. 'I spent eleven years believing I had no name because I had done something wrong. In fact I had no name because someone loved me enough to take theirs away and give me nothing, so that nothing could be traced back to them.' Do you know how many people coming off those boats need to read exactly that sentence?"

"People coming off boats want tide tables and job listings."

"People coming off boats want to know they're not the only ones." She tapped the forme, gently, the way she touched type—as if it were sleeping. "Set it. Print it. And sign it."

"That's the part I can't—"

"You already did." She was smiling now, the slow smile that meant she'd won something hours ago and had simply been waiting for me to notice. "Read your own last line, courier."

I read it. I had written it, of course—I had written it at two in the morning three nights past, in a single unbroken draft that had felt less like composition than transcription, as though the words had been waiting somewhere in my chest for years for the case to be built and the type to be cast—but reading it in metal, upside down and backward as all set type is, gave it the strange authority of anything made permanent:

*My name is Isandro Vasseur. I chose it myself. It fits.*

"There," Odile said softly. "You see? You signed it days ago. You were just waiting for your hands to catch up with your nerve."

* * *

We pulled the first proof at noon.

There is a sound a hand press makes when everything is right—the platen meeting the forme, the bite of paper, the long exhale of the lever—that I had heard ten thousand times as a child through the floorboards of the room where I slept, before I slept anywhere else. For eleven years afterward I had not heard it at all, and had told myself I didn't miss it, the way a man tells himself he doesn't miss a limb. Now I stood with my hand on the lever and Odile at the tympan and Grandfather watching from his chair with his eyes shut—not asleep, listening, the way he listened to everything—and I pulled, and the press spoke, and the sheet came out true.

Perrin's little harbor sat crisp at the top, every rope on every mast distinct. The landings ran clean down the columns. And at the bottom, in type I had set with my own ink-blackened fingers, my name sat in the open, facing forward, spelled correctly, asking nothing.

Grandfather opened his eyes. He looked at the proof for a long time. Then he took his reading glasses off, which was his version of applause.

"Well," he said. "The boy can spell."

"The boy," Odile said, "can *print*."

"The boy," I said, "would like lunch."

* * *

We ate on the step outside, the three of us, on a bench worn smooth by generations of fishermen who had presumably also eaten lunch there and complained about the price of bait. Odile had made the bread herself, which meant it was dense enough to stop a musket ball, and we ate it with cheese and small bitter olives and the satisfaction of people who have done a morning's honest work.

Down at the water, the boats were in. Perrin was arguing cheerfully with a customer about the freshness of a mackerel that was quite clearly neither fresh nor, possibly, a mackerel. Two children chased gulls with the pure commitment of the professionally unsupervised. A woman in a gray traveling coat stood at the harbor rail reading something—a folded sheet, cheap paper, our paper—and after a while she folded it smaller and smaller and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which is where people carry things they intend to keep.

"Do you ever wonder," Odile said, around a mouthful of bread, "how many of them are out there?"

"Our run?"

"All the runs. The Corvel edition. Every landing letter somebody folded up and took away. All the paper we ever pushed out the back window of a burning building." She gestured at the harbor, the town, the road leading inland, the whole invisible web of it. "Where does it all end up?"

"In coat pockets," I said. "In drawers. Under floorboards. In other presses." I thought of the copies Brannoc's cart had carried out ahead of the soldiers; of the border guard who had waved us through with a crumpled broadsheet in his pocket; of the woman at the rail just now, holding her sheet the way you hold a letter from home. "Paper travels. That's the whole secret of it. It looks like nothing. It weighs nothing. Anyone can carry it, and everyone does, and none of them know whose hands it passes through or what it does there. It just keeps arriving."

Grandfather snorted. "Spoken like a courier."

"Spoken like a printer," Odile corrected.

"Spoken," I said, "like someone who is both, and has finally stopped pretending otherwise."

The gulls wheeled over the masts, complaining about everything, agreeing with nothing, certain of their rights. The bell over the fish market rang the hour, small and cheerful. Somewhere up the coast, other towns were waking to other mornings, and in the pockets and drawers and hearts of strangers, our paper was arriving, doing its quiet work, weighing nothing and meaning everything.

Friday, the new run would go out: thirty-one landings, the tide tables, the lists of names. And at the bottom, a letter from the typesetter, signed at last, addressed to no one and everyone—which is, I have come to believe, the correct address for the only kind of letter worth carrying a lifetime.

I went back inside, rolled up my sleeves, and set the next page.

The end.
