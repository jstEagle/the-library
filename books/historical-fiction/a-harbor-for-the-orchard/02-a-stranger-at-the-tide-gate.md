## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The tide gate smelled of herring and old rope, which was to say it smelled like home, though Kestrel had stopped calling anything home three years ago. He came through it every morning the way other men went to chapel — out of habit, out of guilt, and because skipping it felt like tempting something.

Dawn was still deciding whether to happen. Fog sat on the harbor in thick gray blankets, and the fishing boats moved through it as shapes and rumors, their lanterns smeared into halos. Kestrel walked with his collar up and his satchel of tools against his hip, rehearsing the day's work in his head: the final plate, the last coastline, the ink that would end fifteen years of apprenticeship with a single drying stroke.

He was thinking so hard about the angle of the headland at Greywater Point that he almost walked past the man leaning against the gate's stone arch.

Almost.

"You're Eriksen," the man said.

Kestrel kept walking. In Vaelmoor, the correct answer to a stranger's question was always another question or no answer at all, and preferably delivered while moving.

"Kestrel Eriksen," the man said again, louder now, and there was something in his voice — not menace exactly, but patience, the kind of patience that has already counted the exits. "Apprentice to Master Aldous Vane. Former apprentice. I'm told the distinction matters."

That stopped him. Not the words themselves — anyone could read a guild registry — but the name, said plainly, without ceremony, in a city where saying it aloud was considered bad luck. People in Vaelmoor said *the drowned master* or *poor Aldous* or nothing at all. Nobody just said *Aldous* like they were mentioning a neighbor who owed them a ladder.

Kestrel turned.

The stranger was big in the way of men who worked for a living — broad through the shoulders, heavy hands hanging loose at his sides. His face had been rearranged at some point by someone who hadn't finished the job: a scar ran from his left ear down across his jaw and vanished under his beard, pulling his mouth slightly crooked, so that even standing still he seemed about to finish a joke. His eyes, though, weren't joking. They were gray and flat and watchful as harbor water.

"I don't know you," Kestrel said.

"No." The man pushed off the wall. Up close he smelled of tar and salt and something faintly medicinal, like a ship's sickbay. "You wouldn't. Name's Brannic. I need two minutes of your morning."

"People usually ask first."

"Do they?" Brannic looked genuinely interested, as if this were a custom from a foreign country worth noting. "Huh. Two minutes, then. Please." The *please* landed strangely, like a coin flipped to a beggar who turned out to be a magistrate.

Kestrel glanced up the quay. A fishwife was setting out her stall twenty yards off, humming. A dog was investigating something dead. Normal things. Witnessable things. Still, his palms had gone damp inside his gloves, and he noticed — with the detached irritation of long practice — that his right hand had begun its small tremor, the one that visited him at inconvenient moments ever since the night the fleet burned.

"Two minutes," he said. "I have work."

"Everyone does. That's what makes mornings valuable."

Brannic reached into his coat, and Kestrel's whole body tightened before his mind caught up — but the man drew out only a letter. Paper, folded once, sealed with plain wax, unremarkable in every way except the way Kestrel's stomach dropped when he saw the handwriting visible through the fold.

He knew that hand the way you know your own reflection. The slight forward lean of the letters, as if they were hurrying somewhere. The *d* with its flat-topped bowl. The way Aldous crossed his *t*s low and quick, like a man swatting flies while thinking about bigger problems.

Kestrel had spent three years studying that handwriting. Copying it, at first, for guild forms that needed the master's mark after his death. Then, later, alone at night, tracing it just to feel less alone — which was a thing he admitted to no one, least of all himself.

"This is a joke," he said.

"No."

"He's dead."

"Yes," Brannic said. "That's the general understanding. Read it."

"I don't take letters from strangers."

"It's dated last spring."

The fog shifted behind Brannic, and somewhere out in the harbor a bell buoy clanked its dull, patient note. Kestrel heard himself say, "Then it's forged," and heard how thin his own voice sounded, how much it wanted to be believed.

Brannic didn't argue. He simply held the letter out, arm steady, and waited. He was very good at waiting. It occurred to Kestrel, distantly, that this was a man whose profession involved waiting — for tides, perhaps, or for people.

*Don't touch it,* said some sensible part of him. *Take it to the provost. Take it to anyone.*

But the sensible part of Kestrel had been losing arguments to the rest of him for fifteen years.

He took the letter.

* * *

He did not open it at the gate. He made himself walk — past the fishwife, past the dog, past the customs house where the clerks were lighting lamps against the fog — all the way to the coal shed behind the chandlery on Tanner's Row, a spot he'd used since boyhood for the reading of things he wasn't supposed to have. It was dry, dark, and smelled of pitch, and the lock stuck in a way that suggested privacy rather than security.

Inside, he struck a match and held the letter over an empty iron brazier.

He meant to burn it unread. This was not bravado; it was arithmetic. A letter in a dead man's hand, dated after his death, delivered by a scarred stranger at dawn — every part of that equation ended in trouble, and the only variable Kestrel controlled was whether he became a man who'd read such a letter or a man who'd burned one. Burning was survivable. Reading was a door, and doors swing both ways.

So: unread. Burned. Done.

The match touched the corner. The wax seal softened, curled, let go —

— and the paper opened slightly as it caught, and Kestrel's eyes, traitorous, fast-reading, trained since childhood on charts and manifests and everything else that could be skimmed in a heartbeat, took in the salutation before he could stop them.

*Kestrel. If you are burning this, good. You were always the cleverer of us. But you will read it anyway, because I taught you to, and so I'll keep it short.*

The match burned down to his fingers. He swore and dropped it, and the flame died in the brazier's cold ash, and the letter sat in his hand with one corner blackened, breathing smoke.

"Oh, damn you," Kestrel whispered — to the letter, to Brannic, to the dead man who apparently couldn't stop teaching him lessons even from wherever dead men lived. "Oh, damn you twice."

He read it.

It took eleven seconds. He knew because afterward he stood there counting, the way he counted when his hands shook, and eleven was what he arrived at. Eleven seconds, and he could recite it entire:

*Kestrel. If you are burning this, good. You were always the cleverer of us. But you will read it anyway, because I taught you to, and so I'll keep it short. Finish the atlas. Deliver it where you promised. When it's done, go to the tide gate at first fog on any morning within the month and wait. There is a debt between us older than your grief, and grief, however sincere, doesn't cancel debts. Don't look for me. Don't speak of me. And Kestrel — check the soundings at Greywater Point yourself. Trust the water, never the chart. — A.V.*

The corner smoldered on. Kestrel watched it burn with the strange double vision of shock: half of him seeing the fire, the other half already transcribing, filing, storing — every word pressed flat and perfect into memory, because that too was a lesson Aldous had taught him without ever meaning to. Charts can be seized. Papers can be stolen. But a thing carried only in the skull belongs to the skull's owner until the skull stops working.

When the last of the letter was ash, he ground it into the brazier with his boot heel, thoroughly, methodically, the way he'd grind a mistake off a copper plate. Then he sat down on an upturned crate in the dark and discovered that his legs had opinions about holding him upright.

* * *

Here is what Kestrel had done with his grief, these three years: he had built a house out of it and lived in it.

Not a comfortable house. But a sound one, with load-bearing walls. *My master drowned* was a wall. *I finished his life's work because he could not* was another. Every morning when the tremor came, he had laid his palm flat on the desk and thought: *he taught me this grip; he drowned; the least I owe him is steadiness.* Grief had given his days their architecture. People were kind to him for it. The provost gave him the master's old workroom. The harbor pilots nodded to him with respect. Even his nightmares had been, in their way, loyal — Aldous going down into green water over and over, reaching, and Kestrel waking each time with his purpose intact: *finish it, deliver it, honor him.*

And now a stranger with a rearranged face said *dated last spring* and the whole house listed sideways, walls cracking, roof beams singing.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing that made the coal shed tilt around him like a deck in weather: if Aldous had written that letter, then Aldous had not drowned. And if Aldous had not drowned, then the funeral had been theater — the closed coffin (of course it had been closed; the sea gives back what it likes), the weeping guildsmen, the provost's eulogy about a life given to the harbor. All stage set. All painted canvas. And Kestrel had stood at the front of it in his apprentice blacks, genuine tears freezing on his face in the March wind, mourning a performance.

Or — and this thought arrived with teeth — Aldous had faked his death knowing full well who would stand at that coffin. Knowing his apprentice would be watching. Had arranged his own drowning the way he arranged a chart projection: carefully, with an eye to how it would look from every angle.

Had he wept? Had Aldous, somewhere, watched Kestrel weep?

"That's vanity," Kestrel said aloud to the dark shed. His voice sounded wrong, scraped. "That's vanity, assuming he staged it for you."

But the thought wouldn't leave. It circled like a gull over a gutting table.

He thought about the letter's contents instead, because contents were manageable where implications were not. *There is a debt between us older than your grief.* What debt? Aldous had bought his indenture when he was ten — paid the orphanage in Salt Row a sum nobody ever named, taken a scrawny boy with steady hands and no family and made him a mapmaker. That wasn't a debt; that was charity, or investment, depending on your cynicism.

Unless it was neither.

*Check the soundings at Greywater Point yourself. Trust the water, never the chart.* That was pure Aldous — the man had said some version of it a hundred times, tapping the table with two fingers. But why put it in a letter? Why send a stranger to say it? Unless the soundings mattered. Unless someone had changed them.

Kestrel's blood went cold in a slow, professional way. He was a cartographer. He knew better than most what a changed sounding could do. A fathom added here, a shoal erased there, and a ship drawing nine feet of draft would find eight feet of welcome water and then, abruptly, none.

Greywater Point was on the blockade routes. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew that; the council debated little else lately.

"No," he told the dark. "No, no. He's dead. He's dead and this is a fraud and someone wants the atlas, and frauds want atlases for exactly the reasons you're imagining, so stop imagining them."

It was a good speech. It convinced him for nearly four seconds.

* * *

He washed his face at the public pump on Tanner's Row, bought a roll of yesterday's bread from a baker who called him *young master* and overcharged him for the privilege, and ate it walking, because motion helped, motion always helped. By the time he reached the guild steps his face had been reassembled into its working expression — attentive, modest, slightly tired — a mask so worn it no longer felt like wearing.

Provost Halloway met him in the corridor outside the workroom, which was unusual enough to register. Halloway was a small, tidy man with the complexion of someone who spent his life indoors counting things, and he smiled the way accountants sign documents — precisely, and with an eye to the total.

"Eriksen. Good. How goes the final plate?"

"Well, Provost. Another two days' inking and it's ready for proofing."

"Splendid. Splendid." Halloway's gaze drifted, casual as weather, to Kestrel's satchel, his cuffs, his face. "You're up early."

"Couldn't sleep. Went for a walk along the quay."

"Healthy. A clear head for the last stretch." The provost patted him on the shoulder, twice, like a man testing fruit. "The council grows impatient, you know. Big things afoot. Blockade votes, naval commissions. An atlas finished on time reflects well on us all." Another pat. "On you especially."

"I'm aware, sir."

"Of course you are." Halloway smiled his precise smile and moved off toward his office, and Kestrel stood in the corridor a moment longer than necessary, listening to his own heartbeat and wondering — with a lurch he refused to examine — whether *couldn't sleep, went for a walk* was a lie, or whether the walk itself had been the lie and sleeplessness the truth.

Three years ago, lying to the provost would have cost him a night's rest. Now it cost him nothing, and that frightened him more than the letter had. Because Aldous had taught him silence, yes — but this fluency, this smoothness, this was newer. This was his own invention. Grief had built him a house, and it turned out he'd furnished it with lies.

* * *

In the workroom, the final plate waited on the press bed, half-finished, the coast of the northern approaches rendered in fine brown lines awaiting their ink. Kestrel lit the lamps, warmed the ink, uncapped it, and dipped the pen.

His hand was shaking.

He set the pen down. Pressed his palm flat on the desk, the old ritual, the old debt. *Steadiness, master. The least I owe you.*

Except the debt had changed shape overnight, and he no longer knew whom he owed, or what, or whether the man he'd been paying all these years had ever existed anywhere except on paper.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and the bell buoy clanked on, patient, indifferent, keeping its own count.

*Finish the atlas,* the ashes had said. *Deliver it where you promised.*

Kestrel picked up the pen.

He would do exactly that — and then, tomorrow at first fog, he would come back to the tide gate and find the scarred man with the crooked smile, and he would ask him questions until one of them bled.

Because there was one thing the letter hadn't accounted for, one lesson Aldous had never needed to teach: Kestrel Eriksen had spent fifteen years learning to read what people hid in the spaces between lines. And whatever his master had become — drowned, alive, ghost, fraud — the letter had been written by a man in a hurry.

Men in a hurry make mistakes.

Kestrel began to ink the final plate, and his hand, slowly, steadied.
