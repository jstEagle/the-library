## 4. The Admiral's Daughter Reads Maps Too

Isolde Vane had been told, three times before breakfast and twice since, that the chart audit was a formality.

"You needn't trouble yourself, my lady," said the guild clerk, a damp little man named Petch whose ink-stained cuffs suggested he troubled himself about nothing so much as avoiding blame. "The charts are guild-certified. Every plate countersigned. Provost Halloway assures me they're in perfect order."

"Then my audit will be very short," Isolde said pleasantly, "and you can go back to whatever it is you do in that corner."

He did not say what he did in that corner. She suspected it was nothing, done slowly.

Her father had wanted to send Lieutenant Marrec, who was broad and loyal and could not tell a depth sounding from a recipe for plum wine. Isolde had volunteered instead, mostly because her father had said *no* — which in the Vane household was less an instruction than an opening bid — and partly because she had wanted to see the Cartographers' Guild for herself. Six days until the blockade vote. The council would approve or refuse the strangling of Karthen's supply lines based on charts produced in this building, by these hands. It seemed worth knowing what kind of hands those were.

The workroom smelled of linseed oil and cold tea and, faintly, of ambition. Long tables ran beneath tall salt-fogged windows, each one littered with pens, burnishers, plates of copper and zinc, and the particular organized chaos of people who made their living being precisely wrong in useful ways. A dozen copyists looked up when she entered, then looked down again with the practiced indifference of servants pretending not to count the silver.

She counted them anyway. Eleven men, one woman, and one empty stool at the table nearest the tide-side window, its owner's tools arranged with a tidiness that bordered on the devotional.

"Lady Vane." Provost Halloway descended on her like weather, all gray wool and gray beard and a smile she'd have paid money not to receive. "An honor. We've laid out the harbor survey, the coastal series, the channel plates — everything your father's office requested."

"Everything I requested," Isolde corrected gently. "My father's office asked for the certified copies. I asked for the working plates."

Halloway's smile stayed where it was while the rest of his face considered leaving. "The working plates are... drafts."

"Drafts are how errors get into certified copies," she said. "Or out of them. Depending on the day."

"My lady is very—"

"Auditing," she said. "Where shall I begin?"

* * *

She began, as her mother had taught her to begin everything, at the edges.

Her mother — dead nine years now, and still the sharpest voice in any room Isolde entered alone — had spent her widowhood correcting her husband's war tables with a knitting needle. *"Admirals look at the middle of the map, Isolde, because that's where the ships are. Liars work at the edges, because that's where nobody looks. Check the edges first, and check them last."*

So Isolde started with the coastline of the northern reach, the long stony shoulder of the Vaelmoor peninsula where the cliffs dropped into deep water and no ship larger than a fishing skiff had any business going. It was the least militarily interesting stretch of coast in the atlas, which was exactly why she read it twice.

The first pass found nothing. Good drafting, clean soundings, depths consistent with the surveys she'd memorized as a girl — because her father, believing daughters should be decorative rather than dangerous, had never forbidden her the chart room, only neglected to notice she lived in it.

The second pass found it.

There. Between Gullwatch Point and the old salt works: a contour line that bent the wrong way. Not obviously. Beautifully, in fact — the error was drawn with such confidence that it would pass any inspection conducted by anyone who hadn't spent eleven years redrawing this exact coast from memory during the long insomniac years after her mother died. The shoal water off the point was shown extending forty yards further seaward than it truly reached. Forty yards. Enough to make a blockade captain give the point a wide berth. Enough to leave a corridor of unwatched water wide enough for a grain barge, or a dispatch boat, or anything else that preferred not to be counted.

It was either a mistake — a copyist's fatigue, a slip of the pen — or it was a door.

Isolde set down her magnifier and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

Mistakes happened. Charts were human things; she knew that better than most, having watched her father lose two ships to a mislabeled reef and then spend a decade making everyone around him pretend the grief was anger. But mistakes were random. They scattered. This one sat precisely where it would be most useful to precisely the enemy her city was preparing to starve, and it was rendered in a hand so controlled that the line didn't waver by a hair's width across six inches of copper.

You didn't waver like that by accident. You wavered like that by practice.

"Provost," she called, not looking up. "Who drew this plate?"

Halloway drifted over, consulted the corner notation, and said, "Eriksen. Kestrel Eriksen. Our senior journeyman — well, master now, as of the atlas completion. Aldous Eriksen's apprentice, if you recall the name. Sad business, the drowning."

"I don't recall the drowning," Isolde said. "I was twelve. I recall my father coming home wet and furious and not explaining why, which I later understood was the same event."

Halloway laughed, a bit too readily. "Kestrel is the finest hand in the guild. Won't be flattered, of course — the man has all the warmth of a wet slate — but there's no steadier pen in Vaelmoor."

"No," Isolde agreed, looking at the unwavering line. "I can see that."

She did not say: *and someone with a steady pen does not make this mistake.* She did not say: *and I am going to find out whether your finest hand sold this bend of coast to Karthen, or whether something stranger is happening here.* Instead she thanked Halloway for his courtesy, requested the remaining plates be brought to her table, and settled in to do the thing she was actually good at, which was waiting.

* * *

The empty stool's owner came back at midday.

She heard him before she saw him — the particular silence of him, actually, the way the room's noise rearranged itself around a person who moved like he was trying not to be noticed and had been doing it so long he'd forgotten it wasn't natural. When she glanced up from the southern channels, Kestrel Eriksen was standing at his own table with his back half-turned to her, untying his tool roll with the economy of a man who resented every unnecessary motion including, presumably, breathing.

He was younger than she'd expected. Late twenties, maybe. Dark-haired, sea-gray eyed, with the pallor of someone who worked indoors over lamps and the coiled watchfulness of someone who'd learned indoors was not necessarily safe. He didn't greet her. He didn't look at her. He began working on a plate with a focus so complete it seemed less like concentration than camouflage.

Isolde recognized it immediately, the way you recognize a song from childhood. It was her own trick. Stand still, look busy, become furniture. Furniture heard everything.

Well. Two pieces of furniture, then, occupying the same room, each convinced of its invisibility. There was something almost funny in it, if you had her sense of humor, which admittedly her father described as "a structural problem."

She let him work for an hour. Then she carried the northern reach plate across the room and set it down on the corner of his table, directly beside his elbow.

"This contour," she said. "Off Gullwatch Point. Can you speak to it?"

His pen stopped. For a moment — a single held breath — he was utterly still in a way that no innocent man is ever still. Innocent men fidget. Innocent men say "what about it?" with a puzzled blink. Kestrel Eriksen went quiet as a closed door, and in that quiet she heard everything she needed, and also, annoyingly, nothing she could prove.

Then he turned, and his face was mild and slightly bored, and it was a very good face, and she hated how much she respected it.

"The shoal line?" he said. "What about it?"

"It extends forty yards past the true drop-off."

"Does it?"

"You know it does."

"Do I?" His tone was pleasant, faintly weary, the tone of a craftsman humoring a visitor. "Lady Vane, that coast has been surveyed four times in thirty years. Sounding leads drag, currents shift silt, and every survey disagrees with the last by more than forty yards somewhere. If you have newer data, the guild would welcome it. We correct plates constantly. It's most of what we do."

It was a perfect answer. It was *such* a perfect answer. And underneath it, in the way his eyes went to the plate and not to her — deliberately, carefully not to her, the way a man avoids looking at the one thing he cannot stop thinking about — was the second perfect answer, the silent one, the one that said: *you saw it, I know you saw it, and we both know neither of us can afford this conversation.*

Isolde had grown up among officers. Officers lied loudly, with medals. This was different. This was lying the way water lies — smooth, patient, wearing down whatever opposes it simply by continuing.

"Four surveys," she said. "And yet your line matches none of them. It matches a fifth position that doesn't appear in any record I can find."

"Then perhaps the record's incomplete."

"Perhaps." She smiled at him, and had the satisfaction of watching something flicker behind the mildness — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting another fluent speaker of a language he'd hoped was rare. "Thank you, Master Eriksen. You've been very clear."

"I haven't said anything."

"No," she said. "You've been extremely clear."

She took the plate and walked back to her table, and felt his attention follow her the whole way like a draft under a door.

* * *

She finished the audit by lamplight, because the fog came in off the harbor at four and swallowed the windows whole.

By supper she had found two more anomalies — a beacon marked on the eastern channel that had been dark since the spring storms, and a tidal notation on the southern flats that was off by ninety minutes, enough to strand or free a landing party depending on which way you used it. Three errors. All small. All deniable. All sitting, with a precision that no randomness could explain, exactly where they would serve a navy planning to slip through a blockade rather than fight it.

Three doors in one house. Someone had built them. Or — and this was the possibility she kept turning over like a coin she couldn't quite make lie flat — someone had built them for reasons that weren't treason at all. Smugglers bought maps. That was old news; her father's intelligence files mentioned guild leaks going back decades, the way other institutions' files mentioned bad wine and worse debts. A chartmaker selling a few bends of coastline to keep himself fed was corruption, not conspiracy. Corruption she could report. Conspiracy she would have to prove.

But the man himself didn't fit either shape cleanly, and that was what kept her at the table long after Petch had gone home and the lamp oil was low. Traitors were greedy or frightened. She had seen the greedy ones sweat and the frightened ones bolt. Kestrel Eriksen had done neither. He had stood at his table and answered her with the calm of a man who had already survived the worst question anyone would ever ask him, and who carried the answer somewhere behind his ribs like a splinter.

She thought: *he's not selling the coast. He's hiding something in it.*

She thought: *so am I, in a way, sitting in this guildhall night after night, telling my father I'm auditing charts when what I'm really auditing is him.* Because there was arithmetic in the blockade plan she couldn't make balance — tonnages and ration estimates that suggested the fishing quarter would eat the blockade's costs first, and her father signing off anyway, and her learning, lately, to check the edges of his maps too.

Two liars in one workroom, each fluent in the other's language. She almost laughed. Almost.

Instead she took out a pencil — pencil, never ink, a habit from her mother — and on the corner of her audit notes, in a cipher of her own devising that would read as shopping lists to anyone who seized her papers, she wrote:

*Gullwatch Pt. — 40 yds. — E. Steady hand. Watched me see it. Said nothing. I said nothing.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*Ask him nothing more yet. Let him wonder what you know.*

It was, she reflected, blowing out the lamp, a terrible way to run an investigation and a very reliable way to run a acquaintance. Her mother would have approved of one and despaired of the other, and refused to say which was which.

* * *

The fog followed her out through the guild courtyard, thick as wool, the tide gate lanterns smeared into halos. She was halfway to the water stair when she saw the two figures by the gatehouse arch — one broad-shouldered and moving wrong, favoring a leg, scarred even at this distance; the other slighter, hooded, walking fast.

They spoke briefly. Too briefly for comfort. Something passed between them — small, folded, the size of a letter.

The hooded figure walked away toward the harbor stairs with the gait she now knew perfectly: economical, resentful of motion, trying to be furniture.

Kestrel Eriksen stood alone under the arch a moment longer, looking down at whatever was in his hand. Even across the courtyard, even through the fog, Isolde could read the set of his shoulders — and it was not the stillness of a man holding a letter. It was the stillness of a man holding a wound.

She stepped back into the shadow of the buttress and let him pass, and did not follow, and noted the time, and the tide, and the scarred stranger's face as best she could through the murk.

Three errors in the charts. One letter at the tide gate. And somewhere between them, a drowned man's apprentice standing in the fog looking like grief had just come back from the dead to collect a debt.

Isolde pulled her collar up against the damp and started home through the dark, already composing, in the cipher that looked like shopping lists, the sentence she suspected she'd be adding to her ledger by morning:

*Find out who the stranger is. Find out what he delivered. Whatever Eriksen is hiding — it just grew teeth.*
