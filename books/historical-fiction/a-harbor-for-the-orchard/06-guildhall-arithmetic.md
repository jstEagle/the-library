## 6. Guildhall Arithmetic

Provost Edmun Halloway kept three ledgers on his desk, and he was proud of each of them in a different way.

The first was the guild's official ledger, bound in oxblood leather, stamped with the harbor seal, and audited twice a year by men who believed everything they read because it was expensive to believe otherwise. The second was a slim black volume locked in the third drawer of his desk, which recorded what people actually owed him — favors, silences, small debts of gratitude that ripened like fruit left in a sunny window. The third ledger lived nowhere at all. It lived in Halloway's head, and it was the only one that balanced.

He had been balancing it all morning while the rain came down on Vaelmoor like the sky had decided to settle its own accounts.

"Four," he said to the empty office. "Possibly five, if the salt factor can be made to feel guilty."

The blockade vote was six days out, and Halloway counted votes the way other men counted coins — lovingly, repeatedly, and with an eye for the ones that were clipped or false. The admiral's faction held four seats outright: Vane himself, the two harbor captains who owed their berths to him, and old Mistress Corvane, who would have voted for whatever Admiral Vane voted for even if he proposed flooding the fish market and restocking it with cats. The merchant faction held three, and hated the admiral enough to oppose him on principle, which was the most reliable principle in the city. The guild itself held two seats, and those two sat in Halloway's breast pocket like warm stones.

That made nine. Ten votes carried. He needed one more from somewhere, and there were four uncommitted men, any of whom could be bought, flattered, frightened, or — in the case of Deputy Wren, whose wife ran the largest bakery on Cordage Street — fed.

Halloway found something almost tender about Deputy Wren. The man loved his wife's almond buns with a sincerity he had never once shown toward policy. It would take nothing but a basket.

He opened the black drawer, took out the second ledger, and made a note against the coming week: *Wren — 2 doz. almond, delivered via wife's cousin (no trace).* Then, beneath it, because he was a careful man: *Salt factor — guilt, applied gently.*

There was an art to applying guilt gently. You did not threaten a man. You reminded him of things. You mentioned, in passing, that his son's apprenticeship papers had been processed so quickly, so smoothly, and how fortunate it was that no complications had arisen, since complications did arise, sometimes, for men whose patrons felt underappreciated. The salt factor would understand. The salt factor always understood. Understanding was practically the city's chief export.

The rain thickened. Down in the yard, someone shouted at a cart horse that had opinions about the weather, and Halloway allowed himself a small smile. There was a comfort in the ordinary noise of the guildhall, the same comfort a moneylender takes in the rattle of his shutters. Everything in its place. Everything owed to someone.

His eye drifted to the shelf above the hearth, where the Vaelmoor Atlas was to sit, when it was finished, in a case with a lock worth more than the clerk who'd install it.

Ah. The atlas.

Halloway poured himself a cup of wine and considered the atlas the way he considered all valuable things: not as an object, but as an occasion.

It was, taken on its own terms, a masterpiece. Three years of dead master Aldous's surveys and three years of living apprentice Kestrel Eriksen's ink, every channel depth and hazard light and tide table from the Teeth to the river mouth rendered in a hand so fine it looked less drawn than grown. When it went on display before the vote, half the council would pretend to understand it and the other half would pretend not to care, and both halves would walk away convinced that whichever way they voted, the atlas proved them right. That was the beauty of charts. They told everyone exactly what they wished to know about the water.

And the water, these days, was worth a great deal to several parties.

Halloway turned to the black ledger again. Entry after entry, neat as stitching. A certain gentleman with a Karthen accent had paid handsomely, over the years, for the privilege of copying certain guild plates — never anything marked secret, naturally, only things left casually accessible, which amounted to the same thing at a better price. A certain officer of the admiral's staff had paid rather less, because admirals' officers always assumed they were doing you the favor. And a certain smuggling concern up the coast paid in goods instead of coin, which was inconvenient but honest, in the way that wolves are honest.

Everyone bought the maps. That was the joke Halloway never told anyone: the whole city wrung its hands about traitors and blockades and enemy fleets, and meanwhile the guild sold the same coastline to whoever knocked, and called it *access*, and slept soundly. Treason required conviction. Halloway merely required payment. He liked to think the distinction mattered to somebody, somewhere, though he had never met anybody it mattered to.

A knock at the door. His clerk, Pell, a young man with the face of a startled ferret, put his head in without waiting.

"The apprentice's here, Provost. Eriksen. Says you sent for him." Pell lowered his voice, which for Pell meant speaking at a normal volume. "He's brought a box. Big one."

"Then show him in, Pell, and try to look as though apprentices bring boxes here every day. It's nearly true."

* * *

Kestrel Eriksen came in dripping and set the box on Halloway's desk with the exaggerated care of a man handling something explosive, which, in a sense, he was.

Halloway studied him while the boy fussed with the clasps. Twenty-three, perhaps twenty-four. Thin in the way of people who forgot meals when working. Ink to the second knuckle — permanent, that, Halloway had seen men scrub for a week and give up. And the eyes: gray, watchful, tired around the edges. Grief had done its work on him years ago and moved on to other projects, but it had left the place tidy, like a tenant who kept the rooms swept.

Good. Tidy grief was manageable grief. Halloway had built a career on the premise that everyone was grieving something, and that grieving people preferred orders to decisions.

"You'll want a cloth for that," he said, nodding at the boy's shoulders. "You're dripping on my floor, and my floor has opinions."

"Sorry, Provost." Eriksen produced a rag from somewhere — apprentices always had rags about their persons, the way priests had relics — and mopped at the puddle with the efficiency of long practice.

"Now then." Halloway lifted the lid of the box. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the final plate: the outer harbor, the Teeth shoals marked in Aldous's old survey lines, corrected and completed in the younger hand. Even Halloway, who could not draw a straight line with a ruling pen and a prayer, felt the small cold thrill of seeing work done perfectly. "Well. Aldous would have wept. Though I suppose he'd have called it condensation on the lens."

Something flickered across the boy's face — too quick to name, gone before it landed. Interesting, thought Halloway, and filed it where he filed all interesting things.

"He taught me that joke," Eriksen said. "Or one like it."

"He taught you everything except when to stop working, I imagine. Sit down. Wine?"

"No, thank you."

"More for me, then." Halloway poured, settled back, and let the silence run just long enough to be uncomfortable. This was another tool in the kit. Most people rushed to fill a provost's silence with confessions they hadn't intended to make. Eriksen didn't. He simply sat, hands folded, watching Halloway with the patience of a man who had spent three years alone with a burin and therefore knew what real silence sounded like.

Halloway revised his estimate upward. *Careful,* wrote the third ledger, in invisible ink. *This one counts.*

"Six days from tonight," Halloway said, "the council convenes on the blockade question. Before the vote, the atlas goes on public display in the great hall. Every council member will see it. Every captain. Half the merchants in the city will find an excuse to drift through and gawk at it, and the other half will stay away specifically to prove they can't be impressed, which is its own kind of attendance." He sipped. "You will stand beside it, answer questions, and look humble. Can you look humble?"

"I've been practicing for years, Provost."

"Wonderful. You may need it more than most." Halloway set down the cup. "Now, arrangements. The display case is being built — walnut, glazed, locked. You will handle the plate for mounting, and no one else, including Pell, who cannot be trusted with anything heavier than a sandwich. After the vote, the atlas passes formally into guild custody. Archive, restricted section. Understood?"

"Understood."

"There is one matter more." Halloway laced his fingers and adopted the expression he reserved for delivering news that was technically generous. "Given Master Aldous's service, and your own, the council wishes to recognize the completion properly. Your name will be entered beside his in the colophon. Full journeyman standing, backdated to the year of his death, with the stipend that carries."

The boy went very still.

Halloway had watched men receive good news a thousand times, and he prided himself on reading the varieties. Joy was easy — it leaked out of people like light under a door. Relief was commoner and uglier. But this — this stillness, this careful blankness, as though the gift were a weight being tested for a hook — this was rarer.

"You're hesitating," Halloway said pleasantly.

"I'm — grateful, Provost. It's only." Eriksen's thumb moved along the edge of the table, a tiny repetitive motion, a man smoothing a crease only he could see. "Journeyman standing means I'm entered on the rolls. Permanent record. My whole life in the guild's books, signed and sealed."

"That is generally the point of records, yes."

"Of course." The blankness smoothed over, replaced by something presentable. "Thank you. Truly. Master Aldous would have—" He stopped. Started again. "I'll do the guild credit, sir. In the hall, and after."

*I'll bet you will,* thought Halloway, and smiled, and did not say it.

Because here was the thing about Kestrel Eriksen that the black ledger had noted, months ago, in Halloway's small precise hand: *E. — knows more than he says; says more than he knows; useful either way.* The boy had been altered charts for smugglers since his second year — oh, don't look so surprised, boy, provosts are not decorative — small corrections, a buoy shifted fifty yards here, a sandbar softened there, nothing that would sink a friend, just enough to keep certain hulls off certain rocks. Halloway had discovered it inside a fortnight and done precisely nothing, because a man with one small crime was a man you owned, and a man you owned cost less than a man you paid.

And now the war had come along, and everyone wanted the same map, and the mapmaker's dead teacher had apparently developed a habit of writing letters from the bottom of the harbor — because yes, Halloway had heard about the stranger at the tide gate; he heard about everything; it was the entire job — and the arithmetic of the thing had begun to shimmer with possibility.

An atlas that three factions wanted was an asset. But an atlas that *one man* controlled, a man with secrets in three directions and a freshly granted stake in the guild's good graces — that was not an asset. That was an instrument.

"I'll want you here early on display night," Halloway said. "Before the doors open. There are matters of placement, lighting, security — dull things, but they want doing, and I trust no one else's eye."

"Yes, Provost."

"And Eriksen." Halloway let warmth into his voice, measured, spooned out like syrup. "Three years you gave this guild. More than three — you gave it since you were ten years old and couldn't write your own name. Whatever happens with the vote, whatever happens in this city in the next month — and things *will* happen, boy, cities in wartime are like kettles — remember that the guild remembers. Loyalty has a ledger too. Yours is in credit."

The gray eyes came up and met his, and for one unguarded instant Halloway saw something underneath the humility, something dry and level and faintly amused, as if the boy were reading *his* ledgers and finding the sums wanting.

"So does disloyalty, I suppose," Eriksen said mildly.

"Everything has a ledger," Halloway agreed cheerfully. "That's rather the whole art of government."

* * *

After the boy had gone — carrying his box, walking carefully, looking at nothing and missing nothing — Halloway stood at the window a while, watching the rain stitch the harbor to the sea.

Pell poked his head in. "Anything else, Provost? Only Mrs. Pell wants to know if you're staying for supper, and she says if you are, it's herring, and she says you'll say that's fine, and she says—"

"Tell her herring is fine. And send a basket tomorrow to Deputy Wren's household. Almond buns, from Cordage Street, on my account. Compliments of the guild."

Pell blinked. "Buns, sir?"

"Politics, Pell. One day I'll explain it to you, and you'll wish I hadn't." He waved the clerk away and returned to the black ledger, opening it to a fresh page. At the top he wrote, in his smallest hand:

*Display night — six days.*

Below it, the entries arranged themselves almost of their own accord, the way accounts did when you'd kept them long enough:

*Karthen interest — expects access. Price: unchanged.*
*Admiral's staff — expects loyalty. Price: flattery.*
*Council — expects ceremony. Price: spectacle.*
*Eriksen —*

He paused, pen hovering. The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, the cart horse was still arguing with somebody.

*Eriksen,* he wrote at last, *expects freedom. Price: TBD.*

He blotted the page, satisfied. Six days. By then he would know what the boy was truly afraid of, and what the atlas was truly worth, and to whom — and the third ledger, the one in his head, would balance as it always did.

What Halloway could not have known — what no ledger of his, however cunning, had a column for — was that across the city, in the rain, on the long breakwater where the gulls screamed their opinions into the wind, his careful new instrument was already walking away from him, and that a tall young woman in an admiral's cloak was waiting there, watching him come, with a chart of her own folded under her arm.
