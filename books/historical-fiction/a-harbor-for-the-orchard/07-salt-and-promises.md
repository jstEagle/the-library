## 7. Salt and Promises

The breakwater at Vaelmoor was called the Long Tooth by everyone who worked the harbor, because it jutted into the bay like a jaw with most of its teeth knocked out. Kestrel had been climbing it since before he could properly hold a pen. He knew every slick stone, every gap where the mortar had washed out, every place a boy could wedge himself against the wind and pretend the whole gray world was his.

He went there now because he could not go anywhere else.

The atlas was finished. Three years of ink and bone-dust and candle smoke, and it sat locked in the guild strongbox waiting for Halloway's grand unveiling in six days, and Kestrel had walked out of the workroom feeling like a man who has just buried someone only to realize the funeral is for himself.

So: the breakwater.

It was late afternoon, that hour when Vaelmoor turned gold and then copper and then gave up on warmth entirely. Gulls argued over something unspeakable near the fish market. A kelp-cutter came in low and heavy, her crew singing badly enough to scatter the birds. Ordinary sounds. Peacetime sounds, if you didn't count the warships being fitted out beyond the mole, or the fishing boats that hadn't gone out in a week because their captains were afraid of Karthen sails.

Kestrel stood at the far end of the Tooth, where Aldous had stood fifteen years ago with his coat collar up and his hands behind his back like a man addressing an audience no one else could see.

*Finish the atlas,* the dead man had said, *and you're free. That's the bargain, boy. I don't make cheap ones.*

Fifteen years old, salt spray in his face, heart hammering with the word *free*. He'd have signed anything in blood. He'd have signed it in worse.

And now the atlas was finished, and Brannic had pressed a letter into his hands written in a living hand, and *free* had turned out to be a word with a hook hidden in it.

"You're going to fall in," said a voice behind him, "and I'd rather not have to explain why I let the guild's mapmaker drown."

Kestrel did not startle — he'd been trained out of startling by a decade of Aldous's sudden appearances at his elbow — but it was close. He turned. The admiral's daughter stood three stones back, boots planted, arms crossed, watching him with the expression of someone auditing a ledger she already suspected was cooked.

"Lady Vane," he said. "I wasn't aware the harbor required supervision."

"It doesn't. That's what makes walking it so restful." She picked her way closer, which took some doing; Isolde Vane dressed for the council chamber even when she was slumming along a breakwater, and her boots were not built for wet stone. She arrived beside him with dignity intact, mostly, and stared out at the water as though it owed her money.

They stood in silence for a while. It was, unexpectedly, not unpleasant silence. There were maybe four people in Vaelmoor who could be silent without filling it, and Kestrel was beginning to suspect she was one of them.

"The coastline's wrong," she said at last.

He kept his eyes on the horizon. "Which part?"

"The eastern shelf. Where you've drawn the six-fathom line." She said it lightly, conversationally, the way one remarks on weather. "It bends too far east. As though someone wanted a deep-draught ship to believe it could come around the point safely."

The wind picked at Kestrel's collar. His mouth was dry. "Maps are corrected all the time. Soundings vary."

"Mm," said Isolde. "Soundings do vary. Deliberately drawn lines vary less often."

She knew. Of course she knew. She had known since the audit, probably, and she had said nothing then, and now here she was saying nothing again except sideways, at dusk, where no one could hear. Which meant she wanted something. Or she wanted to see what he would do. Or both — those were usually the same thing, in Kestrel's experience.

"Whoever drew it," he said carefully, "might have had reasons."

"People always have reasons." She turned to look at him directly, and her eyes were the flat gray-green of harbor water over sand — you couldn't tell how deep until you stepped in. "Smugglers pay better than guilds, I'm told. And guilds pay better than honesty."

"That's a remarkable amount of cynicism for one sentence."

"I'm told I'm economical."

Despite everything — despite Brannic, despite the letter burned but not forgotten, despite the tremor he'd spent three years hiding from Halloway — Kestrel felt the corner of his mouth move. traitorous thing. "Your father's blockade will starve the fishing quarter," he said, because if she wanted barbed truths, he could fish.

Something flickered across her face, quick as a shadow under a boat. "My father's blockade will strangle Karthen's supply line."

"My father—" he stopped. He never said *my father*. He heard the word hang there between them, absurd and true, and covered it by gesturing at the fish market. "Half this city eats because those boats go out. Blockade them long enough and you'll win your war against fishermen. Very brave."

"They're not blockading the fishing fleet. They're inspecting it."

"With naval crews who can't tell a herring net from a signal net, boarding boats at dawn, in winter." He shook his head. "You've read ledgers, my lady. Read the grain prices in the fishing quarter last time the navy got nervous. Read how many children stopped attending school because they were gutting fish instead."

"I have read them." Her voice had gone quiet. Quieter than the wind. "That's rather the problem, isn't it? I keep reading things I wish I hadn't."

For a moment they simply looked at each other, two people standing on a wall of wet stone above a drowning tide, each holding a fistful of truths they had no intention of spending.

*

She surprised him by sitting down.

Isolde Vane, daughter of Admiral Vane, terror of the council floor, arranged herself on the cold granite of the breakwater with her skirts gathered and her spine straight, as if she were taking a throne rather than a seat on rock crusted with barnacles. Kestrel hesitated, then lowered himself beside her, close enough to talk, far enough to pretend otherwise.

"Why are you here?" he asked. "Truly."

"I could ask you the same. Mapmakers don't generally haunt breakwaters at dusk unless they're meeting someone."

"Maybe I like the view."

"Maybe." She pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and studied her bare hand as if checking it for errors. "When I was twelve, my father taught me to read charts. Not to navigate — to *read*. He said a chart was a confession. Every line on it, someone had decided what you should know and what you shouldn't. He thought that was a beautiful thing." She flexed her fingers. "I was a solemn child. I believed him for years."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was right, but he thought it was beautiful because he was always the one deciding." She put the glove back on. "You draw coastlines for a living. Tell me I'm wrong about that."

Kestrel watched a gull hang motionless over the water, riding wind he couldn't see. "A chart is a confession," he repeated slowly. "That's— yes. That's exactly what it is. My master used to say a mapmaker chooses what survives contact with the truth." He laughed, short and hollow. "He made it sound noble."

"And was it? Noble?"

"He drowned three years ago," Kestrel said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it, and neither of them pressed.

The tide was coming in below them, black water climbing the stones with the patience of something that had never once lost an argument. Somewhere behind them the city lamps were being lit, one by one, little orange confessions against the dark.

"Here's a barbed truth for you," Isolde said. "Since we're trading. I marked you at the audit. Your error — whoever's error — and I've spent two weeks trying to decide whether you're a traitor, a smuggler, or merely a coward."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No. They rarely are, in my experience. My father manages all three before breakfast." She paused. "But here's what troubles me. If you were selling the channel depths to Karthen, you'd have done it cleanly. The forgery was *sloppy* — deliberately sloppy, visible to anyone who actually knew the waters. And nobody who betrays a city does it visibly. So either you're incompetent, which your other work suggests you are not, or..."

"Or?" He shouldn't ask. Every instinct Aldous had ever installed in him said don't ask, don't confirm, stay smooth.

"Or the error is a message," Isolde finished. "Left where the right person would find it. Which means somewhere in this city there's a mapmaker trying to say something he can't say aloud, and the only person fluent enough to hear it is—" she gestured vaguely at herself, "—unfortunately, me."

The wind came off the sea hard enough to make his eyes water, and he was grateful for it. Grateful for any excuse.

"You give me too much credit," he said. "And yourself too much company."

"Do I?" She tilted her head. "Then look me in the eye and tell me the eastern shelf is drawn correctly, and I'll leave you to your view and never trouble you again. One sentence. That's all it costs."

Silence. The tide climbed another stone. In the fish market a woman was shouting cheerfully about mackerel prices as though the world weren't tilting toward war, and Kestrel loved her a little for it.

"I can't say that," he said finally.

Isolde exhaled — not triumph; relief, almost, the sound of someone setting down a weight she'd been carrying alone. "Thank you," she said, and sounded furious about it.

"For what?"

"For not insulting us both. Everyone else in this city lies to me beautifully. My father, the provost, the councilmen, half the officers I grew up with. Do you know how lonely it is, being lied to *well*?" She shook her head. "It was beginning to feel like a personal accomplishment."

"Grief does that," Kestrel said. "Makes liars of people. You build a whole life on top of a lie because the lie is load-bearing, and then you can't take it out without the house coming down."

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. Isolde looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her look away first, out at the darkening water.

"Someone taught you to think like that," she said. "Someone taught you the architecture of lying so well you can see the joists."

"Yes," said Kestrel. "He did."

*

They stayed until full dark, talking about nothing that mattered — the price of indigo, the scandalous singing of the kelp-cutter crews, whether gulls slept or merely waited — and everything that mattered underneath it, in the gaps. She told him, without telling him, that she feared her father more than she admired him. He told her, without telling her, that the letter he'd burned still lived behind his eyelids. Neither asked a direct question. Direct questions cost trust, and trust was currency neither of them had in reserve.

When she finally rose to go, she stood, brushed off her skirts with great precision, and said, "The unveiling. Six days. Halloway means to display the atlas publicly."

"So I'm told."

"When he does — when the charts are spread out for the council — watch who looks at the eastern shelf first." She was already moving away, careful on the wet stones, her voice floating back over the water. "There are three people in this city who'll know what it means. I'm one. I'd very much like to know who the others are."

"Lady Vane." She stopped. He almost said *be careful*, which was ridiculous, she was the dangerous one. Instead: "Why tell me this? You've just confirmed you suspect me. This is the part where you should be reporting me, not confiding."

She considered that, silhouetted against the last light, and gave him the ghost of a smile he suspected very few people ever saw.

"Because whoever bent that line," she said, "bent it *away* from harm. Deep water marked shallow keeps ships off the rocks, Master Eriksen. Traitors don't protect the coast. They sell it." She turned and picked her way back toward the city. "Figure out who else knows that. Quickly. Before someone less patient than I am figures out it was you."

Kestrel sat alone on the Long Tooth as the lamps of Vaelmoor multiplied and the tide gnawed patiently at the stones beneath him.

*Traitors don't protect the coast.* He turned it over. She was wrong — or she was right in a way he couldn't afford to examine, because examining it meant asking what Aldous had been protecting when he faked a drowning and joined the navy that burned the harbor fleet. Protection and betrayal had always shared a roof in his master's house.

And she'd given him six days. Six days until the unveiling, when the atlas would sit open under lamplight and every eye in the council would crawl across his work — including, now, hers, and whoever else she was hunting.

Six days to decide which atlas the city would see.

He got up stiffly and started the climb back, and it was only halfway down the breakwater, hand on the cold stone where a fifteen-year-old boy had once sealed his life away, that he realized he'd never once asked himself the question Isolde had left hanging:

*If the error was a message — who was it meant for?*

Because sitting there in the dark, honest for the length of one breath, Kestrel could no longer remember whether *he* had drawn that line... or whether he had simply, obediently, drawn the one Aldous had left instructions for, three years ago, in a dead man's handwriting that was apparently still warm.

* * *

Back at the guildhall, a lamp was burning in Provost Halloway's window well past any respectable hour, and a clerk Kestrel had never seen before was leaving through the side door with a satchel held the way men carry money.

Halloway's summons was waiting on the workroom table, the wax seal unbroken, the ink still smelling fresh:

*Master Eriksen — attend me tomorrow, first bell. We must discuss the unveiling. And your... arrangements.*

The last two words were underlined twice.

Kestrel read it four times by candlelight, and each time, the underline seemed to get longer.
