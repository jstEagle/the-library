## 8. The Copyist's Sin

There is a particular hour in Vaelmoor when the tide goes out far enough to expose the mudflats, and the whole harbor smells like something ancient and mildly embarrassed about itself. The guild workroom sits close enough to the water that this hour seeps through the shutters along with the cold, and it was in that smell — brine and rot and old money — that I sat down on the third night of my life as a forger, unrolled fresh paper, and prepared to commit treason twice.

Once for each atlas.

I had arranged them side by side on the long table like twins in a crib: the true Vaelmoor Atlas on the left, three years of Master Aldous's unfinished plates and my own trembling completions; and on the right, nothing at all yet, just clean rag paper and a set of goose quills trimmed to Aldous's exact preference. He'd had opinions about quills. He'd had opinions about everything — the grind of the ink, the slant of a coastline, the precise weight of silence a boy of ten ought to maintain while grown men were dragged out of the guildhall. I had absorbed all of it, the way blotting paper absorbs ink, without ever once being asked whether I wanted to be soaked through.

"Right," I told the empty room, because talking to oneself is either madness or craft, and craft pays better. "Let's lie beautifully."

The first thing any honest forger learns — and yes, I am aware that sentence eats its own tail — is that you cannot copy a hand. You can only *become* it, briefly, the way an actor becomes a king for the length of a play. And so I began not with lines but with memory. I closed my eyes and put myself back in the workroom of fifteen years ago: Aldous standing behind me, his breath smelling of clove oil and cheap wine, one finger tapping the back of my hand.

*You draw the coast the way you'd walk it, boy. Confident. A hesitant line tells every reader you're guessing.*

*I'm always guessing.*

*Then guess like a man who isn't.*

I opened my eyes and drew the first stroke of the false coastline.

It came out wrong. Too careful. A hesitating line. I scraped it back with a knife, wiped the ghost of it with bread crumb, and started again, and this time I did the thing I hated doing, which was to stop thinking of it as a forgery. A forgery is nervous work. Nervous hands make nervous maps. Instead I told myself: *this is simply the next plate. You have drawn this coast four hundred times. Draw it a four hundred and first.*

My hands, traitors that they are, believed me instantly.

That was the horror of it, if I'm honest — and the chapter demands honesty, since honesty is otherwise in short supply around here. My hands remembered Aldous better than I did. They knew the little hitch he put at the end of every longitude figure, the way his *f*s crossed slightly left of center, the almost invisible thickening where his pen paused mid-coastline to think. I had spent fifteen years trying to be him well enough to please a dead man, and now that dead man turned out to be alive and extortionate, and my hands were ready to serve him up on demand.

"You're a marvel," I told them bitterly, and they continued being a marvel without my permission.

* * *

By midnight I had the first plate roughed in: the outer harbor, the Teeth marked exactly as the true charts showed them — which is to say, correctly, because a forgery must be correct everywhere except where it matters, and where it matters is a secret between me and the shoals.

Here I should explain what I was actually doing, because even I only understood it fully around the second pot of ink. I was making two books that would tell two stories. The true atlas would stay hidden — in the coffin, as of tomorrow's arrangements, God forgive me and old Tomas Wren, who deserved a coffin unmolested by espionage — and would eventually go wherever truth could do the least damage and the most good, destination still under furious negotiation between my conscience and my terror. The false atlas would go to the guild display, and from there, inevitably, into whatever hands Halloway sold it to, which meant Karthen hands, which meant Aldous's hands.

So the false one had to be perfect enough to pass Isolde Vane's eye — and she had already caught me once, that quiet woman with her quiet pencil, marking my deliberate error like a cat leaving a dead bird on a doorstep — and wrong enough to kill a fleet.

Perfect and wrong. Those were my specifications. Any apprentice could manage one or the other. It takes a master to do both at once, and I was, after all, trained by a master who had faked his own drowning, so the pedigree was impeccable.

The trouble was the tide tables. A coastline can be forged by feel, but numbers betray nerves. I wrote out the depth soundings for the channel approach three times before I got them steady. First attempt, my figures drifted shallow by a foot — too obvious, a whole fleet's worth of obvious. Second attempt, I overcorrected and made them too deep, which would look like flattery. Third attempt, I copied them straight off the true chart with my eyes half closed, letting the hand do its married-to-the-work trick, and only then went back and altered the four critical soundings by increments small enough to seem like weather, not intention.

A sandbar that has shifted two feet since last survey. A channel that narrows where everyone remembers it widening. Nothing a bribed inspector would notice. Everything a navigator steering by moonlight and confidence would discover at the worst possible moment.

"There," I whispered. "Half the truth."

And then I sat very still, because I had heard myself say it aloud, and it sounded exactly like something Aldous would say, and approvingly.

* * *

Somewhere past two in the morning, my body staged its inevitable revolt. The tremor — the one I'd spent years hiding from the provost, the one that lived in my right hand like a tenant who paid no rent — came knocking. I watched the pen shiver against the paper and leave a tiny stutter in the outline of Gull Rock, a flaw no forger could afford.

I got up and walked the length of the workroom. Past the drying racks hung with other men's honest charts. Past the cabinet of master plates, each one labeled in Aldous's hand, each one a small monument to a man who had taught me everything including, apparently, how to destroy things carefully.

On the wall above his old desk hung the one portrait the guild owned of him: a stiff, official thing painted when he received his laurel, showing a lean man with grey in his beard and an expression of mild, patient disappointment, as though the painter himself had shown insufficient rigor in rendering the horizon. I had looked at that face ten thousand times with love. Tonight I looked at it the way you look at a locked door you've finally realized was never locked — you were just told, at an age too young to argue, that it was.

"All right," I said to the portrait. "You bought my indenture when I was ten. You taught me silence, then craft, then survival. You drowned convincingly enough to make me weep at your empty coffin." I refilled my ink pot. "But you never taught me this part. So I suppose I'm teaching myself."

The portrait declined to respond. In my experience this was typical of masters.

I went back to the table and did the thing that saved me that night, the thing I suspect saves every craftsman who finds himself working at the edge of the gallows: I stopped thinking about why I was doing it and thought only about *how*. Terror, it turns out, transposes remarkably well into technique. Every fear became a check. Fear of discovery became: *vary the line weight the way fatigue varies it, because a flawless chart is more suspicious than a tired one.* Fear of the gallows became: *keep the two atlases physically separated, wrap the true plates in oilcloth, never let both lie open in lamplight.* Fear of becoming him became — well. That fear didn't become anything. That fear just sat in the corner like a wet dog, watching me work, and I let it, because a man should have to look at what he's turning into while he turns into it.

* * *

Dawn found me with six plates done and a crick in my neck shaped like the letter S, which felt thematically appropriate.

I allowed myself the luxury of comparing the two atlases side by side, one finished section each, laid under the lamp. To any eye in Vaelmoor they were identical. Sisters. Twins. The same confident coastlines, the same patient hatching of shoal water, the same elegant Aldous flourish in the title cartouche — and there, in the lower margin of each, the same small compass rose I had been drawing since I was twelve, the one with the slightly crooked east point because I'd learned to draw it left-handed during the purge year when Aldous made me practice with my off-hand so the guild inspectors wouldn't recognize a child's work in the margins.

Even my mistakes had provenance. That was the joke I laughed at alone in the cold workroom, quietly, the way you laugh at funerals.

The difference lived in four soundings and one sandbar and a channel width, and in the fact that one book was a weapon and one was — what? An heirloom? A hostage? I wrapped the true plates in oilcloth and stowed them in the floor cavity beneath the third flagstone, the loose one, the one every guild apprentice knows about and no provost ever has, because provosts are promoted from the ranks of men who never were apprentices.

Then I banked the lamp, and that was when I heard it: footsteps in the corridor outside. Unhurried. Familiar in their unhurriedness.

Brannic walked in without knocking, which I was learning was less rudeness than policy. He stood dripping sea-smell onto my clean floor, looked at the false atlas, at the lamp, at my face, and said nothing for a long moment.

"Well?" I said.

"Six plates in three nights." He touched the edge of the topmost sheet with one scarred finger, respectful, the way sailors touch wood. "He'll want to see this."

"He'll see it when it's done, and not before, and not you either."

"He sent word." Brannic produced a folded scrap of paper from inside his coat, and my stomach performed a maneuver the guild would charge extra to chart. "The display. Halloway moved it. Not six days."

I took the paper. Three words in Aldous's hand — *of course* his hand, whose else's, the man wrote to me more faithfully dead than most fathers write alive:

***Five days now.***

I looked at the six finished plates. I looked at the forty-odd remaining. I did the arithmetic every apprentice learns young and hates forever.

"Tell him," I said, dipping a fresh quill, "he'll have it early."

Brannic grunted — approval, possibly, or possibly indigestion; with Brannic the two shared a register — and left the way he'd come, trailing the tide-flat smell of a man who spends too much time on ships that shouldn't be in this harbor.

I sat back down. I uncapped the ink. And somewhere below the floorboards, under a loose stone, wrapped in oilcloth, the truth waited in the dark for me to find out what it was worth.

Four days and counting. Forty plates to forge. One gallows, reserved.

"Guess like a man who isn't," I told my hands, and set the first line of the seventh plate down steady as bedrock.
