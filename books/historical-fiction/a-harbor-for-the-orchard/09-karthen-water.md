## 9. Karthen Water

Brannic had crossed the Karthen Strait eleven times in his life, and every crossing smelled the same: tar, salt, and the particular sourness of men who believed the sea owed them something. The twelfth crossing was no different, except that this time he carried a letter worth more than his ship, his crew, and his own hide stitched together and sold at market.

The frigate *Vigilant Oath* rose out of the fog like a debt come due. She was a beautiful thing, he'd give her that — Karthen shipwrights built their warships the way other men built cathedrals, all clean lines and quiet menace. Her name was painted on her stern in gold leaf that would be the first thing to peel.

The boarding lines came down. Brannic climbed them the way he climbed everything these days: slowly, complaining inwardly, and without looking down.

A midshipman met him on deck, a boy of perhaps sixteen with cheeks like polished apples and an expression of enormous self-importance. "Name and business."

"Brannic. Business is private."

"Everyone's business is private," the boy said. "That's why we ask names."

Brannic decided, in the generous way he decided most things about children, not to throw him overboard. "Tell Master Aldous the tide gate sent me. He'll know."

He watched the word land. *Tide gate.* The boy's apple cheeks went stiff, and he turned and marched off with the urgency of someone carrying news of fire.

They kept Brannic waiting on deck for the better part of an hour, which he understood to be a courtesy — Aldous Vane never made anyone wait who mattered, and never failed to make someone wait who thought they did. Brannic used the time well. He leaned against the rail and watched the chartroom windows aft, where lamplight burned even in daylight, and he watched the officers come and go from it with the particular walk of men carrying orders they hadn't written themselves.

One of them, a lieutenant with ink-stained fingers, passed close enough that Brannic caught the smell of the chartroom itself — lamp oil, seawater, and something else beneath it. Old paper. The smell of maps that had been handled too much by too few hands.

It occurred to him, not for the first time, that he had spent thirty years as a smuggler telling himself he was a free man because no one owned his labor. And here was a man who had been owned since he was ten years old, and who now drew the drowning of cities with lamplight on his face.

*Sentimental,* he told himself. *Old men get sentimental. It's the joints.*

* * *

The cabin, when they finally let him down into it, was larger than Brannic's whole house had been before the fire, and better lit than any room in Vaelmoor. Charts covered every surface. Not just Karthen naval charts — Vaelmoor charts, harbor charts, coastal atlases, some of them so old Brannic recognized the guild seal of two provosts ago.

And at the center of it, sleeves rolled, pen moving, sat Master Aldous Eriksen.

Brannic had prepared himself for a lot of things across that strait. He had prepared himself for a broken man, for a fat man, for a mad one, for a ghost who had gone strange from guilt. He had not prepared himself for this: a man in his late fifties with silver at his temples and ink to the second knuckle, working with the calm of someone darning socks.

"You've put on weight," Aldous said, without looking up.

"You've put on treason." Brannic set the sealed packet on the chart table, deliberately atop a half-finished survey of the Teeth approach. "Twice over, by the look of this room."

Aldous finished his line. That was the thing about him that fifteen years had not changed and never would — he finished his line. Then he blotted it, set down the pen, and looked up with eyes the color of harbor water, and smiled.

It was a warm smile. That was the worst part. It had always been a warm smile. Brannic had watched that smile talk a harbormaster out of seizing three contraband casks of wine, and had watched the same harbormaster drink the wine with him that night, laughing. Some men lied with their mouths. Aldous lied with his whole face, generously, and made you glad of it.

"Brannic Salt," Aldous said. "The tide gate's finest. Sit. There's wine — real wine, not whatever you smuggle now that your conscience has gone soft."

"My conscience went soft the day I started charging honest prices." Brannic didn't sit. "You're supposed to be dead."

"I'm told the funeral was lovely."

"It rained."

"They always do, in Vaelmoor." Aldous poured two cups anyway, unbothered. "Sit down, old friend. You didn't row four miles through fog to stand at parade rest."

That was true, which annoyed Brannic. He sat. He took the wine. He drank it and hated how good it was.

"The guild's terms," he said, and slid the packet forward again.

Aldous broke the seal with a thumbnail and read. Brannic watched him read, and what he watched for — what he had been paid to watch for, though he'd have done it for nothing, or rather he'd have done it because watching Aldous was a habit older than most of his debts — was surprise.

There wasn't any. The silver eyebrows rose slightly at one clause, the one about the atlas being delivered publicly in six days, under council seal. But that was arithmetic, not astonishment. Aldous read the terms of his own extortion the way a merchant reads a bill of lading: confirming quantities.

"They want me to let the boy finish it," Aldous said. "Deliver it into the archive. Publicly."

"That's the shape of it. Provost Halloway's terms. The atlas goes through the guild, gets copied into the council charts, and then —" Brannic turned his cup. "Then a copy finds its way to you. Halloway's price is named in the second page. You'll find it reasonable, knowing him."

"He always was a man who priced both doors of a burning house." Aldous set the letter down and pressed it flat with one palm, gently, the way he used to press wrinkles out of fresh vellum while ten-year-old Kestrel watched from the corner and memorized everything. "And Kestrel? What does the boy say?"

Brannic looked at him.

"What does he say," Aldous repeated, "about delivering my atlas."

"He says nothing. He burns your letters unread and then stands at the tide gate at dawn reciting them back to himself, which I know because I watch him do it." Brannic set the cup down harder than necessary. "You wrote him a letter dated last spring, Aldous. Last spring. Three years after you drowned. What exactly were you doing last spring — checking that your grief had cured properly?"

"I was checking that he'd survive me," Aldous said. "Which required confirming he'd learned to finish things. He has. I'm told the final plate is done."

"Told by whom?"

"By the same sources who tell me what the council eats for breakfast." Aldous smiled again, and this time there was nothing warm in it at all, only the smoothness underneath, like the sea over the shoals. "You think I faked a death and joined an enemy navy on impulse, Brannic. You think grief drove me to it. No. Grief is a tool like anything else. I have been building toward this harbor for nine years."

"And what is *this*?" Brannic gestured at the charts, at the frigate, at the whole gray machinery of it. "What are you building?"

"A navigation." Aldous stood, and Brannic saw that the chart table's center — the sheet he'd dropped his packet on — was a master plan of the Vaelmoor approaches, drawn in Aldous's own hand, marked with depths and currents in a notation so precise it could only have come from memory. Fifteen years of memory. The man had left the harbor and taken its bottom with him.

"Karthen means to blockade," Aldous said. "The council will vote within the fortnight — Halloway is managing the votes, badly, but managing them. A blockade starves a city slowly, and slow starvation makes bad politics. So the Admiralty asked me a simple question: can we take the harbor quickly instead?"

"And you said?"

"I said yes." Aldous laid one finger on the chart, on the deep channel past the Teeth, and traced it home to the inner roads. "Because I know the way in. Because I taught the water to a boy who has now drawn it perfectly, plate by plate, for three years, believing he was honoring a dead man." He looked up. "The atlas isn't a ransom, Brannic. It's confirmation. Every depth Kestrel inks tells me my memory hasn't rotted. When the fleet comes, it will come on his coastline."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere above, boots crossed the deck in a rhythm Brannic's body knew well enough to count: marines changing the watch.

"You're going to burn his city," Brannic said, "with his own map."

"I'm going to *sail* his map," Aldous corrected mildly, "which is what maps are for. Sentiment is for funerals, and I've had mine." He refilled Brannic's cup without asking. "You look at me like I've become something. I haven't become anything. I've simply stopped pretending, which is the only honest act available to a man my age. You should try it. You've spent thirty years smuggling and calling it trade."

"And what do you call letting a city starve?"

"Necessary." Aldous said it the way he'd once said *true north* — flatly, without apology, a fact of the medium. "Vaelmoor belongs to whichever navy holds it. If it isn't Karthen it will be someone else within a decade, and someone cruder. I am choosing the version of conquest that leaves the fish market standing."

* * *

Later — much later, when the packet was signed for and the wine was mostly gone and Brannic was being walked back up to the deck by the apple-cheeked midshipman, who had apparently been instructed to be polite to him now, which was worse — Aldous stopped him at the companionway.

"One more thing," the master said quietly, "since you'll be rowing back through my old waters, and since you're the sort of man who carries stories whether or not anyone pays you to."

Brannic waited.

"Do you remember Tam Weller?" Aldous asked. "Boatswain on the *Merrow's Daughter*. Big fellow, red beard, laughed like rigging in a storm."

"I remember him. Good man. Drowned, didn't he — the night you did. Same storm. They found his body on the shingle below the light house." Brannic paused, and something cold moved down his spine with the unhurried patience of a crab. "They never found yours."

"No," Aldous agreed. "They wouldn't have."

The lamplight between them flickered. Above them the fog horn sounded, long and low, the voice of a coast warning ships away from its teeth.

"I was in the water first," Aldous said. "When the *Merrow* went down off the point. Tam surfaced beside me. Strong swimmer, Tam — stronger than me. He got a hand on my collar. Instinct. Men grab things when they're drowning; it doesn't mean anything except that they're drowning." He spoke evenly, almost kindly, a teacher explaining tide tables to a slow student. "I put him under. Held him there until the grabbing stopped. Then I swam for the light house rocks, and by morning the story was already writing itself: brave boatswain lost, master cartographer lost, storm to blame, God to blame, everyone to blame except the two men in the water, and one of those was dead."

Brannic found that his hand had closed around the hilt of a knife he had no intention of drawing, on a ship where drawing it would kill him, against a man he had liked for twenty years. He made himself let go.

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you'll see him everywhere now," Aldous said simply. "Every time you look at me, you'll see Tam Weller's face under the water, and you'll wonder why you don't feel worse about it. And eventually you'll work out why." He smiled the warm smile, and Brannic understood at last that it wasn't a lie at all. It was the truth of him, worn openly, like a scar. "You won't feel worse because you've been doing arithmetic your whole life, Brannic, same as me. One drowned man against a city taken cleanly. One apprentice's trust against a fleet saved from the shoals. We only differ on whose lives go in which column."

"And if I decide your column's full of shit?"

"Then you'll still deliver Halloway's answer, because you're paid to, and because the fishing quarter burns either way if this goes badly, and you know it." Aldous stepped back into the lamplight of his chartroom, serene as a saint in a window. "That's the beauty of clarity, old friend. It doesn't need your approval. It only needs your oar in the water."

* * *

The longboat took Brannic back through the fog with the muffled oars out, and he sat in the stern doing what he always did on the water, which was counting. Counting strokes, counting risks, counting exits.

But somewhere past the middle of the strait he stopped counting and started remembering instead, which was far more dangerous.

He remembered Tam Weller's funeral. He had been there. Everyone had. The whole quay had turned out, and there had been a widow in gray, and a daughter of maybe seven holding one of her father's old fenders like a doll, and the provost of that day saying words over empty water about a good man the sea had taken.

The sea hadn't taken Tam Weller. The sea had just been there, patient and available, the way it always is, when a man needed somewhere to put a body on his way to a new life.

Brannic thought of the boy at the tide gate. Kestrel, with his burned letters and his steady hands and his three years of mourning worn like a coat that fit him. The boy was building a life inside a grave that wasn't occupied.

And Brannic thought, with the peculiar clarity Aldous had promised him, that there were two kinds of men in the world: the ones who put people under the water, and the ones who pulled them out, and that he had spent thirty years telling himself he was neither.

The fog thinned. Ahead, the Vaelmoor lights came up out of the dark one by one, small and stubborn, the whole city burning its little lamps against the night like a fleet of paper boats.

He had a message to deliver. And for the first time in thirty years of delivering messages, Brannic Salt found himself wondering, all the way to the tide gate, who else ought to hear it — and whether a certain admiral's daughter, who was said to read ledgers the way other people read scripture, might be paying better attention than anyone knew.
