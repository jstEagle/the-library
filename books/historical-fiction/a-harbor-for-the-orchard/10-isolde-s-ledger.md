## 10. Isolde's Ledger

The harbor ledgers of Vaelmoor were kept in a room so damp that the clerks had named it the Oyster. Isolde Vane had been given leave to use it for exactly three days — her father's concession to her insistence that someone check the blockade arithmetic before the council voted on it — and she intended to spend all seventy-two hours of that leave proving herself right.

She was right. She simply hadn't expected to be right about quite so many things.

"More tea, miss?" asked Pell, the junior clerk assigned to assist her, a young man whose entire personality appeared to consist of apologizing.

"No, thank you." She turned another page of the grain import rolls. "Pell. If I asked you how much dried fish the fishing quarter consumes in a winter month, what would you say?"

He blinked. "I'd say... I don't know, miss."

"And if I told you the answer was four hundred barrels?"

"I'd say that seems like rather a lot of fish, miss."

"It is rather a lot of fish," Isolde agreed. "It is precisely enough fish for eleven thousand people to eat badly but adequately through a month when no boats can put out. Which they will not be able to do, under my father's blockade plan, because the net tier will be requisitioned for patrol craft and the fish markets will be closed as a 'security measure.'" She tapped the ledger. "Four hundred barrels against stores of ninety. Do you see the problem?"

Pell looked at the numbers the way a man looks at a dog he suspects might bite him. "That's... a shortfall, miss."

"A shortfall," Isolde said. "Yes. Eleven thousand people, ninety barrels, and a blockade scheduled to last — by the admiralty's own estimate — eight weeks. My father intends to starve Karthen out of Vaelmoor's trade routes and will instead starve our own fishermen first, in their own quarter, under our own flag."

She said it flatly, without heat, because heat was for people who hadn't yet decided what to do. She was still deciding. That was the trouble with being an admiral's daughter: every conclusion she reached had a face attached to it, and the face was usually at breakfast.

* * *

Her father had taught her ledgers before he'd taught her to swim. "Numbers don't flatter," Admiral Corvan Vane used to say, walking her along the seawall with a slate under his arm. "Men flatter. Maps flatter. Numbers just sit there being true until somebody makes them lie." He'd been proud of her aptitude, in his gruff way. He'd also, she now understood, assumed her aptitude would stay pointed outward — at Karthen, at smugglers, at anyone whose arithmetic threatened Vaelmoor.

He had not imagined she'd point it at him.

By the second day in the Oyster she had built a full account of the blockade's human cost: the fishing quarter's dependence on open water, the requisition schedule, the ration projections that some well-meaning supply officer had clearly fudged upward to make them palatable. Fudged, she noted, incompetently. Whoever had done it had added the same forty barrels to each month like a child padding a story. It would not survive one afternoon of hostile questioning in council.

She could send it up the chain. Her father would thank her — stiffly, coldly, but he would thank her — and then he would fix it, or worse, proceed anyway and call her findings defeatist. Or she could take it to the council herself and watch her father's flagship plan founder publicly, with her name on the ballast stone.

Neither option appealed. So she did what she always did when trapped between duties: she went looking for a third door, and third doors in Vaelmoor were almost always hidden inside paperwork nobody else wanted to read.

* * *

It was near midnight of the second day, alone in the Oyster with a lamp and a headache shaped like a ship's bell, that she found it.

She had pulled the old channel surveys — the guild's soundings of Vaelmoor Sound, taken over decades, the kind of hydrographic knowledge that took a lifetime to accumulate and one careless archivist to misfile. She wasn't looking for anything. She was avoiding going home to a house where her father rehearsed his blockade speech aloud to the empty chairs. But she cross-checked dates the way other people bit their nails, and something snagged.

The survey of the Teeth approach — the treacherous shoal-water east of the harbor mouth, the one passage deep enough for a warship if you knew its channel — had been re-sounded fourteen years ago. Standard practice. What was not standard practice was the disposition record. The master plates had been copied twice: once for the guild archive, once marked *foreign request, approved.*

Foreign request. Approved.

Fourteen years ago there had been no war. There had been trade delegations, and one of them — she dug through the correspondence index until her fingers were gray with dust — had included a Karthen naval attaché traveling under a merchant's seal. And someone in the Vaelmoor Cartographers' Guild had handed him the depths of their own harbor approach, plate by plate, sounding by sounding, with a signature on the approval line that had been smudged nearly illegible by time and damp.

Isolde sat back in her chair and listened to the Oyster drip.

Someone had sold the city's throat to the enemy fourteen years ago. Quietly, legally, with proper documentation. Not treason in the dramatic sense — no midnight boat, no hooded figures. Just an approval line, a fee recorded in the receipts column, and the sea itself keeping the secret. The Karthen navy hadn't blundered into knowledge of the Teeth channel. They'd *bought* it. Which meant the coming blockade, which depended on Karthen not knowing Vaelmoor's waters better than Vaelmoor did, was already compromised from the inside.

And the smudged signature — she angled the lamp over it again, hating herself for hoping — belonged to one of three men. Provost Halloway, whose hand she knew from guild petitions and which looped its letters like a man showing off. Master Verren, retired now, whose hand she knew from her father's old correspondence. Or a third name that made her go very still:

*Aldous Crane,* deceased. Drowned three years ago. Whose handwriting she knew because her father kept a framed coastal chart of the northern isles above his desk, signed by Crane, and had once said, in the tone he reserved for things he actually respected, *that man could draw water so true you'd taste the salt.*

Isolde stared at the ghost of the signature for a long time.

A dead man couldn't have sold the channels. Unless he hadn't been dead. Unless the drowning — which had happened, she now remembered with a small cold shock, remarkably close to the anniversary of the foreign request's approval — had been convenient.

No. That way lay conspiracy theories, and conspiracy theories were what people invented when they hadn't done the arithmetic. She made herself write down what she actually knew: three names, one smudge, one sale, fourteen years. Two of the three names belonged to living men with access, motive, and unremarkable reputations. The third belonged to a drowned man and to whatever apprentice had inherited his plates.

Kestrel Eriksen.

She thought about the young mapmaker's coastline — the deliberate error she'd caught in his chart of the southern shallows, a half-fathom's lie tucked into a bend of coast so cleanly that only someone who had grown up reading soundings like scripture would ever see it. He'd watched her find it. She'd watched him watch her find it. Neither of them had said a word, and in that shared silence she had felt something she rarely felt: the presence of another fluent speaker of a language she'd assumed only she spoke.

Now she wondered what else that fluent tongue had been saying behind her back. An apprentice who lied in his charts. A dead master whose signature sat on a fourteen-year-old betrayal. A guild that stood to profit from whichever navy won the blockade vote.

"Duty," she said aloud to the dripping dark, "is cracking, and I don't like the sound of it."

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't make balance: everything she had found tonight argued for suspecting Kestrel Eriksen. And everything she had found tonight also argued that the guild — the institution she'd been raised to trust as the honest mirror of the sea — was rotten at the approval line, and that her father's grand blockade was sailing toward a shoal someone had already mapped for the enemy.

If she reported the Eriksen boy and he was guilty, she'd have done her duty and learned nothing, because the rot went deeper than one apprentice. If she reported him and he was innocent, she'd have spent the only person inside the guild who seemed to notice when maps lied — and she'd noticed, hadn't she, that he noticed? If she reported nothing, she was sitting on treason and a famine, and her father's fleet would pay for her delicacy in hulls and her city would pay in hunger.

Three doors, and every one of them locked from the other side.

* * *

She went home at dawn, slept four hours, and dreamed of ledgers that bled into tide tables, columns of numbers dissolving into channels that led somewhere dark. She woke with salt on her lips though her window faced the orchard hill, not the sea.

At midmorning she dressed plainly, dismissed Pell with a fiction about finishing her audit at the admiralty, and walked down to the guild instead. Not the grand hall — the side entrance by the workrooms, where apprentices came and went with rolled charts under their arms and ink permanently stained into the creases of their knuckles.

She told herself it was investigation. Surveillance, properly conducted. The provost had announced that the atlas would be displayed publicly in five days; any sensible spy would want to see the room, the routines, the comings and goings. Watching the mapmaker was due diligence.

It was due diligence that required her to stand across the lane from the workroom window for the better part of an hour, pretending to read a fishmonger's price board that she had memorized within thirty seconds. *Herring, 2 copper. Cod, 4. Mackerel, 3.* Four hundred barrels a month, she thought. Ninety in stores. Eleven thousand mouths.

Through the window she could see him at his table, bent over his work, lamp lit even in daylight because mapmakers trusted lamplight over sun for fine lines. His hands moved steadily. Whatever tremor the other apprentices whispered about, it wasn't visible from here. He worked the way her father steered a ship through fog: absolutely concentrated, absolutely alone.

He looked up suddenly, as if he'd heard her thinking about him, and Isolde stepped back from the window with a speed that undid her dignity entirely, colliding with the fishmonger's barrow and sending a mackerel flopping against her boot.

"Sorry — pardon —" She dropped a coin in the man's tray, far more than the fish was worth, and walked away quickly while the fishmonger called after her, "That's for looking, not buying, my lady!"

So much for surveillance. She rounded the corner onto the seawall road and stopped, breathing, furious at herself — and found, annoyingly, that her heart was doing something other than fury.

Fine. New plan. She had three names and one smudged signature, and two of those names were old men she could investigate through records. The third was a young man she apparently could not investigate without knocking over fish carts. Very well. Records first. And if the records pointed where she feared they pointed —

Then she would go to Kestrel Eriksen directly, and ask him a question no innocent man could answer calmly, and watch his hands.

She was halfway home before she admitted the flaw in that plan: she'd already seen his hands. Steady. Too steady. The hands of a man who had learned, somewhere, from someone, that steadiness was survival.

Five days until the atlas was displayed. Eight weeks until the blockade, if the vote passed. Four hundred barrels against ninety. And somewhere in the middle of all that arithmetic, a dead man's name sat on an approval line, waiting for someone with the nerve to say it aloud.

Isolde turned toward the admiralty archives, and walked faster.
