## 11. The Night of Two Atlases

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Kestrel set down the false atlas's final coastline and let himself, for one full minute, do nothing at all.

He sat in the guild workroom with his palms pressed flat against the desk, feeling the wood's cold grain, listening to the harbor breathe through the shutters. Two atlases lay before him. To anyone else in the world, they would have looked like twins — the same vellum, the same iron-gall ink aged with a wash of walnut to make it seem three years old, the same compass roses with their slightly lopsided east points that Master Aldous had always insisted gave the charts "honesty." Kestrel had spent eleven nights making the false one lie beautifully, and nine of those nights had been spent learning the strangest truth of his life: that forgery was not imitation. It was authorship with a borrowed name. He had never felt more like a mapmaker than he did making the map that was wrong.

He allowed himself the minute because he knew he would not get another. Then he opened his eyes and got back to work.

The true atlas — Aldous's life's labor, three years of Kestrel's grief inked plate by plate — he had wrapped in oilcloth and tucked into the false bottom of the drying cabinet, beneath a layer of spoiled plates he'd ruined on purpose so that any inspector who went digging would find failure before they found treasure. It was not a perfect hiding place. It was a *plausible* one, and Kestrel had learned from the best that plausible beat perfect every time, because perfect made people curious.

He was inking the false atlas's final marginal note — a small, wry observation about the tide-pools below Gullwatch that Aldous had written in the original and that Kestrel had copied so faithfully he'd reproduced a blot of ink shaped, if you squinted, like a herring — when he heard the boots.

Not one pair. Several. And the particular cadence of men who had been told where they were going and did not like being told.

Kestrel's hands went still above the vellum. In the old days, in the purge years, Master Aldous had taught him the first lesson of the guild: *when the boots come, you do not run, you do not hide, you become the most boring man in the room.* Kestrel had been ten years old, pressed under a desk while provost's men tore the workroom apart looking for charts of the northern channels, and Aldous had sat at that same desk and kept inking, calm as a millpond, until the men left with nothing.

*Become boring,* Kestrel told himself, and blew out the candle, and lit the cheaper lamp instead, the one with the guttering wick that made everything look like a chore.

The door opened without a knock. Of course it did. Knocking was for people with something to hide.

"Working late, Eriksen." The voice belonged to Corven Tam, the provost's chief inspector, a man built like a filing cabinet and with roughly the same warmth. Behind him came two younger men with the eager, slightly seasick look of clerks promoted past their competence. They carried lanterns and a manifest and the particular smugness of men who had been told they would find something.

"Deadline," Kestrel said, not looking up. He kept inking. His hand, thank every salt-cold saint, did not shake. "The provost wants the atlas presented in six days. The atlas does not care what the provost wants. Ink dries when it dries."

"Mind if we look around?"

"You're going to whether I mind or not." He gestured with the pen at the room without raising his eyes. "Mind the wet plates on the left. Touch one and you'll wear that coastline for a week."

One of the clerks snorted. Corven Tam did not. Tam was the kind of man who had never in his life found anything funny, which made him excellent at his job and unbearable at dinner.

They began the sweep. Kestrel had imagined this moment perhaps forty times in the past two weeks, and in every imagining, his heart had hammered and his mouth had gone dry. Now that it was happening, he found himself strangely detached, watching himself work the pen while his mind ran a separate ledger: *cabinet false bottom — they'll check it, it's the first place anyone checks. Spoiled plates on top — good. The true atlas is wrapped in oilcloth — oilcloth reads as rags in bad light. The false atlas is right here on the desk, in plain sight, because the boldest hiding place in Vaelmoor is the one a man is actively working on.*

Tam lifted the drying cabinet's false bottom on the first try, exactly as predicted, and frowned at the ruined plates. "These are spoiled."

"Walnut wash went wrong on a batch of vellum," Kestrel said. "Three weeks of work. I nearly wept. Ask anyone."

Tam looked at him for a long moment. Kestrel returned the look with the flat, tired expression of a craftsman interrupted, and something in his chest noted, with distant interest, how easy the lying had become. That was the part that would keep him up at night, later — not the fear, but the *fluency*.

The clerks went through the drawers. They found Kestrel's lunch (half a herring pie, two days old, which one of them recoiled from with genuine offense), a tin of pens, a love letter from a dockside woman named Maren that Kestrel had written for a colleague and kept as a template, and forty years of guild correspondence that they read with the enthusiasm of men reading someone else's taxes.

"Who ordered the sweep?" Kestrel asked, mostly to keep them talking. Talking men were not searching men.

"Provost's office," Tam said.

"Looking for what, exactly?"

"Contraband charts. Unregistered copies." Tam set down a drawer with a click. "There's talk the atlas has been... circulating."

"Circulating." Kestrel let out a short laugh. "Inspector, I've barely let this atlas circulate to the privy. It doesn't leave my sight. Ask the night porter — I sleep in the chair half the time. My back can testify."

That was true, which helped. The best lies, Aldous had said, were load-bearing walls built on true foundations.

* * *

They were forty minutes in and finding nothing, and Kestrel had begun to believe — dangerously, he would later think — that the night might actually end, when the youngest clerk opened the door to the map room's inner chamber and said, in the bright voice of a man about to ruin everything:

"Inspector. What's this?"

Kestrel looked up.

The clerk was standing beside the long shipping crate that had been delivered that afternoon and that Kestrel had been studiously not looking at all evening, the way one studiously does not look at a thing one is trying not to think about. The crate was long, narrow, and unmistakably coffin-shaped, because it was a coffin — or rather, the shipping case for one — belonging to old Master Fenwick, the guild's tide-tables man, who had died in his sleep three days prior at the respectable age of seventy-one, and who was to be buried on Thursday with full guild honors in the churchyard above the harbor.

"It's a coffin, you idiot," Kestrel said, before he could stop himself, and then, softening it with the weary patience of a man who had explained things to clerks all his life: "It's Master Fenwick. He's dead. It happens to the best of us eventually, though in his case he'd earned it honestly."

The clerk flushed. Tam crossed the room.

Now — here is the thing that Kestrel would spend the rest of his life not quite explaining, even to himself. He had not put the true atlas in the coffin. He had not even thought of the coffin until this exact moment. The coffin was in the workroom for the perfectly ordinary, perfectly mournful reason that Fenwick's plates had been packed in it — the guild's custom was to ship a master's effects with his body, one last journey together — and Kestrel had spent the evening keeping his eyes off it because grief for Fenwick was still fresh and grief was a distraction he could not afford.

But Tam was now standing over the coffin with his lantern, and Tam said, "This wasn't on the manifest of the workroom," and Kestrel heard his own voice say, calm as a millpond, calm as Aldous under the boots:

"No. It's not workroom property. It's a funeral. It was delivered this afternoon for Thursday's service. Fenwick's plates are in with him — guild custom. You can check with the undertaker on Coil Street if you enjoy that sort of thing."

"Open it," Tam said.

The room went very quiet. The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, a ship's bell counted the half hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Inspector," Kestrel said, and here he made the mistake of his life or the best decision of it, he still wasn't sure which, "that is a dead man's coffin, and I am going to need you to have a better reason than 'open it' before I pry the lid off Master Fenwick and disturb whatever's left of him. He spent forty years doing your tide tables. He did *your wedding tide tables.* I was at the wedding. The tide was perfect. You're welcome."

Tam's jaw worked. The two clerks looked at each other, then at the coffin, then at Kestrel, with the expressions of men who had joined the provost's service expecting smugglers and sword fights and had instead been handed a moral problem involving a corpse.

"Guild custom," Tam said slowly, "is that the plates go with the body."

"It is," Kestrel agreed. "It's a stupid custom. Fenwick himself called it 'shipping my mistakes to God.' But it's the custom."

Tam stood there a long moment. Kestrel watched him weigh it — the inspector's instinct against the inspector's career, because Tam was not a fool, and a fool would have pried the lid then and there. A man who opened a beloved dead master's coffin on a rumor, in front of clerks, and found nothing but an old man and his tide tables, would be a joke in the guildhall for a decade. Tam knew it. Kestrel watched him know it.

"Thursday," Tam said finally. "After the burial, the coffin comes back to the guild for the archive. I'll inspect it then. If I find anything in it besides Fenwick and his plates, Eriksen, you'll hang beside whatever I find."

"Then I'll be sure to bury him with nothing but good wishes," Kestrel said.

They left. The boots receded down the stair. The door closed.

Kestrel sat very still for the length of one hundred heartbeats, which he counted, because counting was better than screaming.

Then he got up, crossed the room, and stood looking down at the shipping case that held Master Fenwick — who had taught him, in his one year of instruction before retirement, that the tide waits for no man but forgives almost all of them — and felt the full, horrible, absurd shape of what the evening had just handed him.

Because Tam would inspect the coffin on Thursday. After the burial. Which meant that between now and Thursday, the true atlas — the real one, Aldous's legacy, the thing two navies and one corrupt provost were circling like gulls over a fish cart — had to be inside that coffin. Under a dead man. Traveling to a churchyard and back, guarded by nothing but grief, custom, and the guild's own superstition about disturbing the dead.

Kestrel laughed. It came out wrong, a dry bark with no humor in it, and he put his hand over his mouth and laughed again anyway, because the alternative was to sit down on the floor.

*His survival now depends on a dead man's box.*

He worked fast after that. The oilcloth came out of the cabinet's false bottom; the true atlas, wrapped and bound, went into the space beneath the false plate-liner of the coffin's case, where Fenwick's own plates lay in their velvet cradle — Kestrel lifted them, slid the atlas beneath, and replaced them, and the whole operation took less time than it took to write it, because his hands had been trained by a master forger and grief, it turned out, made the fingers steady.

He paused with his hand flat on the lid.

"I'm sorry, old man," he said quietly. "You'd have found it funny. You found everything funny except low tides." He thought about it. "Actually, you'd have found low tides funny too. You found *me* funny, and I'm no better."

He closed the lid. He wrote out the shipping tag in his best clerk's hand: *Master Aldous Fenwick, tide-tables, forty years' service, to be interred Thursday, Churchyard of St. Brannoc-above-the-Harbor. Effects to return to guild archive following interment.*

Then he sat back down at his desk, picked up the false atlas, and looked at it for a long time.

It was finished. Eleven nights of work, and it was finished, and it was beautiful, and it was a lie from its compass rose to its tide-pool herring, and in six days he would stand in front of the guildhall and present it as the work of his dead master and his own grieving hands, and the provost would smile, and the admiral's daughter would be in the room — *she reads maps too*, a voice in his head reminded him, and not a kind voice — and if Isolde Vane looked at the false atlas the way she had looked at his coastline error in the audit, if she caught the wrongness the way she had caught it before, then everything — the coffin, Fenwick, the whole desperate architecture of his survival — would come down on his head like a collapsed pier.

He thought about that. He thought about it the way a man thinks about the water below a cliff he has already jumped from.

Then he blew out the lamp, because dawn was coming up gray over the harbor, and there was nothing left in the night to do but survive until Thursday.

* * *

Thursday came with a cold wind off the water and a funeral.

The guild turned out in force for Fenwick — half from love, half from the guild's iron law that members attend burials, and Kestrel walked in the procession with the other journeymen, his collar scratchy, his face arranged into mourning, his heart doing something that was not mourning at all but rather a kind of high, singing terror. Ahead of him the coffin went on the bier, and inside the coffin, beneath a dead man and forty years of tide tables, lay the true atlas, and Kestrel kept his eyes fixed on the back of Provost Halloway's head and thought: *you have no idea what you're walking behind, you old spider.*

The churchyard stood on the bluff above the harbor, and from its wall you could see the whole roadstead — the fishing fleet at anchor, the navy's gray shapes at their moorings, the far line of the Teeth shoals white-toothed even in fair weather. Fenwick had spent his life measuring those waters. It was a good place for him to stop.

The priest said the words. The guild sang, badly. They lowered the coffin, and Kestrel stood at the graveside with his hands folded and his mind running its cold separate ledger: *the case returns to the guild this afternoon. Tam will inspect it. The atlas must not be in it when he does. Which means it must come out of it before — which means —*

Which meant that sometime between the burial and the inspection, Kestrel Eriksen was going to have to rob a grave.

Or — his mind corrected, with the precision of a man who had learned bookkeeping from criminals — rob a coffin. In a churchyard. In daylight. While the entire guild stood around it singing.

He was still working the arithmetic when he felt someone come stand beside him at the wall, and turned, and found Isolde Vane looking out at the harbor with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman inspecting a fleet.

"Master Fenwick taught my father's navigation masters," she said, without preamble. "The tide tables. Half the navy learned the roadstead from that old man's numbers."

"He'd have been insufferable about it if he'd known," Kestrel said. "He was insufferable about less."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. She was quiet a moment. Then, still looking at the water:

"Strange thing. The undertaker on Coil Street told my clerk this morning that the coffin case was delivered to the guild workroom four days before Master Fenwick died."

The cold that went through Kestrel was not like the sea wind. It was much, much worse.

"Undertakers," he said, "are notorious gossips with bad memories."

"Mm," said Isolde Vane, and turned from the wall, and looked at him — that look, the one from the audit, the one that said *I have read your coastline and I know it lies* — and said, pleasantly, "The guild archives must be very crowded lately. So many things going into boxes."

She walked away down the path, her gray coat sharp against the green, and Kestrel stood alone at the wall with the harbor below him and a grave at his back that he now had, somehow, to empty before nightfall — while the provost's chief inspector counted the hours, and the admiral's daughter counted everything else.
