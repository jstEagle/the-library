## 12. Holloway's Offer

Provost Halloway kept a ledger of everything in Vaelmoor worth knowing, and the ledger lived nowhere but his own skull.

Not the guild's official books — those were for show, balanced to the copper, audited twice a year by men who believed what they read because believing was cheaper than checking. No, Halloway's true accounts were written in a script of small habits and remembered favors: which councilmen took their wine sweet, which harbormaster had a second family in Ternwick, which inspector could be trusted to look at a document for exactly as long as it took to see nothing in it. Forty-one years in the Chartmakers' Guild had taught him that a city was only a map made of people, and like any map, it was mostly blank space where someone had paid for the blankness.

He sat at his desk above the harbor with the morning light doing its best through leaded glass, and he counted.

The blockade vote stood at nine for, seven against, four undecided — three if you discounted Councilman Ferro, whose indecision was a performance he'd been refining since before the war and would continue long after everyone involved was dead. The Karthen navy wanted channel depths. The Vaelmoor admiralty wanted a blockade that would strangle the fishing quarter into compliance, which was a polite way of saying they were willing to starve their own poorest citizens into patriotism. And somewhere between these two appetites sat the Vaelmoor Atlas — finished at last, after eleven years and two masters — worth more than every ship in either fleet combined, and about to be displayed publicly in five days' time.

Five days. Halloway turned his quill slowly between his fingers and considered how many fortunes could be arranged in five days, provided one moved with care.

There came the knock he'd been waiting for. Not Kestrel Eriksen's knock — the boy knocked like an apology — but his secretary's, brisk and incurious.

"Send him up," Halloway said.

* * *

Kestrel Eriksen climbed the provost's stairs the way water finds its level: quietly, inevitably, taking the shape of whatever contained him. He was twenty-three and looked older in the eyes and younger everywhere else, a combination Halloway had learned to distrust in apprentices and envy in accountants. His hands were inked to the second knuckle, as always, as though he'd been born mid-sentence.

"Provost." He bowed precisely enough to be respectful and not so precisely as to be insolent.

"Eriksen. Sit. Wine?" Halloway poured without waiting for an answer, because declining a provost's wine required a courage nobody brought to this office. "You look tired."

"I've been finishing the atlas."

"Eleven years of finishing. One might think the atlas enjoyed being finished." He smiled to show it was humor, and watched Kestrel return a thin version of the smile, the way a man returns a borrowed coat that doesn't fit. Good. Nervous men accepted hospitality; guilty men refused it. This one accepted. Interesting.

"The display is in five days," Halloway said. "The council will attend. Both navies will have observers. I trust the plates are ready."

"They're ready."

"They'd better be more than ready. They'd better be *perfect*, because in five days that atlas stops being ours and starts being everyone's, and everyone includes people with cannons." Halloway leaned back. "Tell me, Eriksen. When you inked the final plate — the Teeth approach, wasn't it? What did you feel?"

Kestrel blinked. "I don't—"

"Pride? Relief? Grief?" Halloway waved his glass. "I ask because I've watched three master chartmakers finish lifeworks in this room's history, and each of them described it differently. Old Merrick wept. Your master—" a small pause, delicately measured, "—Aldous said he felt nothing at all, which I always thought was the most dishonest thing a mapmaker ever said to me, and Aldous said some very dishonest things."

"He was honest when it mattered," Kestrel said, too quickly.

"Mm," said Halloway, and let the silence do its work. He had learned silence from the tide: patient, total, indifferent to resistance. He let it run nearly half a minute before he opened his desk drawer and set two things on the blotter.

The first was a chart of the northern approaches — a guild chart, guild seal, guild paper.

The second was the same chart. Almost.

"Look at them," Halloway said pleasantly. "Tell me what you see."

Kestrel picked up the first, then the second. Halloway watched his face with the attention other men gave to eclipses. The boy was good — better than good, better than Aldous at his age, possibly better than Aldous entirely, though Halloway kept that opinion in a locked drawer of its own. For four full seconds his expression held nothing. Then something crossed behind his eyes like a shadow under clear water, gone before it could be named, and he set both charts down with hands that did not shake.

"They're the same survey," Kestrel said. "Different copyists."

"They are indeed different copyists," Halloway agreed. "One copyist works in this building, under my seal, for guild wages. The other copyist works for whoever pays him, and last autumn sold a version of these depths to a certain gentleman who represents interests across the strait. Can you imagine. Selling Vaelmoor's waters to Karthen." He clicked his tongue. "The depths are subtly wrong, of course. Shaved here, deepened there. Clever work. Only a trained eye would catch it, and only a trained eye trained *here* would know why it matters."

"An audit would find the discrepancy," Kestrel said carefully.

"An audit already has." Halloway smiled. "Mine. Six weeks ago. Which brings us to the interesting part, Eriksen, the part I've been looking forward to with genuine pleasure." He folded his hands. "The smuggler charts. Plural. Because the northern approaches weren't the only ones copied, were they? The Teeth passages. The old eel-boat channels. Someone with access to the restricted drawers fed a great deal of our coastline to people who should never have had it, over a period of — I'd estimate — three years. Beginning shortly after your master drowned."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Downstairs, faintly, someone laughed at something in the copying hall, the ordinary laughter of ordinary people whose futures were not currently being weighed on a provost's scales.

"I don't know anything about that," Kestrel said.

"No," Halloway agreed warmly. "Of course not. You wouldn't. You're a loyal apprentice who spent eleven years completing a dying man's atlas out of pure devotion, and I believe every word of it, the way I believe Councilman Ferro is undecided." He rose, went to the window, and gestured at the harbor below — the forest of masts, the fisher boats coming in gray with dawn catch, the great slow breathing of a city that did not know how thoroughly it was for sale. "Look at it, Eriksen. In five days the admiralty votes its blockade, and within the month the fishing quarter starves politely while both navies fight over whose flag gets to protect them from the other. The atlas will decide which fleet knows which waters. Do you understand what that means? Whoever holds the true atlas holds the war. And whoever holds a *false* one—" he turned back, smiling, "—holds something even more valuable. Leverage. Insurance. A knife kept quietly in a drawer until the exact right dinner."

"What are you offering me?"

Ah. There it was. Not *I didn't do it*. Not *what do you mean*. Just the clean, professional question of a man calculating terms. Halloway could have kissed him. Aldous had taught the boy well, whoever else had finished the teaching.

"I'm offering you a seat at the table," Halloway said. "Instead of a place on it. Metaphorically speaking. The gallows are literal, of course. Guild treason in wartime — selling sovereign charts to the enemy — there's a gallows on the breakwater specifically maintained for it, and I'm told the view is excellent right up until it isn't."

"You have no proof it was me."

"I have no proof it *wasn't* you, which in front of a wartime tribunal amounts to the same thing, and I have something better than proof." He tapped the forged chart on the blotter. "I have a buyer's description of the man who delivered the northern charts last autumn. Young. Ink-stained fingers. A limp in the left knee from an old fall off the sea-wall — and doesn't every apprentice in this guild have a story about the sea-wall? The description could fit half the junior members. It could be made to fit exactly one." He let that sit. "Or it could remain a vague and useless description forever. That depends entirely on the next five minutes of conversation, and I confess I'm hoping for the best, because I've read your plates, Eriksen, and wasting you would be a crime against cartography."

Kestrel's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "What does partnership mean?"

"It means profit sharing, of course. But more than that." Halloway returned to his chair and sat, settling in the way a man settles when the negotiation has already been won and only the courtesies remain. "It means you keep making maps — beautiful, true maps, because I want the true atlas displayed in five days and admired by everyone. Let the guild sell the truth to the admiralty at a fair markup. And then, quietly, through channels I have spent forty years digging, we sell careful falsehoods to both sides. Karthen believes our shoals are deeper than they are; their heavy frigates hug a coast that will eat them. Our own admiralty believes a passage exists where none does, and comes to me for corrections, paid handsomely, every quarter. Neither navy ever quite trusts its charts, and so neither navy ever quite wins, and Vaelmoor — the city, the guild, you and I — grows rich being the only ones who know what the water actually says." He spread his hands. "War is a market like any other, Eriksen. Most men just lack the nerve to open a stall."

"And the fishing quarter?" Kestrel asked. Quietly. Dangerously. "Does it grow rich too?"

Halloway sighed with genuine regret, the sigh of a man who wished the world were kinder and intended to do nothing about it. "The fishing quarter is weather, Eriksen. We are maps. Maps cannot save anyone from weather; they can only tell the wealthy where to build their houses." He softened his voice. "You're young. You still think the question is *what is right*. Give it ten years and you'll understand the question is *whose right, and at what rate*. I'm offering you the answer early, at a discount. Very few men get such an offer. Most get the tribunal instead."

For a long moment Kestrel said nothing. Halloway watched him the way he watched the tide tables — reading the pull beneath the surface. The boy's thumb had moved, unconsciously, to rub the ink on his forefinger, a habit Halloway had seen in him a hundred times and now recognized as something else: the gesture of a man touching an object to confirm it's still real. Something he was protecting. The atlas, presumably. Or something inside the atlas. Or—

Well. Halloway's ledger had a page for that too. The undertaker's order. The funeral tomorrow. The chief inspector's report of a coffin heavier than grief alone could account for, and the admiral's daughter asking questions in places questions didn't belong. He hadn't risen to provost by chasing every thread; he rose by knowing which threads to hold and when to pull.

"And if I refuse?" Kestrel said.

"Then tomorrow morning I convene a quiet inquiry into irregularities in the restricted drawers, and the inquiry follows wherever the evidence leads, and the evidence — well. Evidence is like water, Eriksen. It finds the lowest point." Halloway smiled again, gently, and meant every word of the next part, which was the strange thing about him: he never lied when the truth served better. "But I don't want that. Truly. I watched Aldous waste fifteen years pretending to be a modest man, and it bored us both. You're the finest hand this guild has produced in a generation, and I would rather be your partner than your executioner. Partners live longer. They also eat better."

Kestrel stood. He bowed — precise, respectful, unreadable — and said, "You'll have my answer before the display."

"Take the time you need," Halloway said graciously. "Though I'd take less rather than more. Opportunities in Vaelmoor have a way of sinking."

The door closed behind the young man with barely a sound. Halloway remained at his desk a while, turning his wineglass, watching the light move in it like something trapped.

He did not doubt the boy would come around. They always did — the clever ones fastest of all, because cleverness was simply the ability to see the trap clearly, and seeing it clearly, to notice that it had comfortable chairs. Aldous had refused a similar offer once, years ago, all principles and trembling virtue — and look where principle had gotten him: a watery grave, or so the record said, and Halloway had long ago stopped believing the record said much of anything true.

Still. He pulled his mental ledger toward him and made an entry, in the script of small habits:

*E. Kestrel — offered terms. Accepted nothing. Refused nothing. Watch the funeral.*

Because there had been one moment, just one, in all that careful conversation, when the boy's composure had flickered — not at the mention of the gallows, not at the threat, not even at the money. It had come when Halloway mentioned the false charts beginning three years ago, shortly after the drowning. Something had moved behind those eyes then, quick and cold, and it had not been fear of exposure.

It had been arithmetic. The same arithmetic Halloway himself performed daily, the silent counting of a man rearranging a plan around new information.

Which meant Kestrel Eriksen already had a plan. And plans, in Halloway's experience, were the one commodity in Vaelmoor that appreciated fastest in the days before a public display.

He finished his wine, rang for his secretary, and gave the day's orders — including a quiet instruction that two of his most reliable inspectors attend a certain funeral tomorrow afternoon, ostensibly to pay respects.

"If anyone asks why the guild sends mourners," Halloway added, "tell them the deceased was a colleague. It's even true." He paused, savoring. "That's the trouble with this city. Everything worth saying is true, if you bury it deep enough."
