## 13. A Promise Made in Peacetime

Fifteen years ago, on the last warm evening of a peacetime autumn, Master Aldous Vane took Kestrel up onto the breakwater with a bottle of plum wine and a compass he never let anyone else touch.

"Mind the stones," Aldous said, though Kestrel had climbed these stones since he was small enough to fit inside a crab pot. "Mind them anyway. A man who stops minding familiar ground is a man who drowns on dry land."

Kestrel had been an apprentice for five years by then, and he had learned the master's liturgy well enough to recite it back at him. "A man who drowns on dry land is a man who wasn't paying attention to the tide."

"Adequate." Aldous said *adequate* the way other men said *magnificent*, and Kestrel glowed like a signal lamp. "Sit. Drink. You're old enough now that the wine will do you more good than harm, and young enough that the harm will be instructive."

The harbor below them was full of ships that summer — masts like a forest, rigging singing in the wind, the fishing fleet and the merchantmen and the navy's gray hulls all tangled together in the golden light. Kestrel had never known the harbor any other way. He thought, in the way of fifteen-year-olds, that it never would be any other way.

Aldous drank, winced, and passed the bottle. "Plum. From the orchard above Tern Street. The widow there presses it herself and cheats on the sugar, which is why it's honest."

Kestrel drank and did not wince, because he was fifteen and determined to be a man about it, and then coughed for a full minute while Aldous watched with the particular pleasure of a teacher whose lesson has landed.

"You've seen the atlas," Aldous said, when the coughing subsided.

It was not a question. The Vaelmoor Atlas lived in the guild's iron-doored vault, and Kestrel had seen it exactly once, two years ago, when Aldous had carried him in on his shoulders — a small boy, then — and unwrapped the oilcloth with the reverence other men reserved for relics. Coastlines inked in three weights of line. Soundings in a hand so fine it looked printed. Every channel, every shoal, every tide-rip from the Teeth to the Salt Gate, drawn by Aldous Vane over twenty years and still unfinished.

"You've seen it," Aldous repeated, "and you know it's not done."

"The southern approaches," Kestrel said. "And the deepwater soundings past the Teeth. You said the Teeth can't be sounded properly except in a hard easterly, and there's been no hard easterly worth the name in—"

"Two years. Yes." Aldous looked at him then, and Kestrel felt the look the way he felt the wine, warm and burning. "I'm getting old, Kestrel. My eyes are going. Last month I misread a sounding by a fathom and didn't catch it until the third pass. A fathom." He said it like a confession of murder. "The atlas needs a younger hand to finish it. It needs *your* hand."

Kestrel's heart did something complicated in his chest. "I'm not ready."

"No," Aldous agreed cheerfully. "You're not. But you will be, and I'll be dead, and the atlas will still need finishing. So." He held out the compass — the brass one, the one with the worn rose and the hairline crack across the north point that he'd had since he was younger than Kestrel. "Take it."

"Master—"

"Take it, and hear the terms, because I've thought about them longer than I thought about the southern approaches, which took me nine years." Aldous turned to face him fully, and the harbor light caught the gray in his beard and made him, for a moment, look like a man carved into a guildhall wall. "Finish the atlas. All of it. The southern approaches, the Teeth, the last of the tide tables. Finish it to a standard *I* would sign, and when it's done — when it's delivered into the guild archive with your name beside mine — you are free. Your indenture ends. No purchase price, no petition, no provost's signature. My word, given on this stone, in peacetime, when my word still means something."

Kestrel looked at the compass. He looked at the harbor, the forest of masts, the whole bright world he had been mapping in his head since he was ten years old and a man had put a hand over his mouth during the purge and taught him that silence was the shape of safety.

"And if I finish it and you don't think it's good enough?"

"Then you'll do it again," Aldous said, "and it will be better the second time, and I will still be old, and you will still be young, and eventually the atlas will be finished. That's the whole trick of it, boy. Everything true gets finished eventually. It's only the lies that need hurrying."

Kestrel took the compass.

Aldous smiled — not the thin professional smile he wore for the provost, but the real one, the crooked one, the one Kestrel would spend the next fifteen years drawing from memory without knowing he was drawing it. "Then it's sealed. Wine, stone, and a compass. The guild would want wax and witnesses, but the guild can go swim." He clinked his cup against the bottle. "Finish it, and you're free."

They stayed on the breakwater until the lamps came out along the quay, and Aldous told him about the orchard above Tern Street — how he'd meant, someday, to buy a cottage there, and plant a tree for every chart he finished, and grow too old and too slow to mind the tide at all. "A harbor for the orchard," he said, half to himself, and Kestrel didn't understand it then, and would not understand it for a very long time.

* * *

Present-day Kestrel stood in the workroom with the compass in his hand and understood it entirely, and wished to God he didn't.

The compass had a hairline crack across the north point. He had never noticed it as a boy. He noticed it now, in the lamplight, at two in the morning, with the false atlas drying under weights on the bench behind him and the true one hidden in a coffin across town.

*Finish it, and you're free.*

He turned the compass over. On the reverse, worn nearly smooth by fifteen years of his own thumb, was the guild's proof-mark — the small stamped anchor that marked every instrument owned by the guild, every tool, every plate, every apprentice. Kestrel had carried the compass for fifteen years and had never once thought about the stamp. It was like the wall of a house. You didn't see it. You lived inside it.

He saw it now.

The promise had been made in peacetime, on a warm stone, with honest plum wine. And it had been kept, in its way. He had finished the atlas. His name was inked beside his master's on the title plate. And the indenture — he had checked, three years ago, when the grief was fresh and the provost's condolences were still wet on the guild's letterhead — the indenture had indeed ended, exactly as promised, the day the atlas was entered into the archive.

He had been free for three years.

He had not noticed.

That was the thing that sat in his chest now like a swallowed hook. Not that Aldous had lied — the man had kept his word to the letter, and Kestrel was learning, in the cruelest possible schoolroom, what it cost to keep a promise to the letter while breaking it in spirit. No: the horror was that the promise had been *true*, and it had still been a leash. Aldous had handed him freedom like a bone to a dog — here, earned, yours, go on then — and Kestrel had taken it and gone on sleeping in the guild dormitory, eating at the guild table, rising at the guild bell, inking at the guild bench, because what else was there? Freedom had been handed to him and he had not known what to buy with it, so he had kept the change.

*You did that,* he told himself, and the telling was honest and it did not help. *No one chained you to the bench. You sat down and picked up the pen.*

But even that was only half true, and Kestrel was, by profession and by temperament, a man who could not abide a half-true chart.

Because Aldous had built him. That was the whole of it. A ten-year-old boy with no family and no name worth writing had been taught which silences keep people safe, and had learned the lesson so well that silence had become his mother tongue. A boy had been taught that survival was its own kind of lie, and had believed it so completely that when the smugglers came to him with coin and threat in equal measure, he had altered the charts without ever once considering the third option, the option that had never been drawn on any map he'd been given: *tell someone. Refuse. Burn.*

Aldous had taught him to read water, and he had never once taught him to read the current he himself was caught in.

*He bought my indenture,* Brannic had said. *He bought you, boy. You were the payment on an older debt.*

Kestrel set the compass down on the bench, next to the false atlas, and looked at his own hands. They were steady. That was the worst discovery of the night, worse than the proof-mark, worse than the arithmetic of the leash: his hands were steady, and they were steady because a dead man — a living man, a *liar* — had spent fifteen years training them, and the training held even when the man turned out to be hollow. He was Aldous Vane's finest instrument. He was a sounding-line with a heartbeat.

"I hate you," Kestrel said to the empty workroom, quietly, because the walls of the guild had ears the way the harbor had tide — everywhere, patient, indifferent. It was the first time he had said it aloud. It tasted like plum wine and did not get easier with the second try. "You made me free and you made me unable to leave. That's not a gift, old man. That's a cage with the door painted on."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the guild bell counted three, and Kestrel counted with it out of habit, and hated the habit, and could not put it down.

* * *

He worked until the lamp burned to oil-slick and the false atlas's southern approaches were perfect — perfect, and wrong, the Teeth drawn a half-mile east of their true teeth, the deepwater channel shifted just enough to invite a fleet onto stone. His hands remembered Aldous's lessons too well. That was the whole terror of it. Forgery was not a new skill. Forgery was the old skill, the first skill, the one he'd learned at ten years old with a hand over his mouth: *be what they expect to see, and they will not look twice.*

He cleaned the pens. He blotted the plates. He wrapped the compass in its cloth, and then, instead of putting it in his pocket where it had lived for fifteen years, he set it on the bench and looked at it a long moment.

He picked it up anyway. Of course he picked it up. A man does not stop minding familiar ground.

* * *

Dawn found him on the breakwater, because the workroom had become a place where a man could hear his own thoughts too clearly, and the breakwater at least had the decency to shout over them.

The harbor was different at dawn than it had been at fifteen. The fishing boats went out in ones and twos now, where once they'd gone in fleets. The navy's gray hulls had multiplied, and the merchantmen had thinned, and the masts did not sing the same song. War did that. War was a tide, and everything in the harbor floated on it whether it wanted to or not.

He thought about the orchard above Tern Street. He thought about a cottage that was never bought, and trees never planted, and a man who had promised a boy freedom on this exact stone and then, by every account, had walked into the sea — or had walked onto a Karthen deck, which amounted to the same drowning with better weather.

*Finish it, and you're free.*

He had finished it. He was not free. And the promise, he understood now, had never actually been about freedom at all. It had been about the atlas. It had always been about the atlas. Aldous had bound a lonely, brilliant, frightened boy to a life's work with the one word that boy wanted more than bread — *free* — and had known, with a master's precision, exactly how long the binding would hold.

Fifteen years. It had held fifteen years, and it would have held thirty, and Aldous had known that too.

"You measured me," Kestrel said to the water. "Like a channel. You took your soundings and you drew me into the chart you needed, and I let you, because you were the first hand that ever reached down and I thought it was lifting."

The tide came in below him, patient, filling the channels it had always filled.

There was a funeral tomorrow. Old Copplewick, the plate-cutter, dead of his heart at fifty-four, and the whole guild would walk to the burial ground in its black wool, and the provost would say the words, and a coffin would go into the ground with a false bottom that Kestrel had built with his own hands. The true atlas was in it now. By tomorrow evening it would be in his hands again — or he would be caught, and the gallows would have him, and the difference between those two outcomes was a dead man's box and his own nerve.

He turned the compass over one last time. The guild's anchor, stamped into the brass. He put his thumb over it, covering the mark, and held it that way a moment — the way you'd cover a brand, the way you'd press a wound — and then he pocketed the compass and stood.

Tomorrow he would walk behind Copplewick's coffin with the rest of them, head bowed, face arranged into the grief the guild expected to see. And somewhere in that procession, if the tide of it went even slightly wrong, Isolde Vane would be watching him — because she had marked him, he was sure of it now, the way he marked a coastline with a deliberate error to see who noticed.

He had built a false atlas for the enemy and hidden the true one in a coffin. There was no chart for the water he was in now.

He started back toward the guild as the bell rang the hour, and behind him the tide kept coming in, filling every channel, keeping every promise the sea had ever made, which was none of them.
