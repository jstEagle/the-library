## 14. The Funeral That Wasn't

The rain held off, which Kestrel considered the day's single mercy and suspected would be its last.

Copplewick's funeral drew half the guild to the Saltfield cemetery — not because anyone had loved Copplewick overmuch, but because the guild required attendance at the burial of a senior cartographer the way the tide required water. Provost Halloway stood at the front in his best grey wool, delivering a eulogy that managed, through some alchemy of phrasing, to say nothing whatsoever about the man it honored. *A steady hand,* Halloway said. *A careful man.* Kestrel, who had shared a workroom with Copplewick for nine years and knew him chiefly for his habit of eating pickled herring at his drafting table and blaming the smell on the ink, thought: *You never once looked at him while he was alive, and now you can't stop describing him.*

Around him the mourners stood in their black wool, heads bowed, umbrellas furled against a sky that had apparently decided to be merciful out of spite. The coffin sat on the bier before the open grave, plain oak, guild-stamped, carried up from the workroom cellar by four bearers who had complained about its weight all morning.

*Of course it's heavy,* Kestrel had wanted to tell them. *It's carrying an atlas worth more than your houses.*

He bowed his head with the rest of them. He arranged his face into grief. And he kept one eye — the trained eye, the eye Isolde Vane had marked three days ago in the map room — on the coffin, because in six hours it would be lowered into the ground with everything he had left inside it, and he intended to make very sure that did not happen.

The priest said the words. The words were old ones, older than the guild, older than the harbor probably, about dust and salt and the sea giving back what it took. Kestrel found this darkly funny given that the sea had taken Copplewick's actual body last autumn — fever, not drowning, though the distinction mattered little to the worms — and what lay in the coffin was a substitute, a forgery of a man made of weighted sandbags and ballast stones, exactly as the atlas inside it was a true thing disguised as a false one.

Everything in Vaelmoor was a substitution now. He had simply gotten good at making them.

The eulogy ended. The mourners filed past the coffin in the traditional order — provost first, then senior masters, then journeymen, then apprentices trailing behind like gulls after a fishing boat. Each mourner laid a sprig of rosemary on the lid. Kestrel laid his sprig down and let his fingers rest there a half-second longer than mourning strictly required, feeling through the oak the faint, impossible weight of fifteen years of his master's coastline drawings, and thought: *Hold on. One more hour.*

Then the procession began to move toward the grave, and Kestrel began to count.

The rite at the graveside would take forty minutes, perhaps fifty if the priest was paid to be thorough, which he was. The lowering would take another ten. After the lowering came the wake at the guildhall — wine, cold meats, Halloway making conversation with donors — and nobody, *nobody*, returned to a grave before full dark. It wasn't done. It was practically sacrilege.

Which meant Kestrel had from roughly four o'clock until dusk to come back with a shovel, commit a felony in front of God and Copplewick, and remove an atlas from a coffin that would be officially sealed by then, replacing it with something of equal weight so that no gravedigger ever noticed the discrepancy.

He had spent two evenings preparing the replacement: a wrapped bundle of lead sheeting and sand, weighed on the guild's own scale to match the atlas within two ounces. He had chosen the shovel from the guild toolshed — the one with the loose handle, because nobody borrowed the one with the loose handle.

It was, he reflected as the first spade of earth went onto the coffin lid and the mourners murmured their amens, an absurd plan. It was also, given the alternatives, the best plan he had. He had built his whole life on plans like this: small crimes committed carefully in service of larger silences.

He just hadn't expected to look up from the graveside and find Isolde Vane watching him again.

She stood across the grave in a black cloak, her father's colors muted to a single grey ribbon at her throat, her expression arranged into suitable solemnity. But she wasn't looking at the coffin. She was looking at him, the way she'd looked at his coastline chart — patient, precise, like a woman reading a depth sounding off a shoal she already knew was there.

Kestrel bowed his head and prayed briefly, sincerely, to whatever god handled funerals and forgeries, and got the distinct impression he was being ignored.

* * *

Isolde had attended forty funerals in her twenty-six years — admirals, captains, one disastrous commodore whose eulogy she still privately treasured for the way the priest had described him as "a bold navigator" without once mentioning that he had navigated directly onto the Teeth — and she had never once seen a mourner touch a coffin the way the Eriksen apprentice had touched this one.

Not grief-gripping. Not the desperate clutch of the bereaved. His fingers had rested on the oak lid for precisely one second, flat-palmed, deliberate, the way a surveyor lays a hand on a table to confirm it's level. She had watched him do it from across the grave and felt the same cold, delighted click in her chest that she felt whenever a chart confirmed itself against the water.

Something is in that coffin.

Well. Obviously something was in the coffin; Copplewick was in the coffin, or the substantial portion of Copplewick that remained after the fever. But something else was in there too. Something heavy enough that four bearers had staggered. Something the apprentice needed to confirm the position of, the way a captain confirms a bearing.

She had traced the coffin order two days ago, because tracing things was what she did instead of sleeping. The guild's joiner had been paid unusually well for a plain oak box — double rate, rush job, delivered to the workroom cellar at night. The cellar log showed Eriksen's signature on the receipt. And the funeral had been scheduled, at the family's request, for the latest possible slot on the calendar, with a wake immediately following at the guildhall.

Someone had engineered a perfect window between burial and darkness.

Isolde stood through the graveside rite with her hands folded and her mind running calculations. If she confronted him here, in front of two hundred mourners, she'd get nothing but a stammering apprentice and a scandal she couldn't control. If she reported her suspicion to her father, her father would send soldiers to dig up the coffin, find an atlas — which atlas? the true one or the false one? — and either way the guild would close ranks around its own and the real question, the question of who had sold Karthen the channel depths years ago, would drown in procedure.

No. If you wanted the truth out of a liar, you didn't set a net. You waited until they were alone, mid-crime, holding something they couldn't explain, and then you simply stood there and let them decide what kind of person they were going to be.

Her tutor in intelligence work — a fat, cheerful Karthen defector named Ombry who drank too much and taught brilliantly — had called this "fishing without bait." You don't offer them anything. You just arrive. What they do next tells you everything.

So Isolde left the wake early — pleading a headache, which was even true, in the way most useful lies are anchored to something real — and walked back out through the cemetery gate as the light went long and gold, and found a spot behind the yew trees with a view of the fresh-turned earth, and settled in to wait.

She didn't have to wait long. The sun had barely touched the harbor cranes when a figure in a dark coat came through the gate carrying a shovel with a conspicuously broken handle, walking with the studied casualness of a man who has rehearsed looking casual in front of a mirror and discovered there is no way to do it.

Isolde smiled in the shadows. Then, because fair was fair and because some small traitorous part of her had already decided how this evening ought to go, she stepped out from behind the yews and said, "You'll want the other shovel. That one squeaks."

* * *

To his eternal credit, Kestrel did not scream. He did not drop the shovel, run, or attempt any of the three lies queued at the front of his mind (*checking the drainage*, *lost a glove*, *Copplewick owed me money*). He simply stopped, turned, and looked at Isolde Vane standing in the gold light with her arms folded, and felt something in his chest that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear.

"You followed me," he said.

"I followed the coffin," she said. "You happened to be attached to it."

"That's a very admiral's-daughter distinction."

"It's a very accurate one." She nodded at the grave. The mound of fresh earth looked enormous in the low sun, obscene, a hill where no hill should be. "I assume you're not here to check the drainage."

"I could lie to you," Kestrel said. "I'm told I'm good at it."

"I'm sure you are. I've read your charts." She said it lightly, but the word landed like a hook set in flesh. *Your charts.* She knew. She had known since the map room, since the deliberate error only a trained eye would catch, and she had said nothing, and she was saying nothing now, standing in a cemetery at dusk offering to help him dig up a coffin.

"Why?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why aren't you reporting me? Why aren't there six soldiers behind those trees?"

"Because soldiers ask questions loudly," Isolde said. "And I want answers quietly." She unfolded her arms. Underneath the composure, he saw it — the same thing he'd seen on the breakwater, the barbed, exhausted honesty of a woman who trusted nothing and was tired of it. "Three days ago I caught an error in your coastline. A deliberate one. Only someone fluent in deception draws errors like that, and only someone honest admits them when caught. You haven't admitted anything yet. So we're going to stand here, and you're going to show me what's in that coffin, and then we're each going to decide whether the other one is worth trusting. Provisionally."

"Provisionally," Kestrel repeated.

"I'm not a romantic, Master Eriksen. I'm the admiral's daughter. Trust is a mooring line — you pay it out slowly and you keep a knife near it."

Despite everything — the grave, the shovel, the felony pending, the letter burning a hole through every quiet hour of his life — Kestrel laughed. It startled him, the sound of it, rusty as an unused winch. Isolde almost smiled.

"The handle really does squeak," she added. "There's a spade by the sexton's shed. I checked."

"You *checked*."

"I told you. I trace things instead of sleeping." She started toward the shed, then paused. "Coming? Or would you rather I describe your exhumation to my father in the morning?"

Kestrel looked at the grave. He looked at the sky, going amber at the edges, the harbor bells ringing the sixth hour across the water. Somewhere out past the breakwater, Brannic was waiting. Somewhere beyond the horizon, Aldous was alive, and the atlas in this grave was the only weapon Kestrel had, and he was about to hand knowledge of it to the daughter of the man whose blockade would decide whether Vaelmoor starved.

Every silence he'd ever learned said: *walk away. Say you lost a glove. Go home.*

But the silences had been authored by Aldous, and Aldous was a corpse that walked, and Kestrel was tired — tired in the marrow, tired of armor, tired of being the only person in Vaelmoor who knew how deep the water went.

He picked up the spade.

* * *

Digging up a grave, it turned out, took far less time than digging one, mostly because the fill was loose and the two of them worked in a rhythm neither commented on — Kestrel shoveling, Isolde hauling the spoil aside with the efficiency of someone raised on ships where idle hands got assigned bilge duty. Neither spoke much. The cemetery filled up with the sounds of evening: rooks arguing in the yews, the harbor bells, the long sigh of tide under the seawall.

When the shovel rang against oak, Kestrel climbed down into the grave — undignified, undignity being the least of his concerns — and worked the coffin lid loose along its seal. Inside, beneath the weighted shroud and the sandbag effigy that represented, as far as Vaelmoor would ever know, the mortal remains of Senior Cartographer Copplewick, lay a long oilcloth bundle.

He lifted it out and passed it up. Isolde received it with both hands, surprised by the weight, and knelt to unwrap it right there on the trampled grass.

The true atlas opened under her fingers like a living thing.

Kestrel watched her face while she looked. He had watched provosts look at it, watched patrons look at it, and none of them had this expression — this slow, hungry, reverent fury, as though the maps were simultaneously beautiful and evidence of a crime, which, of course, they were. Her fingers hovered above the channel soundings without touching, the way you hover above something sacred or dangerous.

"These depths," she said quietly. "The Narrows. The Teeth approaches. This is — no one outside the guild has soundings like this. No one *inside* the guild has all of these together." She looked up at him, and her eyes had gone hard. "This is the book that wins the blockade. Or loses it."

"Yes."

"And Aldous Eriksen drew the foundations of it."

"Aldous drew the bones. I finished it." Kestrel crouched beside her. From his coat he took the letter — Brannic's letter, the one he'd burned unread and memorized entirely, recopied in his own hand from memory two nights ago because he could not bear to carry the original and could not bear not to carry *something*. He held it out. "Read this. Then decide what you think of me. Both outcomes are bad, so take your time."

Isolde read. He watched her lips move slightly over the dates, the demands, the signature — *A.*, in a hand she couldn't know but he could feel radiating off the page like heat. When she finished she sat back on her heels and was silent for a long moment.

"He's alive," she said.

"He's alive, and he serves Karthen, and he wants this atlas as payment for buying my indenture when I was ten years old. Which means I have belonged to him my entire life, including the years I spent mourning him." The words came out level, which surprised him; he had expected them to cost more. "Brannic delivers terms in six days. The guild displays the atlas publicly in six days. Your father votes on the blockade in five. And somewhere in the middle of all that, I have to give a dead man his book without letting it arm the fleet that burned our harbor."

Isolde set the letter down on the grass with great care, as if it might go off. Then she laughed — a short, bleak, genuine laugh.

"Do you know what I found in the harbor ledgers last week?" she said. "Channel depths, sent to Karthen years ago. Someone inside your guild fed them our waters, and I narrowed it to three names, and yours was one of them, and I have spent a week deciding whether to hate you." She looked at him across the open atlas, across the open grave, the rooks wheeling black against the amber sky. "And now you sit in a graveyard and show me the truth without being forced, and I don't know whether that makes you the most honest man in Vaelmoor or the best liar it's ever produced."

"Both," Kestrel said. "My master taught me both. That's rather the problem."

They looked at each other. Two liars, kneeling over a stolen book beside a hole full of sandbags, and between them — fragile, untested, mooring line paying out inch by inch — something that was not yet trust but had stopped being suspicion.

"If I help you," Isolde said slowly, "I need to know it isn't for Aldous. Whatever you feel for him — love, debt, grief, all three — I need to know the atlas doesn't end up serving him."

Kestrel thought of the promise on the breakwater. *Finish it, and you're free.* Fifteen years of believing freedom lived at the end of an inking pen.

"It won't serve him," he said. "I don't yet know how to stop it. But it won't serve him. On that, you have my word — and I know exactly what my word is worth, which is why I'm telling you it's provisional."

Isolde held out her hand — inkstained, chapped, the hand of a woman who audited ledgers by candlelight. "Provisional," she agreed.

He shook it. And that was when the sexton's dog began barking somewhere beyond the yew wall, and a lantern swung into view down the gravel path, and a voice called out, cheerfully horrified: "Hello? Hello, who's down there?"

Isolde blew out a breath. "That," she said, "would be the watch. I may have mentioned to a colleague that I'd be walking in the cemetery this evening."

"You *what*—"

"I needed a reason to leave the wake early that my father's men wouldn't question. Unfortunately, reasons have a way of being checked." She was already stuffing the atlas back into its oilcloth, moving fast and calm. "Back exit, past the yews. There's a gap in the wall wide enough for a coffin, which I also checked, because I check everything."

"And the grave?" Kestrel hissed, scrambling up the slope with the bundle under his arm. "We've dug up Copplewick!"

Isolde paused at the top of the rise, lantern-light growing behind her, and gave him a look of such magnificent, unbothered composure that he would remember it for years.

"We'll say ghouls," she said. "Vaelmoor will believe anything about ghouls. Now *run*, Master Eriksen — the atlas and I are getting acquainted properly, and I want to know every error you've ever drawn before the sun comes up."

Behind them the lantern bobbed closer, the watchman shouting now, the rooks exploding up out of the yews in a black storm — and Kestrel ran, laughing breathlessly at the absurdity of it, a fugitive in a cemetery with a dead man's book under his arm and, for the first time in fifteen years, one person in the world who knew the shape of the water he was in.
