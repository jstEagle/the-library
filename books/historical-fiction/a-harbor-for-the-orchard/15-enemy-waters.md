## 15. Enemy Waters

The grain barge smelled of mice and mildew, which Kestrel decided, somewhere past the third hour in the dark of its hold, was preferable to the alternative smells a hold could offer. He sat wedged between sacks of barley that had seen better harvests, with a lantern he did not dare light and a false atlas wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like an infant.

*You are carrying your master's coffin,* he thought, and then corrected himself: *a copy of it.* The true atlas slept in Vaelmoor, hidden where no one would think to look. This one — this beautiful lie — was the child he was delivering to the man who had raised him.

The barge rolled. The barley sighed. Somewhere above, a sailor cursed a rope with impressive fluency.

"You could have told me the passage took two days," whispered Tam, the smuggler who'd arranged all this, from his own nest of sacks nearby. Tam was seventeen, freckled, and possessed of the unshakable cheer of someone who had never yet been hanged.

"It takes two days because the Karthen pickets count every hull twice," Kestrel said. "You knew that when you named your price."

"I named my price before I knew you were going to be *this* cheerful about it." Tam shifted, dislodging a mouse, which expressed its opinion and fled. "Begging pardon, but you don't talk like most folk who pay to be smuggled. Most folk cry or pray. You keep checking corners like you're pricing them."

"I'm a mapmaker."

"Ah." Tam nodded as though this explained everything. In fairness, Kestrel reflected, it usually did.

He closed his eyes and tried not to do what he had been doing for three days, which was rehearsing the reunion. Fifteen years. He had been ten years old and drowning-adjacent himself, half-starved on the guild steps, when Aldous Renner had looked at him and said, *This one has steady hands*, and that sentence had become the architecture of his entire life. He had imagined the meeting a hundred ways since Brannic's letter: shouting, weeping, striking the old man across the face, falling into his arms. In every version, Aldous said something that made sense of it. In every version, there was a reason. There had always been a reason. That was the trouble with masters — they taught you to believe in reasons.

Now he only wanted the truth, and he was afraid of it the way other men feared deep water.

Sleep came anyway, eventually, the way it does in holds and churches — unwanted but persuasive.

* * *

They put him ashore at dawn on a shingle beach below cliffs the color of wet slate, in a cove so small it appeared on exactly one map in the world — the true atlas, plate forty-one, *Cove of the Gulls, unsuitable for anchorage, good for nothing but secrets*. Aldous had surveyed it thirty years ago. Of course he had. The man had probably charted his own treason before Kestrel was born.

Brannic was waiting, arms crossed, looking like a fence post that had learned to disapprove.

"You came," Brannic said.

"You threatened me."

"Aye. It saves time." He studied Kestrel with those river-gray eyes. "You look like him, you know. When he was young. Same way of standing like the ground owes you money."

"Where is he?"

"Skerry Light." Brannic turned and started up the cliff path without waiting, which Kestrel was beginning to understand was simply how the man moved through life. "There's things you should hear before you see him. Things I'd want heard, if I were you."

"I've heard enough from you."

"No," Brannic said, not turning around. "You've heard what I chose to tell you. There's a difference, and it's the width of a grave." He climbed in silence for a while, boots finding purchase on rock worn smooth by centuries of gulls. Then: "He talks about you. On the ship. Did you know that?"

Kestrel said nothing. Silence was armor; Aldous had taught him that too.

"Tells the navigators about his apprentice. *Best hands I ever trained.* Shows them the harbor charts you finished, the ones they stole copies of, and points out your linework like a proud father at a fair." Brannic reached the top of the path and paused, breathing hard, though whether from the climb or the words Kestrel couldn't tell. "It's not an act. That's the worst of it, boy. I've watched men lie their whole lives and I can tell. When he speaks of you, he means it."

"And when he burned our fleet?"

Brannic didn't flinch. "He means that too."

* * *

Skerry Light had been built to warn ships away from the Teeth shoals and had been decommissioned once better lighthouses made it redundant — a tower whose entire purpose was obsolete, left standing out of habit. Kestrel thought there was probably a lesson in that, and declined to learn it.

Aldous Renner stood at the lamp room's rail, looking out at the gray water, and for one absurd moment Kestrel's heart performed the treachery of leaping — *Master* — before fifteen years and a burned fleet crashed back down upon it like ballast.

He was older. Of course. His hair had gone white at the temples in a way that looked deliberate, as if he'd ordered it from a barber. His shoulders had thinned. But his hands, resting on the rail, were the same hands Kestrel had spent a childhood watching — long-fingered, precise, ink-stained at the cuticles no matter how often he scrubbed. A cartographer's hands. A liar's hands. The same thing, possibly.

"Kestrel." Aldous turned, and his face did something complicated — joy and calculation arriving at the same expression, indistinguishable, which was perhaps the whole tragedy in miniature. "You got my letter."

"I burned your letter."

"Yes." Aldous smiled, and it was the smile from a hundred workroom evenings, warm as lamplight. "But you memorized it first. I know how you read."

The tenderness in it was unbearable. Kestrel gripped the rail because his legs had opinions about standing unsupported. "Fifteen years," he managed. "I buried you. I grieved you. I finished the atlas because you died for it — that's what everyone said, that you drowned rather than let them take it—"

"I did drown." Aldous said it mildly, as if correcting a student's compass reading. "The man who served the guild drowned. Very thoroughly. They even gave him a nice funeral — did you attend? Good turnout, I hope."

"You let us think—"

"I let you think what kept you alive." And there it was, the old voice sliding into place, the lecture voice, patient and terrible. "Kestrel. Sit. No — stand, if you prefer, you always preferred standing when you were frightened. Look at me. When the purge came, when Halloway's predecessors started selling charts to anyone with coin and the council began talking about blockades and starvations, I had two choices. Stay and be used, or die and be free. I chose the second death. It's the more comfortable kind."

"The fishing quarter burned."

Something flickered behind Aldous's eyes — weather passing over water — and was gone. "Wars burn things. I've never pretended otherwise."

"No," Kestrel said. "You've just never been in anything that burns."

The silence stretched. Below them the sea worried the rocks with the patience of something that intended to win eventually.

Then Aldous laughed — actually laughed, short and rueful — and crossed to him, and before Kestrel could decide whether to step back, the old man took his chin in those ink-stained fingers and tilted his face to the light, the way he used to check a pupil's work against the window.

"Steady hands," Aldous murmured. "I said it when you were ten and starving on my doorstep, and I say it now. Let me see them."

Dumbly, Kestrel held them out. Aldous examined them palm and knuckle as a buyer examines horseflesh, turning them over, pressing the calluses. "Still inking plates yourself. Still cutting your own nibs. I can tell by the middle finger — there's a groove there, see, from the pen barrel. I have the same one." He held up his own hand, and yes, the same groove, the same scar across the thumb from a slipped graver in some year before Kestrel existed. "Do you know what that is, boy? That's lineage. That's the only immortality a craftsman gets. Not in stone. In hands."

And God help him, Kestrel felt warmth rise in his chest like floodwater, and hated it, and could not stop it. This was the weapon, he understood suddenly. Not threats. Not Brannic's blackmail. This — the old man's love, genuine, bottomless, deployed with the precision of a sounding line. Aldous had measured him for fifteen years from a distance and now knew exactly where to strike.

"You're proud of me," Kestrel said quietly. "After everything. You're actually proud."

"Immensely."

"So give me a reason. Tell me why the atlas matters enough for all of it — the fake death, the Karthen service, the fleet. Make me understand, Master. One last lesson."

Aldous released his hand and walked back to the rail, and when he spoke again the warmth was still there but banked now, controlled, a lamp behind glass. "Because maps are the only honest weapons in the world. Swords lie about glory. Coins lie about value. Priests lie about everything. But a chart — a chart either grounds your ship or it doesn't. Truth with teeth. I serve the side that understands that, and the guild serves the side that sells truth by the pound to whoever knocks. You've seen inside Halloway's ledgers, haven't you? Don't answer. Your face answers."

"My face is none of yours anymore."

"It's entirely mine," Aldous said gently. "I wrote it. Every steadiness, every silence. Which brings us—" and here he turned, briskly, terribly, the shopkeeper emerging from the father, "—to business."

The oilcloth parcel seemed suddenly very heavy against Kestrel's chest.

"The Vaelmoor Atlas," Aldous said. "Complete. Corrected. In your own hand, which makes it worth double, because your linework has surpassed mine and I include that in my calculations without sentiment. My employers want it. I promised it to them. And here is what you will find remarkable, Kestrel — I promised it to them as *yours to give*. I told them the apprentice would deliver it freely, out of loyalty. They didn't believe me. Nobody believes in loyalty anymore; it's the great casualty of my profession. So I'm obliged to add incentives."

"Incentives." The word tasted like bilge water.

"The fishing quarter," Aldous said, with the same tone he'd once used to introduce a new plate technique — *now, watch the stippling, this is the part that separates artists from clerks*. "Four thousand souls, Kestrel, most of whom know your name, several of whom mended your boots. If the atlas reaches my admirals by the blockade date, Karthen's fleet needs no landing parties in Vaelmoor proper. The city yields; nobody burns. If it doesn't reach them, they assume the guild intends resistance, and doctrine says make an example. Salt the harbor quarter. It's ugly arithmetic. I didn't write it. I merely refuse to pretend I can't read it."

"You'd burn children for a book."

"I'd burn children for *nothing*," Aldous snapped, and for one instant the mask cracked and Kestrel saw beneath it — not a monster, worse, a tired old man doing sums that never came out clean. "That is precisely my point, and precisely why I left. Because staying meant becoming Halloway, smiling while the knives come out, selling chart access to whichever faction pays, and calling it survival. At least out here I choose my arithmetic honestly. Now." He extended his hand, palm up. Open. Patient. The hand that had pulled a starving boy off the guild steps. "The atlas. Please."

Kestrel's heart was slamming. His hands, damn them, his traitor steady hands, were already moving toward the oilcloth knot — muscle memory, obedience older than thought — and he stopped them by main force, an inch from the parcel, trembling visibly for the first time in fifteen years.

Aldous watched the tremor with an expression of profound, scholarly interest. Like a man observing a rare bird.

"You're frightened," Aldous observed. "Good. Fear means you understand the stakes. But look at me, boy, and hear this: whatever you're thinking of doing — some cleverness, some substitution, some trick you believe I won't see — remember that I taught you every trick you know. And I taught you the ones you don't know you know."

"That's the problem, Master." Kestrel heard his own voice go quiet, level, and realized distantly that he sounded exactly like Aldous, that the voice had been installed years ago along with everything else. "You taught me too well. Including the lessons you didn't mean to teach."

For a moment the old man's composure flickered — a single frame of something almost like worry.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

Kestrel lifted the oilcloth parcel and held it, balanced on both palms, an offering and a question mark.

"Fifteen years ago you promised me freedom when the atlas was finished," he said. "I've come to deliver it. Exactly as promised."

And he stepped forward to place it in his master's waiting hands — while beneath the wrapping, under the first leaf, in ink that would not show itself until Aldous was far past the point of return, waited a coastline drawn in a hand indistinguishable from his master's own.

The sea below the tower went on worrying the rocks, patient, certain, keeping count.
