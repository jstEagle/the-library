## 16. Half the Truth

The cabin smelled of Aldous.

That was the first cruelty of the night. Fifteen years, a faked drowning, an enemy navy — and still the man kept his ink pots arranged in the same order: black, sepia, blue-black, the red locked away in the little brass box because red was for corrections and corrections were for failures. Kestrel had learned that order at ten years old, kneeling on a stool too short for him, and here it was on a Karthen frigate, unchanged, like finding your childhood home rebuilt on the deck of a wolf.

He sat at his master's desk in his master's chair and told himself, firmly, that this did not make him his master. The chair creaked the same way it always had. He chose to ignore it.

Outside, the ship breathed. Timbers ticked against the swell, boots crossed the deck above in patient patterns, and somewhere aft a man sang very badly about a girl from the northern isles who had apparently been unkind to him in ways that took eleven verses to enumerate. Kestrel had been aboard since dusk, smuggled into the chartroom as "the cartographer's assistant," which was true in the way that a knife is true. Brannic had stationed himself outside the door with the expression of a man guarding something he already regretted.

"You have until the middle watch," Brannic had said. "After that, Aldous comes down to check your work."

"Then I'll be finished by then," Kestrel said.

Brannic had looked at him for a long moment — that scarred face doing its slow arithmetic — and said, "Aye. That's what worries me."

* * *

The problem, laid out plainly, was this: he had to draw a lie that would survive three examinations.

First, Aldous would examine it. Aldous, who had taught him that a coastline was not drawn but *argued*, stroke by stroke, and who could read another mapmaker's certainty the way a priest reads sin. Second, the Karthen admiralty would examine it — captains and sailing masters who knew these waters by reputation if not by keel, men looking for anything that flattered their hopes too much. Third — and this was the examination no one else knew about — Isolde Vane would eventually see it, because Isolde saw everything, and if she caught him at forgery before the fleet sailed, the whole design collapsed into simple treason instead of complicated salvation.

So the atlas could not be wrong. That was the trap of it. A false chart was easy; any fool could move a shoal or deepen a channel, and any competent sailing master would smell the flattery. No — the chart had to be *true*. True in every line, every sounding, every bearing, every note in the margins. True everywhere except in one place, one choice, one small hinge on which a fleet would swing.

The Teeth.

The shoals ran off the eastern approach like a jaw of broken stone, half-submerged, shifting with the winter storms, marked on the true atlas with the careful hatching Kestrel had spent two years perfecting. Every pilot in Vaelmoor gave them a wide berth out of respect and several out of personal experience. The Karthen fleet, coming down from the north in darkness, would need either to swing far east around them — costing a day, alerting the harbor — or thread the inner passage that only Vaelmoor pilots used, and only in daylight, and only when feeling brave.

Kestrel's task was to make the inner passage look passable at night.

Not safe. Never safe. Aldous would never believe a passage described as safe. It had to look *navigable* — the word itself a kind of dare. Sounding marks showing eight fathoms where there were five. The hatching pulled back just enough, the margin note adjusted from *breaks in any sea* to *breaks in heavy weather*. Small things. Things a tired sailing master, hurrying, trusting, would read once and carry forward in his head as room enough.

The difference between five fathoms and eight was the width of a hair on paper. On water it was the width of a hull.

Kestrel dipped his pen, and his hand was steady, and he hated how steady it was.

* * *

He worked the way Aldous had taught him, which was to say he worked dishonestly about honesty. You never began with the lie. You began with truth, generous and thorough, so much truth that the examiner drowned in it — soundings by the dozen, tidal notes, anchorages, the little marginal sketches of church spires and headland trees that pilots used for bearings. Truth was the varnish. You laid it on thick enough and the eye stopped looking for the flaw underneath.

By midnight he had completed the approaches, the outer roads, the harbor plan itself — all of it faithful, all of it beautiful, and each faithful line a small betrayal of the fishing boats that would sail over those same depths come morning. He paused over the harbor plan and thought of Marta Ostrey's little pink-cheeked boat, the *Dovetail*, and drew her mooring buoy exactly where it belonged, because even a liar owed some debts in full.

Gentle humor, he reminded himself, was how you stayed sane. He allowed himself one: in the margin beside the naval anchorage, where tradition demanded a note on holding ground, he wrote *good mud, holds well* — which was true of the anchorage and, he felt, also of himself lately.

The ship rolled. The lamp swung on its gimbals and threw his shadow across the charts like a second man leaning over his shoulder.

"You'd have checked my tide tables," he said aloud to the empty cabin. "You'd have found the error in the spring rates and made me do the whole sheet again." His voice sounded strange in Aldous's territory. "You'd have been right, too. They're half a knot off. I know. I'm leaving them."

Because that was the final craft of it: the lie needed one discoverable flaw. Not in the Teeth — never there — but somewhere harmless, somewhere that would satisfy any future auditor who went hunting for tampering. Let them find the tide error years from now and conclude the whole atlas was merely imperfect, merely human, merely the work of a man who miscalculated. A forgery that confessed to a small sin was never accused of the large one. Aldous had taught him that too, though he'd taught it as a lesson about humility. Everything Aldous taught came double-shelled: the lesson, and the thing inside the lesson.

Kestrel had spent fifteen years cracking the shells. He was getting faster at it. That frightened him more than the gallows did.

* * *

Somewhere past the middle of the night watch, the bad singer above finally lost his audience, and the quiet that followed was worse. In the quiet, Kestrel's hands remembered things he didn't ask them to.

They remembered the purge year, when he was ten, and Aldous's hand pressing his shoulder flat behind the drying racks while guild officers tore the workroom apart. *Say nothing. Not now, not ever. Which silences keep people safe?* They remembered being fifteen, on the breakwater, salt wind, the unfinished atlas between them like a third person. *Finish it, and you're free.* They remembered learning that freedom, as Aldous priced it, meant belonging to Aldous.

And they remembered — this was the worst, this came back without permission — they remembered Aldous teaching him to forge signatures. Not as crime. As *study*. "To copy a hand truly, boy, you must love it a little. Find the rhythm. Everyone's name is a piece of music they play the same way every day. Now — mine. Play it."

Kestrel set down the pen and flexed his fingers.

Then he took a fresh sheet, and in the lamplight, he played Aldous's music.

It came back to his hand the way a song comes back to a throat you thought had forgotten it. The forward lean of the capital letters, like a man hurrying toward something. The peculiar flat-topped *d*. The way the descenders hooked left, impatient. And the tremor — Aldous had developed a fine tremor in his last Vaelmoor years, a shiver in the long strokes that he'd disguised as style, and Kestrel reproduced it faithfully, because the Karthen admiralty had samples of Aldous's hand going back years, and a signature without the tremor would raise questions.

He was forging his master's hand so well that for a moment, watching the letters bloom onto the page, Kestrel felt the cabin fill with the man — felt him standing at his shoulder the way he had stood a thousand evenings, reading, weighing, approving. *Good,* the ghost said. *Clean work. You were always best when it mattered.*

"That's the trouble," Kestrel told the ghost quietly. "I am."

He signed the certification sheet. *Surveyed and verified, Aldous Crane, Master Cartographer, Guild of Vaelmoor.* Then, beneath it, in smaller script, the attribution that would make the gift complete: *Plates executed under my direction by my apprentice.*

His own name, written in Aldous's hand. He looked at it a long time. It was the most honest forgery he'd ever done — because in every way that mattered, the hand that had made this atlas *was* Aldous's. He had simply finally admitted whose fingers he'd been borrowing all his life.

* * *

The Teeth took him until nearly dawn.

He saved them for last, the way you save the blow you don't want to give. He laid the true atlas open beside the new one — Brannic had brought both, chained together like prisoners — and worked between them, translating truth into almost-truth, checking each alteration against three sources: the original survey notes, the fishermen's soundings he'd collected himself over two years, and his own memory of the water, which was the deepest archive of the three.

Five fathoms became eight. The hatching retreated. *Breaks in any sea* became *breaks in heavy weather*. He added a note in Aldous's voice — confident, experienced, faintly bored: *Inner passage used regularly by local pilots; adequate by night with care.* Adequate by night with care. Seven words that would read, to a sailing master in a crowded wardroom, as an invitation. He knew how men read maps under pressure. They read what they hoped.

When it was done he sat back and looked at the two atlases side by side, and they were twins, and only he could tell which twin would kill.

There should have been a moment. Some cold clarity, some clean hatred, some sense of the scales balancing — the fishing quarter spared, the fleet punished, justice served with a draftsman's precision. Instead what he felt was simpler and sicker: competence. He had done good work. He had done the best work of his life, and it was a weapon, and part of him — the part Aldous had built, the part that loved the craft apart from any use it was put to — part of him was *proud*.

That was what it cost, he understood now, sitting in the grey before dawn with ink dried into the creases of his knuckles. Not the gallows-risk. Not the betrayal. The cost was that you became, briefly and perhaps permanently, the thing the work required. To forge a master you loved him a little. To build a trap you admired the mechanism. There was no drawing a killing map with shaking hands and a closed heart; the water deserved better than that, even the water meant to swallow men.

*I'm becoming you,* he thought at the absent Aldous. *I hate it. I'll stop the moment it stops working. But tonight, tonight I'm the best version of you that ever existed, and I hope it chokes you.*

Above, the bell struck the turn of the watch. Boots on the companionway — unhurried, familiar, a man who had never hurried anywhere in his life because hurry implied the world might surprise him.

Aldous knocked, which was somehow worse than not knocking.

"Enter," Kestrel said, and moved the true atlas beneath the blank charts, and put on his face — the apprentice's face, open, tired, a little awed — the last mask in his kit, and the oldest.

The door opened. His master filled it, greyer, broader, wearing a Karthen uniform like it had grown there, and his eyes went past Kestrel immediately to the desk, to the finished plates lying in their neat fan, and lit up with a pride so genuine, so warm, so exactly the pride Kestrel had chased for fifteen years like a fish chasing a lure, that Kestrel felt it land in his chest like a hook.

"Adequate by night with care," Aldous read aloud, softly, tracing the air above the line without touching it. "Yes. Yes, that's the phrasing. That's how you say it so they hear *safe*." He turned, and his smile was the breakwater smile, the promise smile. "You've surpassed me, boy. Come up on deck. The admiral wants to meet the man who's going to win him a harbor."

Behind his borrowed face, Kestrel smiled, and signed nothing, and followed his master up into the dark — knowing that somewhere south, in a council chamber in Vaelmoor, Isolde Vane was at this hour fighting for the vote that would decide whether his poisoned gift would ever reach water deep enough to kill.
