## 17. The Blockade Vote

Isolde Vane had dressed for battle the way her father had taught her to dress for it: plainly enough that no one could accuse her of theater, finely enough that no one could forget whose daughter stood before them. The grey wool was her mother's, cut down. The only ornament she wore was the harbor seal of Vaelmoor on a plain chain — and beneath her cuff, folded small enough to hide against her wrist, the ledger page that would end her family.

She had not slept. She had not needed to. For six days she had sat in her father's study with three years of harbor ledgers spread across the floor like a tide chart of rot, and somewhere around the fourth night the numbers had stopped being numbers and started being faces: the fisher children of the Salt Quarter, the ones who waved at the grain barges, the ones her father's blockade would teach what hunger meant before it taught the Karthens anything at all.

Her father believed the blockade was mercy. That was the terrible part. Admiral Edran Vane had spent thirty years learning to count lives the way a gunner counts powder, and he had concluded — sincerely, honorably, with all the arithmetic he possessed — that starving a city for one winter was kinder than burning it for three days. He was not wrong by his own lights. He was simply working from charts drawn by men who had been paid to draw them crooked.

Isolde intended to give him better charts.

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and cold stone, as it always did, and the nine seats of the Harbor Council were filling slowly, the way power always moved in Vaelmoor — never hurrying, because hurrying admitted urgency, and admitting urgency admitted fear. Provost Halloway arrived last, as was his right and his habit, robed in guild blue, carrying the atlas itself under his arm like a christening gift. He set it on the presentation table with a small, satisfied pat, the way a man pats a horse he has already sold twice.

"Provost," Isolde said, from the petitioners' floor.

"Daughter Vane." Halloway's smile was a masterpiece of guild accounting: warmth entered on the left side, nothing payable on the right. "We're honored. I understand you've requested the floor before the vote."

"I have."

"Then the council will hear you." He turned to the benches with the air of a man granting a child an extra sweet. "Though I'll remind the members that the atlas before you has been verified by three master cartographers and countersigned by the Admiralty's own surveyor. Whatever concerns may be raised today—" a gracious pause, a small shrug, the shrug of a man who has already counted the votes twice, "—I trust they will be *navigational* concerns."

Laughter, polite and dry, from two of the nine seats. Isolde watched it ripple out and die against Councilor Maren's stony face — Maren, who represented the fishing quarter, who had eaten nothing but bread and anger since the blockade proposal first crossed the table. Good. Maren was hers. She needed four more.

She stepped forward into the center of the chamber, where the morning light came down through the high salt-fogged windows and made everything look briefly honest, and she began the way her father had taught her every briefing should begin.

"Councilors. Six weeks ago, I audited the guild's coastal survey in preparation for this vote. I found an error in the Vaelmoor Atlas — a deliberate error, a half-fathom shaved off the Sable Channel soundings, placed precisely where a Karthen pilot would need depth most. I reported it quietly. I was thanked. The error was corrected." She let that sit. "What I did not report is that the error was not new. It was old. It had been in guild charts for eleven years. And last night, I finished finding out why."

Halloway's smile did not move. His hands did. A small adjustment of the atlas on the table, a quarter-turn, squaring it to the chamber. She catalogued it and went on.

* * *

She took them through it the way Aldous Eriksen — though she did not yet know that name — must once have taken a student through a coastline: feature by feature, danger by danger, each one sounding exactly like the last until the cumulative weight became undeniable.

"The guild maintains two ledgers for channel access," she said. "The public tariff book, which any member may consult. And a private fee schedule, kept in the provost's office, which I have photographed here—" she held up the page, and heard the small collective intake of breath that meant nine people had simultaneously realized what a photograph of a ledger implied about how she had obtained it, "—which records payments from three parties over five years. The Karthen consulate. The smuggling consortium operating out of Gullmark. And—" her voice did not shake; she had rehearsed until it couldn't, "—the office of Admiral Edran Vane, my father, who purchased advance copies of every revised chart so that his blockades would be planned on data the fishing fleet never received."

"That," said Councilor Pell, the admiralty's creature, "is standard military intelligence practice—"

"It is," Isolde agreed. "That is not the crime. The crime is this." She laid the second document on the rail. "Cross-check the private fee schedule against the guild's delivery logs, and you find that the Karthen consulate was not buying copies. It was buying *corrections*. Eleven years ago, someone inside the guild sold the true depths of the Teeth approaches and the Sable Channel to the Karthen navy — before our own patrol boats had them. The error I found in the atlas wasn't sloppiness. It was a back door. Whoever holds the key decides which navy runs aground and which one sails home."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Outside, faintly, a fish-gull screamed over the tide gate, and Isolde thought, absurdly, of a workroom full of ink and lamplight, of a young man with careful hands and a tremor he thought no one saw.

"And the provost?" asked Councilor Maren, giving her the opening like a hand up onto a wet ledge.

"The fee schedule is initialed. The initials are Halloway's. The payments route through a counting house in Gullmark under a shipping name — *Orchard & Tide* — which, when traced, owns a warehouse containing eleven years of guild correspondence, including a letter offering the Sable Channel corrections to both navies, priced separately, whichever paid first." She looked directly at Halloway then, and let him see that she knew, and let him see that knowing had cost her something too. "Provost. Would you like to tell the council what Orchard & Tide is?"

For a long moment Halloway simply looked at her, and Isolde understood that behind those mild eyes a final piece of arithmetic was running — votes, witnesses, exits — and arriving, with a soft guild-bell chime, at zero.

"What I'd like to tell the council," Halloway said pleasantly, "is that this young woman has spent a week alone with stolen documents and a grievance, and that grief makes ingenious forgers of us all. Those initials could be anyone's. That warehouse could be anyone's. And the admiral's daughter accusing the guild of corruption the week before a blockade vote — well." He spread his hands, wounded and reasonable. "You can weigh the timing yourselves."

"I can," said a voice from the doorway, "and I have."

Isolde turned, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because standing in the chamber door, still in his oilskins, salt-crusted and unshaven, was her father.

* * *

Admiral Edran Vane had commanded the harbor fleet for nineteen years, and in all that time Isolde had seen him enter perhaps forty rooms, and he had entered every one of them the same way: as though the room had been waiting for him and ought to be grateful the wait was over. He entered this one differently. He entered it like a man walking out onto ice he had personally tested and did not entirely trust.

"Councilors." He did not look at his daughter. He looked at the ledger page in her hand as if it were a torpedo he had defused with his own fingers. "Yesterday evening, my daughter presented me with her findings. Including the payments routed through my own office." A muscle worked in his jaw. "Payments I authorized. To purchase chart access I believed legitimate. I am here to say three things."

He held up one finger.

"First: the blockade as proposed is built on corrupted data, and a commander who fights on corrupted data kills his own sailors. I withdraw my endorsement of the starvation provision. Full blockade, open grain lanes — the fishing quarter eats, or there is no vote worth having." Murmurs. Pell's face went the color of drowned things.

Second finger.

"Second: I am placing myself under council review for the payments made through my office. If I was duped, I was duped carelessly, and carelessness at my rank is a chargeable offense. If I was not duped—" his voice roughened, and for one unguarded second he looked at Isolde, and it was the look from when she was seven and had fallen into the tidal pool, terror and pride fighting for the same face, "—then my daughter will have done her duty better than her father did, and I will sign whatever judgment follows."

Third finger. He almost didn't raise it. Isolde watched him decide to.

"Third. There is a man in this city who has known for years what the guild was selling and to whom. He came to my survey office six days ago with proof I dismissed because it was inconvenient." Now he did look at her, straight on. "He was right. And I owe him an apology I intend to deliver publicly, after this vote, whatever it is."

*Who*, Isolde wanted to demand, later, urgently, over and over — but the chamber was moving now, the way water moves when the tide gate opens, and there was no holding it back.

Councilor Maren rose. "Then let us vote on the amended order. Blockade of war materials and naval traffic. Grain lanes open under joint inspection. Guild chart access suspended pending investigation." A pause, and then, with the particular relish of a fisherman watching a net close: "And the arrest of Provost Halloway for inquiry into the matter of Orchard & Tide."

"I move the question," said someone. Others said it. The chamber bell rang the division, and Isolde stood in the falling light and listened to her father's career end by a count of six to three, and felt — not triumph. Nothing like triumph. Something colder and cleaner, like the first breath after surfacing too deep for too long.

Halloway did not run. That was somehow the worst of it. As the council sergeants crossed the floor toward him, he straightened his robes, squared the atlas one final quarter-turn on the table — a habit, Isolde realized, a bookkeeper's tic, a man aligning columns even in ruin — and paused beside her on his way to the door.

"You understand," he said, quietly, conversationally, the way he might discuss freight rates, "that whoever sold those channels eleven years ago is still out there. Halloway was the till. Not the current. You've caught the man who kept the books, Daughter Vane. The man who wrote them is holding your atlas in his hands within the week." His smile, going out the door between two sergeants, was almost kind. "Do enjoy the victory. They age quickly."

* * *

By evening the city knew. Of course it knew; Vaelmoor had maybe eleven thousand souls and a shared circulatory system of gossip that put the tide itself to shame. By evening the fish-market wives were telling the story with Halloway arrested mid-sentence and the admiral weeping openly (he had not; his voice had cracked once, which in Vane terms was a full operatic collapse), and by nightfall the story had grown a third act, because a third act was needed:

The atlas.

It sat now under council seal in the vault below the chamber, but that changed nothing, because everyone understood what it actually was. The Karthens wanted it. The Admiralty wanted it. Half the council wanted to burn it and the other half wanted to sell it, and every faction in the city had begun, with the unconscious coordination of ants, maneuvering toward the moment of its delivery — which, per the treaty clock nobody could stop, fell in four days.

Isolde stood on the breakwater near midnight, past curfew, past caring, with the wind pulling her hair loose and the harbor lamps smearing themselves across black water. Her name was ruined in certain drawing rooms already; she could feel it happening at a distance, the way you feel weather coming in a scar. *The admiral's daughter who broke her father.* Let them say it. Her father had shaken her hand on the chamber steps afterward, formally, in front of everyone, and said only, "Your mother would have voted the same way," and gone to draft his resignation, and that was worth every drawing room on the coast.

But Halloway's last words would not leave her.

*The man who wrote them.*

She had three suspects for the original betrayal, narrowed over six sleepless nights: a dead master cartographer named Aldous, conveniently drowned three years ago and therefore perfectly safe to suspect; a guild copyist named Kestrel Eriksen, who had altered a coastline once in ways only a trained eye caught, and who had appeared at her father's survey office six days ago with documents he refused to explain; and a third name, a broker's name, that led nowhere except to a warehouse and a locked door.

Aldous was dead. Dead men didn't buy warehouses.

Which left a quiet young man with careful hands, who had stood in front of her a week ago looking at her the way one liar recognizes another across a crowded room — and who had, she now understood, walked into the lion's den of the Admiralty voluntarily, carrying the very proof that had undone her father's blockade and saved the fishing quarter, asking nothing.

Nobody asked nothing.

She looked out at the dark water, at the far line where the Karthen fleet waited beyond sight, and at the nearer darkness of the guild workrooms where a single lamp, she would have sworn, was still burning.

"Whatever you're doing, mapmaker," she said to the wind, "you have four days. And this time, you don't do it alone."

She turned up her collar and went to find him.
