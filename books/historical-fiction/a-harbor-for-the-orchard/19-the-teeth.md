## 19. The Teeth

Brannic had spent thirty years learning to read water the way priests read scripture, and what he read that night told him they were all going to die.

He stood at the rail of the *Vow of Karth*, third ship in the vanguard line, and watched the phosphorescence bloom green along the bow wave. In Vaelmoor waters, that glow came up strange in autumn — the fisher children called it *the harbor's breath* — and where it burned brightest, the seabed rose to meet you. He'd smuggled grain past these shoals a hundred times. He knew them like his own scars.

The fleet did not.

Behind him, the lantern discipline was perfect. No lights, muffled oars, sails double-reefed. Admiral Vosk's orders, executed with Karthen precision. They would come into the harbor roads before dawn and present the city with a choice: surrender the guild's charts and pay tribute, or watch the fishing quarter burn as an appetizer. Clean. Efficient. The kind of plan Aldous drew on paper with that serene little smile he wore when other men's futures were under discussion.

Brannic spat over the side.

Thirty years of complicity, he thought. Thirty years of carrying letters, moving crates, holding lamps while other men wrote down things that got people drowned. He'd told himself it was just work. A man needed coin; a man took the jobs available; the sea didn't care whose flag flew over the warehouse. It had been easy to tell himself that, right up until it wasn't.

"Course holds?" The voice came soft from behind him. Aldous, wrapped in a boat cloak against the chill, looking for all the world like a kindly uncle out for evening air.

"Holds," Brannic said.

"And the boy's atlas?"

Aldous smiled. Brannic had seen that smile aimed at drowning men once, years ago, and had never quite managed to forget it. "Accurate. Every fathom. I checked it myself against my old surveys."

"You checked a dead man's memory against paper," Brannic said. "That's a strange way to spend an evening."

"It's the only way I've ever spent anything." Aldous settled at the rail beside him, comfortable as a cat on a warm hearth. "You're troubled, old friend."

"I'm cold," Brannic said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Aldous agreed pleasantly. "Cold passes."

They stood together in silence after that, which was its own kind of conversation. Brannic had known Aldous since before the fire, since the years when the mapmaker's treason had still been theoretical — payments for soundings, favors for favors, nothing a man couldn't shrug off by morning. He had rowed the escape boat the night the harbor fleet burned. He had held the lantern while Aldous wept, or performed weeping — even then Brannic hadn't been sure which — and said, *It's done, it's done, no one followed.*

No one had. That was the trouble with being good at your sins.

Now the fishing quarter lay ahead in the dark, a scatter of low lights along the breakwater, the smell of woodsmoke and tar reaching even this far offshore. Brannic found his eyes going to it despite himself. There'd be boats tied up three deep at the tide gate. Nets drying on racks. Children asleep in lofts above their fathers' tackle, the whole quarter breathing slow and trusting, because everyone knew the blockade vote had gone against the fleet action, everyone knew there'd be warning, everyone knew—

Everyone knew what men like him let them know.

"The Teeth are south of our track," Aldous said mildly, watching him watch the shore. "If that's what's chewing at you."

"I know where the Teeth are."

"Of course you do. You drew half those soundings yourself." Aldous chuckled. "Which is why the boy's atlas is such a marvel. He corrected your errors. All of them. Even the ones you made on purpose back when you were selling depth-charges to the harbor trust." He patted Brannic's shoulder with two fingers, light as a bird landing. "Rest, old friend. Dawn comes early, and history doesn't wait."

He went below. Brannic stayed at the rail and did arithmetic.

The vanguard ran five ships: the *Vow*, the *Iron Meridian*, the *Sable Coast*, the *Kestrel's Luck* — someone aboard had a sense of humor — and the lead galley *Ossuary*, which was not a name that inspired confidence in anyone with imagination. Behind them, four miles back, the main body: eleven more hulls riding at slow sail. If the vanguard grounded on the Teeth, the main body would have hours to react. Hours in which a man could put a small boat over the side. Hours in which a fishing quarter could be warned, nets cut, children loaded onto anything that floated.

Hours in which a man might finally stop being what he'd been for thirty years.

Brannic looked at the green fire burning along the hull and thought about Kestrel Eriksen. The boy had handed over the atlas with steady hands and a face like a locked door. Too steady. Brannic had carried enough false papers in his life to know what honest fear looked like, and the boy hadn't shown any. He'd shown something worse: patience. The patience of a man who has already done whatever he intends to do and is simply waiting for the world to catch up.

*He corrected your errors,* Aldous had said. *All of them.*

All of them. Brannic turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. A mapmaker who corrected every error — including the deliberate ones — wasn't making an accurate chart. He was making a *perfect* chart, the kind of flawless document that makes a navigator stop checking, stop sounding, stop doubting. Trust the atlas. The atlas is gospel. The atlas was drawn by a dead master's grieving apprentice who loved him enough to finish his life's work.

And then there was the signature. Brannic had seen it under the lantern in Aldous's cabin: *Aldous Crane*, in Aldous's own hand, dated from beyond the grave. The boy had forged his master's signature well enough to fool the man himself. Which meant the boy had spent years studying that hand. Which meant the boy could have written anything he wanted in that atlas, anywhere, and signed it with a ghost's blessing.

*Or,* said a small voice in Brannic's head, the one that had kept him alive through thirty years of dirty work, *you're flattering yourself. Maybe the chart is true. Maybe the boy is just a boy. Maybe you'll ground us all on nothing but your conscience catching up with you at last.*

Maybe. But the green fire was burning bright off the starboard bow, brighter than it should, and the lead pilot — a Karthen man reading a chart instead of the water — was calling depth figures that didn't match the taste of the current coming over the rails. Brannic had tasted these currents since he was nine years old. They were wrong. Shallow-water current, shoal current, the sea pushing back against a bottom rising faster than any chart admitted.

The Teeth had moved. Shoals always moved; that was the first thing the harbor taught you and the last thing empires learned. And if the boy had drawn them where they used to be — where every old survey, including Aldous's, swore they were — then the chart was perfect and the sea was lying and the *Vow of Karth* had maybe two hours left as a floating object.

Brannic made his decision the way he made most decisions: quietly, and without asking permission.

* * *

He went down the ladder like a man checking rigging, unhurried, nodding to the watch. His sea chest sat where it always sat, and inside it, beneath spare shirts and a coil of good line, lay the things a careful man kept ready: a shuttered lantern, a signal mirror, and three pounds of dry powder wrapped in oilcloth. Not enough to sink anything. Enough to make noise. Noise, on a night like this, was worth more than powder.

His boat was the second gig aft. He'd greased the davit blocks himself three days ago, telling the bosun the lines were singing. Nobody questions maintenance. Maintenance is the most invisible virtue afloat.

He got the gig into the water without a splash, which surprised him; his hands were steadier than his heart. Then he pulled away from the *Vow*'s shadow, oars muffled with sacking, and put his bow toward the fishing quarter, and for the first time in thirty years the direction felt like a choice instead of a job.

The green fire lit his way. He tried not to think about what it meant — the harbor's breath, the children called it, the breath of something vast and patient turning over in its sleep — and failed. Everything meant something tonight. The powder meant something. The mirror meant something. The fact that he'd kissed his wife goodbye in Karthen eight months ago and told her he'd be home by spring meant something, and he intended to survive to find out whether she'd believed him.

Halfway across the dark water he heard it.

Not a crash. Worse than a crash — a long, grinding shriek, timber on stone, the sound a ship makes when the sea decides it's finished being patient. Then another, farther north. Then the bells of the *Ossuary* ringing frantic through the fog, and voices carrying flat and strange across the water, and somewhere behind it all the deep boom of the main body firing guns at nothing, at fog, at their own panic.

The *Sable Coast* struck first, Brannic learned later. Then the *Iron Meridian*, taking rock amidships, going down by the head with her watches still set. The *Vow* scraped clear by twenty feet and the width of a prayer, her keel opened like a gutted fish, limping east with the current pushing her toward deep water she'd never reach in time.

The Teeth had eaten the vanguard of the Karthen navy in forty minutes, in fog, in the dark, without a single shot fired at them. Somewhere on that wreck-strewn water, Aldous Crane was giving calm orders in a voice that never rose, reorganizing survivors, salvaging what could be salvaged, already composing in his head the report that would blame the weather, the pilots, the tides — anything but the atlas he had personally verified. The man survived everything. That was his gift and his curse and probably his religion.

Brannic didn't look back. Looking back was how complicity started; he'd learned that thirty years too late, and he wasn't going to take the refresher course now.

* * *

The fishing quarter woke to the sound of ships dying, and by the time Brannic's gig scraped the tide-gate steps, the whole shore was awake and armed and terrified. Fisherfolk stood along the breakwater with boathooks and gutting knives, watching the fires on the water where the *Iron Meridian* burned her last. Somebody had already launched boats toward the wreckage — because that was the thing about the fishing quarter nobody in any navy ever understood: they'd pull enemy sailors out of the sea as fast as their own, and then argue about whose turn it was to feed them.

"Ashore!" Brannic called, hands raised, standing in the open where the lanterns could see him. "I come with warning, not war!"

A boathook caught his collar and hauled him against the ladder. Above him, a woman with a face like weathered oak looked down and said, "You're the one who's been seen at the tide gate talking to the guild's boy. Brannic, they call you. Smuggler."

"That's me," Brannic said. "Smuggler, carrier, occasional coward. And tonight the only reason your boats weren't burning an hour ago. Listen fast — there's a frigate limping east with her hull opened, and more hulls behind her, and men in that water who'll freeze before dawn. Your boats can reach them before Karthen's own rescue crews can launch. Whatever else happens tomorrow — trials, tributes, whoever the council decides to hang — tonight, the sea doesn't care whose flag they drowned under. Neither should you."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Then she turned and bellowed names down the lane, and the lane boiled, and boats went out.

Brannic stood on the tide-gate steps and watched them go, and something in his chest unclenched for the first time in thirty years. He'd spent so long believing survival was its own justification — carry the letter, hold the lamp, keep your mouth shut, live to carry another letter — that he'd forgotten there was a difference between staying alive and choosing to. Tonight he'd chosen. It had taken him until his hair went grey and his knees went bad and the whole rotten architecture of his life had come down around his ears, but he'd chosen, and the boats were out, and children were sleeping safe in lofts above their fathers' tackle because a smuggler had finally done one honest thing.

It wasn't redemption. He was too old and too practical to believe in redemption. But it was a start, and starts were all anybody ever really got.

* * *

They found him at dawn, sitting on the tide-gate steps with wet boots and empty hands, letting the fishing women bring him tea he didn't ask for. By then the story had run ahead of him: the stranger who warned the quarter, the boats pulling Karthen sailors out of the water alongside Vaelmoor fishermen, the wrecked vanguard, the surviving frigate at anchor under guard with her captain walking calmly among the wreckage of his reputation as though inspecting it for defects.

They brought him before the council at midday, and the room was packed, and the first face he looked for was the boy's. Kestrel stood near Isolde Vane, pale, exhausted, ink-stained to the elbow, and when their eyes met the boy gave him a single nod — the nod of one craftsman acknowledging another's work.

"He knew," Brannic said, when they asked him what he knew. "The mapmaker's apprentice. He built the trap into the atlas and walked it straight through inspection, and none of us saw it, because we were all too busy admiring how well it was drawn." He looked around the chamber, at Halloway's vacant chair, at Isolde Vane sitting rigid beside her father's enemies. "I've carried lies across three seas. I know the weight of them. That atlas weighed exactly what a true atlas weighs. That's how I knew."

They asked him about Aldous. He told them everything — the payments, the soundings, the night of the fire, the escape boat, the weeping or the performance of weeping. He talked until his voice went raw, and when they asked why he'd turned, he thought about lying, because lying was his native tongue, and then he heard himself say:

"Because a man asked me once which silences keep people safe, and I've been answering wrong for thirty years."

Afterward, in the corridor, Kestrel found him. The boy looked older than his years and lighter than his crimes, which Brannic supposed was what honesty cost and paid in the same coin.

"They'll want you to testify again," Kestrel said. "Against him. At the trial."

"I know."

"He'll call you a liar. He'll be gentle about it — that's worse. He'll make them feel sorry for him."

"I know that too." Brannic rubbed his eyes. "One question, boy, and think before you answer, because I'll build my testimony on it. Did you sign that atlas knowing where the Teeth had moved?"

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. Out in the harbor, salvage crews were picking over the *Iron Meridian*, and the sound of hammers came faintly through the window like slow rain.

"I signed it," Kestrel said, "with his hand. In his own cabin. Using his own lessons." His jaw tightened. "He taught me everything I used to destroy him. I figure that's the only inheritance he ever actually earned."

Brannic laughed — a rusty sound, startled out of him. "Then we'll do," he said. "Between the two of us, we'll hang him with his own handwriting."

But when they brought Aldous Crane into the chamber — composed, courteous, grey at the temples, wearing ruin like a tailored coat — and his eyes found Brannic across the crowded floor, he didn't look angry. He looked *interested*. And he smiled the smile Brannic had seen aimed at drowning men, and inclined his head slightly, as if greeting an old colleague at a funeral.

Which, Brannic supposed, was exactly what this was.

The trial began at the next morning's bell, and the first witness they called was the drowned man's oldest friend.
