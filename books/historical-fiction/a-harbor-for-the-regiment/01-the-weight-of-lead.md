## 1. The Weight of Lead

The punch was worn to a whisper, so Beatrix cut a new one.

She had been at it since the lamps were lit, hunched over the graver with the steel blank clamped in her vise, cutting the letter *H* in mirror-image into the face of the metal. It was slow work, the kind of work that asked nothing of you but patience and gave back everything: a letter that would outlive her, that would press itself into copper, then into lead, then onto paper, then into the hands of strangers she'd never meet. Her father said type was the only honest inheritance. You could not lie with a well-cut matrix. The letter was the letter.

Outside, February wind came down the Rhine and rattled the shutters of the shop on Fischgasse. Inside it smelled of oil and hot metal and the tallow candles whose flames leaned whenever the door breathed open. Beatrix blew a curl of brass filings from the steel and held the punch up to the lamp.

The serif on the left foot was too thick. She set it down again, took up the graver, and began to correct herself.

Behind her, at the long table under the window, her father turned pages.

Elias Pemberton kept the ledger of Neuwied the way other men kept dogs — close, fed daily, and slightly feared by the neighbors. It was not an official book. The official books lived at the Rathaus, written in the clerk's careful hand, stamped and shelved and mostly unread. Elias's ledger was the other record: who owed whom what, which children had been born to which houses and under what circumstances, which debts had been forgiven and why, which grievances had been carried so long they had grown roots and put out leaves. Thirty years of the town's private weather, entered in his upright hand, each line dated, each name spelled correctly because he believed a misspelled name was a small theft.

"Frau Krämer," he read aloud now, though he was talking to himself more than to her. "Paid her candle-debt in full, four groschen, but refuses to forgive Herr Brandt for the matter of the goose. Noted again. Third year running."

"The goose matters," Beatrix said, not looking up from the steel.

"It was 1821."

"A grievance doesn't ripen. It pickles."

Her father made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter hadn't gone out of fashion in the shop sometime around her mother's death. He dipped his pen. "Pickles keep, though. That's the trouble."

Beatrix cut away the excess steel from the serif, thin shaving by thin shaving. This was the discipline her father had taught her first, before composition, before casting: *you cannot add, only remove.* Every letter began as a block of solid steel and every act of making it was an act of deciding what did not belong. She had been eleven when she understood that this was also how you survived a town like this one — you decided, daily, what did not belong, and you removed it, and whatever shape remained was you.

She was twenty-six now, and the shape that remained was narrow. She knew this without bitterness. A satchel's worth, maybe two. When she measured her life — and she measured it often, the way misers count coins — she counted what would fit in the leather satchel hanging on its nail by the door. One spare shift. Her mother's psalter, printed in Basel in a type so small you needed young eyes. The good graver with the walnut handle. Six matrices of her own cutting, wrapped in wool. And, if there was room, if the weight allowed, one ledger.

Not the big ledger. Not the thirty years of debts and geese. The small one, the older one, bound in green calf gone soft as cloth, the one her father kept at the bottom of the type cabinet under the tray of fourteen-point, beneath even the locked drawer where he kept his foreign sorts — Greek letters, Hebrew, a run of Cyrillic he'd bought from a traveling Russian and never used. He had never told her what was in the green book. She had never asked. In their family, questions about the past were like letters in the locked drawer: present, necessary, and never set.

But tonight the wind was wrong.

She couldn't have said how she knew. The river ran high; the ice on the gutters cracked and refroze; the baker's ovens had gone cold early because Anton had come down with a fever and old Krämer's son — no, the widow Krämer's son, Anton, nineteen and built like a bread oven himself — had covered for him. Small wrongnesses everywhere, none of them meaning anything alone. But together they made a silence, the way a room goes quiet before someone says something irreversible.

"Father." She set the punch down. "When did you last hear from Mannheim?"

He didn't look up from the ledger. "Why?"

"The foundry there. You wrote to them about the casting machine in autumn. They haven't answered."

"They're busy."

"Everyone in Mannheim is busy being angry. That's different from busy."

Now he looked up. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadow, the same face she saw in her own reflection and pretended not to recognize. "Beatrix."

"I'm not a child."

"No. You're worse. You're a reader." He closed the ledger gently, the way he closed it every night, palm flat on the cover as if taking its pulse. "There is talk. There is always talk. Talk is cheaper than type and worth less. If anything happens —"

"If anything happens, what?"

He considered the question honestly, which was worse than a lie. His eyes went to the satchel on its nail, then to the type cabinet, then back to her. "Then you will know what fits in the satchel better than anyone alive. You've practiced."

"That's not an answer, Father."

"It's the only answer I have that won't frighten you."

Too late, she thought. Too late by about ten years.

* * *

They worked until the candle burned low. He tallied; she cut. Somewhere past midnight she finished the *H*, annealed the punch, struck it into a square of copper softened in the fire, and had her matrix. She held the little rectangle of metal up to the flame and saw the letter sitting in negative inside it, waiting to be filled, waiting to become many.

"Hold it steady and it holds everything," her father said. He had come to stand beside her without her noticing. "That's the whole trade. That's the whole world, if you ask me."

"You don't usually ask me to think about the whole world."

"I'm asking now."

She turned the matrix in her fingers. Copper, warm from striking. It weighed almost nothing. It could be lost down a gutter and never found again, or it could outlast the town, the century, the language's fashions. Weight and weightlessness in one object, depending entirely on what people decided to do with it.

"Father," she said. "What's in the green book?"

His stillness lasted perhaps three seconds. She had learned to read him in increments smaller than breath, and those three seconds told her more than any entry in any ledger.

"Why do you ask tonight?"

"Because the wind is wrong. Because Mannheim isn't writing back. Because you looked at the satchel twice today, and you've never looked at it twice in my life." She set the matrix down on the stone bench, precisely, aligned with its six brothers in their wool. "Because if we're measuring what fits, I should know what matters most."

For a long moment the only sound was the river, far off, pushing ice against the pilings. Then her father crossed to the type cabinet — the great oak one, taller than she was, its drawers labeled in his hand going back to before her birth — and stood with his hand resting on the top of it, not opening anything.

"When your mother was alive," he said, "she used to tell you stories about Basel. About crossing water. Did you ever wonder why she never told the same story twice?"

"She liked new ones."

"She was afraid of the true one." He said it plainly, without drama, the way he entered figures in a column. "One day you'll read it. Tonight isn't —"

The knock came then.

Three blows on the shop door, spaced evenly, unhurried, the knock of a man who has knocked on doors all evening and expects them all to open. Beatrix and her father looked at each other across the lamplit table, and in the space between the third blow and the fourth — there was no fourth; whoever it was waited — she watched her father's hand move, quick and quiet, from the top of the cabinet to the small green book that had somehow already traveled, in these last seconds, from wherever it lived into his coat pocket.

It was midnight. Nobody called on a print shop at midnight except debtors, drunks, and death.

Elias Pemberton opened the door.

A lantern swung outside, and behind the lantern, uniforms — dark blue, wet with river fog, more of them than the doorway could reasonably frame. The officer in front held papers in a gloved hand, and even folded, even at a distance, Beatrix could see the seals hanging off them like ripe fruit.

"Herr Pemberton." The officer's voice was pleasant. That was the worst part; she would remember afterward that it was pleasant. "In the name of the provisional authority, I am instructed to inform you that as of tomorrow morning, this establishment and its contents are subject to requisition. I am required to serve notice tonight." He unfolded the papers. "There are copies. There are always copies."

Behind her father's shoulder, in the dark of the shop, Beatrix's eyes went to the type cabinet. To the drawers of letters. To the place where a small green book could rest beneath a tray of fourteen-point and never be seen by any man who did not already know where to look.

The officer stepped forward, boots dripping on the threshold stone, and held out the first copy for her father to sign.

*Sign nothing,* she thought, with a clarity that frightened her. *And move the book.*

But her father took the pen.
