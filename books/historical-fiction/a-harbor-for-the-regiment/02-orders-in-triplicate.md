## 2. Orders in Triplicate

They came at nine, which Beatrix thought was a coward's hour. Early enough to catch men at their bread and coffee, late enough that the mist had burned off the Rhine and the street stood plainly visible in both directions. If anyone meant to run or shout or bar a door, the whole town would see it done.

She was cutting a matrix when the knock came—three blows, measured, official. The punch slipped. She looked down at the half-finished letter *g* in soft copper and swore quietly at the gouge across its bowl.

Her father did not swear. He set down the ledger he had been balancing since dawn, squared its corners against the edge of the desk as if neatness might count for something, and went to open the door himself.

"Good morning," he said, before anyone had spoken.

There were six of them. Four soldiers in the gray of the provisional authority, rifles slung but hands resting near them in a way that pretended to be casual. Behind them stood a thin man in civilian clothes with a leather document case under his arm and the expression of someone who had rehearsed this speech on the walk over.

"Herr Pemberton." The man consulted a paper. "Aldous Pemberton, type founder, proprietor of these premises."

"You have me," her father said.

"By order of the provisional authority, established by grace of the occupation command—" the man recited it like a psalm he'd been taught by someone who didn't believe in it either, "—all records, registers, ledgers, account books, correspondence, and instruments of public information within this district are hereby requisitioned for inventory and regularization. This includes printing presses, movable type, matrices, and all materials pertaining to the production of printed matter."

The words came out smooth and terrible. *Regularization.* Beatrix had heard the word twice before, both times from clerks, both times about things that would afterward no longer exist.

"Instruments of public information," her father repeated. "You mean my presses."

"I mean everything that prints, Herr Pemberton. And everything that is written down."

The thin man stepped past her father into the shop without waiting to be invited, and that was the moment Beatrix understood the shape of what was happening. Not a search. A confiscation dressed as an inventory. They were not looking for anything in particular. They wanted *everything*.

The sergeant—a heavy man with a waxed mustache and the patient eyes of livestock—began directing his soldiers. One took the front room. One went up the stairs to the flat. Two came toward the workroom where Beatrix stood with copper dust on her apron and a ruined matrix in her hand.

"The ledgers first," the thin man said. He had begun opening drawers.

"There are three," her father said. His voice was level. Beatrix knew that voice. It was the voice he used when a customer tried to haggle down below cost, courteous on the surface and load-bearing underneath. "The commercial ledger, the wage book, and the parish accounts I keep as treasurer of the burial society. You'll want receipts."

"Receipts will be issued."

"Itemized."

"Everything is itemized, Herr Pemberton."

But there was a fourth ledger. There had always been a fourth ledger.

It lived in the bottom drawer of her father's desk, beneath the wage book, wrapped in oilcloth because paper feared damp more than fire. Her grandfather had started it before she was born, and her father had kept it, and last winter—when his eyes began their long surrender to small print—he had put it in her hands without ceremony, the way you hand someone a key they already own. It was not a commercial record. It was the town's other memory: debts forgiven and never spoken of again, children registered late because they arrived in bad seasons, families entered under corrected names, a column in the back where grudges were written down so they wouldn't have to be said aloud. Births. Quarrels. The miller's second wife. The widow Krämer's son, who drowned and whose death the church register got wrong by two years.

And in the front, in her grandfather's careful hand, the entry Beatrix had read a hundred times and never once understood until recently: *Pemberton, Aldous; Pemberton, Marta; child Beatrix — received into the town rolls, provisionally, by dispensation.*

*Provisionally.* Twenty-three years provisionally.

It was in the drawer now. She could feel it the way you feel a stove across a cold room.

The soldier coming toward the desk was young, maybe nineteen, with chapped hands and the apologetic posture of a boy who had joined for the boots. He reached for the drawer.

"Papa," Beatrix said.

Her father turned. Whatever passed between them lasted less than a second, and it contained no words at all, and it was enough. Twenty-two years of reading each other across a workroom.

"Wait," her father said loudly, stepping between the soldier and the desk. "If you are going to inventory my property, you will do it properly. That drawer contains private correspondence. My wife's letters. I will remove them myself, and you may watch me do it, and then you may seal the drawer with your own tape and sign it, and if anything is missing afterward you may answer for it."

It was magnificent and it was doomed and Beatrix loved him completely for it. The sergeant stopped. The thin man looked up from the drawers he was ransacking, interested.

"Private correspondence is also subject to requisition," the thin man said mildly.

"Then requisition it. But you will not paw through my dead wife's letters while your boy here holds a rifle in my workroom. Not in front of my daughter. Have you no procedure at all?"

He was buying time. He was buying time with dignity, spending it like a man who knew exactly how much he had left, and Beatrix made herself move. She crossed to the desk—slowly, a daughter obeying her father, nothing more—and opened the drawer, and lifted out the wage book on top of it, and let the soldier see her do exactly what she was told.

Under the wage book, her fingers found the oilcloth.

"Here," she said, and held out a bundle of letters tied with ribbon—her mother's, real ones, saved from a courtship thirty years ago, which her father reread every November and pretended not to. She had pulled them out from beneath the ledger one-handed, blind, in the space of four seconds, and pressed the oilcloth bundle flat against her stomach inside her apron, and no one saw, because everyone was watching her father make his stand.

The thin man took the letters, weighed them, and almost—almost—had the decency to look embarrassed. "These will be returned after review."

"They had better be," her father said, "or I will pursue it through whatever courts you people still pretend exist."

The sergeant actually smiled at that. A flicker, quickly suppressed. Even the occupiers, it seemed, could tell when a man was right.

They worked through the morning. It was worse than violence, Beatrix thought, watching them carry out trays of type—her type, cast from her matrices, every character cut or finished at some point by her own hands. Violence at least admitted what it was. This was theft wearing a clerk's collar. They stacked the job cases on a handcart like cordwood. They carried out the proof press in slings. The small hand press in the corner—the one her mother had used for calling cards—they left, because the order said *presses capable of production*, and the little one had a cracked platen, and the thin man wrote *deficient* beside it on his list with the satisfaction of a man finding a loophole he liked.

Against the wall, the type cabinet stood: forty drawers of oak and brass, each labeled in her grandfather's script. *Brevier. Long Primer. Small Pica.* And the bottom row, the deep drawers where the foundry sorts lived in their wooden compartments—lead and antimony and tin, dull silver rivers poured into molds.

Beatrix had carried the oilcloth bundle against her stomach for two hours. It was warm from her body now, and heavier than paper had any right to be.

When the soldiers moved to the stairs to bring down the last boxes from the flat, she knelt at the cabinet as if tidying, slid open the deepest drawer of the bottom row, and lifted out the compartment of 18-point lead sorts—a display face nobody had ordered since the old priest died—and laid the ledger in the empty wooden grid, and poured the loose sorts back over it until the metal covered the cloth completely, and closed the drawer.

Forty pounds of lead above a pound of truth. If they wanted to find it, they would have to melt the town down to get at it.

The drawer slid home with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

They detained her father at noon.

Not arrested—*detained*, the thin man explained, as though the word were gentler, as though euphemism were a courtesy extended to the family. "For questioning regarding irregularities in the town registers, Herr Pemberton. A formality. He will be comfortable at the customhouse."

"What irregularities?" Beatrix asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her, and then she understood: this was what fear was for. Fear sharpened. Fear was a whetstone.

The thin man glanced at her, and for the first time all morning he seemed to actually see her—not the daughter, not the apprentice, but a person standing in a gutted shop holding a ruined matrix. "Entries in the burial society accounts," he said. "Discrepancies of name. The provisional authority is regularizing the records of persons resident in the district. Some entries require... clarification."

Clarification. She thought of the word *provisionally*, twenty-three years old, sitting in a drawer of lead.

"He keeps those accounts for the parish," she said. "He has kept them for nineteen years. Ask Father Lorenz."

"We will ask everyone," the thin man said, and it was not a threat, exactly. It was worse. It was a schedule.

Two soldiers walked her father out. At the door he turned, and Beatrix braced herself for some last instruction, some coded farewell—but he only looked at her the way he had looked at her mother's coffin, and said, "Mind the furnace. Bank it low at night. Copper remembers what you do to it."

Copper remembers. She stood in the doorway and watched them take him down the street past the bakery, past the fountain, past neighbors who came to their windows and watched and did nothing, and she memorized every face at every window, not in anger—in *inventory*. The way you count stock. The way you note what you have on hand.

Because that was the thing about a foundry, she thought, going back inside to a shop stripped to its walls. Everyone believed the value was in the type. Let them cart away the type. Type was only the shape that metal remembered being. The matrices were the memory itself—the punches, the cuts, the true forms from which every letter could be made again, forever, as many times as needed.

They had missed the matrices. In all their itemizing, all their triplicate, they had catalogued the presses and the type and the paper stock and never once asked where the letters were born. They were still in the cabinet behind the shop, in their fitted box, wrapped against damp, because matrices were precious and precious things in this house slept downstairs among the dross.

Beatrix banked the furnace low, as instructed. Then she sat down at the emptied desk, in the quiet of a room that had been picked clean as a carcass, and listened to the silence settle.

Above the type cabinet, in a drawer full of lead, the town's true names lay hidden. In the cellar, the matrices waited. In ten days—or a hundred, or however long it took—they would remember how to speak.

She took out her knife and turned the ruined matrix over in her fingers, the gouged *g*, the flaw running through it. Then she set it down, and pulled a fresh blank of copper toward her, and began again.

Whatever they regularized, whoever they erased, the record would be cut in metal first.

That was the inheritance. That was hers.
