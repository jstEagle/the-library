## 3. What the Ledger Knows

I did not sleep. Sleep requires a belief in tomorrow, and tomorrow had been confiscated along with everything else.

By lamplight, with the shutters barred and my father's chair still warm from where they had let him sit one last time, I opened the ledger.

It was a small thing to look at. Half-bound in calf, its corners worn to brown fuzz, the spine cracked so that it fell open of its own accord to pages it had been opened to ten thousand times—my father's habit, or his father's before him. The type cabinet stood against the wall like any other piece of furniture, and inside it, beneath the tray of fourteen-point Bodoni that no one had ordered since the old margrave died, the ledger lay flat as a buried thing. I had slid it in at dawn while the sergeant's back was turned, and all day I had felt it there the way you feel a coin sewn into a coat: present, absurd, indispensable.

Now I took it out and set it on the composing stone and read what we were.

The first pages were my grandfather's hand, upright and patient, the letters of a man who believed ink was a covenant. *Debts of the town, kept honestly.* He had recorded who owed whom, down to pfennigs, down to eggs. A debt of three eggs from the butcher's grandfather to the cooper's grandmother, settled in 1801 by the birth of a calf, which seems an odd currency until you remember that in this town everything is currency: grain, grief, favors, silence.

Then births. Then deaths. Then—the section my father called *the grievances*, though he never said the word aloud—he had simply ruled a line and headed it *Accounts Outstanding*.

And then, near the middle, in a hand I did not know at first because it was smaller and more hurried than the rest, an entry dated the year before I was born.

I read it twice. Three times. The lamp guttered and I trimmed the wick with my thumbnail because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

*Pemberton, J., printer; Pemberton, M., his wife; infant daughter.* Entered under no heading at all, wedged between the death of a ferryman and the price of rope. *Arrived without papers, by night, on the mill barge. Claimed persecution. Unverified. Pardoned and entered into the record by order of Clerk H. Vogel, who vouches for the family personally.*

Vogel.

Herr Vogel, who had held my mother's umbrella at church. Herr Vogel, whose Christmas card my father answered every year with the same formal gratitude. Herr Vogel, who yesterday had stood in our doorway in his new gray coat with the provisional authority's armband and read out the requisition order in a voice like a man reading a shopping list, and who had looked at me—not at my father, at *me*—for one moment too long when the soldiers took him away.

I had spent my whole life believing we were simply here. That a Pemberton had always cut type in this town the way the river had always run under the bridge. My father spoke English to me and German to his customers and never once explained the seam between them, and I had been vain enough—or young enough—to think a seam meant nothing. A child assumes the world was assembled around her for her convenience.

But here it was, in my own family's book: we had arrived by night, undocumented, smuggled on a barge like contraband, and a clerk had made us real with a signature. Someone had told a story for us. Someone had decided we existed.

I pressed my palm flat against the page as if I could feel the hand that wrote it. Not my grandfather's. Vogel's? No—the entry was in the ledger's hand, my grandfather's hand, recording another man's mercy. *Who vouches for the family personally.* A sentence that could be read two ways forever: as gratitude, or as a leash.

My mother would have known which way to read it.

She died when I was nine, and what remains of her voice lives in maybe forty sentences, most of them instructions: hold the burin lower, warm the wax first, never trust a customer who praises your prices. But there was one evening—I must have been seven—when she sat me on her knee in this same shop while my father was out delivering a job, and she held my hands over hers and said, out of nowhere, with the river fog pressing white against the windows:

*"Beatrix. Listen. There are two kinds of people in every town: those whose names are written down because they belong, and those who are written down so they can be found again. You will not know which you are until someone comes to check."*

I had asked her what she meant, naturally, the way children ask and then forget. She had kissed the top of my head and said, *"Nothing. Cut straight lines and no one will ever look."*

No one will ever look. That had been the whole strategy of my childhood. We were quiet, we were useful, we printed everyone's wedding announcements and funeral notices and tax demands, and in exchange nobody examined the seam in our speech. Invisibility wasn't an accident. It was a story, told carefully, maintained daily, by my parents, for my sake—and now the people telling stories were the ones in gray coats, and their story had no room for us in it.

Because that was the other thing the entry taught me, the thing that sat in my stomach like swallowed lead: if we could be pardoned into existence by one man's signature, we could be erased by another man's. The ledger proved we belonged. It also proved we could be un-belonged. It was both the deed to the house and the warrant for eviction, depending on who held it.

Which was why they had taken the others and left me this one. Or thought they had. The soldiers had seized the town archive, the church registers, the customs rolls—everything official, everything *theirs*. This ledger was unofficial. Private. A printer's private accounts. They had taken it anyway, in the general confiscation, because my father had refused to hand it over and so they took the whole shelf—but the inventory list, I had seen it, had described only "miscellaneous bound volumes." To a sergeant with a manifest, a book is a book. To a historian, a book is a weapon or a waste product, and the difference is determined entirely by whether its contents flatter the victors.

Somewhere in the barracks across the square, my father was sitting in a room with this book's siblings, and somewhere in his head he knew what I now knew: that the record of this town—every debt forgiven, every grudge nursed, every name—was about to be rewritten by men whose entire profession is deciding which pasts are convenient.

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest and let myself feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of what I'd understood. Then I got up, because my mother had also taught me that self-pity is a solvent: it dissolves everything, including resolve.

* * *

I worked through what remained of the night, and work is its own kind of thinking.

I pulled the case of fourteen-point Bodoni and set a specimen line, just to feel the steel answer my hands: *THE RECORD OF THIS TOWN SHALL NOT BE WRITTEN BY STRANGERS.* Too grand. Too much like the very broadsheets I despised. I broke it up and distributed the sorts back into their compartments, but the sentence stayed in my head anyway, the way set type stays in your fingers.

The entry gave me an idea about Vogel, though, and the idea frightened me more than the soldiers had. A man who once pardoned refugees might do so again—for a price, for silence, for the ledger itself. And a man who once saved us could destroy us with a single affidavit: *the Pembertons were never lawfully entered; their presence is irregular; recommend removal.* My whole existence balanced on his pen, and his pen now belonged to the provisional authority.

He had looked at me in the doorway. One moment too long. As if checking that I was the person he remembered entering into the book.

Before dawn I heard boots in the lane and put out the lamp and stood in the dark with the ledger under my apron, heart going like a press at full speed. The boots passed. I breathed again.

And in the breathing, the decision arrived whole, the way decisions do after a sleepless night—they don't form gradually, they simply land, fully grown, like a piece of type dropped on the stone.

They were coming to rewrite the town. Fine. Then I would write it first. I had matrices, metal, ink, a press hidden behind the paper stock, and a book full of true names. If the victors' historians intended to decide which of us existed, then the least I could do was make erasure expensive—make it a matter of public argument rather than quiet clerical housekeeping.

I didn't yet know how. I knew only that somewhere out there were people whose names were in that ledger—people pardoned like us, indebted like us, grudged against like us—who would not enjoy being edited.

At first light I tied my scarf, hid the ledger deeper among the type trays, and stepped out into the occupied street to find out who else in this town had a past worth fighting for.

Herr Vogel, I suspected, would want to see me soon. I intended to get to him first.
