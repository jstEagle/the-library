## 4. The Clerk's Bargain

The Customhouse still smelled of tallow and river rot, the way it had when I was nine years old and my mother dragged me there through the rain to register a birth that had happened three weeks earlier in a room above a bakery we no longer lived above. I remembered her hands then—wet wool, cold fingers, pressing mine flat against the counter while Herr Vogel dipped his pen. *Stand straight, Beatrix. A person who stands straight is a person who exists.* She had believed that, or she had needed to believe it badly enough that it made no difference.

Now I stood straight on the same flagstones, and Herr Vogel looked up from his desk as though he had been expecting me for hours.

"Fraulein Pemberton." He did not say it warmly. He said it the way a man reads an entry aloud: name, trade, residence, confirmed. "You received my note."

"I read it."

"And you came anyway. Good. That is a kind of honesty, coming anyway." He gestured to the chair across from him with two fingers, the way one gestures at something that might bite. "Sit. You'll want to sit for this."

I sat because my legs told me to, not because he did. The chair was oak, older than both of us, worn pale at the arms by decades of other people's gripping hands. On the desk between us lay a blotter, an inkwell, three sealed papers weighted with a brass bell, and nothing else. No ledger. No file bearing my father's name. Vogel had always kept his desks clean; it was how he'd survived four administrations without ever once appearing to serve any of them.

"You've come about my father," I said.

"I have come about many things. Your father is among them." He uncapped the inkwell but wrote nothing. It seemed to be habit rather than theater—a clerk's fingers needing to be near a pen the way a preacher's need a book. "He is comfortable, if that word means anything to you. Fed. Not beaten. The lieutenant who holds him has been instructed that he is a man of standing."

"Instructed by whom?"

Vogel smiled. It was a small smile, precise as a cut matrix, and it told me nothing except that he enjoyed being asked. "By me, Fraulein. By letter, over my own signature, which is worth more in this town than most people realize. Your father sleeps in a bed. That is not nothing, in this season."

"No," I said. "It isn't nothing. What does it cost?"

And there it was—the question underneath every other question since the soldiers had marched in, since the requisition order had been nailed to the church door in triplicate, since the cabinet of type had swallowed our family ledger like a whale swallowing a prophet. Vogel set down his pen at last and folded his hands, and for a moment he looked less like a functionary than like the tired old man he actually was, seventy if a day, with ink stains permanent in the creases of his knuckles.

"It costs you a conversation," he said. "Which we are having. And afterward, perhaps, your silence. Which we will discuss."

* * *

I want to record what he said accurately, because accuracy is the only weapon I have ever owned and I am not going to blunt it now. But first let me say what I noticed, because noticing is the other half of the trade.

Herr Vogel's office had changed in six days. Before the occupation, the walls had held pigeonholes—hundreds of them, each labeled in his own immaculate hand, each holding some scrap of the town's paper life: leases, disputes, the fish market tariffs, a twenty-year argument about the boundary of the Krämer garden plot that he had never resolved and never destroyed. Now the pigeonholes stood empty. Every one. The labels remained—*Fischmarkt 1819–1831*, *Grenzscheide Krämer/Brandt*, *Geburten, Auswärtige*—but the slots behind them were bare wood, scoured clean, waiting.

They had taken everything. Not seized it for review—taken it, carted it off in army wagons to wherever provisional authorities keep the paper pasts of towns they intend to rewrite. The labels were left behind like gravestones in a plowed field.

"You're looking at my shelves," Vogel said.

"I'm looking at what's left of them."

"Yes." He said it without apology, which I found worse than apology. "You understand better than most what those shelves were. Thirty-one years I kept this town's memory, Beatrix—may I call you Beatrix? Your mother would have permitted it, and I find I cannot conduct this conversation through titles." He didn't wait for my answer. "Thirty-one years. And do you know what I learned? Memory is not a treasure. It is a liability with interest. Whoever holds the record holds every debt in town, including the debts of gratitude, and gratitude is the most expensive debt there is."

"My father's ledgers weren't debts," I said. "They were names."

"They were both. They were always both. That is why they are in an army wagon instead of on my shelves." He leaned forward. Outside the high window, gulls argued over the fish market, indifferent to regimes. "Let me tell you what happens next, so that you understand I am doing you the courtesy of honesty. In ten days—ten, perhaps eleven, they travel slowly—a commission arrives from the capital. Historians. Archivists. Two men and a notary, and a soldier apiece, because even history travels armed these days. Their task is regularization."

"Regularization," I repeated. The word sat in my mouth like a bad coin.

"A fine word. I didn't choose it. It means they will reconstruct the town's records from whatever survives, and from testimony, and from"—he paused delicately—"the needs of the new administration. The births will be regularized. The property transfers will be regularized. The debts will be regularized. And the names, Fraulein Pemberton, the names in your father's ledger, will be regularized into whatever shape serves the men who pay them."

"And my father?"

"Is a complication. A printer who kept a private registry of the town's grievances—that is a dangerous category of man just now. Dangerous to himself, chiefly, but categories spread." Vogel opened one of the sealed papers, considered it, and did not read it aloud. "There is a version of the next month in which Elias Pemberton is released, his shop reopens under license, and the commission finds his records orderly, incomplete, and dull. There is another version. I have seen the other version applied elsewhere. I signed letters about it elsewhere."

"The ledger buys the good version."

"The absence of the ledger buys the good version." He corrected me gently, the old schoolmaster he must once have been before clerking wore him smooth. "It is not enough to surrender it—they will search regardless, and searches are thorough when commissions are coming. It must be gone. Melted, scattered, sunk in the Rhine, I don't ask how. Gone, and your silence with it. No copies. No memories recited at tables. No names whispered to children. A clean page, Beatrix, on which the commission may write a kind history—one that includes your family, treated generously. Refugees welcomed, pardoned, absorbed. A credit to the town's charity."

The word went through me like a needle finding old scar tissue.

Refugees.

He watched me take the hit and gave it time to land. He knew, of course. He had known for thirty years—he was the clerk who had processed us, whose pen had made us real, whose empty pigeonholes had once held the paper proof that the Pembertons arrived in this town with nothing but a forged French passport and my mother's insistence on standing straight. Entry number forty-something in a register of Auswärtige, filed under a date I could recite in my sleep. *Pemberton, Elias, typesetter. Pemberton, Anna. Child, Beatrix, aged two.* Undocumented. Unregistered. Pardoned—by whom? By a junior clerk named Vogel who had chosen, for reasons he had never explained, to write the entry as though we had always belonged.

My whole life in this town rested on a line of ink a frightened man had laid down thirty years ago to cover a kindness or a crime—I still didn't know which—and I had learned it from my mother's voice, years later, in the dark, when she finally told me what we were. *We exist because someone decided we should, Beatrix. So decide carefully whom you make exist.*

"You knew," I said. "When you registered us. You knew we had no papers."

"I knew many things in 1817." His voice didn't change at all, and somehow that was the most frightening thing in the room. "I knew that the war had made liars of half Europe, and that a typesetter with a baby and a wife who stood too straight was not the danger anyone feared. I wrote what I wrote. It has kept you alive until now." He tapped the desk. "But that era is ending. The commission will not be kind out of sentiment. Kindness must be purchased, and the price has gone up. Surrender the ledger, accept the generous history, save your father, and live. Refuse, and I cannot protect you—not you, not him, not the shop, not the child you once were who exists only in a register that is currently in an army wagon."

* * *

I stood. My knees worked, which surprised me.

"Ten days," I said.

"Ten. Possibly eleven."

"And if I need longer to consider?"

"Then consider quickly." For the first time, something moved behind his eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One archivist of small lives looking at another. "Beatrix. I am going to tell you a thing I have told no one, and you may make of it what you wish. When they emptied my shelves, I asked to keep one folder. Any folder. My choice. The lieutenant laughed and refused me. Thirty-one years, and I was not permitted one folder of a fish market dispute." He looked at his bare pigeonholes. "Do not mistake me for your friend. I am not your friend. I am a man who has learned precisely what records are worth to the people who burn them, and so I know exactly what yours are worth to you. That is all my advice consists of. Price things correctly."

"Thank you for the arithmetic, Herr Vogel."

He inclined his head, almost a bow, almost ironic, and returned to his papers, and I walked out past the empty shelves with their faithful little labels, out into the light where the gulls were still screaming over the fish market as if nothing in the world had ever been written down.

Ten days. The historians were coming with their notary and their soldiers, and the town's past would be regularized—smoothed, sorted, made useful—unless in ten days I could put two hundred true names onto paper faster than a regime could erase them.

I had a press hidden under flour sacks. I had matrices in a false drawer. I had a baker's son who owed my father for a wedding announcement and a widow with a grudge and strong legs for market mornings.

And I had, God help me, ten days.

I turned toward the shop and began to count.
