## 5. Ten Days of Type

**Day One**

*Father —*

They let me bring you paper. I will not waste it on grief.

The shop stands. The soldiers have not found the ledger. It sleeps in the type cabinet, in the case marked for the long-s, behind the trays of ornaments nobody has wanted since Grandfather died. I have moved it twice already, because I am my mother's daughter and she taught me that a hiding place is only safe until someone thinks to look there.

Vogel came yesterday. He wore his new sash and offered me your freedom like a man offering bread he had stolen from my own oven. I refused him. I want you to know that plainly, so you do not lie awake wondering whether I bargained. I did not.

But Father — they are sending historians. Ten days. Men with clean hands who will copy what suits them and burn what does not. Our name is in that ledger as something other than what we are. If they reach it first, we will be whatever they decide we were.

I have an idea. It is a dangerous idea, which is the only kind worth having now. Wait for my next letter before you judge it.

Your daughter,
*B.*

* * *

**Day Two**

*Beatrix —*

You write that you refused Vogel, and I confess my first feeling was not pride but terror, and my second was pride. Forgive me the order of things. A father is allowed his cowardice first; it is how he earns the right to his courage after.

Now tell me this idea of yours, and tell me slowly, because I know your handwriting when you are being clever — it goes very upright, like a woman standing at attention.

Do not do anything foolish alone. That is an order from your father, who has no authority left except this paper.

— E.P.

* * *

**Day Three**

*Father —*

Here is the idea, written upright as you like it.

The historians will regularize the record. Very well. We will irregularize it first. Two hundred broadsheets, printed from our own type, carrying every true entry in the ledger — the debts, the births, the pardons, the names of everyone this town actually is, including the ones the new regime would prefer had never arrived. Including ours. Especially ours. If the truth is nailed to every door in Bingen before their clean copies exist, then erasure stops being easy. A town that has read its own name cannot unlearn it.

I have the matrices. You remember the ones I cut last winter, the ones you called vanity? The alphabet of our own hand. They fit in a satchel. I told you once I measured my life by what fits in a satchel. Now I know why God made me do it.

I need two people. Anton Brandt, the baker's son, because he is strong, stupid-brave, and moves through the market without anyone marking him. And Widow Krämer, because she has delivered bread to half the officers' billets and hears everything, and because she hated the Prussian clerk who falsified the toll records back when hating such men was merely unfashionable.

Do not call it foolish. Call it typesetting. It is the only prayer I know.

— B.

* * *

**Day Four**

*My upright daughter —*

It is typesetting, then. I have thought about nothing else since your letter arrived, and I have torn this page twice, so read what survives.

When your mother and I came up the river with nothing but the clothes we wore and the false surname the smugglers sold us, I promised her we would become invisible. Invisible was safe. Invisible was twenty years of quiet work and no questions. I built this shop inside that promise like a snail builds its shell.

But I am old enough now to know what invisibility costs. It costs being a stranger in the one place you have given your whole life. When the soldiers took me through the square, do you know what I looked for? Not escape. Faces. I wanted one face that knew me. There was none. Twenty-two years, Beatrix, and I was cargo to them.

So: print the names. Print all of them. Print mine where it truly belongs, in the entry for March 1826, refugees, undocumented, pardoned by one Clerk H. Vogel, fee waived. Let the whole town see that the typefounder was once a man with no name at all, and that a tired clerk was kind to him anyway. If we are to be erased, let us be erased loudly, in our own font.

One correction to your plan. Do not use the cellar press. They will billet soldiers above it within the week — I know how requisition works; I have read enough ledgers to know how men think. Move the press tonight. And Beatrix — the matrices you cut, the ones I called vanity? I withdraw the word. Cut two more while you can. An ampersand and a numeral seven. Every broadsheet needs dates, and dates need sevens.

I am proud of you in a way that frightens me.

— Your father

* * *

**Day Five**

The widow Krämer came to the shop at closing, ostensibly about a broken scale, and stood turning it in her hands while I told her everything. She listened the way stone listens — no nodding, no gasps — and when I finished she set the scale down and said:

"Your mother mended my mourning dress in '31. Never charged me. I never asked why a foreigner was so generous." She looked at the type cabinet a long moment. "Now I know. Generosity remembers itself."

Anton was harder. He came for flour money his father owed us and stayed because I showed him the matrices — the little brass letters, each one cut by hand, each one a face. I watched him hold the lowercase *a* up to the lamplight and something shifted in him, the way a boy becomes a man becomes a conspirator in the space of one held letter.

"You want me to carry these?" he said.

"I want you to help me print them. Carrying is Widow Krämer's part."

"And if they catch us?"

"Then they catch a baker's son caught holding letters," I said. "Worse men have been arrested for less honest reasons."

He laughed — actually laughed — and put the *a* back in its row with a care that undid me. "My father says the Pembertons keep to themselves. He says it like a complaint."

"We do."

"Not anymore, though."

"No," I said. "Not anymore."

* * *

**Day Six**

*Father —*

The press is moved. It sits in the coal cellar behind a false wall of brick I built myself, mortared with ash so it looks older than it is. My hands are black to the elbow and I have never been prouder of dirt.

Anton carried the heavier parts in flour sacks. Widow Krämer walked ahead of him through the market singing a hymn, loud and off-key, so that every eye followed the scandal of her voice and none followed the boy. She curtsied to a sergeant afterward. He tipped his cap. I think she enjoyed herself more than is strictly pious.

We set the first line tonight. It reads: THE TRUE REGISTER OF THE TOWN OF BINGEN, AS KEPT BY THE HAND THAT KEPT IT. Eighty-one characters. It took forty minutes because my hands shook, and I let them shake, and set the type anyway. That is the whole secret of craft, Father — the shaking does not stop. You simply learn that it is not a reason.

Nine days until the historians. Two hundred sheets to print. Write to me and tell me the numeral seven is done.

— B.

* * *

**Day Seven**

*Beatrix —*

The seven is done. So is the ampersand. I cut them by lamplight in the cell they give me for an hour each evening, on soft metal with borrowed tools, and the guard thinks I am making a gift for the commandant's wife. In a sense I am. Everything I make now is a gift to somebody's wife, or son, or neighbor. That is what a broadsheet is — a gift you throw over walls.

I have been thinking about your mother all week. She died believing we had successfully disappeared. I used to think that was peace. Now I think it was the loneliest thing I ever gave her, and I gave it because she asked, and love makes you obedient to strange requests.

Set the type straight. Mind the kerning on the capital T; you always crowd it. And when the sheets come off the press, put my name in full — Elias Pemberton, late of nowhere, of Bingen these twenty-two years. Late of nowhere. I have decided that is my place of birth. All of us in that ledger were born there, I suspect. Ask Widow Krämer sometime where she was born. Ask her gently.

— Your father

* * *

**Day Eight**

I asked her gently.

She was sorting bread into the baskets she uses for distribution — flour to the left, loaves to the right, contraband in the middle, though she does not yet know the middle basket is contraband — and I said, "Widow, where were you born?"

She did not stop sorting. "Koblenz," she said. "Before the border moved. Which means I was born in France, or in Prussia, or in a mistake, depending on which clerk you ask." She set down a loaf. "My husband spent nine years petitioning to be registered in the town he'd lived in since he was six. Nine years. He died in the tenth, still legally a visitor." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "So yes, girl. Print the names. Print mine. Print it large."

That evening Anton came with ink — real ink, bought in Mainz with his own wages, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. He would not take payment. "My father owes your father four months' flour," he said. "Consider this the interest."

"That's not how debt works."

"It's how *our* debt works," he said, and I understood I had just heard the founding charter of something, though I could not yet say what.

Three of us around the candle in the closed shop: a refugee's daughter, a baker's son, a widow born in a mistake. None of us, by the regime's arithmetic, entirely real. We passed the matrices between us like communion.

* * *

**Day Nine**

*Father —*

Tomorrow night we print. I wanted to tell you today, in case tomorrow goes badly, that the plan is good and the company is better.

Anton has memorized the guards' rotation by pretending to be slow-witted — he delivers rolls to the customhouse and asks the same questions every day until the sentries answer them automatically. Widow Krämer has recruited three more women for distribution, none of whom know the others, which she calls "the way my husband should have run his petitions." She has also informed me, in the tone of a general accepting a surrender, that the middle basket is hers to fill and hers to carry and I am not to argue.

Two hundred sheets. One press. Three nights' worth of courage compressed into six hours of darkness.

If they take me, the matrices are in the flour bin at Krämer's, under the rye. The ledger stays where it is. And Father — if they take me, do not trade anything for me. Promise me. You spent twenty years buying our safety with silence. Spend nothing more.

— B.

* * *

**Day Ten**

*Beatrix —*

I promise nothing. I am your father; promises are the one currency I have always counterfeited successfully.

Instead I will tell you what I did today, which is that I taught my cellmate — a drunkard named Pfeiffer, arrested for shouting at a colonel — to read. We used the back of your letters. He learned eleven letters and got stuck on Q, which he says is a letter designed by a man who hates other men. When he finally read the word *BINGEN* aloud, unassisted, he cried. I did not ask why. Some things you do not ask.

That is what you are doing, girl. Do you understand? You are teaching a town to read its own name. Whatever happens tomorrow, that has already happened. They cannot arrest a thing that has already happened.

Mind the kerning. Keep your hands moving. The shaking is not a reason.

— E.P., late of nowhere

* * *

I folded his letter into the case marked for the long-s, next to the ledger, and stood a moment in the dark shop with my hand flat on the type cabinet, feeling through the wood the small hard faces of a thousand letters waiting to speak.

Below the floorboards, behind the false brick, the press waited. Above my head, boots crossed the floor of what had been my father's bedroom — four soldiers, billeted, laughing at some joke in a language of jokes I was not meant to know. Tomorrow night they would sleep directly over the sound of the press, and Widow Krämer had already solved that problem too: she would send up, with the officers' breakfast, a bottle of the plum brandy her husband had laid down the year he died, the bottle she had sworn never to open.

"Sacrifice," she'd said, "should at least be useful."

I blew out the candle. In the dark I could hear everything: the boots overhead, the river below, Anton's knock coming early at the back door — two soft taps, pause, one more — earlier than we had agreed.

I opened the door, and the look on his face told me before he spoke that tomorrow had stopped being a plan and started being a countdown.
