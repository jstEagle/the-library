## 6. A Search by Lamplight

"They came to the Krämers' at eleven," Anton said. He stood in the doorway of the back room, hat in his hands, and he had not taken off his boots, which was how I knew he'd run. "A sergeant and two men. They turned over the flour bins. Widow Krämer fed them bread and lies and they took both. They're working down the lane. Ours will be tonight."

"Tonight," I said. "How do you know?"

"Because the sergeant told her so. He thanked her for the bread." Anton's jaw worked. "He *thanked* her."

I lit the lamp and looked at the shop as if seeing it through their eyes: the press under its sheet like a body in a shroud, the cabinets against the wall, the composing stone with yesterday's false work still on it — a receipt for Herr Brandt's funeral wreaths, innocent as milk. Somewhere in the third cabinet from the window, behind the trays of fourteen-point, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of tallow, lay the ledger with every name the town had ever trusted us to keep.

My father had built that cabinet the year I was born. He'd put a false back in it without telling anyone why, the way quiet men hide things — not expecting thieves, just refusing to be surprised by them.

"We move nothing," I said. "If we move it now, we tell them where to look."

"And if they find it?"

"Then they find it in a cabinet full of type, and they'll need a week to know what they've found." I heard my own voice go flat and steady, my father's voice, the voice he used when quoting prices he would not lower. "Anton. Go home. Sleep if you can."

"I'm not leaving you here alone with them coming."

"You're not staying to be caught in the building when they do. That's not courage, it's arithmetic." I put out my hand and, after a moment, he took it. His palm was hot and floured. "Two taps, pause, one. Tomorrow, an hour before dawn. We begin setting."

He went out into the dark and I stood a long time listening to the river.

* * *

They came at half past one.

I know because I had not gone to bed. I had sat in the back room with a single candle and my mother's psalter open on my knees, not reading it, just resting my hands on it the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal. The knock, when it came, was not a knock but a fist, and the candle flame leaned toward the door as if it too wanted to see.

"Open. In the name of the Provisional Authority."

There were four of them this time. The sergeant was a broad man with a lantern held high, and behind him two soldiers with bayonets fixed, and behind *them*, to my astonishment, Herr Vogel, in a coat too good for the hour, holding a paper he did not look at.

"Fraulein Pemberton." Vogel's voice was gentle, apologetic, the voice of a man apologizing for weather. "A search. Routine. You may light more candles if you wish."

"I wish," I said, and lit every candle in the shop until the place blazed like a chapel, because I had decided something in the last three hours: if they were going to search me, they were going to do it in full light, and I was going to watch their faces while they did it.

The sergeant began with the press. He ran his hand under the sheet, patted the frame, looked inside the platen as though a ledger might be folded into iron. One soldier took the front of the shop; the other took the back room, and I heard my mother's psalter thump as he set it down harder than he needed to.

Vogel drifted along the cabinets. He trailed one finger across the drawers of type the way a man trails a finger along a bookshelf, and my heart performed a slow, enormous labor, hauling itself up my throat and lowering itself again, again.

"The third cabinet," Vogel said pleasantly, "was your father's favorite, I believe. From the old days. Before the shop."

"It holds fourteen-point," I said. "Would you like me to show you?"

"That won't be necessary."

But the sergeant had already come around, and he pulled the drawer, and it slid out heavy with lead, and behind it — behind the second drawer, which he also pulled — the false back waited in the dark like a held breath.

He reached past the drawer. His arm disappeared to the elbow. My whole life narrowed to the width of a knuckle.

And then the door burst open.

Not broken — thrown, hard, and there was Anton in the doorway, breathless, hatless, drunk-acting or mad-acting or both, singing. Actually singing, some festival song about the Rhine and maidens, off-key with tremendous commitment, a bottle in his fist that I would later learn contained nothing stronger than watered vinegar.

"Sergeant! Sergeant, thank God — there's a man in the market square, breaking windows, the baker's own windows —"

"On whose authority do you enter—"

"*Windows*, Sergeant! Your name will be in the report if the baker's stock is ruined and you stood here!" And he grabbed the sergeant's sleeve — actually grabbed it — and the soldier in the back room called out something about papers found, and everything happened very fast after that.

What they found was a bundle of receipts. What they found was nothing. But the sergeant's arm was still in the wall of type when Anton shouted, and the sergeant chose — I watched him choose — the street over the cabinet, because a riot reported is a career and a drawer of lead is only a drawer.

They left with the psalter, absurdly, and with Anton between them, "to explain himself at the guardhouse." At the door Vogel paused. He looked at the blazing candles, at me standing among them like a woman keeping vigil.

"You burn a great deal of light, Fraulein Pemberton," he said, "for a shop that prints nothing."

"It's how I pray," I said.

When they were gone I stood in the wreckage of the search and shook. Not quietly. I let myself shake for exactly as long as it took the clock in the corner to tick forty times, and then I stopped, because craft does not permit shaking.

I unwrapped the ledger from its oilcloth and kissed it — actually pressed my mouth to the cover, tasting dust and tallow — and put it back, and slid the drawer home, and then I did the thing I had been afraid to do all evening.

I set the first line.

By candlelight, alone, with my hands still trembling so badly that the first *W* slanted like a drunkard and I had to take it out twice. *The true record of the town of—*. No. Too grand. Grandeur is what the historians sell. I took it apart and set it smaller, humbler, truer:

*We who are named below were born, married, buried, and forgiven here.*

Fourteen-point. My father's favorite size, from the old days, before the shop. Each letter clicked into the composing stick with a sound like a small lock turning, and by the fourth word my hands had steadied, and by the end of the line they were perfectly calm, because there is no trembling that survives the discipline of type — the letters demand to be exact, and exactness is a kind of courage you can hold in your fingers.

I set nine lines before the candles burned low. Nine lines of names beginning with the oldest entry in the book, a family pardoned by a clerk's kindness thirty years ago, entered in no official record anywhere.

At dawn, two soft taps, pause, one.

I opened the door. Anton's eye was swollen shut and split at the brow, and he grinned with the other one.

"Worth it," he said, before I could speak. "Did you set it? Did you start?"

"Nine lines."

"Then make room," he said, stepping past me into the smell of ink and candle smoke, "because I brought the widow, and she has opinions about spelling."

Behind him, in the grey light, Frau Krämer stood with her market basket on her arm and a look on her face that I had seen only once before — on my father, the day the soldiers first came. It was not fear.

It was a woman deciding what she was willing to lose.
