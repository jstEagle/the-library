## 7. The Victor's Historian

He came on the sixth day, and he came politely.

That was the first thing I noticed about Herr Albrecht—that courtesy preceded him like a swept floor. Not for him the boots of the requisition officers, the sergeant's list read aloud in the doorway while soldiers handled our type like stolen silverware. Albrecht arrived at midmorning, when the light through the shop's front windows was at its most forgiving, wearing a coat the color of wet slate and carrying a leather portfolio under one arm as though it were a hymnal.

"Frau Pemberton," he said, though I was no man's wife and never had been. "Or is it Fräulein? Forgive me. The records are—well." He smiled, and the smile had corners to it, neat as a folded letter. "The records are in some disorder."

"They are," I said. "Your men saw to that."

"I did not come to apologize. Apologies are a currency I don't carry." He looked past me into the shop, at the empty cabinet where the case of matrices should have been, at the press with its stone cold and its ink plate scraped clean by my own hand the night before. He looked at all of it the way a man looks at a room where something has recently been buried. "May I?"

I stepped aside. It was my shop. Let him think otherwise if it helped me.

* * *

He walked the length of the workroom slowly, trailing one gloved finger along the composing stone without touching anything that mattered. He paused at the wall where my father had hung the specimen sheets for thirty years—the ampersands like dancing couples, the great fat-faced numerals we'd cut for the customhouse, the little italic alphabet my mother had drawn before I was born. He studied them longer than a soldier would have. Longer than a thief would have, even an honest one.

"You cut these?" He indicated a tray of sorts still standing in the stick, the beginnings of a job ticket I'd abandoned two days ago. A funeral notice, of all things. Old Herr Brandt down the lane; the town had not stopped dying because the town had been occupied.

"Some," I said. "My father cut most. My grandfather before him."

"A dynasty of letters." He said it without mockery, which unsettled me more than mockery would have. "Do you know what I do, Frau Pemberton? Before you decide what to tell me, you should know whom you're telling it to."

"I assumed you were another kind of clerk."

The corner-smile again. "I am the historian attached to the provisional authority. My task is to write the account of these events—the requisition, the unrest, the measures taken—for the ministry in the capital. A clean account. An orderly one." He set his portfolio on my father's desk, precisely centered, and did not open it. "Every regime writes its own history, Frau Pemberton. That is not villainy. That is simply how time works. The question is never whether a history will be written. The question is who will be in it."

"And who decides?"

"The survivors, mostly." He let that sit between us like a coin on a counter. "And whoever holds the documents."

* * *

I made tea because my hands wanted something to do besides shake. This was an old trick of mine: give the body a small honest labor and it forgets the large dishonest fear. I poured his cup and mine and sat across from him at the workbench, and for a moment we might have been two tradespeople discussing an order—a wedding announcement, perhaps, or a bill of lading.

"You know why I've come," he said.

"To ask about the ledger."

"To ask about *ledgers*." He opened the portfolio at last and drew out papers with the unhurried grace of a man dealing himself cards he'd already counted. "There are eleven in this district alone. Shop ledgers, parish registers, a customs book from the old mill. Your father kept—" he consulted a page "—an unusual practice, I understand. Debts, births, grievances. All in one book. The sergeant called it a gossip ledger. I suspect it is rather more than that."

"It's a record," I said. "Of the town. As the town kept itself."

"Yes." He closed the portfolio. "Which is exactly the problem."

I waited. He was a man who liked an audience; I could see that already. Historians are a vain tribe—I knew this from my father, who distrusted them the way printers distrust anyone who wants words without paying for them.

"When the authority regularizes the record," Albrecht said, "the town will be described. Its population accounted for. Its debts settled or forgiven by decree. Its—dispositions of residence clarified." He chose each phrase the way my father chose a sort: nothing wasted, nothing accidental. "Some names will appear in that account. Others will not. This is not cruelty, Frau Pemberton. It is compression. A history that includes everyone is a history no one can read."

"My family has been compressed before," I said. "By clerks kinder than you."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, but recognition. The look of a man who has found the exact page he came for.

* * *

"Here is what I can offer," he said, and he leaned forward slightly, and I understood that everything before this had been prelude, the tuning before the piece. "Your father is detained. His health, I'm told, is not robust. The cellars of the guardhouse are damp in March. These things happen."

"These things are arranged."

"These things *happen*," he repeated gently, "and they can also unhappen. If the Pemberton ledgers were surrendered—destroyed, actually destroyed, pulped and burned with a certificate of destruction filed properly—then your father's file would describe a printer of good standing, briefly inconvenienced by an administrative error. Released within days. Compensated, even. And your family would enter the official history." He paused, savoring the word. "*Kindly.*"

Kindly. I turned the word over the way I turn a doubtful sort in the candlelight, looking for the flaw in the face of it.

"There was a pardon once," I said. "A clerk's entry. Thirty years ago. My family came to this town with nothing—no papers, no rights, less than nothing—and a clerk wrote us into existence with three lines of ink. Do you know his name?"

"Herr Vogel," Albrecht said. "Yes. He is with the authority now. He remembers the entry."

"He told you about it."

"He summarized the district records for me. It is my habit to verify summaries against originals." He folded his hands. "You see my difficulty. If the original survives—if it surfaces after the official account is published—then every name in it becomes a contradiction. And contradictions, Frau Pemberton, are what regimes cannot forgive. Not in others."

"And so you burn the evidence of your own mercy."

"And so we write the mercy *larger*," he said. "Without the embarrassing arithmetic underneath. Listen to me." For the first time he dropped the courtesy half a degree, and what showed beneath was not cruelty but conviction, which is far harder to argue with. "I have read the drafts. I know what the account says now about this district. *Foreign elements, irregularly documented, since regularized.* Eleven words for four hundred lives. But I have discretion in the appendix. Families of established trade—printers, for instance, a respectable craft, German-speaking, contributing—such families can be named. Given a paragraph. Their arrival described as *resettlement*, their status as *restored*. Your mother's story could be in the permanent record of this province, Frau Pemberton. Read by schoolchildren. Your father released to see it."

The tea had gone cold in my cup. I held it anyway, for the weight.

It would have been so easy to say yes.

That is the thing I want written down somewhere, in a hand that outlasts both of us: it would have been *easy*. He was offering my father's freedom, my mother's name in a printed book, my family made gentle and permanent in the memory of the province—all of it for the price of paper I could surrender and sleep after. No one would ever know what had been lost, because the loss would be invisible. That is the genius of a clean story. It doesn't feel like erasure. It feels like tidying.

I thought of my mother's voice saying *we were carried here, Liebchen, like freight, and someone wrote us real*. I thought of the widow Krämer's name in the ledger beside a debt her dead husband never meant to default on, and how she had touched that page with two fingers, like a face. Four hundred lives in eleven words, or four hundred lives in truth, ragged and contradictory and ours.

A history no one can contradict, I thought, is a history no one needs to believe.

"No," I said.

Albrecht sighed—not angrily. Almost with respect.

"I thought not," he said. "The ones who say yes are never the interesting ones." He rose, gathered his portfolio, buttoned his coat with three precise movements. At the door he stopped. "Four days, Frau Pemberton. Then the historians arrive from the capital, and the record is regularized with or without your cooperation. After that, whatever surfaces is not history. It is forgery. Think about which side of that line you wish your family to stand on."

"Thank you for the tea," I said, because I could not think of anything else that wasn't either begging or a threat.

He inclined his head. "The tea was yours. So is the choice." And then, softer, almost kindly, at the threshold: "For what it's worth—I hope you choose badly. A history with only one author is a very dull book, and I am paid whether it is dull or not."

The door closed behind him. I stood a long moment among the cold type and the specimen alphabets, my heart going hard and steady as a press screwing down.

Four days. Two hundred broadsheets to print, and a cellar full of soldiers overhead.

I went to find Anton.
