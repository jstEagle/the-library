## 8. Night Press

### BEATRIX

The cellar smelled of lamp oil, damp stone, and hot lead—the smell of my whole life compressed into one room beneath the soldiers' boots.

Above us, the billeted men of the Second Regiment moved through the shop like weather. We had learned their rhythms in three nights: the sergeant's cough at ten, the boots crossing to the privy at eleven, the long silence after midnight when even sentries trust a town that has been quiet for a week. Anton lay on his back under the stairs with an ear pressed to the floorboards, counting.

"Third watch is asleep," he whispered. "Or pretending."

"Pretending counts," I said. "Pretending means they've decided we're harmless."

We had carried the press down in pieces over four nights—chase first, then the platen wrapped in flour sacking, then the screw, which Anton took apart and reassembled twice so it would fit inside two empty beer kegs. The type came last, in the bread baskets Widow Krämer carried on her hip like she was hauling loaves to market, because no soldier stops a widow for bread. Two hundred forms of small pica. My father's matrices, cut by his hand over thirty years, wrapped in my mother's shawl.

I set the first line by candlelight with the candle shielded behind a flour tin. *To the Inhabitants of Grünstadt.* The letters bit into my thumbs the way they always did, that small familiar pain, and my hands shook—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the strangeness of setting these particular words: names. Ninety-one names. The ledger's whole confession, set in metal.

My mother's name was third from the bottom. *Elisabeth Pemberton, née Vogelsang, entered this town 1811, unrecorded.* Unrecorded. The word I had read forty times since finding it now looked different upside down, mirrored, waiting for ink. When it printed, it would be right-reading. True-reading. A thing you could hold up to the light.

"Compositor," Anton whispered. "You're staring at your own name."

"I'm checking the kerning."

"You're terrified."

"Both things can be true." I slid the line into the stick. "That's what the lead is for. Lead doesn't shake. You put enough of it down in order, and the shaking doesn't matter anymore."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My father says printing is just gossip with a license."

"Your father," I said, locking up the forme, "is about to be very licensed indeed."

* * *

### ANTON

Here is what nobody tells you about a hand press: it wants rhythm. Papa pulls his oven doors the same way—shoulder, wrist, let go—and so when Frau Pemberton showed me where to set my hands on the bar, my body already knew the argument between weight and timing. Pull too hard and you bruise the impression. Too soft and the type kisses the paper instead of biting it. She said *bite*, and I thought of her face when the sergeant kicked me, how she had gone white but hadn't looked away, and I understood that bite was the whole point of the night.

Two hundred sheets. Dampened paper stacks between felt blankets so the ink would take. She inked the balls—I refused to let her pull and ink both, though she was faster—and each pull took maybe six seconds if we were smooth, eight if we were careful, twelve if the soldiers upstairs shifted in their sleep and we froze with the bar half-raised like a pair of thieves in a woodcut.

Which we were. Thieves in a woodcut. I kept almost laughing about it and kept having to stop, because laughing in a cellar while a regiment sleeps overhead has a specific sound to it, and the sound carries.

By sheet sixty I had stopped being afraid. This is the true thing I will tell my children someday, if there are children and someday: somewhere around sheet sixty the fear burned off like morning mist, and what was left underneath was joy. Not happiness—joy, the older, rougher thing. My arms burned. Ink climbed to my elbows. Every pull drove ninety-one names into rag paper, and every sheet I laid face-down on the drying lines was one more door the truth could knock on.

"Seventy-eight," Frau Pemberton said.

"Anton."

"We're committing treason, Beatrix. I'd like to commit it as your friend."

She looked at me across the press, ink on her chin, candlelight making her eyes enormous. "Then commit it as my friend, Anton, and mind your register."

### BEATRIX

Widow Krämer arrived at three with the unhurried tread of a woman who had buried a husband, raised a daughter alone, and outlived two French administrations, and who regarded a Prussian occupation as a professional inconvenience.

She read the proof sheet once, holding it close to the candle. Her lips moved over the names. At the bottom—*Printed in Grünstadt, by its own hands*—she made a sound low in her throat, not quite a laugh.

"My husband's name is in there," she said. "Debts section, page nine. He owed your father eleven gulden for funeral notices. He died before he paid them."

"He paid them," I said. "There's a line struck through it. Someone forgave the debt in the margin. It's initialed E.P."

She was silent. Her thumb rested on the struck-through line as if checking whether the ink had dried. "Your mother," she said finally. Not a question. Everyone in this town had decided years ago that Elisabeth Pemberton had been a merchant's wife from downriver, respectable, unremarkable. The ledger knew better. The ledger always knew.

"She wrote everyone into that book," I said. "Births, debts, grievances. And one clerk wrote her in. One line, in 1811. That's all any of us are—one line somebody bothered to keep."

Krämer folded the proof along its crease, precisely, and tucked it into her bodice. "Market opens at five. Soldiers don't come before seven; they think bread is beneath them." She took the first bundle of twenty-five. "I'll need more than that. There are two hundred doors in this town and I have been delivering to most of them for thirty years without anyone asking why."

At the foot of the stairs she paused. "Child. If they catch me—"

"They won't."

"If they catch me," she repeated, with the patience of a woman correcting a typesetter's error, "you print again anyway. That's the arrangement. Grief is a debt too, and I mean to see it collected."

Then she went up into the dark with her bread baskets and her dead husband's name against her heart, and I heard the street door close soft as a psalm.

* * *

### ANTON

Pulling the last sheet felt like setting down something heavy that turned out to be lighter than expected—or heavier. Both. Frau Pemberton hung it on the line with the others and stood looking at two hundred rectangles of gray paper, face-down, anonymous, drying in a cellar while upstairs a regiment dreamed its dull dreams.

"Now comes the part I hate," she said.

"The waiting?"

"The waiting." She blew out the candle except for the stub we kept for the stairs. "Setting type is craft. Printing is labor. Waiting is just you and your conscience in a dark room, and my conscience snores."

It snored audibly, in fact, within the hour—she fell asleep sitting up against the kegs, mid-sentence, and I sat turning the press screw slowly, uselessly, keeping my hands busy because my head wouldn't stop running the arithmetic. Two hundred sheets. One town of perhaps nine hundred souls. One hundred and eighty of those souls would read the broadsheet and say nothing. Ten would be angry it existed. Ten would be angry it was true.

And one—a single one, some baker's boy or ferryman or girl cutting matrices in a cellar—would read the ninety-one names and understand that a record is not a thing history keeps. It's a thing people keep, on behalf of history, whether history thanks them or not.

At dawn Krämer knocked twice on the cellar hatch, our signal. I lifted it. She stood in the gray light smelling of yeast and river fog, her baskets empty, her face lit up like Easter.

"Doors," she said. "Every one of them. Tucked into the lintel cracks, weighted under milk jugs, slipped into the bakery queue. By the time the sergeant takes his coffee, Grünstadt will know itself."

"And the soldiers?"

"Oh, the soldiers got theirs too." Her smile was a terrible and beautiful thing. "One on the customhouse door. One on the church door. And one—" she held up a finger "—folded neat as a love letter, pushed under the billet door above your heads."

Beatrix was awake by then, and I heard her breath catch. "Krämer. That wasn't the plan."

"No," the widow agreed pleasantly. "But the plan was mine to improve. Let them find it, child. Let them read their own orders next to ninety-one names. Fear works best when it arrives with breakfast."

### BEATRIX

We came up into the shop at first light, and the light was wrong—or rather it was perfectly ordinary, which was worse. The river ran. The gulls argued over the fish market. Somewhere a cart complained up the hill. The world had not stopped to notice that everything in it had changed overnight.

Anton went home through the back lanes. I stayed to sweep the shop, because sweeping is what innocent people do, and because my hands needed employment or they would start writing things.

They came at half past seven. Not the sergeant—the other kind. A lieutenant I didn't know, gray-faced, with the broadsheet crushed in his fist like a dead bird, and behind him two men from the customhouse, and behind them, moving fast through the crowd that had gathered as crowds do when trouble walks openly, came Herr Vogel.

Vogel's eyes found mine over the soldiers' shoulders. He did not look triumphant. He looked like a man watching a clock he had wound himself begin to strike.

"Fraulein Pemberton." The lieutenant's voice cracked on the title; he was younger than the crisis required. "This document appeared throughout the town during the night. Sedition. Names of persons under administrative review, distributed to inflame the population. The provisional authority requires—"

"Requires," said a voice from the crowd, and it was the ferryman Brandt, who had not spoken to me in four years. "Requires. Interesting word. Does it require the names be wrong, Lieutenant? Because my grandmother's name is on that paper, and I'd like to hear you say it's wrong in front of her street."

Another voice. Then another. The crowd was not dispersing. The crowd was doing the opposite of dispersing.

Vogel stepped forward, and up close I saw the thing I had bet two hundred sheets of paper on: he was afraid. Not of the lieutenant. Of the crowd. Of the record, loose in the town, multiplying in memory beyond anyone's power to seize.

"Fräulein," he said quietly, "the historian Albrecht requests your presence at the customhouse. Today. Regarding the ledger."

I wiped my inky hands on my apron, in front of everyone, and let them see the ink.

"Tell him," I said, "that I'll bring my own copy."
