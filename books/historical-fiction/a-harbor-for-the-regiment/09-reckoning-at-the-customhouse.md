## 9. Reckoning at the Customhouse

The summons came at dawn, folded twice and left under the door by a boy who ran before Beatrix could tip him. She read it standing in the cold of the shop, her fingers still black from the night's work, and understood that the ink on her hands was the reason for the paper in them.

*You are required to appear before the Provisional Authority at the Customhouse, nine o'clock, this morning. Bring nothing.*

Bring nothing. As if she were a woman who owned anything they hadn't already counted.

She washed at the pump in the yard, watching the water go grey, then black, then clear. Under the nails it would stay for days; she knew that now. Some things you could not scrub out, and had stopped wanting to.

Her father's last letter lay in her apron pocket, the one Anton had carried out of the guardhouse folded inside a loaf heel. *If they offer you a bargain, Bea, look at what they're asking you to hold in one hand and notice what they're hiding in the other.* He wrote like he set type—economical, every word paid for.

At half past eight she kissed the widow Krämer on both cheeks in the doorway of the bakery, where two hundred broadsheets had been born and one hundred and ninety-three doors now wore their truth like a struck match wears its flame. The town knew. That was the whole of her arsenal: the town knew, and knowing is a thing that cannot be requisitioned.

"Say it plain," Frau Krämer said, gripping her wrists. "Whatever they offer. Say it plain and make them repeat it."

"And if they detain me?"

"Then the whole market will know by noon, and the whole market will be *insufferable* about it." The old woman's eyes were dry and bright. "Go. You've made us all into witnesses. Let them see what that costs."

* * *

The Customhouse had been built for cargo, not judgment, and the Authority had done nothing to soften it. The great room smelled of river damp and old tobacco, its ledgers of tolls still shelved along one wall like a congregation of the dead. A long table stood where bales of hemp used to be weighed. Behind it sat Albrecht, and beside him, slightly lower, slightly to the side, Herr Vogel.

Vogel would not meet her eyes. Forty years ago he had written a pardon into a margin with a steady clerk's hand and saved three refugee lives; twenty years after that he had stopped speaking of it; and now he sat with his hands flat on the table as though pressing something down that wanted to rise.

Albrecht was courteous. He was always courteous. It was his most dangerous quality.

"Fräulein Pemberton." He gestured to the single chair facing them. "Thank you for coming. I trust the town has been—lively—since yesterday morning."

"There were names on the doors," Beatrix said. "People read them."

"So I'm told. Two hundred copies." He opened a folder before him with the care of a man handling evidence or scripture. "Printed on a hand press, cellar work, good impression. Whoever set that type knew letters the way a mason knows stone." He looked up. "It was your father's type, Fräulein. Your matrices. Your press. And the names—" he turned a page toward her, and there they were, her own setting reproduced in a clerk's careful copy, "—are the names from a ledger this office seized eleven days ago. Which means either the ledger escaped us, or a copy of it did."

Beatrix said nothing. Silence, she had learned, was also a kind of type: it set the space around the words.

"You understand our difficulty," Albrecht went on, reasonably, terribly. "A record exists that contradicts the record we are building. Not in its facts—we do not dispute the facts; facts are cheap—but in its *authority*. When two records disagree, the town must learn which one to believe. We had hoped to teach it gently." He closed the folder. "Then someone taught it first."

From the window behind him she could see the square, and she let herself see it deliberately, the way her father had taught her to sight a line of type down its edge. The market stalls were out. People moved among them slowly, and every few moments one of them glanced up—at the Customhouse, at its windows, at her. They had come to the market the day after the broadsheets the way people come to a church after a funeral: because absence requires attendance.

"They're watching," she said.

"They are always watching. The question is what they conclude." Albrecht folded his hands. "Here is what I am authorized to offer, and I will say it once, plainly, as you would wish. Your father is released today. The shop reopens under your name—you have earned that, frankly; your work is better than his ever was. No charges. In exchange: the ledger, or its copy, whichever survives. And a public statement that the broadsheet contained errors. Clerical errors. Nothing more."

"And the names?"

"The names will appear in the official record," Vogel said suddenly, and his voice cracked on the second word like bad type under pressure. Everyone looked at him. He smoothed the table with both palms. "In the official record, properly contextualized. The town's history has—difficult chapters. We will present them accurately but *proportionately*. History is proportion, Fräulein. A name in a footnote is still a name."

"A name in a footnote is a name buried upright," Beatrix said.

Albrecht smiled faintly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd noted in a margin. "And yet footnotes survive. Burned books are remembered as ash; footnoted books are remembered as books. I am offering you the difference between martyrdom and inclusion. Most people, given that choice, choose to be included. It feels like winning. It often *is* winning." He leaned forward. "Your family came to this town with no papers, Fräulein. Pardoned by a clerk's mercy, absorbed by a printer's silence. Everything you are began with an act of erasure—someone decided your family's arrival should be forgotten so that they could be kept. I am offering you the same mercy, at scale. That is what a state is. A story everyone agrees to tell so that everyone can stay."

There it was. The clean story, offered kindly, the way her mother had once described the bread handed to refugees at the border: *they give it to you warm so you don't ask what's in it.*

Beatrix thought of the ledger hidden in the type cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the case of fourteen-point, where no searcher had thought to look because no searcher believed a book could hide among its own alphabet. She thought of her mother's voice reading entries aloud on winter nights—not the debts, never the debts, but the births and the grievances and the small stubborn fact of each name spelled correctly. *We keep the record,* her mother had said, *because the record keeps us.*

"I have a counter-offer," Beatrix said.

Albrecht raised his eyebrows, genuinely interested. Vogel's hands stopped moving.

"The ledger exists. One copy. I will surrender it—today, into this room, publicly, before witnesses." She saw Albrecht begin to smile and kept talking, because the whole thing depended on pace, on setting the line so it could not be broken mid-word. "In exchange: my father walks out of the guardhouse through the front door, in daylight, past the market. And the names are read aloud—here, in this room, from the original page, by an officer of the Provisional Authority, once a year, every spring, when the regiment musters in the square. Read aloud, not footnoted. Spelled correctly. All of them. Including mine."

The silence lasted long enough for her to hear the river.

"That is not how records work," Albrecht said finally.

"No," Beatrix agreed. "That is how *memory* works. Records can be burned, corrected, regularized. Memory needs a body and a voice and a date. You want the town to believe your story. Very well—but a story told once is propaganda, and a story told every spring becomes the town's. You cannot requisition that. You tried; it's on my doors."

"And if we refuse?" Albrecht asked softly. "If we simply take you, take the shop, search until we find the ledger, and write the history without any of you?"

"Then you'll find it," Beatrix said. "Eventually. In a week, perhaps. And you'll burn it, and the town will watch you burn it, and every person who read those doors yesterday will know exactly what you burned and why—and they will tell their children, and their children will tell theirs, and your clean story will spend the next hundred years smelling of smoke. Or—" she leaned forward now, matching him, "—you accept my terms, and every spring the Authority itself stands up and reads the names, and the erasure becomes impossible because the erasers perform the remembering. I am offering you the only version of this where you are not the villain. I'd have thought a historian would recognize a bargain with the future when it sat down across from him."

Albrecht studied her for a long moment. Outside, a cart passed, and above its rattle she heard—faintly, unmistakably—the sound of the market carrying on, unhurried, unafraid, full of people who had chosen to be outside her window at exactly this hour.

"It is the town, then," Albrecht said quietly. "You're betting on the town."

"I'm not betting," Beatrix said. "I checked the type. The town is set."

He almost laughed. Beside him, Vogel rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the square with his back to them both, and when he spoke his voice was the voice of a clerk reading a line he had waited forty years to correct.

"The terms are acceptable."

Albrecht turned sharply. "Herr Vogel—"

"I wrote the pardon," Vogel said, not turning around. "I have been correcting it ever since. Let me finish the job." He came back to the table, drew a fresh sheet toward himself, dipped his pen, and began to write in that same steady hand—the hand from the margin, the hand from the ledger—with the words taking shape under it: *Terms of Settlement, read and witnessed.*

By noon her father walked out of the guardhouse into daylight, blinking, thinner, upright, and the market did not cheer, because cheering would have given them permission to disperse it. Instead the whole market simply stopped—every stall, every scale, every haggling tongue—and watched him cross the square, and then, as one, went back to work, which in that town was the loudest possible ovation.

And in the Customhouse, under seal and signature, the ledger changed hands, and the date of the first public reading was entered into the record:

Spring. When the regiment mustered.

Beatrix walked home through the market with her father beside her, and at the bakery door Frau Krämer pressed a warm roll into her hands and said, "Well?"

"Well," Beatrix said, "we have an appointment every spring."

Her father looked at her a long moment, then at the roll, then at the town going about its business around them like a tide that had decided, at last, whose shore it belonged to.

"Then we'd best reopen the shop," he said. "We have matrices to cut."
