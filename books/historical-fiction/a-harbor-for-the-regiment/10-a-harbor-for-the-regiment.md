## 10. A Harbor for the Regiment

One year, and the door still stuck.

Beatrix had oiled the hinge twice in April and once more in May, but the shop door had its own opinion about weather, and on the morning of the first spring muster it opened with the same reluctant groan it had made the day the soldiers first walked through it. She had considered replacing it. Herr Brandt the joiner had offered, free of charge, in that tone men used when they meant to be refused politely. She had kept the door. Some stubbornness was worth preserving; it reminded her whose shop this was.

The sign above it said PEMBERTON & DAUGHTER now. Her father had painted the letters himself, standing on a borrowed ladder with his coat off despite the cold, and when he came down he had looked at it for a long time and said nothing at all, which from him was a eulogy, a christening, and a blessing rolled into one.

Inside, the presses stood where they had always stood. The type cabinet—walnut, scarred, its third drawer still hiding the groove where a ledger had lain through ten days of occupation—occupied its corner by the window. The new ledger sat open on the counter, because Beatrix kept it open now. There had been a time when the record of this town lived shut away, read only by her father's finger running down columns of debts and births and grievances. Now anyone could come in and ask to see it, and did: the baker checking what Anton's grandfather had owed in 1831, the widow Krämer confirming the date of her husband's burial, children sent on errands who lingered over pages like tourists in a country they hadn't known they owned.

She had cut three new matrices that winter. One for the umlauts the old sets had lacked, one for a smaller numeral her father swore no job would ever need and which she had needed twice by February, and one—the third—for a single letter, a *B*, cut finer than any she had ever made, because the sign painter's brush was not the same as the founder's art, and if her name was going to sit over that door for the next forty years, it would sit in lead she had struck herself.

The morning smelled of ink and cold river. Through the window she could see the square filling.

* * *

They came at nine, as the proclamation said they would. The regiment—the same one, or what remained of it after the winter's reorganizations, its colonel a different man now, its colors mended—mustered on the cobbles where the requisition carts had once stood in their gray lines. But this year there were no carts. This year the soldiers stacked arms at the edge of the square and left them there, unarmed and unguarded, while their officers walked to the customhouse steps and took off their caps.

The town gathered the way water gathers toward a drain, slowly and then all at once. Frau Krämer arrived with her market basket empty on purpose, so her hands would be free. Anton came straight from the ovens, flour still dusting his forearms to the elbow, and stood beside Beatrix without asking whether he should. Her father stood on her other side, and she noticed—he would have hated being noticed—that he held his hat in both hands like a boy at church.

Herr Albrecht read the names.

That was the arrangement, and it had held through the winter without anyone quite believing it would. The historian had not resigned; he had been retained, which in the strange arithmetic of the new order amounted to nearly the same thing but paid better. He stood at the customhouse rail with the fair copy of the ledger—the copy Beatrix had supervised, line by line, initialing each page—and he read aloud the names of the town as it actually was: every debt forgiven or outstanding, every birth registered and unregistered, every family entered under its true history, including one line near the beginning that read, in careful clerk's hand, *Pemberton — arrived undocumented, 1811; pardoned by decree of Clerk J. Vogel.*

Vogel himself was not there. He had retired in January to a cousin's house upriver, and whether shame or strategy had moved him no one said, though Frau Krämer said both, loudly, at market, twice a week.

The names took an hour. Some people wept. Some laughed at entries they'd forgotten—a fine levied against the butcher's father for an illegally fat goose in 1829 drew applause. And some stood very still, the way Beatrix had once stood very still in front of her own family's entry, learning that invisibility had been a story told for her benefit, and that the telling of a truer story could be done out loud, in daylight, by a man in uniform who had once been her enemy.

When Albrecht finished, the colonel stepped forward and spoke four sentences about the regiment's debt to the town—debt being the word chosen, deliberately, Beatrix suspected, by someone who understood what a ledger meant here. Then the officers returned to their men, the drums sounded, and the regiment marched once around the square past the shop door, past her name in lead above it, and Beatrix watched soldiers she had smuggled truth around look up at the sign, and one of them, very young, touched his cap to her.

* * *

In the afternoon the archive received its copy.

It was a small ceremony, smaller than the muster, and better for it. The archivist, a round, precise man named Dorn who had spent the occupation years hiding municipal papers inside a false wall and therefore enjoyed a local reputation approaching sainthood, unlocked the cabinet with two keys and waited, hands folded, while Beatrix carried the ledger in herself.

"Customary," Dorn said, "for the depositor to state the terms."

"The copy stays," Beatrix said. "The original stays in my keeping. Anyone may read it. No one may amend it—not the council, not the garrison, not the archive itself. Corrections require my initials and a dated note in the margin. Erasure requires—" she paused, and found she was smiling— "requires outliving me."

"And after you?"

"After me, whoever holds the shop. It's in the deed."

Dorn wrote it down. Her father witnessed it. Frau Krämer witnessed it, and pronounced it the first honest paperwork done in that building since the French, which Dorn chose not to dispute.

Walking back afterward, her father was quiet for two streets, which she had learned to let run its course.

"When your mother and I came here," he said finally, "we wanted only to be unremarked upon. That was the whole ambition. A life too dull to record." He turned his hat in his hands. "You've made us remarkable instead. I keep waiting to be afraid of it."

"Are you?"

He considered. "I keep waiting," he repeated, and left it there, and bought two rolls from Anton's stall, and paid full price without haggling, which those who knew him understood to be a kind of fireworks.

* * *

The shop closed late that evening, later than usual, because the day had brought orders along with everything else—a printer downriver wanting matrices, the council wanting five hundred copies of next year's market regulations (a commission that would have been unthinkable twelve months ago, when the council had been a rumor wearing uniforms), and a wedding invitation from a couple who wanted their names set properly, in lead, because paper wore out and they intended to last.

Beatrix banked the press fire and stood a moment in the doorway before locking up, looking across the square. The regiment's camp had gone quiet. Lamplight moved behind the customhouse windows where Albrecht worked, presumably entering the day into whatever record historians kept for themselves. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

A year ago she had measured her life in what fit in a satchel. Type, matrices, one ledger, one name. She had believed inheritance was a thing you carried out of a burning place, small enough to hold, precious enough to hide.

She looked up at the sign. PEMBERTON & DAUGHTER. Lead she had struck herself, holding to oak and brick, over a door that stuck.

Inheritance, it turned out, was also a thing you planted. A thing that stayed put and grew roots into other people's lives until pulling it up became impossible—until the town itself held the ledger's names in its mouth, speaking them every spring, and the regiment that had come to erase them now mustered to remember them, and the record survived not because she hid it but because everyone knew where it lay.

Her father appeared beside her, coat on, hat straight, ready for the walk home.

"Lock it," he said. "Early start tomorrow. That wedding party wants proofs by Thursday, and I promised Frau Krämer we'd print her a proper notice about the geese."

Beatrix turned the key. The lock caught, held, and the shop stood dark behind her—full of type, full of matrices, full of names—keeping its own counsel until morning, when she would open the door that stuck, and begin again.
