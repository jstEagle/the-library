## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The letter arrived at dawn, as the shelling paused for its morning courtesy.

Quentin Ishikawa stood in the chancellery's anteroom while the undersecretary — a gray man named Pell, whose hands shook only when he thought no one was watching — slid the envelope across the desk. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Wax the color of dried blood held the flap down, pressed with the mission's courier seal: a bridge over water, the republic's old promise that things could be carried from one shore to another and arrive unchanged.

"Final dispatch," Pell said. "You'll memorize the contents before you seal it again. Nothing written on your person. You understand."

"I understand."

"You understand what happens if this doesn't reach the harbor office by Thursday?"

"The extraction fails," Quentin said. "I'm still in the city when they come through the eastern gates."

Pell looked at her then, really looked, the way men look at furniture they are deciding whether to burn. "The codes inside are worth more than either of us. More than the whole chancellery, if I'm honest. There are people who will pay a great deal to know what's in that envelope, and people who will kill a great deal to keep them from knowing." He pushed it another inch across the desk. "Don't open it anywhere with windows."

* * *

She took it to the archive room because it was the only interior chamber without windows at all, a concrete throat of a room where the republic kept its dead paperwork, and she sat beneath the single bulb and broke the seal with a thumbnail.

The first thing she noticed was that the wax had already been broken once.

It was subtle. The kind of thing twenty years as a courier taught a body to see the way a jeweler sees paste. The fracture line ran clean through the bridge of the seal but the two halves had been married back together with a thread of fresh wax, slightly paler, applied by someone with a steady hand and insufficient patience. The original break should have been hers. The protocol was explicit: seals were broken only in the presence of the recipient, or by the courier herself under orders. Someone had read this letter before her. Someone had wanted to know what she would be carrying, and had decided she could be trusted to carry it anyway — or had decided nothing at all, and simply been careless, which was worse.

Quentin sat very still and let the dread settle into her bones like cold water finding its level.

Then she unfolded the page.

It was coded in the mission's third cipher, the one they called the Lamplighter, and she worked through it twice, once slowly, once fast, until the numbers lived behind her eyes independent of the page: grid references for the harbor office, the ship's false registry, the rotation of the checkpoint passwords through Thursday midnight, and a final sequence — nine digits — that she understood without being told was the abort signal, the word that would burn the whole network if it came to burning. She recited it forward. She recited it backward. She folded the page along its original creases, matched the halves of the broken wax, and dripped new sealing wax from her travel kit over the seam, pressing her own signet into it so that the bridge-over-water now bore a second impression, faintly doubled, like a thing seen through moving water.

Whoever had opened it before her had done it carefully. But careful hands left traces, and traces meant someone in the chancellery was already working for the other side — or working for themselves. In a dying republic those were often the same profession.

She put the letter inside her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a second heart.

* * *

The checkpoints began at the canal.

There were four between the chancellery and the river district, and each one had grown its own personality over the winter, the way siege conditions made everything organic and rotten at once. The first was manned by regular army boys who still saluted the old flag and mostly wanted cigarettes. The second belonged to the Interior Ministry and wanted papers, all papers, papers for existing. The third had changed hands twice in a month and nobody seemed sure whose side the men with the machine gun were on, including, possibly, the men with the machine gun. The fourth was new since Tuesday.

Quentin moved through them the way water moves through stones — around, over, wearing them down by patience rather than force. Her courier's pass was stamped in three colors. Her face was the face of a functionary, forgettable by design; she had spent two decades making it so. At the second checkpoint a lieutenant with a chilblained nose made her open her satchel and pawed through the routine dispatches — municipal water requisitions, a consular complaint about looted warehouses, the small bureaucratic sediment a collapsing state still produced daily, like a body producing hair and nails after death.

"Courier work," he said, not looking up. "Still running letters while the world ends."

"Letters outlast worlds," Quentin said. "That's most of what history is."

He grunted. He waved her through. Behind him, on the canal embankment, two soldiers were dumping something into the water and trying not to look at it, and she fixed her eyes on the middle distance and gave them their privacy, because that too was part of the discipline: seeing only what was safe to have seen.

* * *

The typefoundry stood on Voss Street, which everyone agreed was a coincidence and no one believed.

She passed it the way she always did — not slowing, not hurrying, her stride exactly the stride of a woman with deliveries to make. But her eyes went to the upper windows the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Smoke rose from the foundry's chimney, thin and ordinary, the smoke of a crucible melting type metal at precisely the temperature old Voss had insisted upon for thirty years. Through the ground-floor glass, lit warm against the gray morning, she could see the long benches, the racks of matrices, and — briefly, in profile — Mara Voss, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up with what looked like a pencil, lifting a mold with both hands as though it were something alive that might startle.

Quentin did not stop. Stopping was a data point. Everything in this city was a data point now, and someone was collecting them.

But her chest did the thing it did, the small contraction, the held breath of a woman walking past the only warm room in a freezing world, and she catalogued it and filed it and walked on, because that was the training, that was the whole architecture of her life: feel everything, show nothing, survive.

At the corner she allowed herself one glance back. Mara had come to the window — perhaps to shake a rag out, perhaps for air — and stood looking down the street with the unguarded expression of someone who did not yet know she was being counted. The light caught the metal dust on her forearms. She raised one hand, not quite a wave, not quite nothing — the gesture of a person acknowledging another person across a distance that had never been discussed.

Quentin lifted her satchel half an inch. A courier acknowledging a citizen. Deniable. Measured. Twenty years of practice compressed into four centimeters of movement.

Mara smiled, or the light made it look like smiling, and turned back to her work.

* * *

The routine dispatches took her until midafternoon. A consulate. A bank that was quietly converting its ledgers to another currency. A newspaper office where the presses had stopped and the editor received his final edition like a man receiving last rites, kissing the top page before locking it in a drawer. Each delivery was a small ritual of normalcy performed over an abyss, and Quentin performed them flawlessly, and hated how flawless she had become at pretending.

By four o'clock the shelling resumed, farther east, walking itself toward the rail yards in patient increments. By five the sky over the eastern districts had the bruised amber color it got when the refineries burned. The city had maybe days. Everyone knew it the way animals know weather.

Back in the safe flat above the shuttered pharmacy, Quentin sat with the letter on the table and studied the seal under the lamp, turning it, tilting it, reading the doubled impression like tea leaves.

Someone had opened it. Someone had read the codes. And whoever it was had then resealed the letter and sent it on to her anyway — which meant one of three things. Either they were loyal and checking her, or they were traitor and copying her, or they were frightened, and frightened people in collapsing capitals did the most dangerous arithmetic of all: they calculated what a sealed letter was worth, and to whom, and what it would buy when the flags changed.

She thought of Pell's shaking hands. She thought of the lieutenant at the canal. She thought of Mara at the window, metal dust to the elbow, smiling at nothing.

The codes sat complete and orderly behind her eyes, nine digits and forty references, a whole escape route folded into memory. The letter in her coat was just its shadow, its receipt, its proof of delivery. Deliver it Thursday and she boarded the ship Friday. Lose it and she burned with the archives.

It should have been simple. For twenty years everything had been simple, because she had made it simple: carry, deliver, forget, survive. No names kept that weren't necessary. No attachments grown that couldn't be amputated.

She looked at the seal again, at the ghost of the stranger's thumbprint pressed into the cooling wax beside her own, and felt the first cold thread of understanding pull tight:

Something had already gone wrong. Before the story began, before this morning, before the letter ever reached her hands — someone else had been inside it. And tomorrow, she decided, watching the amber light die over the rooftops between her window and the foundry chimney, tomorrow she would steam it open herself, and find out exactly what she had been carrying past all those guns.

The bulb flickered. Somewhere east, the shelling found the rail yards, and the whole floor trembled like a struck bell.

Quentin put the letter back inside her coat, against her heart, and waited for dark.
