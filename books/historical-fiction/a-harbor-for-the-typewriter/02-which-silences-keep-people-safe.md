## 2. Which Silences Keep People Safe

The typewriter was a Corona Four, and it had not needed repair for six years.

Quentin knew this because she was the one who had broken it — or rather, she was the one who had loosened the drawband just enough, in the winter of '43, to give herself a reason to come back. A courier learned early that a mission required a pretext, and a pretext, maintained faithfully over years, became indistinguishable from a life. She carried the little machine now in its dented case, its weight familiar as a heartbeat, through streets where the sandbags had begun to sprout weeds no one troubled to pull.

*Which silences keep people safe.* Her handler's old catechism. Aldric had drilled it into her the first month: *A silence is a wall or a window, Ishikawa. Learn which kind you're standing behind before you lean on it.* Twenty years of leaning, and she still could not always tell.

The foundry stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row like an old dog too loyal to leave a burning house. Its windows were papered now against blast — the city ordinance since spring — but light came through the seams, gold and steady, and beneath the shelling's distant arithmetic Quentin could hear the sound she had crossed half a city to hear: the click and fall of type being set by hand.

She stopped at the door. She always stopped at the door. It was a ritual without name — three breaths, the case handle shifted to her left hand, the right hand smoothing her coat front as if the letter there might show through wool. Then she knocked.

Mara Voss opened it the way she did everything, all at once, as if hesitation were a currency she refused to carry.

"The Corona," Quentin said. "You said—"

"I said nothing. You said." Mara stepped back to let her in, and her mouth did the thing it did — the almost-smile, banked like coals overnight. "Third time this year. Either your machines are dying of grief or you are."

"Both," Quentin said, and heard how easily the lie came, and hated how easily it came.

Inside, the workshop smelled of hot metal and linseed oil, of solder and the faint sweetness of the type-casting alloy — lead, antimony, tin — a smell Quentin had privately named *the smell of permanence*, because every letter cast here would outlive every regime that had ever tried to burn one. The matrices hung in their cases along the wall like a congregation. Her father's father had cut some of them; Mara said so the way other people said *my grandfather built this table*, with the plainness of people who have never had to hide what their families made.

Old Voss sat at the casting bench, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted silver. He nodded at Quentin without rising — courtesy rationed, these days, like everything else. "Courier girl. Tell them at the ministry we need brass. They keep promising brass."

"They keep promising everything," his son Emil said from the composing stone, not looking up. He was twenty-three and had his sister's dark brows and none of her mercy; he set type the way soldiers dug trenches, fast and flat and furious. "They promised the war would be short."

"This isn't war," Old Voss said. "This is weather. Wars end."

Quentin set the Corona on the workbench and opened the case, and Mara came to stand beside her, close enough that Quentin could feel the warmth of her arm without touching it. This was the geography they had negotiated over years, unspoken, unmapped: proximity without claim. A country of two inches.

"Show me what's wrong with it," Mara said.

"There's nothing wrong with it."

"I know." Mara's voice dropped below the men's hearing, below the tick of the composing stick. "There's been nothing wrong with it since 1943. You loosen the drawband. You're very good at it. I've watched you do it twice."

Quentin kept her hands moving, unscrewing the ribbon spool, buying seconds. "Then why do you let me keep bringing it?"

"Because you always fix something else." Mara took the platen from her, turned it, ran her thumb along the rubber. "Last time it was the escapement on Emil's Remington. Before that you re-soldered Papa's lamp. You come here to mend things you can admit to mending." She set the platen down and looked at Quentin straight on, which few people could bear. "What is it you can't admit to?"

Outside, a shell fell somewhere past the rail yards, and the jars on the shelf rattled their small glass teeth. Old Voss didn't even glance up. Emil swore softly and kept setting. The city had taught everyone which sounds deserved fear.

"My mother's name," Quentin said.

It came out before she had decided anything, the way blood comes out. Mara waited. She was good at waiting; she stood over a composing stone eight hours a day.

"It wasn't Ishikawa. That was my father's diplomatic name, the clean one, the one that opens doors. My mother was—" Quentin looked at the matrices, at the small reversed letters hanging in their ordered rows, each one a mirror-image waiting to be pressed into something legible. "Her name would have gotten us both moved east in '29. So I buried it. I was nine. I learned to say *Ishikawa* the way you learn a prayer, until I forgot it was anyone else's."

* * *

Vilna, 1929. The winter she turned ten.

Her mother had woken her before dawn, hands cold as keys, and dressed her in clothes that belonged to a girl who did not exist — good wool, foreign cut, a coat with no history in its seams. There had been a man from the consulate in the kitchen, smoking, patient the way officials are patient with furniture.

"You will be called Quentin," her mother said in the language they spoke only at home, the language that was itself becoming evidence. "Your father's name protects you. Mine cannot follow you. Do you understand? What you are about to forget, you must forget completely. Not hidden. Forgotten. A hidden thing can be found."

"But I'll know it," the child had said. "I'll remember."

"No." Her mother had knelt then, so their faces were level, and Quentin remembered — across twenty years and two wars — the exact geometry of that moment: the stove ticking as it cooled, frost ferns on the black glass of the window, the smoke curling up from the man's cigarette like a question no one would answer. Her mother had held her face in both hands.

"You will remember that you chose it," her mother said. "That will be enough. That will have to be enough."

And the child had chosen. She had stood in the consulate corridor while a clerk wrote *Ishikawa, Q., dependent of staff* into a ledger, and she had felt her mother's true name go out of her like heat from an open door, and she had not cried, because crying was a thing the girl in the ledger would not need to do. That night she practiced the new name in the fog of the train window until the letters came smooth. *Q-u-e-n-t-i-n.* By morning it was true. By spring it was the only truth she owned.

What she had never told anyone — not Aldric, not the archives, not God — was that forgetting had not taken. The name was still there, sealed and re-glued, carried against her heart like a dispatch she had never dared deliver. She had spent twenty years learning that a silence is not a wall. A silence is a room you lock yourself inside, and the key is the first thing you lose.

* * *

"You're somewhere else," Mara said.

"I'm here." Quentin fitted the drawband back onto its hook — snug, correct, a machine mended honestly for once. "I'm always here. That's the trouble."

Emil had drifted over with the pretense of a damaged quoIN, and now he leaned on the bench, watching them with the frank appetite of the young for other people's secrets. "You hear the lists are real?" he said. "My friend at the telegraph office says they've got names already. Teachers. Editors. People who printed the wrong pamphlets in '38."

"Emil," Old Voss said, without turning from the caster.

"Papa, someone has to say it—"

"The last time lists were real, I was carrying matrices across the border in a flour sack, and your grandfather's punches are on this wall because of it. Lists are real. Regimes are not. This one is already coughing blood." He pulled a fresh sort from the mold, blew on it, held it to the lamp: a small bright *e*, still warm, perfect. "Metal remembers, courier girl. That's the whole trade. Whatever they burn, we can cast again."

Quentin looked at the letter in his hands — the small warm letter, the most ordinary letter in the alphabet — and felt the sealed weight against her own ribs shift, as if the thing had turned over in its sleep.

"Mara," she said. "If you had to leave. If it came to leaving. Would the matrices go first, or the people?"

The workshop went quiet in the way rooms do when a question has landed too precisely. Emil frowned. Old Voss raised his head at last, and his eyes, in the lamplight, were the color of cooled metal.

"What a strange thing to ask," he said pleasantly.

"A courier asks about cargo."

"And a Voss answers about family." He set the *e* down among its brothers, in its case, in its place. "People first. Always people. Metal we can recast. We cut these once from nothing; we can cut them again. But a hand that sets type—" he looked at his daughter, at his son, and Quentin saw him see, briefly and terribly, everything Quentin could not say — "a hand like that takes twenty years to make, and there is no mold for it."

Mara had not taken her eyes off Quentin through any of it. Now she picked up the Corona and closed its case herself, gently, ending the repair that had never begun.

"Come Thursday," she said. "Bring the machine. Or don't bring the machine." A pause, measured out like lead into a mold. "Just come."

* * *

That night the shelling reached the gasworks, and the sky over the harbor went the orange of cooling bronze. Quentin sat in her room with the Corona between her knees, not working, listening to the city count its losses, and thought about her mother's hands, cold as keys, and about a small bright *e* still warm from the mold.

*People first,* the old man had said, and meant nothing by it, and everything.

She thought: *I have delivered eleven years of dispatches through checkpoints and sieges and I have never once opened one early, and tomorrow I am going to open the last one.*

Not for duty. Duty had given her the letter; duty could not tell her what was in it. But she had watched Mara's hands move over the composing stone today, sure and quick and alive, and she had understood with a clarity that felt like falling that some silences protect people, and some only protect the silence.

Tomorrow she would steam the seal. Tomorrow she would read what she had been carrying past all those guns, pressed against her heart like a second name.

The bulb flickered. In the east, the rail yards burned, and the light through her curtain was the color of type metal, cooling toward something permanent.

Quentin put the letter on the table in front of her, and this time she did not put it away.
