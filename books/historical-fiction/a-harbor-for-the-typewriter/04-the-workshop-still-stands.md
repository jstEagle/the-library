## 4. The Workshop Still Stands

The shell fell on the tannery district at half past six in the morning, close enough that the crucible trembled on its chain and a skin of lead skinned across the surface before Papa could skim it.

"Late again," said Emil, from the casting bench. "Even the artillery keeps better hours than you, Mara."

"I was up early," she said. "I heard it land. I counted to ten and went back to sleep."

"Liar."

"One hundred and ten," she amended, and he laughed, and their father told them both to hush or they'd spoil the pour.

It had become the family liturgy: joke first, work second, worry never — at least never out loud. The shelling came most nights now, walking itself closer in a slow circuit around the old city like a drunk looking for his street. In daylight the capital still pretended. Trams ran, badly. The bakery on Vodna Street opened at seven, and if its windows were boarded you could smell the bread through the gaps. Papa shaved every morning with cold water and complained about the price of soap as though prices were the war's worst crime.

Mara had stopped arguing with him about the lists.

* * *

They were casting fourteen-point this week — an order from the university press for a run of examination papers that would probably never be sat, but the order was paid, and paid work was a kind of prayer. Her father worked the caster the way he'd worked it for thirty years, thumb on the lever, eyes on the metal, reading the flow of lead into the moulds the way other men read weather.

"The matrix is chattering," he said without turning. "Third row. See to it."

She saw to it. She loved this about the work — that it could not be lied to. A type either cast true or it didn't; a letter either stood square on its foot or it wobbled through a whole page of lies. You could not charm a crooked sort into straightness. Out there — she tipped her chin toward the shuttered window, toward the city with its queues and rumors and the new posters going up on the ministry walls — out there everything bent. In here, only the lead mattered, and the lead was honest.

Emil was sorting the morning's yield into the job case when he said it, lightly, the way you'd comment on the weather: "Tomasz says there's a list. Names. People who'll be picked up after the transition."

"There's always a list," Papa said. "There have been lists since the Romans. Nobody reads them because nobody can spell."

"These are typed," Emil said. "He says the clerks are already copying them into the registry. He saw one. Our name might be on it."

"Our name." Papa pulled the lever. Lead hissed into steel. "Voss. Half the country is named something like Voss. And why would a government arrest the men who make its letters? Who prints their proclamations, their banknotes, their glorious five-year plans?" He shook his head at the crucible. "You don't burn your own library, boy."

"You do," Emil said quietly, "if you're writing a different book."

Mara kept her eyes on the matrix rack. She had learned to listen to her father's refusals the way she listened to the shelling — noting where each one landed, measuring the distance, saying nothing. He wasn't a fool. He was a man holding a foundry together with his hands, and a belief with his teeth, and if he let go of either one the whole day collapsed. So she said nothing, and Emil said nothing more, and the caster went on making its small iron music: clack, hiss, trim, drop. Clack, hiss, trim, drop. Fourteen-point, honest as bread.

* * *

The courier came on Thursday.

Mara heard the bell over the door and knew the step before she looked up — quick, even, boots worn at the outer heel, someone who walked cities for a living. Quentin Ishikawa. Though that wasn't how anyone here thought of her; she was the courier woman, Miss Ishikawa, the one who'd brought the portable machine in last month with a jammed escapement and a story about a cousin who owned it. Mara had fixed the cousin's typewriter in an afternoon. There had been no charge, and Quentin had insisted on paying anyway, and the argument had ended with two cups of terrible coffee and forty minutes of talk about type slugs, of all things, which should have been dull and was not.

That was three weeks ago. She had come back twice since. Repairs, always repairs. This time it was a platen, she claimed. Mara looked at the machine on her bench and thought: there is nothing wrong with this platen. But she said, "Give me an hour," and Quentin said, "I have nowhere to be," and that was strange too, because couriers always had somewhere to be.

Her father liked Quentin, in his gruff way. "A working woman," he'd pronounced, approvingly, the second visit. "She carries things for the legation, and she doesn't chatter." Mara's brother had made a face behind Papa's back that meant *ask her to dinner*, and Mara had thrown a piece of quoin at him.

Today Quentin didn't sit. She drifted along the wall where the specimen boards hung — a century of faces cast in this room, every weight from six-point pearl to sixty-line wood type — and read them like scripture.

"My father cut some of these himself," Mara said, coming to stand beside her. "Before I was born. That one—" she touched a serifed capital, "—he was seventeen."

"It looks like handwriting," Quentin said. "But decided. Like handwriting that has made up its mind." Her fingers hovered near the letter but didn't touch it. Careful hands. Mara had noticed that before; the courier handled everything as if it were evidence.

Then, without turning: "If you had to leave. Quickly. What would you take?"

Mara blinked. "From the shop?"

"From anywhere. Say the trains stop. Say the border—" A pause so brief it was almost invisible. "Say you needed to be somewhere else by Friday. What goes in the case?"

"The matrices," Mara said, before she'd decided to answer. "Papa's punches. Some of them are the only cuts left in existence — foundries burned in the last war, designs lost. If those go, a hundred years of letters just... stop existing. No one would ever set a line in them again." She laughed a little. "Ask me what I'd take for myself and I'd say my coat and Emil, in that order."

Quentin did laugh then, but it was a strange sound, thin at the edges, like paper tearing quietly. "In that order," she repeated.

"Why do you ask?"

"Curiosity." She turned back to the specimen board. "Occupational. Couriers spend their lives thinking about what can't be replaced."

* * *

That evening Mara mentioned it at supper — the question, the hovering, the hour spent not repairing a platen that needed no repair.

"She's thorough," Papa said. "Legation people are. They check everything twice."

"She asked what we'd carry out of a fire, Papa."

"People ask strange questions in wartime. It passes the time." He buttered his bread with the same deliberation he gave everything. "Besides, she likes the shop. Everyone likes the shop. Letters are the last civilized thing left in this city."

Emil said nothing at supper, but afterward, on the stairs, he caught Mara's sleeve. "I asked Tomasz to get me a copy of the list," he said. "Whatever it costs."

"And if our name is on it?"

"Then we'll know what to pack." He tried to smile and couldn't quite finish it. "Matrices first. Apparently."

* * *

Friday brought the shelling close again — the railway yard this time, close enough that the windows breathed inward and outward like lungs, and Papa stood in the doorway between the shop and the house with his hand flat against the frame, feeling the building's pulse, and said, "Solid brick. She'll outlive all of us," and for once nobody joked.

Saturday the courier came again.

This time Mara watched her properly, the way she'd been learning to watch her for three weeks and hadn't let herself admit she was doing it. Quentin stood at the bench while Mara stripped the platen — finally admitting aloud, with a grin, that there was nothing wrong with it, and watching something flicker across Quentin's face that might have been relief or might have been shame — and Mara catalogued what she saw. The courier's coat was the same as always, gray, serviceable, buttoned to the throat. But underneath the competence she had begun to look like someone carrying water in both hands across a long room, careful not to spill, terrified of spilling. Her eyes went to the door whenever footsteps passed on the cobbles. When Emil came down from the casting loft she started slightly, and covered it by asking him about the caster, and Emil, who could talk about casters until the sea froze, happily obliged, and Quentin nodded in the right places while her gaze kept returning to the racks of matrices on the far wall — counting them, Mara thought. Or memorizing them.

"Miss Ishikawa," Mara said, when Emil had gone up again. "Why do you keep coming back?"

"Your repair work is the best in the city."

"The machine was fine."

"Then perhaps," Quentin said, meeting her eyes at last, and there was that carefulness again, that terrible composure with hairline fractures running all through it, "I wanted to know what a room full of letters sounds like when it's being honest."

Mara should have laughed. It was the kind of thing you laughed at. Instead she heard herself say, "Something's wrong. Isn't it. Something you know."

The bell over the door rang. A customer — Mrs. Brandt, wanting a wedding invitation reprinted, insisting that weddings must go forward, that civilization depended on it — and the moment broke apart like type dropped on stone, scattering, unrecoverable. Quentin paid for the repair she hadn't needed. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at Mara, and at the racks of shining sorts, and at old Voss bent over his caster in the lamplight, and her mouth opened on whatever it was —

— and closed.

"Not yet," she said. Almost to herself. Then, louder, lighter, a courier's voice, a mask sliding home: "Next week. I'll bring the cousin's machine again. It develops troubles remarkably fast."

The bell rang. She was gone.

Mara stood in the middle of the shop, in the smell of hot lead and oil, listening to the caster go — clack, hiss, trim, drop — and felt for the first time that the sound was not a heartbeat but a countdown. Somewhere out in the darkening city, shells were finding their streets, lists were finding their clerks, and a woman who walked like the world weighed nothing had just looked at her father's workshop the way people look at houses they are saying goodbye to.

Upstairs, Emil's chair scraped. Down the street, Mrs. Brandt's wedding plans rustled on. The lead cooled in its moulds, each letter standing square and honest on its foot, waiting for words that no one had yet dared to set.
