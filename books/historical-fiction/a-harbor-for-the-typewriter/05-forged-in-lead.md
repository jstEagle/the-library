## 5. Forged in Lead

The typefoundry's back room smelled of hot metal and lamp oil, and it was there, on a workbench meant for trimming casting sprues, that Quentin Ishikawa committed her first treason.

She had told herself it would feel like falling. It did not. It felt like typesetting.

Mara had left the portable press unlocked — a trust Quentin had not asked for and could not refuse. The sorts were arranged in the wooden cases by a logic older than any alphabet she'd been trained in: capitals above, lowercase below, the small letters nested like seeds. Her own mission's cipher wheels were elegant, classified, expensive. This was better. This was a machine built by a man who believed every letter deserved to be cast true.

She set the first line slowly, one character at a time: **MINISTRY OF THE INTERIOR — EXIT PERMIT**. Her fingers remembered the trade from a life she'd buried under three other names. In 1936, in another collapsing country, she had spent four days in a print shop in Trieste learning to justify a line of type so she could pass as the printer's niece while waiting out a warrant. She had never touched a composing stick since without her hands remembering.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere beyond the wall, artillery spoke its flat, indifferent syllables, and the windowpanes buzzed but held.

*You are a courier,* she reminded herself. *Couriers do not forge documents. Couriers memorize, carry, deliver, and vanish.*

But couriers also read what they carry, and she had read it, and now there was a list in her head that would not leave: Voss, Emil; Voss, Mara; Voss, Daniel. Detain upon consolidation. Priority: exemplary.

*Exemplary.* As if a family could be a lesson taught to the others.

She inked the form she'd stolen from the Interior Ministry's overflow bins — genuine paper, genuine watermark, only the names and numbers false — and pulled the lever. The impression bit clean. Mara's father's type was better than the government's.

Quentin looked at what she'd made and understood, with a strange clarity, that she had already chosen. Everything after this was just logistics.

* * *

The border clerk's name was Ferenc Halas, and he kept his office in a customs shed near the east quay where the smell of bilgewater overpowered even his cigarettes. He was fifty-ish, soft-jawed, with the eyes of a man who had learned long ago that regimes were weather and he intended to be an umbrella.

"You want six exit visas," he said, not looking up from his ledger. "For a family of metalworkers. During a siege."

"Three," Quentin said. "And two are already processed. I need the third corrected — a birthdate error — and I need transit stamps for the river route before the bridges close."

Halas turned a page. Turned another. "River route is restricted to diplomatic personnel."

"I am diplomatic personnel."

"Diplomatic personnel don't come to me through the fish market." He smiled without warmth. "Diplomatic personnel send aides."

"Then consider this a personal errand of mine, and my gratitude a personal matter." She set a small oilcloth packet on his desk. Inside: American cigarettes, a wristwatch with a cracked crystal that still kept perfect time, and forty grams of medical morphine in a pharmacy tin — currency in a city where the hospitals had run dry weeks ago.

Halas looked at the packet the way other men looked at their children. Then he looked at her. "This isn't for you. You've got your own way out. Whoever these papers are for — you're paying like it's blood."

"Metalworkers are hard to replace."

"So are couriers who ask too many questions of clerks." He tapped ash into a saucer shaped like a sailboat. "I'll do the stamps. But the correction has to go through verification, and verification is run by Colonel Brandt's office, and Brandt signs nothing these days except detention orders." He let that word sit between them. "Unless someone vouches."

"I'll vouch."

"With what name?"

The question landed precisely where he aimed it. Quentin kept her face still. Twenty years of practice, holding a face still. "With the mission's name. The republic still honors its couriers, Ferenc. Even dying republics pay their debts."

He grunted, pulled a rubber stamp from a drawer, breathed on it like a man warming his hands, and began. Watching him work, Quentin felt the recklessness rise in her like floodwater — not fear, exactly, but a kind of drunkenness. Each stamp was a door opening. Each door opened with money and lies was a door the new regime would find sealed against it. She was using the machinery of her duty — the access, the cover, the immunity of the courier's pouch — to dismantle the very state that duty served.

It should have felt like betrayal. It felt like setting type.

"One condition," Halas said, sliding the stamped sheet across. "When this city falls — and it will fall, girl — you were never here, and I was never generous. I have a family too."

"Don't we all," Quentin said, and took the paper.

* * *

Aldric summoned her at seven that evening to the consular annex, a villa on the hill whose windows had been crisscrossed with tape against blast. He received her in the study, standing at the mantel, a glass of the consulate's terrible brandy untouched beside him. He was sixty, precise, gray in the way that stone is gray — not aged so much as worn smooth by decades of handling secrets.

"The final dispatch," he said. "Its condition."

"It's intact." The lie came out level, unhurried. She had rehearsed it in the tram, watching the ruined boulevards slide past. "The seal shows old damage — someone broke and reglued it before it ever reached me. I noted it in my report. But the contents are secure. I've memorized them as ordered."

"And the seal itself?"

"Untouched since it came into my custody."

Aldric regarded her for a long moment. Outside, a searchlight swept the hillside and slid away, indifferent. "Do you know why we break seals and re-glue them, Ishikawa? Before handing them to people like you?"

"To test the courier."

"To test whether the courier notices." He picked up the brandy, didn't drink. "A courier who doesn't notice is useless. A courier who notices and says nothing—" He paused, and the silence had teeth. "Is either very honest or very compromised. I haven't decided which you are."

"The seal was damaged before me. That's the truth, and it's all the truth there is."

"Mm." He set the glass down. "The letter goes aboard the *Corvina* with you when she sails. Not before. Not separately. You understand — in these last days, we trust no channels but flesh."

"I understand."

At the door, he added, without turning: "Your mother tongue. When you count under your breath — you do it when you're nervous, did you know? — it isn't French, and it isn't English. Curious thing, a courier with a buried language."

Quentin walked down the hill through the blackout with her heart going like a telegraph key. He knew something. Perhaps not everything — Aldric traded in fractions, always — but enough to hold over her, the way a man holds a match over a fuse to see which way the wind blows.

Let him look, she thought. Let him dig. By the time he found the name she'd buried in 1929, she would be gone, and so would the Vosses, and the letter in her coat lining would be worth exactly the paper it was written on.

The recklessness had stopped feeling like a choice somewhere on the walk down. It felt like gravity now — inevitable, directional, pulling her toward the harbor.

* * *

She worked through the night in the foundry's back room, and it was there, near dawn, that Mara found her.

Not Mara herself — not yet. First it was the sound of the street door, the particular creak of the third stair, and then Mara stood in the doorway in her father's oversized cardigan, hair tied back with a strip of lead foil, looking at the spread of forged papers, the composing stick, the press, and Quentin's ink-stained hands.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. The lamps burned low. On the bench, half-finished, lay an exit permit bearing the name *Voss, Mara* in type cast by her own father's hands.

"You said you were repairing the portable," Mara said quietly.

"The portable didn't need repair."

"No." Mara came closer, picked up the permit, read her own name on it. Her thumb moved across the printed letters the way she touched fresh castings — checking the edges, testing the weight. "These are good. Better than good. Where did you learn—"

"Another country. Another war. Another name." Quentin heard her own voice and was startled by how tired it sounded, how un-courier-like. "Mara. There are lists. Your family is on them. I can't tell you more than that yet because telling you more endangers you faster than the lists themselves. What I can tell you is that by Friday, your father will be on a barge with the matrices, your brother will be overland through the pass, and you—" She stopped.

"And me?"

"You'll be on the *Corvina*. With me."

Mara set the permit down with great care, the way she set down type she didn't want to pie. "How long have you known?"

"Two days."

"Two days." She repeated it without inflection, which was worse than shouting. "And in those two days you sat in our kitchen and ate my mother's recipe off my father's stories, and you watched me cast sorts, and you asked about the shelling like it was weather."

"Yes."

"Why?" And then, before Quentin could build a wall of explanation: "Not the lying. I understand the lying — everyone lies now, it's practically municipal policy. I mean why us? Why spend whatever you're spending — and I can see you're spending it — on a family of typefounders?"

Because I have loved you for six years and never once used your first name aloud where anyone could hear it. Because twenty years ago I erased myself to survive and I have regretted it every single day since, and I will not watch you be erased.

What Quentin said was: "Because letters matter. Because someone has to keep the words alive when the men with lists come for the people who make them."

Mara studied her for a long time — the same searching look she gave a suspect font, hunting the flaw. Then she rolled up her sleeves and pulled a second chair to the workbench.

"Then teach me the justification," she said. "If we're forging my family's salvation, we're going to do it properly. Papa would never forgive crooked margins."

They worked until the windows went gray. And it was in that gray light, Mara's shoulder warm against hers, that Quentin heard the footsteps on the third stair — heavy, unhurried, and wrong — and saw Aldric's shadow fill the doorway before either of them could move.

He looked at the press. At the permits. At the two women sitting side by side in the lamplight among the scattered treasons.

"Well," Aldric said. "Now I have decided which one you are."
