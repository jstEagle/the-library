## 6. What the Handler Knows

The tea in Aldric's office was always too hot and never drunk. Quentin had learned that years ago — the cup was furniture, a prop in the theater of a man who wanted you to believe he had all the time in the world. He sat behind his desk now with his hands folded, gray suit, gray face, gray eyes that were not gray at all when they chose not to be.

"You've been busy," he said.

"I've been working." She kept her coat on. The room smelled of coal smoke and old paper, and beyond the shuttered window the city's artillery spoke in its new, shorter sentences — closer every day, the intervals shrinking like a pulse quickening toward the end.

"Working." He turned a paper on his desk one quarter-turn, so it faced her. It was a requisition log from the border bureau. Her name — *Quentin Ishikawa*, the name she wore like a borrowed coat — appeared on it three times in eight days. "Exit endorsements. Transit passes. Blank stock drawn against the harbor authority's seal."

"Routine courier documentation."

"Three times in eight days is not routine. Once is routine. Twice is diligence. Three times—" he folded his hands again "—is a woman building something."

Outside, a shell landed somewhere past the river district, and the tea trembled in rings across its surface. Neither of them looked at the window. Looking would have been an admission that the world outside still mattered.

"The mission concludes in four days," Aldric said. "One letter left to move. You deliver it, you board the *Corvane*, you are on open water by Thursday night. Twenty years of service, closed out clean." He paused. "Unless something has happened to that letter."

Quentin felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it did on ships before you could see the water move. She had re-glued the seal herself, had matched the wax with candle shavings and a coin warmed at the stove, had practiced the fold until her hands knew it blind. But Aldric had been reading dispatches since before she'd learned to hold a pen steady, and he had just told her, without telling her, that he knew.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," he agreed mildly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Aldric watched her sit very still, the way she always did under pressure — not frozen but *economized*, every motion stripped to what was necessary. He had trained half that economy into her himself, back when she'd come to him at nineteen with forged papers and a face that gave away nothing because it had already given everything away once and survived.

He opened the second drawer of his desk and took out a folder that had traveled further than most diplomats. Inside: a birth register page from a coastal province, brittle, stamped with a customs house that no longer existed. A school ledger. A photograph of a girl of perhaps eleven standing beside a woman in a printed dress, both squinting into sun.

"Your mother had good cheekbones," he said. "You have them too, under the right light. This light would do."

"Those documents are forgeries," Quentin said.

"Of course they are. I made two of them myself." He let that settle. "But the register is real. I checked it in '31, when you first came through my office, and I have checked nothing since, because there has never been a reason to. Until this week."

He slid the photograph across the desk. The girl in it stood with her weight on one hip, chin lifted, already refusing to apologize for existing. The name written beneath it in faded ink was not *Quentin*. It was not even close to *Quentin*.

"You buried her well," he said. "Accent, papers, habits — twenty years, and I'll grant you've never once slipped. Not in drink, not in fever, not in '43 when we lost the safehouse and you went three days without sleep. That kind of discipline isn't training. That's grief." He leaned back. "Someone made you bury that name, and you buried it so deep you built a person on top of the grave."

The shells had stopped. In the silence, the building's pipes ticked as the boiler cycled, patient as a clock in an empty house.

"What do you want, Aldric?"

"The letter. Intact. Sealed as issued, contents unexamined — or examined and resealed so cleanly that no one in the capital will ever know the difference. You hand it to the naval attaché on the quay Thursday morning. That's all."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, I develop a sudden blindness." He tapped the folder without opening it further. "The exit papers. The blank stock. Whatever names are on them, whatever family they carry out — I saw a clerk's ledger yesterday and I will see nothing tomorrow. And this—" he nudged the photograph another inch toward her "—stays in my drawer until the day I die, which, given the current trajectory of this city, may be sooner than either of us plans."

* * *

She walked afterward through streets that no longer pretended to be ordinary. Shopfronts boarded in patterns that had stopped being temporary. A queue outside the bakery that moved with the grim orderliness of people who had decided panic was a luxury. Somewhere behind her, the typefoundry's chimneys still put up their thin gray threads, and she did not let herself look, because looking had become a tell.

*Bargain*, she thought. *Deliver the letter and save them.*

Except the letter named them. Delivering it intact meant handing the regime its own list, confirmed, authenticated, timed — the Vosses' names traveling under her seal to the men who would knock on their door within the month. Aldric's blindness would last exactly as long as it suited him, and his protection was a drawer, not a wall. One photograph. One register page. Enough to hang her; enough, if he changed his mind or his masters, to hang everyone whose papers bore her forgeries.

And yet — if she refused, if she ran, if she burned the letter in a wastebasket tonight — the extraction died with it. No ship berth. No harbor clearance. The *Corvane* would sail without her and without the case of type and without Mara's father's hands cupped around the matrices like a man carrying embers.

He had built the trap carefully, her handler. Twenty years of knowing her, and this was what the knowing was for.

* * *

That evening she went to the workshop under cover of returning a repaired escapement. Mara was alone, sweeping metal shavings into a dustpan, sleeves rolled, forearms smudged silver to the elbow. Lamplight caught the cases along the wall — dozens of drawers of lead letters, a whole civilization filed alphabetically.

"You're doing it again," Mara said, without looking up.

"Doing what."

"Lingering. Asking questions with your eyes instead of your mouth." She set the dustpan down and faced her. "My father says you're just a careful courier. My brother says you're a spy. I think you're someone who keeps almost saying something and then doesn't."

The words landed where Aldric's photograph had landed — in the same bruise. Quentin held out the escapement wrapped in cloth, and when Mara took it their fingers overlapped on the fabric for one breath longer than repair work required.

"If I asked you to trust me," Quentin said, "in the next few days, without explanations — could you?"

Mara studied her. Behind her the furnaces ticked as they cooled, and the smell of hot metal hung in the air like a language only this room still spoke.

"My father trusts lists more than people," Mara said. "I'm the opposite. So don't make me choose yet. Just — whatever you're not saying, say it soon."

* * *

Aldric spent the evening of the same day writing a report he did not send.

He wrote it longhand, in the cipher only his opposite number in the capital could read, and he wrote it in conditional tense: *Courier may be compromised. Letter's integrity uncertain. Recommend holding final extraction pending verification.* Then he read it back, struck a match, and burned it in the ashtray, watching the ink curl and lift.

Because the truth was simpler and uglier: he did not want the verification. Verification would force his hand, and his hand did not want to be forced.

He had recruited her at nineteen off a bench outside a burned consulate, a girl with false papers and nowhere to be anyone, and he had told himself for twenty years that he'd done it to save her. There was truth in that. There was also the fact that a courier who had erased herself once would never risk being erased twice — that fear, properly maintained, was the finest lock ever devised. He had kept her identity in a drawer the way other men kept blackmail, and had called it guardianship.

Now the city was falling, the mission was ending, and he found he wanted — not the codes, precisely, not the republic's dying secrets — but one clean act. One thing done correctly before the lights went out. If she delivered the letter, he could let the rest go. He had already decided that. He had known it since he laid the photograph on the desk and watched her not flinch.

What he had not told her — what he would keep in the drawer alongside the rest — was that the list in that letter had reached his own desk six days ago. That he had read the name *Voss* and understood, from three weeks of her requisitions, exactly what she intended to do about it. And that he had said nothing then either.

Everyone in this story, he thought, feeding the last ash to the match, was keeping a silence for someone else.

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless. At the harbor gate, gulls argued over the fish market's leavings, and the *Corvane* lay at her berth with steam already breathing from her stacks, impatient as a held breath.

Aldric stood by the customs shed with the final dispatch envelope in his inner pocket and the departure papers in his hand. Quentin walked toward him through the crowd — refugees, porters, a family pushing a piano on a cart because some things apparently could not be left — and he saw that she had chosen. He saw it in her walk before she said a word. She had the particular set of shoulders of a person who has decided what she is willing to lose.

"You have the letter," he said. Not a question.

"I have the letter."

"And the papers go to the Vosses."

"They board within the hour, or I don't hand anything over. Not to you, not to the attaché, not to God."

He looked at her for a long moment — the girl from the register, the woman she had built over the grave, the courier who had spent three weeks turning her mission's machinery against itself with a forger's patience. The shells had started again inland, soft thuds like doors closing in a distant house.

"One condition," Aldric said, and held up the departure papers between two fingers. "You give me the letter sealed, unopened by any naval man until the ship is underway. And you tell me the name. The real one. Not for the file — the file burns tonight. I want to hear it said once, out loud, before this whole country goes under. I want to know it survived."

Quentin stared at him. Of all the prices he might have named, this was the one she had armored herself least against.

"That's all?" she said.

"That's all." He almost smiled. "Consider it one silence returned."

Behind her, at the foot of the gangway, Mara Voss was waiting with a typewriter case in her arms, scanning the crowd. Quentin turned back to her handler, and her hand went into her coat, to the envelope, to the seal she had re-glued with candle wax and a warm coin — and inside it, to the decoy page she had slept badly beside for three nights.

"Then listen," she said quietly. "Before I was Quentin Ishikawa, my name was—"

And she told him.
