## 7. Moving Pieces

### Quentin

The father left on a Tuesday, when the shelling paused long enough for the trams to run.

Quentin had planned it that way — not out of superstition but arithmetic. Checkpoints thinned when the guns spoke and thickened in the quiet, soldiers emerging from their shelters like men remembering they had orders. So Anton Voss went out under the guns, an old man with a bad hip carrying a leather case of type matrices against his chest, dressed as a piano tuner, his papers identifying him as Emil Brandt of the northern provinces.

He had argued. Of course he had argued. He was a man who had spent fifty years making letters that would outlast him, and now she was asking him to abandon the foundry itself — the furnaces, the casters, the walls his grandfather had raised.

"The matrices are the foundry," Quentin told him. "The rest is metal and brick. Metal and brick can be replaced. You can't."

He looked at her then the way Mara sometimes did — as if measuring her for something, deciding what weight she could bear. "You've done this before," he said. Not a question.

"No," Quentin said, which was true, and also the most elaborate lie she had ever told.

She walked him to the freight yard herself, one courier among dozens in a city full of couriers, her diplomatic satchel slung across her body like armor. At the second checkpoint a lieutenant with wet eyes and shaking hands made Anton open the case. The matrices glittered — hundreds of small leaden faces, each one a letter, each one a small silvered death if the man understood what he was looking at.

"Piano parts?" the lieutenant asked.

"Type," said Anton Voss, because he was incapable of lying beautifully and chose instead to lie plainly. "For printing. I'm taking them to be recut in the north."

The lieutenant stared at the little letters a long moment. Somewhere beyond the river a shell fell, and the ground carried its argument up through their boots. The man waved them through. Perhaps he thought: printers are nothing. Perhaps he thought: I have a father too. Quentin never learned which. She only knew that she stood in the freight yard until the train swallowed the last carriage, and then she stood there longer, doing sums.

Two down. Three to go. And the hardest piece still sleeping every night above a workshop full of evidence.

### Mara

Her brother Tomas left overland, in the bed of a gravel truck, and Mara did not cry until after the truck was gone, which she considered a professional accomplishment.

They had rehearsed it in the back room among the paper stock, her hands black to the wrist with ink. Quentin had taught her the forger's grammar — how a genuine stamp sits at a slightly different pressure than a forged one, how borders age documents artificially with tea and a warm iron, how the most dangerous error is not a wrong name but a *careful* name. "Amateurs make everything perfect," Quentin said, guiding Mara's hand on the exit permit. "Officials don't trust perfection. Leave one flaw. Let them catch something small so they stop looking for something large."

"You learned this where?" Mara asked. "Couriers don't forge papers."

"Couriers carry everything," Quentin said. "Papers are just cargo that talks."

Mara let it go. That was the arrangement now, the strange treaty between them since the night Quentin had come to the workshop after closing and told them — part of it, a careful third of it — that names were on lists. Her father had called it panic talk until Quentin laid the letter's contents before him without opening it, reciting from memory, and watched the old man's face go the color of uncast lead. After that, no one argued about whether. Only about who first, and by what road.

Tomas went north overland because the trains were being searched for military-age men. He would cross the mountains with the gravel, walk two nights, and meet their father in Halvenstad. Mara had sewn money into his coat lining and pressed the family photograph into his hand, and he had grinned at her with his terrible confidence — *I'll be drinking your health in Halvenstad before you finish the next font* — and climbed into the truck bed like a boy going to a fair.

She cried after. Then she went inside and set type for four more false permits, because grief was a luxury with a delivery date, and hers wasn't due yet.

### Quentin

The informant problem arrived on Thursday, wearing a neighbor's face.

His name was Pell, and he kept the tobacco kiosk on the corner below the workshop, and for twenty years he had sold Anton Voss his pipe tobacco and commented on the weather. Now he stood at the corner watching the workshop windows with the particular stillness of a man being paid by the hour, and when Quentin passed he touched his cap and said, pleasantly, "Busy times at the foundry. Late nights. Lights burning."

"Rush orders," Quentin said. "Everyone wants printed things these days. Certificates. Permits." She watched his eyes flick to her satchel, to her face, back. Filing her away.

That evening she moved the forging operation. It was not enough — Pell had already seen too many late lights — but it bought days, and days were the currency the whole plan traded in. They worked now in the cellar of the chandler's shop three streets over, by agreement with a woman who owed Aldric a debt Quentin had quietly inherited, and Mara came through the back way each night with ink on her cuffs and questions in her mouth.

It was Mara who finally asked the one Quentin couldn't dodge.

They were alone in the cellar, the others gone home, a single lamp guttering over the drying permits. Mara set down her pen with the deliberate care of someone setting down a weapon.

"My father thinks you're a courier who grew a conscience. Tomas thinks you're in love with me and bad at it." She didn't smile. "I think you're both and neither. You knew what was in that letter before you opened it. You knew our names were coming off a list the way you know the time without looking at a clock. Nobody memorizes codes for a republic for six years and doesn't learn to read its rot. So tell me the rest. All of it. Because you're spending my family like coin, Lior—" and Quentin's whole body flinched at the name, the buried one, the dead one, spoken softly in a cellar by lamplight, "—and I won't spend mine blind."

The silence stretched. Quentin could hear the distant guns counting, patient, unhurried. She could lie again. It would cost almost nothing; she was expertly good at it; she had been lying since 1929, since the day her mother cut her hair and burned the papers and told her the girl she'd been had died of fever, and the survivor must never once, never ever, say her own name aloud.

"Mara Voss," Quentin said, "my name is Lior Ishikawa."

And then, because a name alone was just another sealed letter, she opened it. The consulate childhood. The purge that took her grandfather. The years of erasure — the new papers, the new accent, the self filed down and polished until it reflected nothing. The mission. The codes. The letter naming the Voss family, entrusted to her precisely because she was nobody, because she had made herself nobody, and the discovery that the republic intended to spend her silence on their detention.

"I was supposed to deliver that letter and disappear," Quentin said. "Delivering it saves my life. Burning it ends my career and probably my liberty. Every forgery I've made this week is treason. When this is over, if it's ever over, I will be a stateless woman with a dead name and a living warrant. I'm telling you this so you understand what I'm asking. Not trust. Trust is for people with options. I'm asking you to believe that I chose, knowing all of it, knowing the price, and that I'd choose it again tomorrow."

Mara was quiet a long moment. The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the river.

"Tomas was right about one thing," she said at last, and now she did smile, small and crooked and devastating. "You're terrible at it. At hiding. Twenty years of practice and you look at my father's matrices like they're scripture." She picked up her pen. "All right, Lior Ishikawa. Finish my mother's permit. She leaves Saturday, and she'll want to argue about the hat she's allowed to take, and you're going to sit there while she argues, because families argue. That's what you get for joining one."

* * *

### Quentin

Saturday morning the mother went west with the fish merchants, arguing about hats, and by Saturday night only Mara remained in the city that was no longer a capital of anything except its own ending.

They sat in the darkened workshop — one last night among the cases and casters, packing what could pass for luggage — when Quentin heard the step on the stairs and knew it before the knock. Heavy, unhurried, official.

Aldric did not wait to be invited. He filled the doorway with his coat still smelling of rain and government, and his eyes went around the room — the packed crates, the cold furnace, Mara standing very straight beside the empty type case — reading it all with the fluency of a man who had read a thousand rooms like it.

"Courier," he said. "We should talk about your final dispatch. Down at the harbor, tomorrow, before the ship sails." He smiled without warmth. "Bring the letter. Bring everything you haven't told me. And Miss Voss—" his gaze settled on Mara, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, "—do give my regards to Halvenstad. Your father travels well, I hear. As does your brother. As, I expect, will you."

The door closed behind him. In the silence, the shells resumed across the river, counting toward morning.
