## 8. The Last Dispatch

The harbor smelled of coal smoke and brine and fear, the way harbors do when everyone in a city is trying to leave at once and half of them know they won't.

Quentin came down the ramp road with the case of type under one arm — forty pounds of lead dressed as a courier's spare kit, Mara's father's matrices wrapped in oilcloth beneath the sorts, and her heart doing arithmetic she could not stop: *four hours to tide, three papers still unsigned, one letter that must be handed over intact or none of it matters.*

The *Corinthian Maid* sat low in the water at berth nine, a rust-streaked freighter taking on passengers who were officially cargo. Refugees were being counted like bales. A lieutenant with a clipboard moved down the gangway line, and behind him, at the foot of the quay where the lamplight pooled yellow and hard, stood Aldric.

He had not signaled her. He had not needed to. Twenty years of working for quiet men had taught Quentin that the worst meetings were the ones arranged without arrangements — the ones where the other party simply *appeared*, already knowing everything, patient as weather.

She did not break stride. Breaking stride was a confession.

"Courier." Aldric's voice carried just far enough. He was holding an envelope of his own, thin, unsealed, and he turned it over once in his fingers like a man shuffling a single card. "Walk with me to the end of the pier."

The pier ran out into gray water. The shelling had stopped an hour before dawn — the guns were saving their shells now, everyone said, saving them for the city itself — and the silence between reports felt less like peace than like held breath. Gulls worked the pilings. Somewhere behind them, a siren wound down mid-wail, giving up.

"The Voss woman," Aldric said, when they reached the rail. "Mara Voss. Type compositor. Father, Elias; brother, Tomas. Booked on this ship under names you purchased from Clerk Brenner for a sum I have already verified." He said it without heat, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "You've been busy, Ishikawa. Forging exit papers with foundry type. Lying to me about the condition of the final dispatch. Trading our mission for a family that isn't yours."

Quentin looked out at the water. The tide was coming in; she could see it moving along the barnacled legs of the pier, dark and unhurried, indifferent to regimes. Her hands, inside her coat pockets, rested against the letter — the true one, re-sealed with wax she'd matched from memory, and beneath it, folded flat, the decoy page she had written through the night in a cipher hand so careful it had felt like prayer.

"I don't know what you think—"

"Don't." Aldric let the word drop between them like a coin on stone. He opened his thin envelope and drew out a photograph, creased once, and held it where the lamplight from the shore could reach them both. "Harbor office keeps files. So does the old regime, and so will the new one. Do you want to tell me who this is?"

She didn't look. She knew what it was. There was only one photograph left in any archive that could hurt her: a girl of eleven on a dock in another country, another lifetime, standing beside her mother, both of them squinting into sun, both of them wearing the faces of people who would be dead within two years. People whose name Quentin had buried in 1929 with more care than she had ever buried anything since.

"You went digging," she said quietly.

"I went *checking*. There's a difference. Digging is curiosity. Checking is procedure." He slid the photograph away. "Here is what happens now. You give me the final dispatch, seal unbroken, codes legible, and I burn my copy of that photograph and lose my memory regarding four passengers named Brenner. The ship sails. Everyone lives. That is the whole of my offer, and I will make it exactly once, and then I will make a different call instead, to different men, who are less tired than I am."

The wind came off the water and pushed at her coat, at the weight of the type under her arm, at twenty years of discipline built like a seawall against exactly this moment. Quentin understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that she was going to lose no matter what she chose. That was what Aldric had never grasped, or perhaps had grasped too well: the trap was not designed to be escaped. It was designed to make you pay either way. The only question left was what you bought with the payment.

*Deliver it intact,* the courier in her said, the voice she had trusted since 1929, since the first time she erased herself to survive. *The mission is the mission. Your name is your life. Hand him the page and walk up the gangway clean.*

But she had spent six days setting type by lamplight while the city burned, and somewhere in those nights the voice had changed. It sounded like Mara now. It said: *a name is not a life. A life is what you carry out of the fire.*

"The seal," Quentin said, "is intact."

"Show me."

She took out the envelope. Heavy cream paper, the mission's crest embossed in dull gold, the courier's wax unbroken — her wax, laid over the seam where someone else's had been broken and re-glued weeks ago, a flaw she had discovered too late and reported to no one. Aldric turned it in his hands. His thumb found the edge of the flap and stopped there, resting, testing, a man weighing whether to open a door he suspected had already been opened.

"Tide's in ninety minutes," he said. "If the codes inside are wrong, or missing, or altered in any particular, the ship doesn't sail. Not the Vosses. Not you. Not the four hundred souls currently standing on that gangway believing they're cargo." He looked at her then, and there was something almost like grief in it, which was worse than anger. "I liked you, Ishikawa. You were the best of them. That's why this hurts."

"It shouldn't," she said. "You made your bargain. I'm keeping mine."

His thumb pressed the wax. The seal cracked cleanly down the center — she had tempered it thin for exactly that, so that breaking it would look honest — and he drew out the pages.

Quentin watched him read. She had learned, in twenty years of watching men read things, to read their reading, and Aldric gave her nothing: no flicker, no breath, no shift of weight. He was better at silence than she was. That had always been the trouble with quiet men; you could never be certain which silences kept people safe and which ones were simply waiting.

Inside the envelope, as far as any eye would ever prove otherwise, lay the final dispatch. Page one: the codes, complete, accurate, every cipher key memorized and transcribed in her own disciplined hand, because she had decided at three in the morning that she would not lie about *that* — the republic's secrets would die honestly, or not at all. Page two: the extraction protocol for herself, routes and safehouses, rendered useless by a single substituted date. And page three — the list, the terrible list, the names of the families to be detained when the regime consolidated, the Vosses third from the top in a clerk's indifferent typing —

Page three was gone. In its place, cut to identical dimension, aged with tea and pressed flat under the forge weights until it matched, was a decoy: a plausible continuation of routing instructions, bland as porridge, signed with a stamp she had cast herself from a matrix Elias Voss had cut without ever asking what it was for. The true page three was folded against her ribs right now, warm from her body, and its destination was not a ministry but a fire, as soon as she could feed it to one.

Aldric reached the second page and stopped.

The pause lasted four seconds. She counted them against her own pulse. Then he squared the pages, slid them back into the envelope, and held out his hand — not for the letter, but palm-up, open, waiting.

"The photograph," he said.

She gave it back. He tore it in half, then halves again, and dropped the pieces into the black water, where they spread and whitened and sank.

"Brenner's papers will be stamped within the hour," Aldric said. "Four passengers. I trust the count is correct."

"It's correct."

"And the letter goes forward with the morning pouch. Unread beyond this pier. If anyone asks, I inspected it and found it sound." He tucked the envelope inside his coat, and for a moment he stood looking out at the water the way she had been, and the wind moved both their coats, and they might have been colleagues at the end of an ordinary assignment. "Your mother," he said, without turning. "Her name. I looked it up out of spite, and then I sat with it all night, and I'll tell you what I concluded. Whoever she was, whatever we called her — she got you onto a boat once already. She'd have wanted you on this one."

Quentin's throat closed. She had not wept in twenty years; she was not going to begin on a pier, in front of a man who collected other people's names in files. "Thank you," she managed, and the words cost more than the forgery had.

"Don't thank me. I'm not kind. I'm tired, and the arithmetic favors you." He straightened his collar, resumed his shape as a functionary, a man made of procedures. But as he turned to go, he paused, and his voice came back quieter, almost lost under the gulls. "One thing, courier. The page you removed — whatever it says — burn it yourself. Don't trust it to any stove you don't stand over. Men like me check ash."

Then he was walking back up the quay toward the harbor office, coat buttoned, step even, carrying the mission's death inside his coat like a man carrying a lunch.

Quentin stood alone at the rail with the case of type and the folded paper against her ribs, and she waited until his figure was small, until it turned the corner of the customs shed, before she let her hands shake. They shook badly. They shook the way hands shake after holding a heavy thing perfectly still for a very long time.

Ninety minutes to tide. Papers to collect. A family to count aboard. And somewhere in the city behind her, troops were massing for the entry everyone could feel the way you feel weather in an old scar.

She lifted the case and started up the quay toward the gangway, toward berth nine, toward Mara, who would be waiting at the rail with her father's matrices and her brother's grin and questions Quentin had promised to answer once the water was under them.

Halfway there, a bell began to ring from the direction of the typefoundry district — not a church bell, not a warning siren, but the workshop's own alarm, the one Elias Voss had rigged years ago to call the men in early. It rang ragged, urgent, and then stopped mid-stroke.

Smoke was rising over the eastern rooftops, thin and black and already wide.

* * *

Quentin ran.
