## 9. Fire and Type

The troops came in at first light, and the fires came with them, but the workshop burned before either arrived.

Mara smelled it from three streets away — hot lead and pine resin and something sweeter underneath, the varnish her father had mixed himself for thirty years, the smell of every working morning of her life going up as smoke. She had left the house at dawn to fetch water and had turned the corner onto Gasse Street with two full pails and stopped so abruptly that a woman behind her walked into her back.

The windows of the typefoundry glowed orange. Not broken — glowing, lit from inside, as though someone had built a furnace out of the whole building and stoked it to its final heat.

"Quentin," Mara said, though Quentin was not there. Quentin was supposed to meet her at the corner of the fish market at six o'clock with the last of the papers, and it was not yet six, and the workshop was burning, and her father was already gone overland with the matrices sewn into his coat, and her brother was somewhere past the second checkpoint if he'd made the second checkpoint, and there was nothing left in this city now that could be saved except what they could carry on their backs.

She set down the water pails. She did not remember setting them down; she only remembered the sound they made, cracking against the cobblestones, spilling into the gutter, running pinkish through the ash already drifting down like filthy snow.

* * *

They had told her four days ago. Quentin had come to the house after dark, no pretext this time, no portable typewriter needing repair, no courier's errand, and stood in the kitchen with her hat in her hands like a woman at a funeral she couldn't name the deceased for.

"There are lists," Quentin said. "Your family is on one of them."

Mara had waited for her father to argue. He didn't. He sat very still at the table with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, looking at his own hands, which were stained the permanent gray-black of a man who had set type for half a century, and then he said, "Then we go," in the same voice he used when a job came in wrong and had to be redone overnight. No grief. No rage. Just the flat arithmetic of a craftsman: the work cannot be saved, save the tools.

Her mother would have argued. Her mother was nine years dead and buried under a stone that would soon be under other people's law, and Mara found she was glad, viciously glad, that her mother hadn't lived to see this.

"You knew," Mara said to Quentin, alone, by the stove. "How long have you known?"

"Three days."

"Three days." The word tasted like copper. "You stood in my father's shop and watched him show you the new sorts he cut, and you drank our coffee, and you knew."

"Yes." Quentin didn't flinch away from it. That was the thing about Quentin — she never flinched, and somehow it made everything worse, because you could see how much each non-flinch cost her, the way you can see the strain in a bridge. "I needed time to make the papers. If I'd told you sooner and the papers weren't ready—"

"You decided what we could bear. You decided for us."

"Yes," Quentin said again, and her voice finally cracked on it, one hairline fracture across all that discipline. "I've been deciding for twenty years what people can bear. It's the only skill I have. And I'm telling you now because I'm done with it. I'm done being the silence."

Mara had wanted to hit her. She remembered that clearly, the way her hand had curled, the way the stove ticked in the quiet. And instead she had said, "Then teach me the rest. Whatever's left to do — I do it too. No more deciding alone. Not even you."

And Quentin had looked at her then the way she looked at nothing else in the world, like Mara was a document worth forging her whole life to deliver, and said, "All right."

So for four days they had worked side by side at the workbench, cutting and casting under lamplight while the shelling came closer, printing exit visas on paper aged with tea, Mara's hands learning forgery as fast as her father's hands had once taught her letterforms. Four days. It should have been years. It should have been their whole lives, the two of them bent over the same small fire.

Now the shop where they'd done it was burning, and Mara was running toward it, because Quentin was not at the fish market, and Quentin had gone back for something.

* * *

She found her in the alley behind the foundry, dragging the type case.

Not a person. A case. Thirty kilograms of lead sort in a wooden tray with rope handles, and Quentin hauling it through the back door of the burning building with her collar black and her sleeves smoking, coughing in the wet rags tied over her face.

Mara grabbed the other handle without asking what was in it. She knew what was in it. Her father's emergency stock — the matrices too valuable to trust to a coat, the ones they'd agreed would be destroyed rather than carried, the ones Quentin had evidently decided otherwise.

"The stairs are gone," Quentin gasped. "I went up for the case and the stairs went. I had to come down through the casting room."

"The casting room—" Mara's voice broke on it. The casting room where her father had held her up to the crucible when she was seven, where she'd learned that letters were born liquid, poured molten into brass molds, that every word began as fire. "Is anyone—"

"Empty. Everyone who worked here got out weeks ago or left with your father's people." Quentin pulled the case another meter and staggered. Above them a beam groaned and shed embers into the alley like orange bees. "It's just us and the lead."

"Then leave it! Quentin — it's lead, it's heavy, it's—"

"It's your father's alphabet," Quentin said, and Mara shut her mouth.

They hauled it together, ten meters at a time, stopping to breathe in the smoke, and Mara understood suddenly why her father had refused to believe in the lists right up until the day he packed his coat. It wasn't denial. It was this: that a man's whole life weighs thirty kilograms, fits in a wooden tray, and burns in eleven minutes, and the mind simply refuses the arithmetic until the body forces it.

The roof went in while they were still in the alley. They heard it rather than saw it — a long exhalation, a collapse, a geyser of sparks standing straight up into the gray dawn like a tree of fire. Heat washed over them. Mara turned and watched through the smoke as the sign above the door — VOSS & SOHN, LETTERS OF EVERY TONGUE, her grandfather's lettering, gold on black — peeled and blackened and let go, crashing into the street.

Her brother's name was on that sign. Her mother's. Every birthday candle, every printed playbill, every funeral notice her family had ever set — the workshop had been the family's memory, externalized, in lead.

"It's only metal," Quentin said beside her, hoarse. "You told me once. Letters aren't in the lead. They were never in the lead."

"I know what I told you." Mara wiped her face and came away with it black. "I want it to be untrue today. Let me have today where it's untrue."

Quentin took her hand. Right there, in an alley, in a city falling to men with lists — took her hand, soot to soot, and held it, and said nothing, and for once the silence between them was not a hiding place but a shelter.

Then the church bell started ringing the wrong pattern — three-and-one, three-and-one, the alarm signal — and they picked up the case and ran for the harbor.

* * *

The streets were full of everything a city becomes when it ends. Soldiers in field-gray moving in loose files along the quays, faces young and bored and frightened. Civilians streaming the same direction with prams and birdcages and grandmothers' portraits. A man playing an accordion badly outside a shuttered café, playing it as though the music were a form of argument. Smoke lying over the rooftops in long gray ropes. Somewhere a looter's hammer, somewhere a scream cut short, mostly just the enormous indifferent noise of history changing hands.

They moved with the current, two more women among thousands, the type case between them disguised under sacking and a coil of rope. At the first checkpoint a boy soldier with a rifle too big for him waved them through without looking, because Mara was crying and crying women were everywhere and invisible. At the second, an officer actually looked — at the case, at their hands, at Quentin's courier's papers — and Quentin produced a dispatch folder and said words like *priority diplomatic traffic, harbor authority* in the flat bored tone of a woman carrying nothing heavier than paperwork, and the officer stepped aside, and Mara understood that she had spent four days watching a master at the one craft Quentin had never chosen: erasure.

At the third checkpoint, at the foot of Pier Nine, there was Aldric.

He stood apart from the soldiers, in a good coat that had gone shabby at the cuffs, holding his hat against the wind off the water. He looked exactly as he had in the handler's office the night he'd offered Quentin his bargain — patient, tired, a man who had run out of country ahead of his retirement. When he saw them coming he didn't signal the guards. He walked to meet them.

Mara's grip tightened on the rope handle. Quentin put her free hand flat against the case, between Aldric and the lead, a small gesture that was somehow both surrender and defense.

"Aldric," Quentin said.

"Miss Ishikawa." His eyes went to the case, then back. "The ship leaves in forty minutes. The manifest is with the harbormaster. Your papers are... sufficient." A pause, wind snapping the canvas of the pier awnings. "All of them. For everyone on your list."

Mara watched Quentin absorb it — that he knew, that he had always known, that the bargain had been kept from the far side too. "The letter?" Quentin asked.

Aldric reached inside his coat and drew out the final dispatch, the sealed one, the one whose contents had burned a city block of Mara's life down to this quay. He held it a moment, weighing it, and Mara saw something move behind his face — not regret exactly; something older and wearier, the look of a man auditing forty years of silences and finding the interest unpaid.

"The codes were verified," he said. "The mission is closed. What the mission contained beyond the codes—" he turned the letter once in his fingers, then handed it to Quentin, unopened, seal intact — "is no longer intelligence. It's just paper. Paper belongs to whoever carries it out."

"You're letting us go."

"I'm letting everyone go who's on this pier," Aldric said. "That's the entire extent of my authority now, and I intend to use all of it." He put his hat back on. As he turned away he paused, and said, without looking at either of them: "In 1929 a girl erased her own name to survive. I spent twenty years thinking that was a tragedy. Standing here, I think perhaps it was only a delay. Deliver yourself, courier."

He walked back down the pier into the smoke, and did not look around, and the soldiers at the checkpoint did not stop them, and Mara understood that a silence had just been returned to them like a debt paid in full by a stranger who owed it.

* * *

The gangway of the refugee ship was a river of bodies going the other direction from everything Mara had ever been. They climbed it with the type case swinging between them, and at the top a clerk with a manifest shouted questions over the wind — names, names, names — and Quentin gave hers, and Mara gave hers, and neither name was quite true yet, but both were true enough to board.

Below, the city burned. From the water it was almost beautiful, which was the most obscene thing Mara had ever seen — the old town a corona of orange, the foundry district a pillar of black, sparks rising and bending seaward like a congregation bowing. Somewhere in that light was the sign with her family's name. Somewhere in it was the bench where she'd set her first line of type at seven years old, crooked and proud. It was all going into the sky, and the sky was taking it, and there was nothing to do but stand at the rail with soot in her hair and a hundredweight of alphabet at her feet and weep without sound.

Quentin stood beside her and put the sealed letter into her hands.

"You read it," Quentin said. "On the ship. Tomorrow, at dawn, out here where none of it can follow us. Whatever it says — mission, confession, orders — it gets read aloud. No more sealed things. Not between us."

Mara looked down at the letter. Wax seal, courier's knot, twenty years of other people's secrets pressed flat under it. Then at Quentin, whose face was still black with smoke, whose eyes were the clearest thing on the whole burning coast.

"And what's in the case?" Mara asked, nodding at the type at their feet, because she couldn't yet say anything larger.

Quentin almost smiled. "A harbor," she said. "We're going to need letters where we're going. Someone will have to build the words."

The ship's horn sounded, long and low, and the gangway came up, and Pier Nine slid away, and with it the country, the lists, the bell still ringing its wrong pattern faintly across the water. Mara stood at the rail with the letter in one hand and Quentin's sleeve in the other, and behind them the old world gave itself entirely to the fire, and in the case below the deck, letter by letter, lead by cooling lead, the new one waited to be set.

Dawn was four hours away. The letter was in her hands.
