## 10. Her True Name

The ship had a name once — *Meridian Star*, painted in flaking gilt along her stern — but the harbor master's ledger listed her only as Vessel 44, refugee tonnage, and so that was what she became: a number carrying three hundred numbered people out of a country that had stopped answering to its own name.

Quentin stood at the rail long after the coast dissolved into haze. The smoke from the city still hung on the horizon like a bruise that wouldn't set, a brown-gray smear where the typefoundry had been, where the ministry quarter had been, where twenty years of her careful life had been. She kept waiting to feel the loss arrive. It didn't. What she felt instead was lighter and stranger — the sensation of a shoulder easing after years of carrying something she'd forgotten was heavy.

Behind her, the deck was settling into the rhythms of exile. Children chased each other between the lifeboats until their mothers caught them. An old man had already claimed a patch of sun and was mending a sock with the concentration of a surgeon. Someone had produced a harmonica and was playing badly, and no one minded.

Mara found her there. She always knew how to find her; Quentin suspected she'd known for years, back when knowing would have been dangerous for both of them.

"You haven't slept," Mara said.

"Neither have you."

"I slept two hours against a crate of my father's matrices, dreaming he was complaining about my kerning." Mara leaned her forearms on the rail beside her. Salt had stiffened her hair; there was a smudge of soot along her jaw that she hadn't noticed or hadn't bothered with. "He'll be in Rotterdam by Thursday if the trains run. Tomas is somewhere overland with a forged face and my mother's ring sewn into his coat. And we are here." She said it like a proof, like setting the last line of a page and pulling a clean proof. "We are actually here."

"We're here," Quentin said, because some sentences you repeat just to hear them hold weight.

Mara reached into her coat and drew out the letter.

It looked smaller than it had in the ministry safe, smaller than it had steaming over the kettle in Quentin's rented room while her hands shook and her whole future lay open on the table like a dissected thing. The wax seal was ruined — Aldric's thumbprint in it now, and before that Quentin's knife, and before that whoever had first broken it and re-glued it, the original sin that had started everything. The paper was warped from steam and seawater. But it was intact, and it was theirs.

"You could burn it," Quentin said. "No one would know. No one's left to check."

"I've spent three weeks not knowing things people decided I shouldn't know." Mara turned the envelope over in her fingers. "I'm done with sealed things. Besides—" she glanced sideways, and there was the ghost of the workshop smile, the one she used to give across the composing stone when her father said something pompous about the dignity of the trade, "—you said there was more in it than codes. You said it half-read like a confession. I want to know who wrote it. Anyone who chose love over duty in that building deserves to be remembered by somebody."

The dawn was coming up gray-gold behind them, lighting the wake. Below them, in the purser's office, a clerk was sharpening a pencil for the passenger manifest. Quentin could hear the scratch of it through the open port, absurdly loud, the most important sound in the world.

"Read it, then," she said.

* * *

Mara read standing up, facing the rail, her voice pitched low enough that only Quentin and the gulls could hear. The mission portion came first, dry as dust, exactly as memorized: cipher keys, contact schedules, the account numbers of a republic's last unspent loyalty. Then the tone shifted, mid-page, the way a hand changes when it stops writing for an office and starts writing for itself.

*"To whoever opens this — and someone will, someone always does —*

*You will have noticed by now that this dispatch serves two purposes, and that one of them cannot survive the other. I have written it this way deliberately. Codes can be re-cut. There is no second copy of what follows.*

*I was a courier too, thirty years ago, for a government that no longer exists and whose enemies I cannot even name without embarrassing myself. In those years I carried letters between two people who loved each other across a border neither could cross, and I read every one of them, and I never told anyone, and when the war came I burned them all rather than let them become evidence. I have regretted the burning ever since. Not the reading. The burning.*

*So understand what I am doing now. The list appended to these codes names civilians for detention. I have verified it. I could have separated it — sent the list under separate seal, delayed it, lost it in the way documents are lost — and perhaps saved myself the trouble of this confession. Instead I am sending it forward, because duty is real, and because I trust that whoever carries this will do with it what I could not do with those letters: choose.*

*A courier's whole art is silence. It took me thirty years to learn that silence is not neutrality. Whatever you decide, decide it as yourself. That is all the advice I have.*

*— E. Marchetti, Second Secretary (ret.)"*

Mara lowered the page. For a moment neither of them spoke. The harmonica below had stopped; somewhere a baby was crying with the tireless indignation of the very young.

"E. Marchetti," Quentin said. "I delivered his dispatches for six years. He used to give me peppermints and ask about my family. I told him I didn't have one." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "He knew. He must have known the whole time. He wrote this knowing whose hands it would end up in."

"He wrote it knowing it might end up in mine." Mara folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way she handled type. "Half mission, half confession. He built you a door and disguised it as a cage." She looked at Quentin then, fully, and Quentin made herself hold it. "You opened it before you knew any of this. Before the bargain, before the fire. You chose with nothing but the list in front of you and me on the wrong side of it."

"Yes."

"And you never once considered telling me. Three weeks of forging papers in my own workshop, using my own type, and you'd have put us on a boat and vanished at the gangway."

"Yes," Quentin said again. It cost more than the first time. "That was the plan. It was a good plan. It protected everyone."

"It protected everyone except you."

"The plan wasn't supposed to include me."

"No," Mara said softly. "It wasn't. That's the part I'm angry about. Not the lies — I understand the lies, I grew up watching my father lie to inspectors twice a week. I'm angry that you decided your life was the payment, and you never asked the rest of us whether we'd agreed to the exchange rate." She took Quentin's hand then, right there at the rail, in front of the mending man and the mothers and the sea. Her palm was ink-stained even now, some pigment in the creases that soap wouldn't reach. "So here is the new arrangement. No more silences kept *for* people. If you're afraid, say it. If you love someone, for God's sake say that too. We are refugees on a rust bucket in the North Sea. We have nothing left to protect except each other, and I intend to protect you loudly."

Quentin looked at their hands, at the small harbor of them, and felt the last of the old discipline go out of her like heat from cooling metal.

"I love you," she said. It was the easiest sentence she had ever spoken and it had taken twenty years. "I have loved you since 1943, since the air raid drill, since you fixed my typewriter ribbon upside down and blamed the manufacturer."

"The ribbon *was* defective."

"The ribbon was fine. You wanted an excuse to keep the machine overnight."

"I wanted an excuse to keep *you* overnight. The machine was incidental." Mara's grin cracked through the exhaustion, through the soot, and for a moment she was nineteen again across the composing stone, and the city behind them hadn't burned yet, and none of it had happened — and then the moment passed, mercifully, into the present, which was worse and better and real.

Below them, the purser's clerk called out in bored official French: *"Manifeste! Passagers, par ordre de liste!"*

* * *

The line moved slowly. Quentin watched it the way she'd once watched checkpoints — cataloguing exits, counting guards — and then deliberately stopped. There were no exits off a ship at sea. There was only forward.

When her turn came, the clerk slid the manifest toward her without looking up. A printed form. Columns for name, nationality, next of kin. At the top, in the clerk's indifferent hand: *Nom du passager, en toutes lettres.*

En toutes lettres. In full letters. In all letters.

Quentin picked up the pencil. Her hand did not shake. Twenty years ago, in a border town whose name she had also buried, a girl had learned that certain names got people killed, and had shaved herself down to a neutral shape — Quentin Ishikawa, courier, citizen of nowhere in particular, a woman built entirely of deniable gestures. That girl had been right. That girl had survived. Quentin honored her, the way you honor a soldier who held a position at terrible cost.

And then she did what E. Marchetti had asked. She decided as herself.

She wrote: *Ren Ishikawa.* Her mother's name. The name from 1929, from before, from the kitchen with the bad stove and the radio turned low — the name that had gone into the ground so she could live, coming up out of it now into the gray morning light, small and complete on the line. The pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath. Let them emboss it. Let it be evidence.

The clerk glanced at it, stamped it, moved on to the next column. Next of kin.

Quentin thought of her father's matrices riding east in a crate marked MACHINE PARTS. Of Tomas somewhere on a night road with a ring sewn in his coat. Of a workshop that existed now only as smoke and as the case of type sitting at Mara's feet, rescued sorts enough to set a single line, maybe two.

She wrote: *Mara Voss.*

The clerk read it, frowned faintly — kinship was usually blood, on these forms — and then looked up properly for the first time, at the two women standing together at the desk with their shared case of lead between their feet, and whatever he saw made him shrug and stamp it. The republic was dead; its forms were dying slower; somewhere in the gap between the two, people were quietly rewriting what kin meant.

"That's it?" Mara asked as they stepped aside. "Twenty years, and it's one pencil line?"

"It was always going to be one pencil line." Quentin looked at the manifest, at the two names stacked together in her own handwriting, the first honest document she had executed in her adult life. "Everything else was just learning to hold the pencil."

Mara crouched, opened the type case, and began sorting — quick, expert, her fingers reading the sorts by touch. "Help me set something. My hands need work or they'll start believing yesterday happened."

"What should it say?"

Mara looked up at her, dawn full on her face now, the smoke finally gone from the horizon behind them, only water ahead.

"Set my name," she said. "Then set yours. We'll lock them in the chase and carry them wherever they'll take us — proof, if anyone ever asks, that we were both here, under our own names, together."

Quentin knelt beside her in the lead-dust smell of home, and began to compose.
