## 1. The Seamstress's Silence

There were three hundred and twelve stitches in the collar of an occupation officer's dress coat, and Hazel Crane knew this because she had counted them, one winter evening when counting was safer than thinking.

She did not count them again. That was the point of knowing a thing once — you carried it with you, folded small, the way she carried everything: the number of steps from the workshop door to the harbor wall (ninety-one), the number of days since the republic's government had boarded a ship and left its people standing on the quay like furniture set out for a sale that never happened (forty-seven), and the number of people alive who knew what Hazel Crane did after the lamps went down in the workshop of Herr Lindqvist, Tailor and Supplier to the Diplomatic Service (one, and she saw her in the mirror every morning, and she did not entirely trust her).

The workshop sat on Rope Lane, which had once smelled of tar and ambition and now smelled mostly of damp wool and the cheap tobacco the occupation soldiers smoked while they loitered outside shops they had no intention of buying anything from. It was a narrow building, three floors squeezed into the space of two, with windows that fogged in the morning and a stove that had opinions about coal. Herr Lindqvist — seventy years old, half-deaf in the left ear, entirely deaf to anything he did not wish to hear — had run the shop since before the war, before the occupation, before the republic itself, back when the city had belonged to an empire that had also, in its day, believed it would last a thousand years.

"Crane," he said that morning, not looking up from his cutting table, "the Voss fitting is moved to Thursday. And you're to take the colonel's greatcoat in at the shoulders. He's been eating victory again."

"Yes, Herr Lindqvist."

"And stop saying yes to me in that voice. You sound like a woman agreeing to something terrible."

"I am a woman agreeing to take in a greatcoat."

He grunted, which from Herr Lindqvist was a full paragraph of affection, and Hazel took the greatcoat off its hook and carried it to her station by the north window, where the light was honest and did not flatter anyone's seams.

* * *

The morning passed in the ordinary liturgy of the shop. Pins in a pincushion shaped like a tomato that had lost its tomato-ness years ago and become simply a pincushion with ambitions. The kettle on the stove that whistled slightly flat, so that tea in the workshop was always a small disappointment. Marta Vinter at the next table, hemming trousers and humming something from before the war, off-key in a way that had become, through sheer repetition, almost musical.

"You're quiet this morning," Marta said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always quiet *loudly*. Today you're quiet quietly." Marta bit her thread and squinted at her. "Which means either you slept badly or you're thinking. And you never sleep badly. You sleep like a well-run shop — everything in its place."

Hazel smiled the smile she kept for Marta, which was warm and gave away nothing, and said, "The colonel's shoulders."

"Ah." Marta nodded with great seriousness. "The colonel's shoulders could darken anyone's morning."

And that was true, and also it was nothing, and Hazel had gotten very good at the difference. That was the first rule, though it had never been written down anywhere: *give away nothing, and give it away warmly.* People did not suspect a woman who laughed at their jokes about the colonel. Suspicion needed cold to grow. So Hazel kept herself warm the way a stove keeps a room — evenly, patiently, and with the fire hidden behind iron.

The colonel's greatcoat was a beast of a thing, double-breasted, with braid on the collar that had to be unpicked and resewn and unpicked again because the colonel, like all men who had never held a needle, believed that tailoring was a matter of opinion. Hazel worked on it through the morning with the automatic tenderness she gave all garments — even the ones she hated, even the ones that belonged to men she despised — because a seamstress's hands could not afford grudges. Grudges made stitches crooked. Crooked stitches got people noticed. And being noticed, in this city, in this winter, was a thing that happened to other people, loudly, and then did not happen to them at all anymore.

At noon, the soldiers came in.

They came in most days, not to raid — raids were rarer, and came with different shoes — but to be measured, or to complain about measurements, or to stand near the stove and take the warmth that the city's coal ration had stopped providing. This one was young, with a face like an unlined envelope, and he wanted his uniform jacket let out across the chest.

"Growing," he said, with a little laugh, as though his body were a joke someone else had written.

"Muscle," Hazel said, running the tape across his back. "Or bread."

"Bread," he admitted. "There's no bread at home. There's bread here. A man follows the bread."

"A man does," Hazel said, and wrote the number down, and did not think about the fact that the boy complaining about bread was wearing the uniform of the army that had emptied the granaries. Or rather — she thought about it, briefly, the way you touch a hot pan to confirm it's hot, and then she put it away. There would be time for feeling later. There was always later. Later was the one thing the occupation could not ration.

He left with his jacket and his joke, and Marta looked at Hazel over the tomato pincushion and said, "He's somebody's son."

"Everybody's somebody's son."

"That's what worries me about you, Hazel Crane. You say the truest things in the flattest voice."

Hazel laughed — a real one, which surprised her — and went back to the colonel's shoulders, and the afternoon wore itself down like a heel on a boot.

* * *

Herr Lindqvist left at six, as he always did, pausing at the door to deliver his nightly benediction: "Lock the door. Bank the stove. If the building burns down, I want it to be someone else's fault." Then his footsteps faded down Rope Lane, and the workshop belonged to the evening.

Hazel banked the stove. She locked the door. She drew the curtain over the north window — the one facing the street — and left the east window's curtain open exactly four fingers' width, because a window that was fully curtained every single night was a window that said *something is happening here*, and a window with a careless gap said *a tired woman lives here*, and tired women were the safest thing in the world.

Then she sat down at her own table, in her own corner, and took out the lining.

It was a coat lining, dove-grey silk, cut for a diplomat's evening coat — one of Herr Lindqvist's legitimate commissions, or legitimate enough. And sewn into the left front panel, in thread of the same dove-grey, so that the stitches vanished into the weave unless you knew where to look and what the stitches were for, was a message.

She had not written it. That was the beauty and the terror of her position: she did not compose the dispatches, she only carried them. The words came to her folded inside the linings of coats sent for repair from addresses she was not told, in a hand she had never seen, and her task was to read them — no. Not even to read them. Her task was to *verify* them, which was different, and then to sew them forward, into the linings of coats that would travel where cloth traveled and paper could not.

Paper was stopped at checkpoints. Paper was opened by men with unlined envelopes for faces. But a coat? A coat was a coat. The occupation searched coats for weapons, for papers, for bread. No one in the history of armies had ever searched a coat for its stitching.

The rules — the code-master's rules, though she had never met him, never seen him, knew him only as a voice on a scrap of silk once, and as the architecture of her nights — were seven, and Hazel knew them the way she knew the three hundred and twelve stitches of a collar:

One: never carry a message you have not verified against the key, and never keep the key anywhere a search could find it. The key lived in Hazel's head, in a form no examiner could extract — a sequence of stitch-lengths she had memorized as a hymn, long-short-long, the way her mother had once taught her a psalm.

Two: never carry a message twice by the same route. Coats went out through the diplomatic laundry, through the theater wardrobe, through a widow who sold buttons and asked no questions because she was paid in butter.

Three: never know the whole. Hazel carried the left shoe of the message, so to speak, and some other seamstress in some other city carried the right, and neither of them could betray what she did not possess.

Four: if you are taken, you are a seamstress. You have been a seamstress since you were nine years old. You know nothing but seams, and you will weep, and the weeping will be true, because it will be for yourself.

Five: trust the work, not the people.

Six: the work is not the cause. The cause may change, may fail, may be sold. The work — the truth, carried cleanly, delivered whole — is the only thing that does not rot.

Seven: *when it ends, put down the needle.* She had never been told when it would end. She suspected no one knew.

Hazel verified the message against the hymn in her head — it checked, long-short-long, all the way down — and then she did the thing she did every night, which was to unpick the dove-grey stitches and resew them into a different panel, a different rhythm, a different coat. The message would leave tomorrow inside the lining of a theater wardrobe mistress's traveling cloak, bound for Riga by way of a woman who believed she was doing a favor for a cousin.

It took her two hours. Her fingers knew the work so well that her mind was free to wander, and her mind, being a well-run shop, mostly kept everything in its place. But some nights — and this was one — a thought got loose among the shelves.

The thought was this: *the republic is gone, and no one has told the needle.*

The government had sailed in October. The code-master — Elias Brandt, though she only knew the name the way you know the name of an author whose book you live inside — the code-master had drowned the same month, so the network's last circular had said, a boat lost in the channel, a body never recovered, the work suspended pending reorganization. *Pending reorganization.* Hazel had read those two words eleven times. They were the kind of words that meant either *we are being careful* or *there is no one left to be careful with*, and she did not know which, and rule five said trust the work, and the work had gone silent, and a silent work was not a work at all. It was a habit.

She finished the last stitch, bit the thread, and held the coat up to the lamp. The lining hung smooth and innocent, dove-grey, unremarkable, carrying in its left breast the small stubborn truth of a city that had been told to forget itself.

"You'd never know," she said to it, and was immediately embarrassed, because talking to coats was the first symptom of the loneliness she did not permit herself to have.

She banked the stove again, lower. She washed her hands in the cold water at the back, watching the last of the dove-grey thread go down the drain — a habit, though the thread left no more trace than a whisper. She took the key — not a key, the *hymn*, the stitch-rhythm — and folded it away in the place where she kept her mother's psalm and her father's face and the sound of the harbor ice creaking in the winter she was six. Everything in its place.

Then she climbed to her room under the roof, which was cold in a way that had stopped being an insult and become a personality, and lay down, and slept — as Marta had said, like a well-run shop.

* * *

Forty-seven days the republic had been gone. Forty-eight, when Hazel woke to a morning that felt wrong before she knew why.

She knew why by the time she reached the workshop door: the shoes. The soldiers who loitered wore boots, heavy, bored. The shoes on Rope Lane that morning were quiet ones, and there were too many of them, standing in doorways, pretending to read a notice board that had held the same notice since November.

Not a raid. Not yet. A *watching*. Which was worse, because a raid was weather and a watching was climate.

Herr Lindqvist was already inside, and he did not say good morning, which meant he had seen them too. "The colonel's coat," he said instead, loudly, in the voice of a man performing normalcy for an audience. "The shoulders will not take in themselves."

"No, Herr Lindqvist."

The day passed in a silence with ears in it. Marta hummed nothing at all, which was the loudest thing in the room. Hazel took in the colonel's shoulders and thought about rule four and about the dove-grey message currently traveling toward Riga in a theater cloak, and whether the woman carrying it had looked at the shoes on her own street this morning, and whether she had turned back.

At four o'clock, the quiet shoes moved on — or seemed to — and at six, Herr Lindqvist delivered his benediction and left, and Hazel locked the door and stood in the dark workshop with her heart going like a treadle machine.

And that was when the knock came.

Not the knock of a soldier — soldiers did not knock. Not the knock of a client — clients knocked twice, apologetically. This was three knocks, evenly spaced, patient, the knock of someone who had knocked on doors before and knew exactly how long to wait between.

Hazel did not move. Rule five. Rule one. Rule four, standing by.

Three knocks again.

Through the gap in the east curtain — four fingers' width, a tired woman's window — the streetlight showed her a slice of Rope Lane, empty, and then a man stepping into it from the doorway of the chandler's shop opposite. Tall, travel-worn, with the posture of someone who had spent a long time being careful about his posture. He did not look at the window. He looked at the door. And he stood in the cold with his hands at his sides, waiting, as though he had all the patience in the world and had brought it with him from somewhere very far away.

Hazel stood in the dark with the key to the door in her pocket and the key to everything else in her head, and counted — one, two, three, four — the way she counted stitches, the way she counted everything, because counting was safer than thinking.

The man raised his hand to knock a third time.

And Hazel, who trusted no one, who trusted the work and not the people, who had given away nothing warmly for two years and slept like a well-run shop — Hazel heard herself say, quietly, to the dark, in a voice she did not recognize as her own:

"Who drowns in October and knocks in December?"

The knock fell, soft as a pin dropping.
