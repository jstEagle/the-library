## 2. A Letter in a Dead Man's Hand

Hazel opened the door the way she did everything now: on a count.

One to turn the key. Two to set her weight against it, so that if it swung inward she would be the thing that decided how far. Three to look — not at the man's face, which was what he'd expect, but at his hands first, then his shoes, then the space behind him where an accomplice would stand if accomplices were the sort of thing that stood politely in doorways on a Tuesday night in December.

The space behind him was empty. The snow was coming down in that lazy, administrative way snow had of falling on a port city that no longer believed anything good would come from the sea. And the man himself — tall, coat buttoned wrong by one, a traveler's crease down the front of him like he'd been folded into a train compartment for two days and unfolded hastily at this end — looked at her with the expression of someone who had rehearsed this moment on the journey and was discovering, too late, that rehearsals are useless against a woman holding a door half-shut.

"Frau Crane," he said. "Ivo Markun."

"I didn't ask."

"No," he agreed. "But you were going to, eventually, and I thought I'd save us both the suspense."

Hazel did not laugh. She noted, with some professional irritation, that she had wanted to, and filed that away as evidence against him. A man who could make you want to laugh on a doorstep in December was a man with tools you hadn't inventoried yet.

"The shop is closed," she said.

"I know. I waited until it was closed on purpose." He glanced up and down the street — the fishmonger's dark windows, the lamplighter's abandoned post, the long white nothing of the quay beyond — and lowered his voice to something just under the wind. "May I come in? It's about a letter."

"People write letters all the time. There's a whole profession built around delivering them. They have little hats."

"They don't deliver this one," he said. "Because the man who wrote it has been dead since October. Officially. Very officially. There was a notice in the *Herald* and everything, and we both know how much the occupation loves its paperwork when the paperwork buries something."

The cold came through the gap in the door and found the place under Hazel's ribs where she kept her careful life, folded small, and pressed against it.

She should shut the door. Every rule she had memorized in Elias Brandt's dry, patient voice said shut the door. Rule four: never open for a name alone. Rule seven: grief is a lever; anyone who mentions the dead in the first minute is either mourning or mining. Rule eleven, the one she'd written herself in the back of her pattern book in pencil so faint it might have been an accident of the grain: *if your heart moves before your hands do, wait until they agree.*

Her hands wanted to shut the door. Her heart — traitorous, uncounted thing — wanted to hear the rest.

"Three minutes," she said. "You can stand in the cutting room. You will not touch anything, you will not sit on anything, and if you lean on my cutting table I will know you've never worked honest cloth in your life and I'll throw you out accordingly."

"That seems fair," said Ivo Markun, and came in out of the snow.

* * *

The workshop after hours was a different animal than the workshop by day. By day it belonged to the occupation's uniforms — the grey-green wool, the brass buttons Hazel polished until they flattered their wearers into looking more legitimate than any of them felt, the collars she stitched so precisely that a captain's rank sat on his neck like a verdict. By day she was Frau Crane, seamstress, quiet as pins, invisible as thread. But at night the shop remembered what else it was. The bolts of civilian cloth went soft in the dark like sleeping animals. The great shears lay in their drawer with a gravity that felt deliberate. And above the fitting room, in the loft where nobody official ever climbed, there was the small locked chest that held the only library Hazel owned: forty-one genuine dispatches in the hand of a dead man.

Elias Brandt had taught her the code the way other men taught children prayers — a line at a time, without hurry, as though there were all the time in the world. *Numbers are cheap,* he used to say. *Anyone can make numbers. What matters is where you put them.* He had shown her how a cipher lived inside ordinary stitching: the spacing of basting stitches as dashes, the direction of a seam allowance, the count of tacks along a hem. A dispatch sewn into a lining could pass over a checkpoint officer's very eyes and he would see only tailoring, because tailoring was the one language men with guns never bothered to learn.

He had drowned in October. That was what the notice said. Lost from the ferry in rough water, body unrecovered, effects returned to no one because there was no one. Hazel had read it three times, then folded it into eighths and burned it in the pressing stove, and had not permitted herself to think about it since, except, of course, constantly, usually at three in the morning, when thinking kept its appointments the way creditors do.

Now here was a stranger standing in her cutting room dripping harbor snow onto her floorboards, saying the dead man's name like it was a key he'd been given.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question. She made herself say things that weren't questions so that he couldn't answer them sideways.

"I worked with him. Before." He turned his hat in his hands — a nervous habit, she noted, or a performed one, and she genuinely could not tell which, and the not-telling made her teeth ache. "Two years ago I was in the coastal cell. Courier work, mostly. Elias ran the cipher end. He was—" Markun paused, and for a moment whatever performance he was running dropped, and underneath it was something tired and real. "He was the only person I ever met who could make arithmetic feel like loyalty."

"And then?"

"And then October happened. Or October was said to happen." From inside his coat he drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, and laid it on the cutting table — carefully, she noticed, keeping it clear of the chalk dust, which meant he'd been told about the table rule before, or had manners, or had studied her, and each possibility was worse than the last. "I'm not going to hand it to you. You'll pick it up yourself or you won't. That seemed important to you."

"It is important to me."

"I figured. You counted my footsteps from the door to here. Nine steps, and you watched every one."

Hazel had, in fact, counted nine footsteps, and was annoyed to have been caught at it, and more annoyed that being caught felt less like failure and more like being *seen*, which was a sensation she had spent two years having surgically removed.

She untied the string.

Inside the oilcloth was a single sheet of paper, folded once, and even before she opened it her hands knew. Hands know handwriting the way they know cloth — before the mind catches up, there's already a verdict in the fingertips. The paper was good stock but not new. The ink had aged evenly. And the writing itself —

She had forty-one examples of this hand locked in a chest over her head. The slightly left-leaning capitals. The *b* with its flat-footed base, like a man standing firmly on principle. The way Elias crossed his sevens — never once, always twice, "because one stroke is an opinion and two strokes are a fact." She had copied that habit into her own record-keeping without ever deciding to.

It was his hand. There was no doubt of it, and the doubt arrived anyway, enormous, because the hand was attached to a drowned man.

*To the needle-woman at the harbor shop,* the letter began. No name. Of course no name. *By the time you read this, I will have been dead for some months, and you will have been right not to mourn me until you finish reading.*

Hazel read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she read difficult patterns — breaking it into sections, checking each seam.

The letter did not explain. It assumed. It referenced a signal protocol that had never been written down anywhere, never spoken aloud outside a single afternoon two winters ago when Elias had stood at her shoulder in this very room and walked her through a verification sequence "for emergencies only, which I pray will mean never." Three numbers drawn from the shop itself. The number of bolts of navy serge on the wall. The number of stairs to the loft. The number of needles in the third drawer, which varied, which was the whole point, because Elias had insisted the final figure be counted fresh each time — *"the world changes, Hazel; the code must check the world, not memory."*

The letter gave its own figures: nineteen bolts, thirteen stairs, twenty-two needles.

Hazel went and counted.

Nineteen. Thirteen. Twenty-two. She counted the needles twice because her hands had begun to tremble and trembling hands miscount, and the second count also said twenty-two, and somewhere between the first needle and the last, the floor of her careful world quietly gave way and she discovered there had been nothing underneath it after all — just more stitching, held up by habit.

He was alive. Or he had been alive when this paper was bought. Or someone had tortured the sequence out of him before the water got him. Or someone had studied him long enough to counterfeit not just his hand but his philosophy, which would require knowing that the needles had to be counted fresh, which would require—

"Frau Crane." Markun's voice, low. "You've gone very still. In my experience that's either very good or very bad."

"It's neither. It's bookkeeping." She heard her own voice and was grateful for it: level, dry, a shopkeeper's voice, though the woman operating it felt like a coat with the lining torn out. "This verification sequence existed in exactly one head besides mine, and that head is supposed to be at the bottom of the strait."

"Yes."

"If he's alive and didn't send this, someone stole from him. If he's alive and did send this, he let the whole network believe he was dead for two months while I stitched dispatches into linings for a ghost. If he's dead, then whoever wrote this learned things that should have died with him." She set the letter down on the table, square to the edge, because her hands needed a job. "Every branch of that tree is rotten, Herr Markun. Which one am I holding?"

He didn't answer immediately, and she was fair enough to give him credit for that. A liar would have answered fast.

"There's a fourth branch," he said finally. "That he's alive, compromised, and couldn't tell any of us he was alive — because the people watching him are the same people who read our mail. Because if one courier learns Elias breathes, and that courier is taken, then the occupation owns the resurrection." His jaw tightened. "He faked the drowning, Hazel. Or it was faked around him. Either way, the point of a dead code-master is that his codes stay trusted while he works from the dark. This letter is him reaching out of the dark. It's the only thing he's sent since October. And it says—"

"Don't." She put up one finger, and he stopped, which surprised them both. "Don't tell me yet what it says. If I hear the mission first, I'll weigh it against the letter. If I weigh the letter first—" she tapped the page, "—then whatever you ask for afterward gets measured against the truth instead of the hope. I've seen what hope does to couriers. Hope is how networks die. Somebody asks for one small favor, and the favor is warm, and warmth is how you get people to carry things they shouldn't."

Markun looked at her for a long moment. Snowmelt dripped off his coat hem onto the boards, tick, tick, tick, and Hazel counted the drops without meaning to — five, six, seven — because counting was still safer than thinking, and she was beginning to suspect it always would be.

"Elias told me you'd be like this," he said. "He said, 'The needle-woman will trust the stitching before she trusts me, and God help you, that's correct.'"

"He said *needle-woman*?"

"He said it affectionately."

"He said it the way a man says it about a tool he relies on." But something in her chest loosened a quarter-turn anyway — the specific, treacherous comfort of being accurately described by the dead. She pulled out the stool at the end of the table and sat, finally, because her knees had opinions. "One question before anything else. Answer it straight, because I will check it against records you don't know I keep. The cell you say you served in — the coastal one, two years ago. Who carried the March dispatch to Riga?"

"A woman called Ansa," he said promptly. "Red scarf, terrible singing, carried it in a herring barrel because she thought barrels were funny."

Ansa. Red scarf. The singing — God, the singing, Elias complaining about it for a week. Hazel closed her eyes briefly. The letter was genuine. Whatever else was true, however deep the trap might go, that page had been written by Elias Brandt's living hand, and the verification sequence had been counted fresh, and the world changed and the code checked the world.

Which meant the code-master was checking the world from somewhere, and had chosen her — the needle-woman, the tool who relied — to finish something.

She opened her eyes. Outside, the snow kept falling on the harbor, on the gunboats, on the customs house where the occupation stamped its approvals, on all the grey machinery of a state that had already begun writing this republic out of its own books. In here, the stove ticked. The shears waited. Twenty-two needles lay in their drawer like a small faithful congregation.

"All right, Herr Markun," Hazel said. "You have more than three minutes now, and I hate that. Tell me what the dead man wants sewn — and then tell me why I should believe that part too."

Markun reached back into his coat, and what he brought out this time was not paper.

It was a measuring tape, tailor's grade, worn soft at the numbers — and printed along its length, in figures so small you'd take them for a maker's mark unless you had been taught by Elias Brandt where to look, was a row of numbers that made Hazel's blood go cold and bright at once.

She recognized the start of a cipher she had never seen finished.

Nobody had. It wasn't supposed to exist yet.
