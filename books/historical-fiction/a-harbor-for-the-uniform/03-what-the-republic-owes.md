## 3. What the Republic Owes

I had rehearsed the speech eleven times on the walk from the harbor. In my head it went beautifully. In my head, the seamstress set down her shears, listened with grave attention, and said something noble like *I have been waiting for this night since October.*

What actually happened was that she lit a lamp, turned its wick down to almost nothing, and looked at me the way a customs officer looks at a suitcase that has been declared as containing only shirts.

"Sit," she said.

There was one stool in the workroom, and it was covered in chalk dust and cuttings of grey wool. I sat on it anyway. It was lower than I expected, which meant that for the duration of the most important conversation of my life I would be looking up at a woman with pins in her cuff like a hedgehog wearing an apron.

"My name is Ivo Markun," I began.

"I know."

"You—"

"You announced it to the entire street outside the door, at full volume, like a man calling a ferry." She folded her arms. "Either you are very brave or very new."

"Both," I admitted. "It's a long story."

"It usually is." She pulled a second stool from under the cutting table and sat opposite me, knees nearly touching mine, close enough that honesty would be difficult and lying impossible. "You have until the lamplighter finishes his round. He passes this window at half past eleven, and when he's gone, so am I."

Outside, the wind came off the harbor carrying salt and coal smoke and the particular cold of a city that had learned to keep its voice down. Through the frosted glass I could see the occupation's flag hanging limp over the customs house across the street, too heavy with other people's emblems to move even in weather like this.

"Elias Brandt sent me," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise exactly, more like a door opening onto a room she'd boarded up long ago and was furious to find still furnished.

"Elias Brandt drowned in October," she said. "The harbor police recovered his coat. His sister identified the body. There was a notice in the paper."

"The coat was his. The body was not."

"That's what they all say."

"'They'?" I leaned forward despite myself. "Who else has come to you?"

Her jaw tightened in a way that told me I'd just asked a question she considered none of my business, and also — this was the interesting part — that the answer was *no one*, and she knew she shouldn't have let me see how much that admission cost her.

"Tell me why you're here," she said. "Not who sent you. Why."

So I told her. Not the rehearsed version — the rehearsed version had a dramatic pause in the middle of it that died immediately upon contact with her stare — but the true one, as much of it as I could carry.

"In September," I said, "before the armistice terms were signed, before the occupation commission moved into the treasury building, there were four days. Four days in which the republic's gold reserve was moved out of the vaults. Officially, it was evacuated inland to protect it. Officially, it sits in a bank in a neutral country right now, waiting for a legitimate government to reclaim it."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it never left the city. It was loaded onto two barges, transferred to a freighter flying a flag nobody has ever heard of, and it has been sitting in this harbor ever since, under paper ownership so layered that by the time anyone untangles it, the gold will be in three other countries and the men who stole it will be ministers somewhere warmer."

The seamstress didn't gasp. She didn't go pale. What she did was reach over, pick up a spool of thread, and begin winding a loose end back onto it with great concentration, which I would later learn was what she did instead of screaming.

"How do you know this?"

"Because Elias Brandt counted it." I watched the thread wind. "He was inside the treasury office for six years. He kept the ledgers — the real ones. And when they moved the gold, he copied every manifest, every signature, every false entry, and he encoded the whole of it into a cipher only he could read. Then he drowned himself very thoroughly, left his coat on a pier, and vanished."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I was the man who rowed him to the freighter." I let that sit for a moment. "I've known Elias since we were seventeen years old stealing printing ink for the student press. When he decided to die, he needed someone who could keep a secret and pull an oar. I qualified on both counts. Barely. He weighs more than he looks."

"He always did," she said quietly, and then caught herself, and the catching-herself told me more about their history than anything she might have said aloud.

* * *

She stood then, walked to the window, and checked the street with a curtain twitch so practiced it couldn't have been her first. The lamplighter hadn't come yet. We had minutes.

"Suppose I believe you," she said, back still turned. "Suppose I believe Elias is alive and the ledger exists. What do you imagine I can do about it?"

"The delegation arrives next month," I said. "The peace delegation. Twelve men going north to sign whatever document the occupation puts in front of them — the one that makes the theft legal forever, retroactive, unappealable."

"I know about the delegation. I'm fitting three of them already." Her reflection in the dark glass was unreadable. "Minister Voss among them."

"Then you know they'll wear ceremonial uniform. Full dress. And you know who makes those uniforms."

She turned around slowly.

"The tailors of this workshop make them," I said. "One tailor in this workshop has been sewing dispatches into linings since the first winter of the occupation, using a stitch-code designed by Elias Brandt, in patterns no inspector has ever flagged, because who searches the seams of a coat? A ceremonial uniform crosses borders with the delegation. It gets photographed. It gets worn to dinners where foreign journalists sit within arm's reach. If the proof of the stolen treasury were sewn into such a lining — if someone on the delegation carried it past every checkpoint without knowing they carried it —"

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"No." She came back toward the table, and now there was heat in it, finally, the first thing in her that wasn't ice. "You don't understand what you're asking. The network has rules. Rules Elias wrote himself, and rule the first is: *one courier, one route, one purpose.* A dispatch goes into an ordinary garment, travels with an ordinary traveler, reaches one ordinary contact, and dies there. Nothing important enough to kill for goes anywhere near anything important enough to search. A peace delegation is the most examined cargo in this country. Every officer in the port will want his name in the report of having inspected them. You want me to put the single most dangerous document in the Baltic into the exact coat every eye in the occupation will be staring at."

"Yes," I said.

"It's madness."

"It's the only chance the truth will ever get." I stood up too, and the stool scraped, and we both froze and listened to the empty shop for three full seconds before continuing in whispers, like criminals, which technically we were. "Think about what happens otherwise, Hazel. The delegation signs. The treaty ratifies the transfer. The gold becomes legally theirs — not stolen anymore, just *theirs*, with receipts. In ten years there will be schoolbooks printed saying the republic sold its own treasury to pay its debts. Elias spent six years copying manifests so that wouldn't happen. Are you telling me his rules matter more than his life's work?"

"My rules are what's kept me alive."

"Are they?" I regretted it the moment I said it, but I didn't take it back. "Because I've been watching this network from the outside for three months now, since Elias went into the ground that isn't holding him. Do you know what I've found? Routes cold. Contacts scattered. Half the drop points in the city are being watched by men who shouldn't know they exist. Whatever protected you before October is dying, Hazel, and discipline alone won't save you. Discipline is a beautiful thing. So was the republic."

That landed harder than I intended. I saw it hit — saw her hand go briefly to the edge of the cutting table, steadying herself against something that wasn't the table.

"The lamplighter," she said.

Through the glass, far up the street, a small figure with a pole was making its way along, touching flame to the lamps one by one, each globe blooming yellow against the snow. Our time was measured now in lamplight.

"One question," she said, very fast. "If the ledger is real, if the cipher is finished, why does Elias need me at all? He built the code. He wrote the dispatches all through the occupation. Why does a dead man suddenly need a seamstress?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for three days, the hole in the middle of my story, the part where I had to decide — standing in a freezing workroom with a woman who trusted nothing and everyone's life folded into her needle case — whether I was going to lie to her.

Elias had been clear. *Tell her the minimum,* he'd said. *She'll do the work if she believes it's safe. Tell her everything and she'll refuse, and she'll be right to.*

But I had rowed that boat. I had watched Elias step onto the freighter with a canvas bag of papers and a face like a man leaving his own funeral, and I had spent the autumn watching the occupation tighten around a city that used to be free, and I had decided somewhere in the cold weeks since that the minimum was a kind of theft too.

"There's a complication," I said.

"Of course there is."

"The cipher isn't finished. Elias encoded the manifests, yes — but the final section, the section that ties the false entries to names, dates, signatures, the section that makes it evidence instead of arithmetic — he never completed it. He was interrupted." By soldiers, actually, at the treasury door, which was why the fake drowning had become necessary rather than merely convenient, but that detail could wait. "He needs someone to complete the transmission. Physically. Stitch by stitch, according to his system. And there are perhaps four people alive who know his stitch-codes well enough to attempt it."

"Three," Hazel said. "Two of them are dead."

We looked at each other across the cutting table, over the drifts of chalk and wool, and I understood that I had just watched her count herself and decide to admit it.

"The fourth," she said slowly, "is whoever wrote that letter you showed me. Because that letter was written in his hand, and either he's alive, or someone alive can imitate him well enough to hang me."

"Yes," I said. "That's the bargain. That's the whole ugly shape of it. You bet your life that the letter is genuine. I bet mine that you're the person Elias says you are. Neither of us gets a receipt."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamplighter's figure grew larger in the window, pausing at the lamp directly opposite the shop, and the light through the frost turned from moon-white to candle-yellow, and I thought: *she's going to say no, and I'm going to have to go back to Elias with nothing, and the gold will rot in that harbor until the century turns and nobody remembers there was ever a republic at all.*

Then Hazel Crane did something I did not expect. She reached beneath the cutting table, drew out a flat wooden box, opened it, and laid on the table between us a single strip of linen — plain, undyed, about the length of my forearm — stitched along its length with rows of tiny marks that to any eye in the world meant nothing at all.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked.

"A dispatch."

"It's the first dispatch I ever carried. November of the invasion year. A list of arrested printers, sewn into a mourning veil, delivered to a widow who was herself on a later version of that list." Her fingers rested on the stitching without touching it. "I've kept it for three years to remind myself what the work costs. I look at it whenever I think about quitting, and I ask whether the next name will be worth the next risk."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I'm looking at it," she said, "and I'm asking something different. I'm asking what it's worth if the whole country ends up a name on somebody else's list."

The lamplighter moved on. Our lamp stayed dark; she'd paid the fine that excused her house from lighting, another small invisibility, one of hundreds she wore like the pins in her cuff.

"I need conditions," she said.

"Name them."

"First — I verify everything. The cipher system, the letter, your story, all of it, against records I hold. If one thread doesn't match, I stop, and you leave, and you never come back."

"Agreed."

"Second — I choose the garment and the placement. You don't touch needle or cloth. You're a rower and a messenger, and from what I've seen of your talent for announcing yourself to streets, possibly not even that."

"I contain multitudes," I said, "but mostly fear and rowing."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth then — brief, quickly disciplined, but unmistakable. The first crack in the ice. I filed it away as a man files the first sign of spring.

"Third," she said. "When this is done — whatever happens, however it ends — you tell me the whole truth. Everything Elias didn't send you to say. I'll know if you don't. I spend my life reading what people hide in their linings, Mr. Markun, and yours is showing."

"I'll give you the whole truth," I said. And meant it, mostly, in the way a man means things at half past eleven at night in a city full of informers — which is to say completely, except for the parts that would break her heart and stop the work, and I told myself those parts weren't lies, only stitches not yet placed.

Hazel blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark her voice came steady and quiet, the voice of a woman closing her shop at the end of a perfectly ordinary day.

"Come tomorrow after the fitters leave. Four o'clock. Bring the rest of the cipher key and don't bring your appetite for speeches." A pause. "And Ivo Markun? Walk out the back. The front door has a view of the customs house windows, and the man in the third window drinks tea at this hour and pretends to read."

I felt my way along the wall to the rear stair, heart hammering, and it wasn't until I was out in the yard with the snow squeaking under my boots and the harbor breathing somewhere beyond the fences that I realized what had just happened.

I had come to recruit a seamstress.

Instead, in the space of forty minutes, she had taken command of the mission, set its terms, scheduled its beginning, and dispatched me through her own private exit like a parcel she'd inspected and approved.

Elias had warned me about her, I remembered suddenly. Standing on the pier in October, wrapping the rope in my hands, he'd smiled for the only time that whole terrible night and said: *Whatever you do, don't try to lead her. Just point her at the work and get out of the way.*

Tomorrow at four o'clock, I thought, pulling my collar up against the wind. Tomorrow we would find out whether the letter was real, whether the ledger could be finished, whether one needle in one frozen workshop could unpick everything the occupation had sewn shut.

Behind me, above the workroom, a curtain moved once and was still.

She was watching to make sure I made the corner safely.

It was, I would think many times afterward, the closest thing to kindness I'd been shown in a year — and it was also, knowing Hazel Crane, surveillance.
