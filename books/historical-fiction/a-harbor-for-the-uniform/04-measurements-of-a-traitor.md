## 4. Measurements of a Traitor

The commission arrived on a Tuesday, which Hazel thought was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days she distrusted everything.

It came as commissions always did: a docket slipped under the workshop door by a courier who never met her eyes, written in the careful clerk's hand of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. *One ceremonial uniform, full dress, for His Excellency Minister Voss. Fitting required. Completion required before the fourteenth of December.* The occupation had stamped its approval across the bottom in violet ink, the way one stamps approval on a funeral wreath.

Hazel read it twice at her station, then folded it into her apron pocket and went back to hemming trousers, because that was the discipline of it — the whole discipline of it. A seamstress who flinched at paper was a seamstress who would soon flinch at everything.

But underneath the hemming, beneath the steady whisper of the needle, her mind was already laying out cloth.

A ceremonial uniform. For Voss. Before the fourteenth of December.

Ivo Markun had stood in the cutting room three nights ago with his dead man's letter and his hungry eyes, and he had said, in the tone of a man reciting scripture he wasn't sure he believed: *the cipher goes into the lining of a uniform worn by someone the world will be watching.* She had told him it was impossible. She had told him the network's rules forbade it. She had told him several other things in a voice like a slammed door, and then — because she was tired, and cold, and because Elias Brandt's handwriting had looked up at her from that letter like a hand reaching out of water — she had not said no.

Now the Ministry itself had delivered the door, and all she had to do was walk through it without anyone seeing her walk.

* * *

She began, as she began everything, with the records.

Her records lived in three places. The first was the shop's own ledger, open on the counter where any inspector might read it: measurements taken, alterations charged, buttons replaced at two pfennig apiece. The second was the pattern book under her bed at home, which contained nothing incriminating at all, only numbers arranged with a private logic — sleeve lengths and chest measurements that were also dates, waist sizes that were also names, a whole alphabet of the republic stitched into ordinary tailoring. And the third place, the one no ledger recorded, was inside her own skull, where Elias Brandt had spent four winters teaching her what to remember and, more importantly, what to forget.

*The code is not in the stitches,* he used to say, tapping his temple with a chalk stub. *The code is in the woman holding the needle. Burn the paper. Keep the woman.*

So on Wednesday evening, after the last apprentice had gone home and Marta had left with her coat buttoned to the chin against the harbor wind, Hazel unlocked the flat drawer where the genuine dispatches' twins were kept — the practice pieces, the calibrations, sewn and unpicked and sewn again until Elias pronounced them clean. She laid them out on the cutting table under the good lamp and set Ivo Markun's letter beside them.

Then she did what she did best. She read cloth.

Elias wrote the way he cut a lapel: economical, with a slight forward lean to the letters, the crossbar of every *t* angled like a man leaning into weather. His descenders — the tails of *g* and *y* — stopped short, as though he distrusted anything that reached too far below the surface. On the practice linings, pinned labels in his hand repeated these habits across six years. The letter Ivo carried matched. It matched so well that Hazel felt the first cold drop of unease slide down her spine, because forgery this perfect was either impossible or it wasn't a forgery at all.

And yet.

There was the date. The letter was dated the ninth of September. Elias had drowned — officially, publicly, witnessed by two harbor policemen and a very cooperative newspaper — on the third of October. So far, so consistent with Ivo's story: written before the death, held back until after.

But paper remembers things people don't ask it to. Hazel held the letter to the lamp and studied the fold lines, the way the ink had settled into creases, the faint blooming of damp along the lower edge. The letter had been folded small — small enough for a coat pocket — and refolded often. It had been carried a long time. That much was true.

What troubled her was simpler and worse: the ink. The lower half of the page, where the cipher reference sat — the passage only she could verify — was written in an ink that sat slightly *on top* of the paper, fresher than the rest, a different batch entirely. The upper half was old. The lower half was new.

Someone had taken an old letter and added to it. Or someone had written the whole thing recently with two inks, to make it look carried. Either way, the letter was a garment with a seam hidden in it, and Hazel had built her life on finding exactly those seams.

She sat back down on the cutting stool and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

*Ivo Markun,* she thought. *Who taught you to lie with paper?*

* * *

Thursday morning she took Voss's measurements.

This was theater she knew how to perform. The Minister arrived with two aides and a driver who smoked by the door, and he stood on the fitting dais with his arms held out at the angle of a man being crucified politely, and Hazel circled him with her tape like a tide coming in.

"Your Excellency has kept his figure," she said, which was true, and cost nothing.

"Tailors say that to everyone," said Voss.

"Tailors say it to everyone whose figure they intend to flatter. Yours requires no flattering." She wrote the number in the ledger. Chest, forty-two. In her private arithmetic, forty-two was the fourth month, February — the month in 'sixteen when the first dispatches had gone south through the fishing fleet. She noted the coincidence and did not smile.

Voss was sixty-one and had the face of a man who had attended too many funerals for colleagues still technically alive. He had been in the old government, before the occupation's Commission took over the ministries and left men like Voss sitting behind desks like furniture in a repossessed house. He had signed things. He had protested other things, quietly, in memoranda nobody read. Whether he knew about the network — whether he had ever known — was a question Hazel had asked herself perhaps four hundred times over the years, usually at night, usually arriving at the same answer: *if he knew, we'd all have been arrested years ago; if he didn't know, telling him would kill us faster than the Commission could.*

So she measured him, and said nothing, and let the tape answer questions he didn't know were being asked.

At the shoulder seam she paused longer than she needed to, fingers resting on the wool, and felt him register the pause — a man of his vintage registered pauses the way sailors registered changes in wind. But he said nothing, and she moved on, and the fitting ended, and the aides collected their hats, and the door closed behind the last smell of government cigarettes.

Marta appeared at her elbow with a pincushion strapped to her wrist like a weapon.

"Forty-two chest," Marta said, reading the ledger upside down, which Marta could do, along with reading lips through walls and detecting a lie at forty paces. "Big man for a small country."

"Every man in this country is too big for it lately."

"Hm." Marta threaded a needle without looking at it, a trick Hazel had envied for nine years. "You'll want help on the ceremonial. Full dress is two women's work if it's to be done before the fourteenth." She said it lightly. Too lightly. The way one says something one has already decided about.

"I can manage."

"I'm sure you can." Marta bit the thread. "You've been managing a great deal lately. Late hours. Private projects. Last Tuesday you locked the cutting room drawer twice." She smiled, warm as a stove. "I count things, Hazel. It's my one talent besides the buttonholes. You've known this since the year we both started."

"I count things too," Hazel said.

"Yes," said Marta. "That's what worries me. Two counters in one shop is either very safe or very dangerous, and I've been trying for a week to work out which." She went back to her machine, humming something tuneless, and Hazel stood there with the tape coiled in her fist like a small pale snake, feeling suspicion move through her in both directions at once — toward Ivo, toward Marta, toward the woman in the mirror who had broken her own first rule by saying yes to a stranger with a beautiful forgery.

* * *

Friday night, she tested Ivo himself.

He came at eleven, entering through the coal yard the way she'd instructed, smelling of smoke and river, carrying bread and a bottle of the terrible cherry liqueur that passed for hospitality in this part of the city. He had the cheerful recklessness of a man who had decided fear was a luxury item, and Hazel found it simultaneously exhausting and — she admitted this only in the privacy of her own skull, where even Elias couldn't audit her — not entirely unpleasant.

"You look," he said, setting the bottle down, "like a woman who has found something."

"I look like a tired seamstress."

"You look like a tired seamstress who has found something. There's a difference. My sister was one." He said it easily, but Hazel caught the small hitch in it, the way a hem catches on a nail. Another thread to pull later.

She led him to the cutting table, where the ceremonial uniform lay in pieces: front panels, collar, the great sweeping coat skirt, and the lining lengths — silk, dove-grey, ordered special through the trade house, unimpeachably legitimate, paid for from the shop's own account. On top of the lining lay a sheet of paper bearing rows of her pencil marks: groups of figures, innocuous as inventory counts.

"The first section," she said. "Encoded in Elias's system. Stitched into the left forepart lining as a running stitch variation — you'd never see it unless you knew the stitch intervals. Read it back to me."

Ivo picked up the paper. His eyes moved. Hazel watched his face the way she'd watched Voss's shoulders — for the pause, the tell, the place where the cloth pulled wrong.

He read it back correctly. Every group. Then he frowned at one point near the end and said, "This cluster here — you doubled the interval. Deliberately?"

"It's a checksum. Elias's rule. If the interval breaks, the reader knows the message was tampered with en route." She took the paper back. "You knew that. Which means either you worked with him long enough to learn his checksums—"

"I did."

"—or he told you what to expect before he sent you, which means he expected me to test you, which means—" she laid the paper down flat, smoothing it with both hands, buying herself a moment, "—he knows me well enough to know I'd test you. Fine. That part holds together. Here's what doesn't." She looked up at him. "Your letter is two inks, Ivo. The top half is old. The bottom half — the part with the cipher reference, the part calibrated specifically for me — is newer. Weeks newer. Maybe months. Someone wrote that passage onto an older page, or forged the whole thing with a split-ink trick to make it look carried. So either Elias wrote your letter and then someone amended it—"

"—or someone is using his plan without him," Ivo finished. He had gone very still, in the way of men who have just heard a floorboard creak overhead. "Or using his plan *with* him, under conditions I don't want to imagine."

"Which brings us to the question you haven't answered since the night you walked in." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, because gentleness got further with frightened men than sharpness did, and Ivo was frightened, whatever his grin did to hide it. "Who gave you the letter? Not who handed it to you — who *gave* it. Who decided it should reach me now, in December, attached to a mission that requires the Ministry to order a uniform from my shop?"

For a long moment the only sound was the harbor wind worrying the window frames.

"Elias," Ivo said finally. "Through channels. Through two people, and one of them is dead now, so you can't check, and I know how that sounds—"

"It sounds like every cover story ever constructed."

"It sounds like that because the truth usually does." He leaned forward, palms flat on the table, on the dove-grey silk, on the future of the republic in its unfinished state. "Hazel. I can't prove it to you tonight. I know that. But consider the shape of it. If the Commission wanted to catch your network, they wouldn't need a ceremony and a uniform and a cipher sewn by hand. They'd kick the door in tomorrow. They've kicked doors in for less. This — this elaborate, ridiculous, theatrical arrangement — this has Elias's fingerprints all over it. Nobody else alive thinks in garments. Nobody else alive would design a trap — or a rescue, or a message, I honestly no longer know which — that has to be *tailored*."

Despite herself, despite the suspicion sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, Hazel felt the corner of her mouth move. Because it was true. It was horribly, unmistakably true. The whole scheme had the absurd elegance of a man who once smuggled a fleet dispatch out of a naval hospital inside the piping of a dressing gown.

"Suppose I believe you," she said. "Suppose it's really his plan. Then why doesn't he come finish it himself?"

Ivo looked at her, and for the first time since the coal yard, he didn't manage the grin.

"That," he said, "is the question I've come three hundred miles to stop asking myself."

They stood there in the lamplight, two people holding opposite ends of the same unraveling thread, and outside, somewhere down toward the water, a whistle blew — once, short, the kind of whistle that meant nothing, except at eleven at night in an occupied city it meant everything.

* * *

Saturday, Marta stayed late.

Hazel heard the machines go quiet one by one, heard the apprentices leave, heard the bolt of the front door — and then heard, instead of footsteps retreating, the small domestic noises of someone making tea on the back-room ring. When she came out of the cutting room, Marta was sitting with two cups poured, which was either friendship or an ambush, and with Marta it had historically been both.

"You've got a man coming through the coal yard," Marta said, without preamble, into her tea.

The room tilted very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Hazel kept her face where it was. Nine years of practice, and it held — mostly.

"There's a loose board in the yard fence," Marta continued. "Rattled all Wednesday night. And Thursday there were cigarette ends by the coal bins, and they weren't yours, because you chew mint when you're working late, you have since 'seventeen, and don't ask me why I know that, I know everything, it's my curse." She looked up. Her eyes were steady and kind and absolutely merciless. "I'm not asking what it is. I want that understood. I'm not asking, because the day I ask, I become a person who knows, and persons who know get questioned, and I have a brother whose debts are — complicated. All I'm telling you is: the yard rattles, Hazel. Fix your fence. Whatever walks through it, walk it quieter."

She drank her tea. Hazel stood in the doorway with a hundred answers lined up behind her teeth and let none of them out, because Marta had just performed, in the space of ninety seconds, the most precise demonstration of professional courtesy Hazel had ever received — and the most chilling. Marta knew. Not everything. But enough. Enough that her brother's debts were suddenly a fact in the room, sitting between the teacups like a third guest.

"Thank you," Hazel said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it.

"Don't thank me." Marta rinsed the cups, unhurried. "Just remember, when your fence rattles some night and it isn't your coal-yard man — that I warned you the yard rattles. And that I chose to tell you instead of anyone else." She hung her apron on its hook. "Goodnight, Hazel. Mind the fourteenth. Big deadline. Deadlines make people careless."

The door closed behind her. Hazel stood alone in the workshop among the ghosts of uniforms, the lamp buzzing, the harbor breathing somewhere below, and turned Marta's words over: *the day I ask, I become a person who knows.*

Which meant, Hazel thought with a cold settling in her stomach, that Marta already suspected there was something worth knowing — had perhaps suspected for years, keeping her counting silent out of the same disciplined loneliness Hazel practiced — and had chosen tonight, of all nights, to say so.

Or it meant something else. Something with violet stamps on it. Something with a brother's debts attached.

Outside, down in the dark of the coal yard, the loose board in the fence rattled — earlier than Ivo ever came, earlier than anyone should be walking there at all.

Hazel put her hand on the lamp key and listened.
