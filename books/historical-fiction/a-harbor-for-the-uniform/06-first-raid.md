## 6. First Raid

The trouble with hiding a republic inside a coat is that the coat must remain an ordinary coat.

Hazel Crane had learned this the way she learned most things — slowly, at night, with her fingers aching and a candle burning down to its last stubborn inch. A lining that hid nothing was just cloth. A lining that hid something had to hide it so well that even the cloth forgot. The cipher sections Ivo had brought her were sewn into panels no wider than a man's hand, worked into the canvas interlining of the ceremonial uniform for Minister Voss, and every stitch of them had to look like what they pretended to be: honest padding, honest reinforcement, the dull architecture of a garment nobody would ever think about twice.

She was three days into the work when the bells of the garrison church rang eleven, which meant nothing, because the bells rang eleven whether the world was ending or merely Tuesday.

It was Tuesday.

It was also, as it turned out, the world ending — or at least the small world of Brandt & Sons, Diplomatic Tailors, which had outlived four governments and one fire and was not, in Hazel's private opinion, going to survive the winter regardless.

She heard the boots before she saw them.

That was the thing about occupation soldiers: they never learned to walk softly, because softness was for people who needed forgiveness. These boots came down the cobbled lane outside the workshop like hammers looking for their nails, and Hazel's hands went still over the half-finished lining, and her heart did something remarkable — it didn't race at all. It simply stopped, politely, like a servant stepping out of the way.

*The coat,* she thought. *The officer's coat.*

It hung on the third rail from the window, finished that very morning — a great grey wool overcoat for Captain Dresler, lined in slate silk, buttons polished, collar brushed, awaiting only its owner's shoulders. It was beautiful work. It was exactly the sort of work that got inspected.

The door burst open on the second knock, which told Hazel everything about how much knocking had been intended as a courtesy.

There were five of them. Four soldiers and one officer — a thin man with a face like a folded letter, all creases and no warmth, wearing the armband of the Provost's Office rather than regimental colors. He carried a clipboard, which frightened Hazel more than any rifle would have. Rifles killed people. Clipboards filed them away forever.

"Gentlemen," said Herr Folded-Letter, to the room in general, "you will stand where you are."

Marta was at the pressing table near the window, a steam iron in her hand, and she did not put it down. She stood there with the iron hovering over a pair of trousers like a small angry priestess, and she said, in her flat harbor accent, "You'll scorch nothing. That's the Voss delegation's order. You'll be paying for scorched trousers out of—"

"Madam." The officer looked at the iron, then at Marta, then back at the iron, in the manner of a man deciding whether a cat was worth the trouble of kicking. "Put it down."

Marta put it down. But she put it down *slowly*, with the particular insolence of a woman who could make even obedience look like an insult, and Hazel felt something twist in her chest that was either gratitude or dread. Possibly both. With Marta it was usually both; the woman had been covering Hazel's late hours for a week now without asking a single question, which was either loyalty or reconnaissance, and Hazel had not yet decided which kept her awake more.

"I am Provisor Kessel," said the folded-letter man. "This establishment is subject to inspection under Article Nine. You will answer questions directly and you will touch nothing." His eyes went around the room — the rails of uniforms, the bolts of wool stacked like cordwood, the long cutting table scarred by forty years of chalk and shears. "Whose shop is this?"

"Brandt's," said Marta. "Dead man's shop. We keep the lights on."

Kessel made a note. Hazel watched his pencil move and thought, absurdly, *he writes left-handed, he'll smudge if he rushes, a man who smudges his notes is a man who reads them twice.* This was the code-master's training talking — Elias Brandt's voice in her head, patient as a metronome: *When you cannot act, observe. When you observe, catalogue. Fear is information too.*

She catalogued. Two soldiers at the door. One at the window, young, whose rifle strap was twisted — nervous, new, probably conscripted from somewhere with better weather. One moving down the rails of coats with a rod, poking into pockets with the bored thoroughness of a customs clerk. And Kessel himself drifting toward the cutting table, where the morning's chalk marks still lay on the cloth like a constellation nobody had named.

"Business has been good?" Kessel asked the room.

"Business has been dead," said Marta. "Like everything else."

"And yet." He touched a sleeve. Grey wool, diplomatic grade, the kind of cloth that hadn't been legally sold in this city since the summer. "And yet you cut cloth like this for dead business."

"For the delegation," Hazel heard herself say.

Her own voice surprised her — steady, unhurried, faintly bored, the voice of a tired seamstress who had answered this question a hundred times. She stepped forward from the corner where she'd been working and let him see her: apron dusted with thread-ends, thimble still on her middle finger, eyes red-rimmed from the candle. Let him see a craftsman, not a courier.

"The peace delegation requires full dress and greatcoats," she said. "Their schedule is the Provisional Government's schedule. If the cloth offends anyone, I suggest writing to the ministry. They're very good at letters."

Something flickered across the nearest soldier's face — the ghost of a laugh, quickly strangled. Even the occupiers, it seemed, had opinions about the ministries.

Kessel turned to her fully. Up close he smelled of ink and cold coffee. "Your name?"

"Hazel Crane."

"You're not local."

"No one is, anymore."

He studied her the way a tailor studies a client — measuring, she thought wildly, but measuring for what? For the gallows or the file? Then he smiled, and the smile had no more warmth than the clipboard. "Article Nine permits us to examine all goods prepared for official persons. You'll open your storeroom."

*The storeroom,* Hazel thought, and behind the thought, quick and bright as a pin-prick: *the uniform is not in the storeroom.*

Because three nights ago, unable to bear the risk of leaving Voss's ceremonial tunic hanging among its brothers where any raid would find it in the first five minutes, she had done something that violated every rule she'd memorized and every instinct she owned. She had moved it. She had taken the half-finished cipher lining — the panels of coded stitching, hours of Elias's mathematics rendered in silk thread — and she had basted them, temporarily, crudely, into the *interlining of Captain Dresler's finished overcoat*, where they lay now beneath slate silk, disguised as ordinary canvas padding, waiting for a night quiet enough to move them back.

It had seemed clever at two in the morning. It seemed, at present, like the single most insane decision of her life, because the officer's coat hung ten feet from a soldier with a poking-rod, and Article Nine apparently permitted the examination of goods prepared for official persons, and there was no article anywhere, she suspected, permitting the examination of goods hidden *inside* goods prepared for official persons, except possibly the firing-squad article.

"The storeroom's through there," Marta said, jerking her chin. "Mind the pins. Last man who minded nothing bled on the cashmere."

Kessel went. The soldier with the rod continued down the rails, coat by coat, and Hazel stood very still and performed, with her hands, the only magic she knew: she picked up a spool and a needle and began, calmly, to hem a trouser cuff that did not need hemming, while her entire soul hung on a hook by the window wearing Captain Dresler's name.

The rod went into a diplomat's frock coat. Out came a glove, a receipt, a boiled sweet in paper. The soldier ate the sweet.

The rod went into a staff captain's tunic. Nothing. Next.

The rod reached the end of the rail. Turned. Began coming back along the second row, and Hazel understood with terrible clarity that the second row held the finished orders awaiting collection, and that Captain Dresler's coat hung third from the window, and that the soldier was approaching it at the pace of a man with nowhere else in the world to be.

She thought about Elias's rules. *Never be interesting. Never volunteer. Never run, because running is the loudest confession there is.* She thought about Ivo Markun, wherever he was — probably in some cellar decoding her first sewn section, probably trusting her, poor fool — and about what Article Nine would do to Marta, who had covered for a stranger for a week out of what might have been kindness and might have been something else entirely, but who did not deserve the firing squad for either.

And she thought — this was the shameful part, the part she'd remember at odd moments for the rest of her life — she thought about the coat itself. The beautiful, stupid, magnificent coat. *They can't search it properly,* a small cold voice said in her head. *Not without un-stitching the lining, and soldiers don't sew. Soldiers destroy. They'll rip it, and ripping will find the canvas, and finding the canvas will find the cipher, unless—*

Unless someone gave them a reason not to look closely. Unless the coat became, before their eyes, something already claimed, already inspected, already *boring*.

"Provisor," Hazel said, and her voice came out clear as a struck bell, though she felt it vibrate through her whole skeleton. "If you're examining goods for officials, you should know that coat by the window is Captain Dresler's. It passed inspection this morning."

Every head turned toward her. The soldier with the rod stopped, hand on Dresler's coat, looking suddenly like a boy caught reaching for the jam.

"Passed inspection," Kessel repeated, reappearing in the doorway with chalk dust on his sleeves. "By whom?"

"By me, and by Fräulein Weiss." She nodded at Marta, who — bless her, curse her, bless her — picked up the cue without dropping a beat.

"Dresler's people collect at four," Marta said. "You want to explain to the Captain why his coat smells of soldier? Because I don't. I've explained enough things to captains this year."

A silence followed, the kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Hazel kept her needle moving. *Stitch, pull, stitch, pull* — a hem that didn't need existing, existing anyway. Somewhere outside, a tram groaned past on iced rails, and the sound of it, ordinary and municipal and utterly indifferent to her survival, steadied her more than any prayer could have.

Kessel walked to the coat. He lifted the collar. He ran his thumb along the lapel seam — right where, six inches lower and two inches deeper, thirty-two characters of stolen treasury lay sleeping in silk thread — and Hazel discovered that a person can sweat so thoroughly and so quietly that no one notices, provided her hands keep working.

"Good wool," Kessel said.

"It's the Captain's wool," Marta said. "We just make it behave."

Another silence. Then Kessel dropped the collar, made a mark on his clipboard, and said, in the tone of a man conceding a chess piece to gain the board, "The storeroom inventory does not match the ledger. There are nine bolts of grey listed and eleven on your shelves."

"That's because your quartermaster can't count," said Marta.

"He counted these."

"Then he counted them twice, once for himself and once for whoever he sells the extras to. Ask him about the fourth-floor storehouse on Ritter Lane. Ask him nicely, with witnesses."

And that — that was the moment Hazel understood she had misjudged everything, because Kessel actually paused. Actually wrote something down. Actually looked at Marta with the expression of a man recalculating a sum, and then snapped the clipboard shut and said, "Inspection concluded. The establishment remains subject to further examination." He gathered his soldiers the way a magnet gathers nails, and the boots went back down the lane, hammers seeking other nails, and the door closed behind them with a click that sounded, to Hazel, like the last page of a book turning.

Nobody moved for a full minute. The tram rattled by again. Down in the yard, the neighbor's dog resumed its lifelong argument with the gulls.

Then Marta picked up her iron, set it back on the stove, and said, without looking around, "Well. That was fun."

"Marta—"

"Don't." The iron hissed against damp cloth. Steam rose between them like a curtain. "Don't thank me, don't explain, don't tell me what's in that coat, because whatever it is, I've now lied to the Provost's Office about it, and I'd rather not know whether I'm a patriot or an idiot until somebody tells me which one gets fed."

Hazel stood in the middle of the workshop with the unneeded hemmed cuff in her hands, and felt terror drain out of her body and leave something colder and harder in its place — the feeling of steel after quenching, she supposed, if steel could feel anything, which it couldn't, which was perhaps why steel was useful.

"They'll come back," she said.

"Of course they'll come back. They always come back. Kessel wrote my name down — I saw him do it, the smudgy little spider." Marta pressed a seam with vicious precision. "So here's what happens next, Miss Crane, since apparently we're in this together whether I agreed or not. You're going to finish whatever it is you're finishing, and you're going to finish it fast, and you're going to stop doing it at night with a candle like a heroine in a bad novel, because candles in windows get shops burned down in this city. And then—" the iron paused, hovering, and when Marta spoke again her voice had lost its armor entirely, gone quiet and raw, "—and then you're going to tell me if my brother's name is on whatever list you're keeping. Because the officers who came today knew about debts. Debts have a way of becoming questions, and questions have a way of becoming coats like yours, and I would like to know, before it happens, whether I've been protecting a friend or a trap."

The steam settled. The clock over the door ticked with the patience of a thing that had outlived empires and intended to outlive this one too.

Outside, far down the harbor road, a whistle blew — two short, one long. The signal, Ivo had told her, that the watcher had seen something worth reporting.

Hazel folded the useless hem flat, set down her needle, and began, very carefully, to take Captain Dresler's coat apart.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon with the door locked and the blinds drawn, unpicking the basting stitches she'd sewn in panic and resewing the cipher panels into the ceremonial tunic where they belonged — deeper now, subtler, each section split and scattered so that no single seam held more than a fragment. Her fingers shook for the first hour. By the second hour they had stopped shaking, because hands, Hazel had found, are braver than hearts; they know their trade, and trade is a kind of courage that doesn't require believing you'll live.

At dusk, a knock came at the back door — three slow, two fast. Ivo's knock. She let him in, and he took one look at her face, and one at the tunic spread across the table with its lining freshly closed, and said, "What happened?"

"A man with a clipboard," Hazel said, "nearly read a republic off a coat hanger. Your friend Elias trained me well. It's the only reason we're not all in a cell on Ritter Lane."

Ivo went pale in the manner of men who have imagined cells often and specifically. "Dresler's coat — you hid it in *Dresler's* coat?"

"It was inspired."

"It was insane."

"It was both. Most true things are." She pulled her shawl tighter and finally asked the question that had been sitting in her chest all day, heavy as wet wool. "Ivo. Marta's brother. Do you know his name?"

He blinked. "Why?"

"Because she saved my life today, and the officers mentioned debts, and if your ledger or your cipher or your dead man's plan touches her family, I want to know before the Provost's Office does." She held his gaze. "Because I broke my own rules today, Ivo. I trusted someone. And if trust is going to be the death of me, I intend to at least choose it with my eyes open."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere down the dark water a ship moaned against its ropes like something dreaming.

"There's something I should tell you about the ledger," he said quietly. "About what else is written in it. But not here — and Hazel?" He glanced at the blinds, at the door, at the tunic lying between them like a sleeping animal. "After today, I don't think we have as much time as Elias's letter promised. The man who wrote that letter may have promised it months ago. To the wrong people."

Hazel's hands, still warm from the day's work, went cold all the way through.
