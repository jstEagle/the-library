## 7. The Delegation Arrives

The snow came down on the harbor like ash that had lost its nerve, drifting rather than falling, and Hazel watched it from the workshop window while Marta swept the fitting room for the third time in an hour.

"You'll wear a groove in those boards," Marta said.

"Better a groove than a stain. Do you know what happens to a seamstress who stains the ceremonial uniform of a minister?"

"She gets shouted at by the minister."

"She gets *shot*," Hazel said pleasantly, "by whichever side is nearest at the time. That's why we sweep three times."

Marta snorted and leaned the broom against the wall. She had been strange since the raid — quieter, watchful, her hands always busy with something that didn't need doing. Hazel told herself it was the natural aftermath of having six occupation officers turn over her thread boxes. Anyone would be strange after that. She repeated it to herself the way she repeated stitch counts: until it became true enough to work by.

The delegation was due at ten. The peace delegation — though no one in the shop said those words without a small, bitter curl of the lip, because everyone knew what kind of peace it would be. The kind dictated from the occupying chancellery and signed by men in uniforms Hazel herself might sew. The republic's last diplomats, going abroad to negotiate with nothing in their pockets but dignity, and even that on credit.

Herr Brandt — no. Not Herr Brandt. Hazel caught herself at the thought and pressed it flat, the way she'd press a seam. Elias was drowned. Elias was dead. The man who had taught her that a message could live in the space between stitches had gone under the ice of the river in October, officially, and she had mourned him in the only way permitted: silently, and while working.

The cipher uniform hung in the back room under a dust sheet, half-finished, its lining holding the first section of the most dangerous document in the city. Every time Hazel walked past it she felt the way one feels passing a sleeping dog of uncertain temperament.

At five minutes past ten, the door opened and cold poured in wearing fur collars.

There were four of them. A secretary with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic posture of a man who spent his life apologizing. A military attaché whose boots were too new for his rank — Hazel noted the creases, unbroken across the toe; he had bought them for this occasion, poor man, dressing up for his country's funeral. A woman, Frau Lindqvist, second secretary or third or whatever title they gave women so they could pay them less, sharp-eyed and carrying a portfolio like a shield.

And behind them, shaking snow from his hat with the air of a man who had done it in better doorways, Minister Voss.

Hazel's needle nearly slipped.

She hadn't seen him since before the occupation consolidated — two years, nearly three. He had been younger then, or she had been more naive, or both. He had come to the shop then too, not for himself but for a courier's coat, and he had stood very close while she pinned the hem and said, in the flat tone men used for discussing weather, "This lining seems loose at the left shoulder, Fräulein Crane. You'll want to reinforce it." And inside the lining had been a dispatch that kept two resistance couriers out of prison, and neither of them ever spoke of it again, because that was the whole grammar of the language they shared.

Whether he remembered — whether he was still *safe* — was another question entirely. Men changed. Men broke. Men signed things.

"Minister." The shop's owner, old Herr Kessel, emerged bowing from the cutting room like a hermit crab from its shell. "An honor. We have prepared everything. Tea? There is tea."

"There is always tea," Voss said mildly. "Thank you, yes."

They were ushered into the fitting room, where Marta had set out chairs and mirrors and the little velvet stools that made clients feel important and made seamstresses' knees ache. Hazel brought the garments: the ceremonial coat for the attaché, the day suit for the secretary, the traveling dress alterations for Frau Lindqvist, and for Voss — the full-dress uniform of a republic that existed mostly in archive drawers now, dark wool with silver braid, the collar embroidered with the old crest that the occupation had banned from public display but permitted, with exquisite irony, on diplomatic dress.

"It fits worse every year," Voss complained, shrugging into the coat once Hazel held it open. "I have not grown wider. I am certain of it. The coat has simply given up hope."

"Coats do that," Hazel agreed, settling pins into her mouth-cushion. "Wool is honest. It remembers everything you've done to it."

"Then mine will remember better days." He looked at her in the mirror — a flicker, there and gone, the length of a breath. "You are new since my last visit, I think. Or newly promoted."

"I've been here some years, Excellency."

"Hm. Hands like yours don't look new." He raised his arms obediently as she measured across the chest. "My tailor in the capital used to say you could read a person's whole history in their stitching."

"Your tailor sounds like a flatterer."

"He sounds like a dead man now, unfortunately. Most of my acquaintances are." He said it lightly, the way tired men say heavy things, and Hazel felt the grief move through the room like a draft under a door.

Frau Lindqvist was being fitted by Marta at the far mirror, and the attaché stood with his arms out while Kessel fussed over the braid, so for perhaps forty seconds the conversation between Hazel and the minister belonged to no one else. Her hands worked. Tape measure, chalk, pins. And beneath the work, the other work: the question, ticking like a clock inside her ribs. *Do you know? Are you still ours? Can I trust you with the thing sewn into your own coat?*

Because that was the arrangement Ivo had described, the bargain she had struck in a moment of vertigo with a stranger bearing a dead man's handwriting: the final section of the cipher, the treasury proof, would travel in the lining of Minister Voss's ceremonial uniform, across the border, into the hands of foreign presses. Voss was the vessel. Whether Voss consented to be the vessel was, per Ivo, immaterial. Per Hazel's own conscience, somewhat more complicated.

And per the letter — Elias's letter, that impossible letter — there was a signal Hazel could give if the moment was right. A recognition, sewn in plain sight.

* * *

The fitting proceeded through the middle hours of the morning, an assembly line of ceremony. Kessel took the secretary's measurements with the solemnity of a priest administering last rites, which, Hazel reflected, was not far wrong. Frau Lindqvist turned out to have a dry wit deployed entirely under her breath, mostly at the expense of the attaché's new boots, and Marta — to Hazel's relief and faint surprise — laughed at one of the jokes, a real laugh, quick and startled out of her, as if it had escaped custody.

"The boots are very fine," Frau Lindqvist observed, watching the attaché admire himself.

"One must present a united front," the attaché said, missing it entirely.

"To whom? We're negotiating with people who own the boot factory."

Marta bit her lip. Hazel kept her eyes on her chalk line and did not smile, because ministers' fittings were no place for smiling, and also because she was busy.

She was measuring Voss's shoulders, and while the tape passed around him her pin cushion — the little velvet roll strapped to her wrist — was within easy reach of the coat draped over the chair beside them. And in the coat's collar stand, where the braid covered the canvas, there was a spot she had chosen weeks ago: dense, hidden, invisible to any eye that didn't know to count.

Elias's rules, rule the third: *A message offered openly is a confession. A message hidden is a hope. Know which one you can afford.*

She could afford a pin-prick. One prick, in the pattern she knew by heart — the same mark she'd sewn into eleven linings over three years, the sign that said, in a language with no alphabet: *I am here. I remember. Speak if you dare.*

Her fingers found the pin. The room was laughing about something — the attaché's hat now, or the tea. Voss stood with his back to her, patient as furniture, looking out the window at the snow and the gray water beyond it.

Hazel pricked the canvas. Once. Precise as a heartbeat.

Then she smoothed the collar flat, stepped back, and said, "Arms down, Excellency. Let's see how it hangs."

* * *

He did not react. That was the maddening thing about diplomats; they were trained from birth to have no faces. He turned, examined himself in the glass, tugged the hem, pronounced the shoulders "acceptable, barely," and asked whether the trousers could be ready by Thursday. Kessel said Thursday was possible if the heavens cooperated. Voss said the heavens had not cooperated with anything lately, so they might as well start.

But when the party gathered to leave — hats, gloves, portfolios, the small chaos of departure — Voss paused at the door and turned back as if he'd forgotten something.

"Fräulein Crane."

"Excellency?"

He crossed the room toward her, unhurried, and held out his right hand — the glove held loosely in the left, the hand itself bare. A handshake, from a minister to a seamstress. Unusual. Not scandalous. Just unusual enough.

His grip was dry and firm, and as he shook her hand he said, quietly, in the weather-tone: "The left shoulder of my old coat. It never did hold, despite the reinforcement. I've often wondered whether the fault was in the sewing—" his eyes came up and met hers, steady, dark, unreadable as deep water— "or in what was sewn."

Hazel's heart performed a maneuver she had not authorized.

"The lining was sound, Excellency," she said. "Whatever was carried in it was sounder."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a smile's outline, the way a crest shows through cloth when the light is right.

"Thursday, then," he said, loudly, for the benefit of the room. "And see the braid is straight. If we are to surrender our dignity, we shall at least do it neatly."

* * *

Afterward the shop exhaled. Kessel retired to his office to drink the tea meant for the delegation and feel important. The attaché's coat went onto the finishing rack. Snow kept sifting past the windows.

"Well?" Marta said. She was folding Frau Lindqvist's altered panels with more force than the fabric deserved. "The famous minister. Is he as grand as they say?"

"Grand?" Hazel considered. "No. Tired. The kind of tired that comes from carrying something too heavy for too long and being forbidden to put it down."

"Mm." Marta's needle flashed. "He looked at you strangely."

"He looks at everyone strangely. It's a diplomatic disease."

"No — not strangely. Familiarly." Marta's eyes came up, mild and terrible. "Like a man greeting an old colleague he can't name in company."

The scissors in Hazel's hand were suddenly the most interesting objects in the world. "Years of fittings," she said. "Ministers blur together."

"Mm," Marta said again, and returned to her folding, and Hazel understood with cold clarity that she had just been measured — tape passed around her, chalk marked, allowances noted — by a woman she had underestimated for three years.

* * *

That night she stayed late. She always stayed late; it was her alibi and her habit and her prayer. In the back room, under the lamp with its shade angled against the window, she unpicked the temporary basting along Voss's collar stand and checked the pin-prick: clean, single, correct. Then she sat with the cipher uniform across her knees and thought about shoulders.

*I am here,* the prick had said. *Speak if you dare.*

And he had spoken. *Was it the sewing, or what was sewn.* Which meant he remembered the courier's coat. Which meant he knew — had known for years — that the tailor shop was more than a tailor shop, and had said nothing, and kept coming back, and worn the banned crest on his collar into occupied streets because someone had to carry the flag somewhere and cloth was the last place it survived.

It also meant he might know about the cipher. Might suspect. Might, when the uniform crossed the border and the presses began to speak, understand exactly what his coat had been carrying — and either forgive her for using him or hang for it alongside her.

Hazel threaded a fresh needle and bent to the lining, and found her hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed her. Grief did that. It wasn't only fear. It was the sight of Voss standing in his doomed uniform complaining about his waistline while the country it represented dissolved like sugar in the harbor water. It was Frau Lindqvist's joke about the boot factory and the way her voice cracked on *negotiating*, just slightly, just enough. It was the whole delegation, dressed in ceremony for a funeral they were pretending was a conference.

A nation does not die all at once, she thought. It dies the way a coat wears out — at the elbows first, then the seams, then one day the whole shoulder gives and everything underneath is exposed. Someone should write that down. Someone with more time.

She stitched until her eyes ached. The second section of the cipher went in stroke by stroke, each knot placed according to Elias's counting-verse, which she still recited under her breath like a rosary — *one for the needle, two for the thread, three for the secret best left unsaid* — and by the time she tied off the final knot and pressed the lining closed with her palm, the window had gone fully black and the snow had stopped and the harbor bell was ringing the hour: midnight, and then the quarter, and then—

Footsteps. On the stair. Soft, deliberate, more than one pair.

Hazel blew out the lamp in the same motion that sent the uniform into the dust-sheeted cupboard, and stood in the dark with her heart going like a shuttle, listening — and heard, beneath the footsteps, a sound worse than boots: the small metallic click of a lock being tested from the outside.

Someone had keys to her shop. And they were coming up.
