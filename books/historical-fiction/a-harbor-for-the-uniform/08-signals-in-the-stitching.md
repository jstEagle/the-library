## 8. Signals in the Stitching

Ivo Markun had learned to read in three alphabets and two of them were lies.

The first was the Latin script every schoolboy in the republic was taught, the one that named streets and signed receipts. The second was Elias Brandt's cipher, which looked like nothing at all — a language of numbers dressed up as a merchant's ledger, columns of figures that meant flour or coal or nothing whatsoever. And the third, the one nobody had ever written down, was the alphabet of people's faces: the small betrayals and loyalties that flickered across them when they believed no one was counting.

He was fluent in all three. It was the third that kept him awake at night.

Now he sat in the back room above the chandlery on Rope Walk, where he'd rented a cot from a widow who charged him double because she suspected him of something and couldn't imagine what. The room smelled of tar and old rope and the kerosene stove he wasn't supposed to have. On the table before him, laid out under the stove's weak orange light, was Hazel Crane's work.

She had brought it to him that afternoon wrapped in brown paper, tied with plain string, carried through the streets as if it were nothing more than mending taken home for a slow evening. She hadn't said much. She never said much. She had simply set it down, tapped the paper twice with one finger — *the left sleeve first*, she'd mouthed, because Elias's protocol forbade speaking cipher matters aloud even behind locked doors — and gone back out into the grey afternoon like a woman delivering shirts.

*Left sleeve first.* As if there were any chance he'd start anywhere else. He smiled at the memory of her finger tapping the paper, that brisk little metronome of a woman who trusted him exactly as far as she could throw him, and possibly not that far.

He untied the string now, carefully, the way you open anything that might be watching you.

The garment inside was not yet a uniform. It was a coat front and two sleeves, cut for a broad-shouldered man — Minister Voss, he presumed — the wool dark blue, the stitching so fine that at arm's length it read as ordinary tailoring. Only when he turned the sleeve inside out did the second country appear: a lining of pale silk, and along its inner face, worked in thread slightly darker than the fabric itself, rows of stitches that would be invisible against skin, invisible against light, invisible against everything except a needle-sharp eye and a codebook held in the head rather than the hand.

Elias had designed the system years ago, back when the resistance still had money for silk. *A dispatch should look like nothing,* Elias used to say, threading his needle with those enormous careful fingers. *Nothing is the only disguise that never wears out.* He'd been proud of it, the way a composer is proud of a theme — and vain about it too, though he'd deny vanity with the same solemnity he denied everything else.

Ivo took out his notebook — a real notebook, bought from a real stationer, filled with invented accounts of a fictional import business — and began to work.

* * *

Decoding Elias's stitch-cipher was less reading than listening. Each group of stitches mapped to a figure; each run of figures resolved, through the key they'd agreed on in that first desperate meeting, into letters. It demanded patience of the kind Ivo had once had in abundance and now had to ration like sugar. His eyes tired quickly these days. Too many nights squinting at harbor ledgers by candlelight, too many years of pretending not to recognize faces.

But this — this came back to him like a dance his feet remembered.

The first section ran the length of the left sleeve, perhaps four hundred stitches, an hour's careful transcription before the arithmetic even began. He copied the groups into the notebook, checked each twice, then set about the reduction. Outside, the gulls argued over the harbor's leavings. Somewhere below, the widow dropped a pot and swore at it with real feeling. The stove ticked.

By nine o'clock the letters stood in his notebook in his own cramped hand, and Ivo sat back and read what Hazel Crane had sewn.

It was the treasury evidence — or the beginning of it. Dates. Transfer numbers. The names of three banks in three cities, two of them foreign, all of them conveniently far from the occupation's auditors. Sums that made his breath go shallow even now, even expecting them, even having seen Elias's original ledger pages once and only once, in a cellar in 1917, by the light of a lamp with a sock over it. Forty million crowns, give or take the rounding of thieves. Carried out of the republic in diplomatic trunks while its soldiers went without boots.

*There,* Ivo thought. *There it is. Sewn into a coat that will sit at a peace table while men who stole a nation argue about its corpse.*

He felt the old lift in his chest, the purpose rising clean. This was why he'd come to this cold city and let himself be overcharged by a suspicious widow. This was the thing that mattered. In six weeks the delegation would travel, the coat would travel with it, and somewhere beyond the border a press would set the figures in type and the world would have to look at what the occupation had done.

He should have stopped there. A wiser man would have stopped there.

But the sleeve continued. Past the last group of treasury figures, the stitches didn't end. They changed — subtly, the way a piece of music changes key without announcing it. The rhythm of the groups shifted. The intervals stretched.

Ivo frowned at it.

At first he assumed error. Hazel was new to this particular cipher — she knew the old dispatch codes, yes, but this system was Elias's later work, the refinement he'd been developing through the autumn before he drowned. Perhaps she'd miscounted. Perhaps a stitch had pulled. He went back through the transcription twice, found nothing wrong, and realized slowly, with a feeling like a floor giving way, that nothing *was* wrong.

The change was deliberate. It was in Elias's hand as surely as the letter had been. A second layer — a cipher within the cipher, keyed differently, buried in the same silk. Elias had always loved his nested boxes. *If they break the outer wall,* he'd say, *let them find an empty courtyard.*

Ivo reached for the secondary key. There was only one candidate: the old cell-book, the numbering system from the early days, which Elias had retired officially in 1916 and which Ivo alone, as far as he knew, still remembered completely. That was the point of it. That was the trap of it. A key shared by two men, one of whom was dead.

His hands were steady. He noted that with detached interest, the way you note the weather.

The second layer resolved in under an hour. It was short. It was a list.

* * *

Names.

Nine of them.

He read them once, then again, then a third time with his finger under each word as though the words might rearrange themselves if unwatched. They did not rearrange themselves. Names held their shape the way wounds held their shape.

Two he recognized immediately — minor customs officials, men whose cooperation with the occupation had been an open secret, the kind of corruption everyone shrugged at because the alternative was despair. Fine. Expected. Elias had always kept files on such men; half the resistance's tradecraft rested on knowing which clerk could be bribed and which had already sold himself cheaper elsewhere.

The third name made him sit very still.

Kessel. Anton Kessel. Quartermaster of the harbor relief office — the man who signed the chits that moved food to the occupied districts, who had quietly padded every manifest so that a fraction of each shipment reached families the occupation had written off. Ivo had broken bread with Kessel. Had slept under his roof in March, in the bad week when the safehouses burned, when Kessel had hidden him in a cellar full of sauerkraut barrels and complained cheerfully the whole time about the smell.

*Kessel takes his cut,* Elias had said once, when Ivo asked. *And passes the rest along. That arithmetic works.*

And now here was Kessel's name, stitched into silk by a dead man's design, on a list that would cross a border and land in foreign newspapers beside the word *collaborator*. Not *patriot with a price*. Collaborator. The list had no footnotes. Lists never do.

Ivo poured himself the inch of spirit he allowed himself on working nights and did not drink it. He held the glass instead, watching the lamplight swim in it, and made himself think like Elias instead of like a man who owed someone his life.

Because here was the question that wouldn't lie flat: why bury this list inside the treasury cipher at all? The treasury evidence was the mission. The mission had a shape, a deadline, a purpose — expose the theft, force the world's hand, give the dying republic its one clean wound of truth. The collaborator list served none of that. Worse, it endangered all of it. Any examiner who broke the outer layer might, with time and luck and malice, reach the inner one — and then the story in the foreign presses would not be *occupation steals millions* but *resistance names traitors*, and every man on that list would hang, and every man who might have testified would suddenly have excellent reasons to help the occupation find whoever sewed the coat.

Unless the list was meant to be found. Unless it was the point.

Or unless —

Ivo put the glass down untasted. He was good at arithmetic and bad at this next part, the part where you followed a suspicion toward a person you loved.

Elias had faked his death in October. Officially drowned off the mole, body never recovered, a grief-stricken network mourning a man whose funeral Ivo had attended with genuine tears and a genuine sense of fraud, because some animal part of him had known even then, standing at the rail in the rain, that the sea had not been given the courtesy of the truth. Elias had planned his own disappearance with the same loving thoroughness he gave to ciphers. Which meant Elias had also planned *this* — the letter, the timeline, the final transmission — before October. Before whatever had actually happened to him.

Which meant the list had been stitched into the plan months ago. By a free man. A man choosing his names.

Or by a man who already knew what was coming for him. Who had known, perhaps, that the occupation was closing in, and who had decided — Ivo's stomach turned at the thought — that if he was taken, the network should be seeded with poison in advance. Decoy traitors. False confessions. Names that would burn trust like a firebreak, burning the resistance back to a defensible line.

That was an Elias thing to do. That was exactly, precisely, horribly an Elias thing to do — the chess-player's mercy, sacrificing pawns you loved to save a game you might lose.

Or the occupation had him already, and the whole letter was bait on a hook, and Ivo was the fish being reeled toward a quay where men with rubber truncheons waited. The thought arrived and refused to leave. He examined it honestly, turning it over, looking for the flaw. The letter's hand was right. The cipher's structure was right. But both predated October — Hazel would verify that, she had the archive — and things that predate October can be deployed by anyone who possesses them. Dead men's keys fit many doors.

He looked at the list again. Nine names. Two corrupt clerks. Kessel.

And the fourth name.

The fourth name he had not yet let himself read properly. It had been sitting at the edge of his vision all evening like a guest he was pretending not to see. Now he looked at it straight on.

*Reeder, M.*

Marta Reeder. Finisher at Crane's workshop. Eleven years at the trade, according to what little Ivo had gathered. The woman who — if Hazel's terse reports could be credited — had begun asking questions. Why the late hours. Why the special commission. Why the extra silk ordered under a false invoice number that Ivo himself had arranged.

He laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry note with nothing behind it, startling the gulls off the roof.

Of course. Of course Elias had put her on the list. Or the occupation had put her there through Elias. Either way, the circle closed neatly: the woman asking questions at the workshop was named, in secret stitches, as a traitor — and the man who'd smuggled the false invoice into the workshop's paperwork was sitting in a rented room above a chandlery, holding the proof, drinking nothing.

Had Hazel seen this section yet? No. She'd told him plainly: she sewed, she verified the outer layer against her archive, and she passed the work to him unread past the verification marks. Protocol. Elias's rule — *the seamstress stitches, the reader reads, neither knows the whole.* Designed to protect them both. Tonight it meant Hazel had spent weeks putting needle to silk without knowing she was embroidering a death list into a minister's coat.

Tomorrow she would know. Because protocol also said the reader reported the content to the stitcher — how else could she judge risk at the needle? — and Ivo would have to sit across from that composed, guarded woman and tell her that the garment under her hands contained, among other things, the name of her colleague. Her friend, possibly. People who worked twelve hours side by side over the same cloth developed attachments whether they approved of them or not.

And he would have to watch her face while he said it. Third alphabet. Faces.

He gathered the pages, squared them, and sat a moment longer with the sleeve draped over his knee, feeling its weight — nothing, really, a few ounces of wool and silk and thread, less than a loaf of bread, and heavy enough to sink a republic.

Outside, snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the window like someone patiently trying a series of keys. Down in the street a man stopped beneath the lamp opposite and stood there long enough to light a cigarette, and Ivo marked him without moving — tall, army greatcoat, civilian cap, the posture of a man waiting rather than smoking — and marked, a minute later, the man's unhurried departure toward the harbor road.

Probably nothing. The city was full of men waiting out the cold.

He blew out the stove anyway and finished the night's work by memory and moonlight, because caution was the only inheritance Elias had left him that appreciated in value.

* * *

Hazel received the news the next morning in the workshop's cutting room, between the delivery of Voss's trousers and the noon whistle, standing with chalk dust on her fingers as though the conversation were a fitting and she had measurements to take.

Ivo laid it out as gently as the facts allowed. The treasury evidence was sound — better than sound, it was devastating. But there was a second layer, and the second layer was names, and the names included a quartermaster who fed starving districts, and — he watched her face do something very small and very controlled around the eyes — a finisher at her own bench.

"Marta," Hazel said. Not a question. Just the name, set down on the table between them like a tool she was deciding whether to pick up.

"The key is Elias's. The layer is his construction. But the list itself—" Ivo spread his hands. "Either Elias chose those names freely, in which case he believed Marta Reeder betrays this workshop. Or he didn't choose them freely, in which case we are sewing a lie into a minister's coat, and the lie will kill people."

Hazel looked down at her chalk-dusted fingers for a long moment. When she raised her head, her voice had the flat calm he was beginning to understand was her version of screaming.

"She asked me yesterday," Hazel said, "why the commission coat needed a double lining." A pause, precisely measured. "I told her it was for warmth."

"And?"

"And she nodded," Hazel said, "and went back to her bench, and stayed an hour late, hemming officer's trousers that were due Friday. Which either means she believes me." Her eyes came up and held his, grey and level and colder than the harbor in February. "Or it means she wanted me to think she does."

Somewhere across the workshop, faint through the wall, a sewing machine started up — a thin, steady, patient sound, like something being stitched that could not be undone.

"Find out which," Hazel said, "before I sew another inch. Because if Elias put her name in that silk—" she picked up her chalk, and her hand, Ivo noticed, was perfectly steady, steadier than his own had been all night— "then one of us taught a dead man to lie. And I intend to know which one of us it was."
