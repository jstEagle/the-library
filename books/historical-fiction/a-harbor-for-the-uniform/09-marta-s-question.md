## 9. Marta's Question

The trouble with secrets, Hazel had long ago decided, was not the keeping of them. Keeping was easy. Keeping was a discipline like any other — you folded the secret small, you pressed it flat, you stored it in the dark of yourself the way she stored finished linings in the cedar cabinet, and you did not take it out to look at it when no one was asking. No. The trouble with secrets was that they changed your hands.

Hazel had noticed it first in October, a week after Elias Brandt went into the sea that did not have him. Her fingers had begun to hesitate at the wrong moments — a half-second's pause before she cut a thread in front of Marta, a too-careful smoothness when she handed over a finished sleeve. A seamstress's hands were supposed to be fluent. Hers had developed an accent.

And Marta Vilkaitė, who had worked the next table over for eleven years and could read a person's tension the way she read a crooked lapel, had noticed.

Of course she had. That was the whole catastrophe of Marta: she noticed everything and said nothing, which Hazel had always taken for dullness and now understood to be its own kind of cunning. Marta was a broad, slow-seeming woman with flour-pale skin and a laugh that arrived late, like a guest who'd gotten lost on the way. She hummed off-key. She kept a tin of candied ginger in her workbasket and offered it to apprentices until the apprentices learned to fear it as a test. She had buried a husband in the influenza and raised a brother on her own wages, and she stitched buttonholes so perfectly regular that inspectors assumed they were machine work, which she considered the highest compliment available to a human being.

Hazel had spent four months lying to her by increments, and every increment had felt like pulling a stitch too tight — the fabric puckering somewhere out of sight.

* * *

It came to a head on a Thursday evening in December, with frost ferning the workshop windows and the gas lamps turned low because low lamps were cheaper and also because Hazel had asked for them low, claiming eye strain, which was a lie built on top of other lies like bricks on bricks.

She was alone. Or believed herself to be. The delegation uniform hung on the dress form near the window — Minister Voss's ceremonial coat, half-finished, its inner canvas basted but its lining still open along the left side like a wound awaiting surgery. The cipher lay coiled beside it in a ribbon of paper no wider than her thumb, thirty-one rows of figures in Elias's cramped hand, each row destined for its own channel of stitching, invisible unless you knew to look at the spacing of the fell, the depth of the herringbone, the places where a careful eye would find nothing at all because careful eyes saw what they expected.

Hazel had the lining folded back and the first row pinned when she heard the stair.

Not Ivo's step — his was quick and slightly uneven, a man who'd learned to walk quietly on boats. Not the night watchman, who coughed at the landing the way other men knocked. This was a familiar step, heavy through the heel, unhurried in the way of someone who has decided not to be hurrying.

Marta came up the last three stairs carrying two enamel mugs, and the smell of chicory coffee arrived before she did.

"You left your lamp burning," Marta said. "I saw it from the yard. I thought, either Hazel is working late again, or we are being robbed by someone very tidy." She set one mug on Hazel's table without asking. "So. Tidy robber."

"Marta." Hazel's hands did the thing now — the accent thing — moving too smoothly to cover the pins, closing the open lining an inch, two inches, casual as breathing, except that breathing had never once been casual since October. "It's nearly nine. You should be home."

"I should be a great many places." Marta lowered herself onto the apprentice stool, which creaked under her the way it creaked under everyone, and wrapped both hands around her mug. She looked, Hazel thought, like a woman settling in for weather. "My brother is at his aunt's. The stove at home eats coal and gives back nothing. And I have been watching you lie to me for four months, and I thought tonight I would rather know than wonder."

The chicory smell suddenly seemed very loud.

"I don't know what you—"

"Hazel." Marta said her name the way she said *interfacing* or *selvage* — a technical term, requiring accuracy. "You burn more lamp oil than the rest of the shop together. You lock the cedar cabinet now, which you never used to do, and you keep the key on a ribbon under your collar, which I know about because you check it eleven times a day, and I counted. You started pressing your seams twice after the officers came in October. Twice. Nobody presses twice unless they're hiding thread between the passes." She sipped her coffee. "And for three weeks there has been a coat on that form that does not appear in the order book."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a pipe knocked in the walls like a polite fist.

"Say it isn't so," Marta said mildly. "Lie to me properly, with details, and I will believe you, because I want to believe you. Or don't." She looked down into her mug. "But understand what you're choosing. If you lie to me and I find out later — and I would find out later, Hazel, I find out everything, it's my only talent — then whatever we have been for eleven years becomes something I have to re-examine, all of it, every kindness, wondering what it was covering. Whereas if you tell me the truth—" she shrugged, an enormous, geological shrug, "—then at least I know what I'm risking."

"And what," Hazel said carefully, "do you think you're risking?"

Marta set the mug down. When she lifted her head, her face had lost its softness entirely, and Hazel saw, with a strange vertigo, the woman underneath — the one who had buried a husband at twenty-nine and gone back to the shop the next Monday because the shop paid by the day.

"I think I'm risking the firing squad," Marta said. "Or the prison on Kalm Street, which is worse, they say, because at least the squad is quick. I think whoever you're working for — and it's someone, it's been someone since autumn, since Elias Brandt drowned, since you came back from his funeral looking like a woman whose house had burned down while she stood in the yard — I think that someone would not thank me for knowing. And I think you would not be able to protect me." She folded her hands on her knees, enormous capable hands, hands that had sewn ten thousand buttonholes. "That's what I think I'm risking. So."

* * *

Later — much later, years later, in rooms with different light — Hazel would try to reconstruct the arithmetic of that moment and never quite recover it. What she remembered was not reasoning. It was smaller than reasoning. It was the memory of Marta's hands the week after Hazel's mother died, appearing wordlessly with a covered dish and taking over the mourning crepes because grief makes sloppy stitches and Marta knew it. It was the candied ginger, offered a hundred times, refused a hundred times, still offered.

Every rule said no. The rules Elias had drilled into her — *the cell is one person; the chain is the seam, not the people; a secret shared is a seam unpicked* — every rule said send her home, lock the door, move the coat, change the pattern, become a stranger to her forever if necessary. The rules existed because people were taken to Kalm Street and came back different, or didn't come back, and everything they'd ever known came back with them in the shape of questions.

But the rules had also produced this: four months of a puckered seam, of counting to eleven under her collar, of lying with her hands to the one person in the city who read hands.

"The coat doesn't exist," Hazel heard herself say. "Officially. There's no order book entry because there's no order."

Marta nodded slowly, as if confirming a measurement.

"It carries something," Hazel went on, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears, steady and thin as wire. "In the lining. Something that needs to reach people outside the country. People who are being lied to about what happened to money that belonged to — " she stopped. Named no nation. The rules, even breaking them, held their shape.

"To the republic," Marta said quietly. "To whatever's left of it."

"Yes."

"And Elias Brandt?"

The name landed like a dropped spool. Hazel looked at her.

"He taught you," Marta said. Not a question. "Years ago. I used to see him in here evenings, before he got important, before the code work made him a name people whispered. He'd stand exactly where you're standing, and you'd show him a seam, and he'd look at it longer than any tailor looks at a seam." She picked up her coffee and drank, and her voice went wry around the rim of the mug. "You think nobody notices anything in this shop. We are tailors, Hazel. Noticing is the entire profession."

Hazel sat down on the worktable's edge, because her legs had opinions about standing. The gas lamp hissed. Outside, the frost thickened on the glass, and beyond it the harbor lights burned in their usual patient row.

"He's dead," Hazel said. "Officially."

"Officially." Marta tasted the word. "You've started saying 'officially' about things. Did you know that? Since October. Officially drowned. Officially closed for inventory. It's become a whole vocabulary with you." She leaned forward. "Is he?"

And here was the place where Hazel could have stopped — where the rules screamed loudest, where a sensible conspirator would have given Marta just enough to hang her loyalty on and no more. But Ivo's face rose in her mind instead, Ivo with his letter in a dead man's hand, and beneath him the memory she couldn't shake from Chapter Six, the raid, the officer's glove trailing along the cedar cabinet, and Marta stepping forward with her big calm voice saying *the officer's coats are here, sir, shall I show you the winter stock,* and meaning it, or seeming to mean it, and Hazel never having dared to ask which.

"You know I can't answer that," Hazel said.

Marta studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — the late-arriving laugh arriving early, in miniature, at her own expense.

"No," she said. "But you didn't deny it. Which from you is practically a signed confession." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and gathered both mugs. "All right. Here is what happens now. Tomorrow you'll discover that the second press pass on the delegation coat has been done badly — badly enough that it must be redone, and the redoing will require the lining opened again, and I will be the one assigned to help, because I am the fastest hand in this shop and everyone knows it. You won't ask me to do anything. You won't tell me anything further. But if a moment comes when you need a pair of hands that aren't yours — hands that can be in the room when you can't —" she paused at the top of the stairs, backlit by the hall lamp, suddenly and enormously there, "—you'll say the word 'ginger,' and I'll understand."

"That's absurd," Hazel managed.

"It's a system," Marta said with dignity. "Elias presumably had one. Mine involves candy." She started down the stairs, then stopped. "One more thing. The officer who came in October — the tall one, with the gloves — he came back Tuesday. While you were at the quay with your young man." A beat, delicately brutal. "Yes, I've seen him too. He asked for you by name. He asked when you worked late. I told him you keep banker's hours and go home to your sister." Her voice flattened. "He wrote it down anyway, Hazel. In a little book. So whatever is in that coat — finish it quickly. Men with little books don't come back a third time to chat."

Her steps descended, unhurried, heavy through the heel. The street door opened and shut. The cold came up the stairwell for a moment, smelling of coal smoke and snow, and then the workshop was quiet again, quieter than before, because now the quiet had a witness in it.

Hazel stood a long time with her hand flat against the delegation coat, feeling through the wool and canvas the paper ribbon sleeping inside, thirty-one rows of a dead man's figures. Eleven times a day she had checked the key at her collar. She reached for it now, out of habit — and stopped.

Then she took the key from its ribbon, walked to the cedar cabinet, and — after a glance at the empty stairwell, after listening to the pipes knock their polite fist in the walls — left it, for the first time in four months, unlocked.

It was either the stupidest thing she had ever done or the beginning of something she had forgotten the shape of. The lamp hissed. Outside, the frost grew one crystal thicker, and down in the dark of the harbor, unseen, a smuggler's boat was already making arrangements that would cost more than anyone yet knew.
