## 10. The Harbor at Night

The smuggler's name was Anselm, and he charged by the kilogram.

Not per head — per kilogram. Ivo had learned this on his second visit to the tavern at the edge of the fish quay, a place called the Salt Barrel that smelled of tar, herring brine, and the particular despair of men who gambled away their winter wages before the ice came. Anselm sat in the back corner with a tankard of something dark and a ledger open before him like a priest with scripture, and when Ivo asked about passage out of the city, the old man had looked him up and down with the frank appraisal of a horse trader.

"Seventy kilos," Anselm said. "That's what you weigh, more or less, with your boots and your coat and whatever you're carrying in your pockets that you think I don't know about."

"And if I'm carrying something heavy?"

"Then you're paying for it honestly, which is more than most of my customers can say." Anselm tapped the ledger. "I've moved priests who lied about their vestments. I've moved a countess who swore her jewelry case was books. Books!" He laughed, a sound like gravel poured into a bucket. "I weighed them in front of her. Made her watch the needle swing. You want honesty in this business? Buy it by the kilogram."

It was, Ivo had to admit, the most transparent arrangement he'd encountered in six years of resistance work. There was something almost restful about a man whose corruption was priced and posted.

Tonight the Salt Barrel was half empty, which meant either the fishing had been good or the patrols had been bad. Ivo couldn't tell anymore. He took the chair across from Anselm, ordered nothing — Anselm's beer was famously an insult to barley everywhere — and kept his hands flat on the table where the light could find them.

"You look worse than last time," Anselm observed.

"I've been sleeping badly."

"In this city? A novelty." The old man closed his ledger with the care of a man closing a coffin lid. "You wanted three routes. North along the coast to the Finnish boats. East over the ice road, if the weather holds cruel enough. And south through the estuary on my cousin's barge, God rot his lazy soul. I have answers for all three. Some of them are even true."

"Let's start with the ones that cost the most."

"Naturally." Anselm leaned forward, lowering his voice beneath the tavern's clatter without appearing to — a skill Ivo suspected he'd learned moving contraband under the nose of the previous occupation, and the one before that, and possibly the Tsar himself. "The north route is dead. Since Tuesday. There's a patrol boat anchored off the breakwater now, and they're boarding everything with a hull. They took two of my colleagues' cargoes. One of those cargoes was people."

Ivo felt the cold settle deeper into his coat. "The ice road?"

"Ice road's honest but slow, and you'd need papers I can't forge anymore. The new stamps have a thread in them. An actual thread, sewn into the paper. Can you imagine?" He shook his head with genuine professional admiration. "Somebody in their forgery office is very proud of himself, and someday I'm going to meet him and buy him dinner."

"And the south?"

"The south is open. For now." Anselm turned the ledger around and slid it across, tapping one line with a blunt finger stained black by decades of ink. "But there's a complication, and the complication has a number attached."

Everything, Ivo thought, had a number attached lately. The whole republic was being sold off by weight, like Anselm's passengers.

* * *

The number was four hundred marks.

"For information leading to the arrest of Elias Brandt," Anselm read aloud, his voice flat with contempt. "And — this is the part you'll enjoy, boy, so brace yourself — 'and any person found to be continuing or completing his cryptographic work.' Two hundred additional. It went up on the garrison notice boards yesterday morning. They're calling him a saboteur now. Before October he was just a clerk who drank too much. Now he's a saboteur worth four hundred marks, which tells me somebody important finally opened his files and didn't like what fell out."

Ivo kept his face still. Four hundred marks was a fortune. It was a year's wages for a dockhand. It was enough to make an honest man dishonest and a dishonest man enthusiastic.

"Four hundred," he repeated.

"Five hundred by Friday, if I know how these things go. They always start low and climb. Like auctioning a horse they haven't caught yet." Anselm pulled the notice from inside his ledger — of course he'd stolen a copy; the man probably stole copies of his own birth certificate out of habit — and smoothed it flat on the table between them.

Ivo looked at the printed description. *Middle-aged. Grey at the temples. Walks with a slight favoring of the left knee.* Someone had watched Elias closely enough to catalog his limp. That detail landed in Ivo's chest like a stone dropped down a well. You didn't learn a man's limp from paperwork. You learned it from following him, week after week, through streets and markets and church doors, until his way of walking became as familiar as your own.

"They know he's alive," Ivo said quietly.

"They suspect he's alive. There's a difference, and the difference is why the price hasn't gone higher. If they knew — if they had him — there'd be no notice at all. Just a quiet cell and a quieter end." Anselm retrieved the paper and folded it back into his ledger. "No. This is a fishing notice. They're casting wide and hoping something bites. Which brings me to my advice, which you'll ignore, because everyone ignores it: whoever is finishing this man's work should finish it quickly and be somewhere else. The water around this job is getting crowded, and I don't only mean my boats."

* * *

They settled terms. Three berths on the cousin's barge — Ivo did not say for whom, and Anselm did not ask, because asking would have required him to raise the price and both men preferred the fiction that the cargo was crates of machine parts. Payment in half now, half at the quay. Departure window: the last week of December, tide and weather permitting, weather rarely permitting.

"Your friend the seamstress," Anselm said, just as Ivo stood to leave. "She still breathing?"

The question was casual. Anselm's questions were always casual. That was what made them dangerous.

"She's breathing."

"Good. Keep it that way. Women who sew for that shop have a habit of stopping." The old man raised his tankard an inch — a toast, a warning, possibly both. "Tell her Anselm says hello. She'll pretend she doesn't remember me. She remembers me. I fitted her father for a burial suit once, back when fitting was all I knew how to do. Finest set of seams on a dead man this port ever saw."

Outside, the cold hit like a slap from a stranger. The harbor lay grey and shivering under a sky the color of dishwater, masts swaying against the warehouses, gulls wheeling and screaming their grievances at no one. Ivo turned up his collar and began the long walk back toward the workshop district, taking the route he always took — never twice the same, but always the same *kind* of route: past the cooperage, through the alley behind the chandler's, across the square where the fishwives argued about prices in voices that could strip paint.

He counted the watchers the way Hazel counted stitches. That was how he thought of it now; she had infected his thinking. *Every seam is a decision,* she'd told him once, exasperated by his fumbling attempts to understand the cipher work. *You choose where the needle goes and where it doesn't. Miss one stitch and the whole lining tells on you.*

He passed the cooperage. Nothing. The chandler's alley held only a cat with the insolent stare of a creature who had never once been occupied. The fishwives were arguing about herring. All normal. All quiet.

Which was, of course, exactly when he saw the man by the fountain.

* * *

He was pretending to feed the pigeons, which was the oldest trick in the surveillance book and insulting to everyone involved — the watcher, the watched, and the pigeons, who deserved better than to be props. He wore a fishmonger's apron over civilian clothes, which fooled nobody because no fishmonger in the history of the trade had ever stood idle beside a fountain for forty minutes. His hands were wrong too. Fishmongers' hands move quick and greedy. This man's hands hung still, patient, the hands of a man waiting for something to walk past him.

Ivo knew those hands.

He knew them from a cellar on Kastani Street, three years ago, lamplight and cigarette smoke and Elias Brandt teaching six of them the first principles of the cipher — *never repeat a key, never trust a pattern, the enemy reads rhythm before he reads letters.* The man by the fountain had been the youngest of them then, sharp-eyed, quick to laugh, forever volunteering for the dangerous errands because danger made him feel chosen. His name had been Lauri. Or he had claimed it was Lauri. In that cellar, names were coats you put on and took off, and Ivo had long since stopped believing anyone's coat.

Lauri had vanished fourteen months ago, right after the occupation tightened its grip. Everyone assumed he'd been taken. Ivo had lit a candle for him, privately, in the Church of Saint Olaf, feeling like a fraud the entire time because he hadn't especially liked the man — but you lit candles for your dead regardless of affection. That was the whole point of candles.

Apparently the candle had been premature.

Ivo did not stop walking. Stopping was the amateur's death; every instinct Elias had drilled into them screamed *keep moving, keep your pace, keep your eyes soft and forward.* But his mind was moving fast enough for both of them, turning the sighting over and over like a coin examined for counterfeit.

Lauri by the fountain. Lauri watching the approach routes to the workshop district — because that was what this street was, whether the man knew it or not; the fountain square fed directly toward the tailor shop by way of two side lanes. And the notice in Anselm's ledger, the five-hundred-mark net cast wide over the city, fishing for a drowned man and anyone finishing his work.

You didn't need to be a code-master to do that arithmetic. Even Ivo, who had been Elias's worst student — a distinction Elias had announced warmly and often — could solve it. The cell had been turned. Not broken, not arrested: *turned*. Somewhere in the occupation's machinery there was a room with a lamp and a file full of names, and Lauri had sat in that room and decided his skin was worth more than his oaths, and now here he was, feeding pigeons that weren't hungry, wearing an apron that lied about him as thoroughly as his name ever had.

Or — and here the cold thought arrived, sliding in under the door of his mind like winter itself — perhaps Ivo was wrong. Perhaps it was merely a man who resembled Lauri. The world was full of young men with still hands.

Except the left boot. Lauri had worn his boots laced in that peculiar crossed fashion, some village habit he'd once explained at length to a cellar full of bored conspirators, and the man by the fountain wore his the same way, laces crossing high, neat as a signature.

A signature. Yes. Ivo almost smiled at the bitter joke of it. The man had signed his betrayal in bootlaces.

* * *

He walked on. Past the square, past the shuttered bakery, down toward the canal where the ice was forming in grey plates along the edges. Only when he reached the iron bridge did he permit himself to pause, hands on the freezing rail, breath steaming, a tired man looking at dirty water — a pose so innocent it was nearly invisible.

Three possibilities presented themselves, and he arranged them in order of dread.

First: Lauri was watching the district generally, part of the wide net, knowing nothing specific. Bad, survivable.

Second: Lauri had narrowed the search to the workshop itself. Worse. The raid in November had come close; Hazel had hidden the cipher lining inside a finished officer's coat while soldiers turned drawers over ten feet away. Close calls were not free. Each one taught the hunters where the walls were thin.

Third — and this was the one that made his stomach fold over on itself — Lauri was watching because someone inside the workshop had already talked. Not betrayed, maybe. Not even willingly. Just... pressured. Frightened. Leveraged. People broke the way ice broke: silently at first, hairline fractures you couldn't see until the whole sheet gave way under you.

Marta's face rose unbidden in his memory — the pale, competent colleague, the way her eyes had tracked Hazel's late hours. He pushed the thought away and it came back, the way guilty thoughts do, patient as tide.

He had to get word to Hazel tonight. Not a note — notes were how networks died, paper was evidence with legs — but a visit, a signal worked out weeks ago: the curtain drawn to the second hook meant *come to the back stair after midnight*, the third hook meant *stay away entirely*. He would give her the second hook and tell her everything. Anselm's prices. The notice. The man by the fountain with the honest boots and the lying apron.

And underneath it all, one more thing he would not tell her yet, because he was carrying it the way you carry a splinter driven deep — Elias. The description in the notice, *grey at the temples, favoring the left knee*, had been accurate down to details only intimate observation could supply, and that accuracy gnawed at Ivo like rust at a chain. Either the occupation had hunted Elias for months with extraordinary patience, or someone had handed them the portrait ready-drawn. Someone who knew the limp. Someone from the cellar on Kastani Street.

He straightened from the rail and set off toward the workshop district, choosing a route that doubled back on itself twice and ended where it began, because paranoia, he reflected grimly, was at least a craft you could practice. The light was failing. Lamps were being lit along the quay one by one, little orange defiance against the early dark, and the harbor smelled of ice coming, the season turning hard and final.

Behind him, in the square, the man by the fountain finally abandoned his ungrateful pigeons and drifted into the crowd — walking, Ivo noted, with an easy, unhurried stride, no favoring of any knee at all, heading not toward the garrison barracks as a patrolman would, but toward the commercial quarter. Toward the shipping offices. Toward, though Ivo could not yet prove it, the very tailor shop where a seamstress was at this hour bending over a ceremonial uniform, stitching a republic's stolen truth into its lining, one careful stitch at a time.

Ivo lengthened his step. Midnight was hours away and suddenly very far.

He just hoped he'd reach the back stair before the net drew tight — and that when he told Hazel what he'd seen, her steady needle-hand wouldn't shake. Because there was one thing he hadn't said aloud to Anselm, one thing he'd only admitted to the frozen canal water: the letter that had started all of this, the letter in the dead man's hand — Ivo had read it a hundred times now, in lamplight, in daylight, in his dreams. And the more he read it, the more certain he grew that it had not been written in October, when Elias supposedly drowned.

It had been written in August. Months before. Which meant someone was executing a plan Elias had made long ago — with or without Elias. And Ivo was beginning to fear that the answer was sitting in Hazel's archive, in her careful collection of genuine dispatches, waiting in plain ink for someone to compare the hands.

Tomorrow, he would ask her to do exactly that.

Tonight, he had a curtain hook to turn, and a story to tell that he already knew she would not want to believe.
