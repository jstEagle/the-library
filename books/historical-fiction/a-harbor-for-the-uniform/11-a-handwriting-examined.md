## 11. A Handwriting Examined

The trouble with keeping an archive of a dead man's hand was that you had to steal it one piece at a time.

Hazel had been doing it for two years without quite admitting to herself that this was what she was doing. Every dispatch that passed through the workshop — every lining Elias Brandt had sewn his instructions into, every scrap of tissue paper bearing his cramped, forward-leaning script — Hazel had copied before it left her hands. Not the contents; the contents went where they were meant to go and were forgotten as fast as decency allowed. What she kept was the *hand* itself. The way Elias crossed his *t*'s like a man slashing through undergrowth. The way his capital *B* leaned back on its spine as if suspicious of whatever followed it. The little hooks he put on his *y*'s, always curling left, like tails trying to escape.

She kept them in the bottom of her pattern book, interleaved between the tracing sheets for collars and cuffs, written out in her own careful reproduction of each specimen, dated, numbered. Forty-one entries. She had once joked to herself — in the grim interior voice she reserved for jokes — that if tailoring ever failed her, she could make a living as a forger. It was not entirely a joke. Elias himself had told her, early on, in the flat tone he used for the only lessons that mattered: *A hand can be copied. A hand cannot be copied twice the same way. That is how you catch a liar.*

She had thought of those words perhaps four hundred times since Ivo Markun had pressed the letter into her palm three nights ago. And every one of those times, she had found a reason not to open the pattern book.

Tonight there were no reasons left. Marta had gone home at seven — home being a room above a bakery that smelled, according to Marta, of everything except bread. Old Kessel the cutter had left even earlier, complaining of his hip and of the government in equal measure. The workshop was hers alone, lit by one lamp turned low, the great cutting table between her and the door like a moat.

She set Ivo's letter down on the white wool of the ceremonial coat's unfinished sleeve, because she could not bear to let it touch bare wood. Then she took out the pattern book, turned past the collars, past the cuffs, to the pages at the back, and began.

* * *

The first thing she checked was the simplest thing, and the thing most forgers got wrong: the slant.

Elias wrote leaning forward. Not slightly — decisively, the way a tired man writes when he wants the sentence finished before his hand gives out. Every genuine sample in Hazel's archive leaned at what she estimated, holding her pencil against the page, to be somewhere between twelve and fifteen degrees off vertical. It was consistent across two years and forty-one specimens. A man's slant was his posture on paper; it did not change unless the man did.

Ivo's letter leaned eleven degrees. Perhaps ten and a half.

Hazel sat back and looked at it for a long moment, feeling something cold settle behind her ribs that was not quite fear and not quite relief. Eleven degrees was close. Close enough to fool anyone who hadn't spent two years measuring dead men's handwriting against their own insomnia.

But it was not fifteen.

She made herself keep going. One inconsistency proved nothing; ink dried unevenly, moods shifted, a man writing with a numb hand or on a ship's knee might lean differently. Elias had taught her that too, or rather he had taught her its opposite: *One variance is accident. Two is doubt. Three is design.*

She looked for the second.

It took her twenty minutes, and when she found it she laughed — a short, ugly sound in the empty workshop — because it was so small. The *y*. The escaping tails. In every genuine dispatch, Elias hooked his *y*'s left. All of them, without exception, including the ones written during the October crisis when, by Marta's account, the man had been running on coffee and fury and no sleep whatsoever. But in the phrase *the harbor will hold*, in Ivo's letter, the *y* in *harbor* curled right.

Right. Like any ordinary man's *y*. Like a *y* written by someone who had studied Elias's hand but whose own habit had betrayed him at the eleventh letter of a forty-word document.

*Two,* Hazel murmured to the lamp. *That's doubt.*

Her breath fogged faintly in the cold air of the shop. She should stop here. Two variances were enough to take to Ivo, enough to say *explain this*, enough to watch his face while she did it. That would be the sensible course. That had been her rule for two years: never act on suspicion you haven't confirmed three ways, because suspicion acted on prematurely was just panic wearing a uniform.

But her hands were already turning the page.

The third variance she found near midnight, and it was the one that undid her.

Elias dated everything. Dispatches, memoranda, the grocery lists he sometimes smuggled cipher practice into — everything carried a date in his peculiar compressed style: the month abbreviated in the old fashion, the day given first, the year last, all run together without spaces like a stitch count. *XII.14.XIX.* Hazel had seen it dozens of times. She had reproduced it dozens of times.

Ivo's letter was dated *XI.2.XIX.* The second of November.

The day before Elias Brandt drowned.

Hazel set the letter down very carefully, as though it might go off. She stood, walked to the window, looked out at the harbor — black water, the occupation's patrol boat riding at anchor with its insolent lights — and walked back. She picked the letter up again. She read the date again.

*XI.2.*

Now. Now she permitted herself to think it plainly, the thought she had been circling for three nights like a moth around a flame too honest to name:

If the letter was written on the second of November, then either Elias had written it the night before he died — which was possible, men did strange accounting at the end — or it had not been written by a drowning man at all. It had been written *before*. Months, perhaps, before. Written and then held back, banked like a fire, waiting for the moment someone needed a dead man's authority.

And there was only one reason to need a dead man's authority.

Because the living version of him couldn't give it.

* * *

She did not sleep. She considered it — lay down on the cot in the back room with her boots on, the way she'd learned to in the bad autumn — but her mind kept working the problem the way her fingers worked a stubborn seam, worrying it, turning it, unable to leave the loose thread alone.

Here was what troubled her most, and it was not the date. Here it was, laid out in the dark like pattern pieces on a table:

The letter had been *good*. That was the devil of it. Whoever had composed it knew things only Elias's circle could have known — knew about the treasury ledger, knew about her role in the network, knew the verification phrase that had made her blood go cold and obedient. This was not some occupation clerk's clumsy bait. This was a forgery built from the inside, by someone who had access to Elias's papers, Elias's methods, possibly Elias's own draft notes.

Which meant one of two things, and both of them were terrible in the specific, tailored way she had come to expect from her life.

Either Elias had written the letter himself, months ago, planning his own disappearance — in which case the plan had continued running after his death like a clock no one had remembered to stop, and someone else now held the works.

Or someone else had written it using what Elias left behind — in which case the plan was not continuing at all. It was being *steered*.

And steered toward what? Toward her, obviously. Toward the workshop. Toward the one seamstress in the city who could turn a cipher into a lining and a lining into a coat that would sit on the shoulders of a peace delegation while foreign ministers shook hands over the corpse of a republic.

Toward the ceremonial coat currently hanging three rooms away, half-finished, with its secret channel already basted into the left front panel.

Hazel stared at the ceiling. Somewhere below, the tide knocked softly against the pilings, patient as arithmetic.

*If it is a trap,* she thought, *then it is the strangest trap ever built. Because the trap requires me to do exactly what I would have done anyway.*

That was the thought that finally made her sit up.

A trap that asked her to sew false ciphers would make sense. A trap that lured her to a meeting with soldiers would make sense. But this letter asked her to do the one thing the resistance most wanted done: finish Elias's final transmission, expose the stolen treasury, get the proof out of the country stitched into ceremony where no examiner would think to look. If it was bait, it was bait made of the truth. And who set a trap baited with the truth?

Someone who wanted the truth delivered *in a particular form*, said the cold voice at the back of her mind. Someone who needed her hands specifically — her skill, her archive, her access — but did not care whether she believed.

Or someone who needed the mission completed precisely because Elias, alive or dead, could no longer complete it himself.

* * *

Dawn came gray and salt-smelling. Hazel had not moved from the cot, but she had, at some hour she could not name, stopped thinking in circles and started thinking in straight lines, which was her body's signal that a decision had been made somewhere below the level of her permission.

The decision was this: she would say nothing. Not yet.

Not to Ivo — because if Ivo was innocent, he was also, she now realized with uncomfortable clarity, nearly useless. He had delivered a letter he could not authenticate, described a plan he seemed to know only in outline, and flinched — she had seen it, that small hitch when she mentioned Elias's habits — at least once when the conversation touched the code-master directly. Innocent men flinched too. Guilty men flinched better.

And not to anyone else, because there was no one else. That was the whole architecture of her life: no one else. Two years of disciplined loneliness, and she had told herself it was strength, and tonight — this gray, salt-smelling dawn — she understood it was also a cage, and cages are convenient for the people who build them.

She rose, washed her face in the cold basin, and went out into the workshop to begin the day's work like an honest woman.

Marta was already there. Of course Marta was already there; Marta arrived with the gulls and left with the lamplighter, and had lately taken to watching Hazel with an expression that hovered between concern and accusation, like a woman deciding which of those to lead with.

"You didn't go home," Marta said. It wasn't a question. Marta's questions never were.

"I fell asleep here."

"On the cot. With your boots on." Marta nodded at Hazel's feet, where the evidence of a sleepless night was drying in salt-colored rings around the ankles. "You only sleep in your boots when you're frightened."

"I sleep in my boots when I'm efficient."

"Ah." Marta picked up her pressing cloth and smoothed it over the ironing board with unnecessary tenderness. "And the letter," she said, to the board rather than to Hazel. "The one the tall stranger brought. Did it tell you what you wanted?"

Hazel's hands, reaching for her thimble, went very still. She had told no one about the letter. She had told no one about Ivo. The workshop had been empty, the door barred —

"The tall stranger," Marta repeated, still addressing the ironing board, "who waited across the street for two hours on Tuesday night, smoking cigarettes the way other people pray. He's been there again since. Twice." She looked up then, and her face was doing something complicated — fear and loyalty wrestling openly, neither winning. "I notice things, Hazel. It's the whole of my talent. So before you decide what to tell me — and you will decide, because you've been deciding for three days and it shows — I'd ask you to remember that I noticed the officers' car outside on Thursday, too. Before the raid. An hour before. Parked where no car parks."

The workshop was very quiet. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead.

"Marta," Hazel said carefully, "what did you see on Thursday?"

"I saw that someone had told them to come." Marta folded the pressing cloth, corner to corner, precise as a confession. "And I have been wondering ever since who that someone was, and whether they are still telling."

She set the cloth down and walked to the window, where the morning light caught the gray in her hair and the exhaustion under her eyes, and stood looking out at the street as though the answer might be parked there again.

Hazel stood in the middle of the workshop with her thimble in her hand and the forged letter burning a hole in the pocket of her apron, and understood that her neat, terrible arithmetic — the archive, the angles, the eleven degrees of doubt — had just acquired a variable she had no measurements for at all.

Someone had told the officers to come.

And Marta knew.
