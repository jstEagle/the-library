## 12. The Informer's Coat

Marta Kalnina had a theory about buttons, and it was this: you could tell everything about a man by the way he treated his buttons. A man who kept his coat buttoned to the throat in all weather was hiding something. A man who let them hang loose was careless with himself and would be careless with you. A man who checked them constantly, thumbing each one in turn like a rosary, was afraid — and afraid men were the most dangerous kind, because fear made them predictable, and predictability made them useful.

The officer who came into the workshop on the second Tuesday of December wore his buttons like a rosary.

She noticed him before he spoke. She always noticed. Years of working in a room full of sharp-eyed women had taught Marta that the world divided itself into two sorts of people: those who looked at fabric and those who looked at people looking at fabric. He was the second sort. He stood just inside the door of Krastin's Tailor Workshop with snow melting on his shoulders and his cap held politely against his chest — polite being the worst possible sign — and he watched Hazel Crane's hands move over a sleeve seam as though he were memorizing them for later.

"Frau Kalnina?" he said, though no one had told him her name. That was how she knew.

"That depends," said Marta, "on who's asking."

He smiled the way winter smiles. "Lieutenant Dorn. Occupation Administration. I wonder if I might trouble you for five minutes."

"We're busy."

"I can see that." His gaze went past her, along the rows of worktables, to the window where the light came down gray and thin as cheap thread. "Perhaps not here, then. There's a café on the corner. The coffee is terrible, which is why nobody goes there. Privacy, you understand."

Marta folded her hands over her apron. Somewhere behind her, she heard Hazel's needle pause — a small thing, a half-second of silence in a room that never stopped moving — and then continue. Hazel missed nothing. That was the trouble with Hazel Crane: she missed nothing, and she trusted nothing, and Marta had spent three months now wondering which of those qualities had kept them both alive so far and which one would get them killed.

"I don't drink coffee with strangers," Marta said.

"Then we shall be quick friends first." Dorn set his cap on the nearest table, which was an intimacy, a small theft of space, and Marta hated him for it more than she could say. "Your brother, Jānis. He works the docks, doesn't he? Third shift. Strong fellow, they say. Good at carrying things."

The floor did not tilt. Marta had been raised on stories about moments when the floor tilts, and she had long ago concluded that such moments were invented by novelists, because in life the floor stays exactly where it is while everything else gives way beneath you.

"He carries crates," she said. "It isn't interesting work."

"No. But it is *indebted* work." Dorn picked up a spool of thread from the table, black, turned it once between finger and thumb. "He owes money to men who owe money to the Administration. Debts are like coats, Frau Kalnina. They pass from hand to hand, and eventually someone must wear them."

"You've come about a debt." Relief, briefly — debts she understood. Debts were honest. "You should have led with that. It would have saved us both the theater."

"It is not only about a debt." He put the spool down, precisely where it had been, aligned with its neighbors. "It is about what a woman in this workshop saw on the fourteenth of November, when Lieutenant Brandt's men searched these premises without finding certain items they were assured were here."

*Certain items.* Marta's mouth went dry. On the fourteenth of November there had been shouting and boots and a captain who smelled of tobacco turning over drawers while Hazel stood very still by the pressing bench with her hands folded, and Marta had stepped forward and said, quite calmly, that whatever they were looking for wasn't here because she'd have seen it, she saw everything in this workshop, it was her gift and her curse. And the captain had believed her, or pretended to, because a plain woman of forty-one with flour on her apron and thread burns on her fingers was invisible, and invisibility was the one inheritance her mother had left her that proved valuable.

She had done it without thinking. That was the horror of it, looking back. She had lied to armed men the way she'd hand someone scissors — automatically, kindly, without considering whose hand she was putting them into.

"I remember the search," Marta said. "I remember nothing else worth remembering."

"Of course." Dorn smiled again. "Well. The café is there if your memory improves. Debts, Frau Kalnina, are patient things. So am I." He retrieved his cap, settled it on his head, thumbed the top button of his coat — checking it, rosary-fashion — and walked out into the snow.

Marta stood a moment longer. Then she went back to her table, took up the trousers she was hemming, and discovered that her hands, which had never once betrayed her in twenty-six years of needlework, were shaking too badly to make a straight stitch.

* * *

Jānis came home at six in the morning smelling of tar and cold iron, as he always did, and found his sister sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of tea she had forgotten to drink. This was unusual enough that he stopped in the doorway with his boots half off and studied her the way he studied weather.

"Somebody's died," he said.

"Nobody's died."

"Somebody's going to, then. You've got the face."

"I haven't got any face. I've got a perfectly ordinary face."

"You've got the face you had when Papa sold the cow." He sat down across from her, heavy, careful, a big man made bigger by worry. At thirty-four he was still built like the farm boys they'd both been, shoulders like a doorframe, hands that could palm a whole loaf. Their father used to say Jānis had been issued to the family twice as large as necessary to compensate for something. Marta had spent her childhood assuming the something was her.

"There was a man at the workshop yesterday," Marta said.

Jānis went still in a way that answered questions she hadn't asked yet, and she understood — with a sensation like a stitch pulled too tight — that he had known this was coming. Perhaps not Dorn specifically. But the shape of it.

"What did he want?" Jānis asked, and his voice was too casual, the way a hem is too casual when it's hiding a patch.

"He wanted to talk about your debts."

"My debts are my own business."

"Your debts are apparently the Administration's business now. Which makes them mine." She turned the cup slowly on its saucer. "How much, Janis? Truly. Not the number you tell me. The number."

He looked at the table. When he finally spoke, the number was worse than she had feared and better than she had dreaded, which meant it was exactly the number of a drowning man — survivable alone, unsurvivable together, and ruinous either way if anyone chose to push your head under.

"The men I borrowed from," he said quietly, "sold the paper. Upward. Twice. Now it belongs to people who'd rather have favors than coin."

"What kind of favors?"

"Marta—"

"*What kind.*"

He rubbed his face with both hands. "Carrying things. Off certain ships, onto certain carts, past certain eyes. Nothing—" He stopped. Started again. "Nothing I'm ashamed of. Yet."

That *yet* sat between them on the table like a third cup.

"And this officer thinks," Marta said, "that I can help pay it off."

"He didn't say that to me. He wouldn't come near me; I'm watched already, lightly, the way you watch a horse you're thinking of buying." Jānis lifted his head. "What did he actually ask you?"

And there it was: the question she could not answer honestly, because answering honestly would mean telling Jānis about the fourteenth of November, about the captain and the lie, about the half-finished garment Marta had seen Hazel tuck inside an officer's finished coat with the smoothness of a card sharp — about the night hours, the locked drawer, the way Hazel sometimes pricked messages into paper patterns in little clusters of pin-holes that Marta had pretended not to see because pretending not to see things was, she was beginning to understand, the whole of her talent.

"He asked whether I remembered anything about a search in November," Marta said. "I told him I remembered nothing."

"Is that true?"

Marta looked at her brother — her great foolish loyal brother, who had taken out loans against his strong back because their mother's lungs had needed medicine and their father's pride had needed burying — and she lied to him for perhaps the fourth time in their lives.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded, satisfied, and went to bed, and Marta sat alone in the kitchen while the harbor fog came up the street and pressed against the windows like something wanting in.

* * *

Three days later, Dorn came back, and this time he brought the coat.

Not his own coat. A uniform coat, occupation-gray, good wool, well-cut — cut better than the occupation deserved, Marta thought, handling it, because whoever had made this pattern had loved tailoring more than the regime paying for it. Dorn laid it across the counter in the back room where Marta worked the fine repairs, away from the other women, and said:

"This needs altering before Thursday. Take it in at the waist. There's a man coming who'll be wearing it, and he wants to look his best."

"Alterations are Hazel's department for uniforms."

"Hazel Crane's department, yes." Dorn examined a row of hooks on the wall with the air of a man browsing a museum. "But this particular commission comes through me, and I'd rather it came through you. Consider it a kindness. Paid work, in a slow season."

"Paid by whom?"

"By the Administration. Who are, after all, your employers now." He let that sit. Then, mildly, terribly: "There will be others. Coats, alterations, small commissions. You have good eyes, Frau Kalnina. Everyone says so. You see everything in this workshop, I'm told. Your gift and your curse — isn't that how you think of it?"

Marta's blood went cold and then colder, because she had never said that aloud to anyone in her life. It was an interior sentence, a private joke, hers alone since girlhood. Which meant either that she was transparent as glass, or that someone had talked, or — likeliest and worst — that Dorn had simply guessed it, because guessing was what such men did, the way spiders guess at flies.

"I don't recall saying that," she managed.

"No. People rarely recall saying the truest things." He smoothed the coat's lapel with one finger. "Take it in at the waist. And Frau Kalnina — if you happen to notice anything while you work. Loose threads. Odd linings. Letters that arrive for colleagues after hours. Anything a woman with your gift might notice." He shrugged pleasantly. "Debts are patient things. So am I. But my superiors are neither, and your brother's creditors answer to them, and Thursday has a way of becoming Monday."

He left. Marta stood with her hands flat on the gray wool and felt the whole shape of the trap at last, felt it the way she felt a bad pattern — not by examining it piece by piece but by knowing, instantly, in the wrists, that it would never hang right on anyone.

They weren't asking her to betray Hazel. Not yet. They were asking her to wear the coat. To accept it, alter it, hold it against her body, let it become hers. The informing would come later, sewn into her gradually, stitch by stitch, until one day she'd look down and find the garment finished and herself inside it, and there would be no taking it apart without destroying the cloth entirely.

* * *

That evening she almost burned it. The coat lay across her kitchen chair where she'd carried it home in her work bag — she'd told herself it was safer at home than hanging in the workshop where Hazel might see it, which was true, and also a lie, because the true reason was that some part of her wanted it out of that building, away from Hazel's knowing hands, quarantined.

She stood over the stove with the firebox open and the coat over her arm and thought: one coat. One gray coat, and Jānis walks free, and the debts go quiet, and I go back to hems and buttonholes and pretending not to see pin-pricks in pattern paper. One coat against one person. Hazel, who trusts no one, who keeps her own counsel, who has secrets enough that losing one coat's worth of them shouldn't even mark her—

Except it would mark her. It would end her. Marta knew that now, knew it the way you know a thing you've known for months and refused to read. Whatever Hazel carried in her linings, whatever moved through that workshop at night in coded stitches — it was bigger than a coat, and Hazel was carrying it alone, and the reason Hazel trusted no one was that trust, in this city, in this winter, was a currency that bought you a rope.

And yet. And *yet.*

Because Marta had also seen Hazel teach the apprentice girl to roll a cuff properly, patiently, twice over, with none of her usual frost. Had seen her give half her bread to old Krastin's widow without a word, wrapped in brown paper as though it were an alteration. Had heard her, one evening, humming — badly, tunelessly, secretly — some folk song their grandmother might have sung, in a language the occupation had forbidden in schools, and had understood in that moment that Hazel Crane was not made of ice at all. She was made of the same thing Marta was made of. Fear, worn well-tailored.

You could tell everything about a person by the way they treated their buttons. Hazel buttoned herself to the throat every single day.

Marta closed the firebox. She hung the coat on the chair, took up needle and thread, and began to take in the waist — because refusing was impossible, and burning was impossible, and the only move left was the oldest one a woman of her trade possessed: do the work, do it well, and watch. Watch what Dorn did with the coat. Watch who wore it Thursday. Watch whether the coat was ever merely a coat.

Her needle moved. Outside, the fog thickened. In the workshop drawer, three streets away, Marta knew without knowing how she knew, there lay a half-sewn lining full of somebody's dangerous truth, and tomorrow Hazel would ask her — Marta saw it coming the way you see weather — Hazel would ask her something, or notice something, or trust her with something, and Marta would have to stand there wearing her new coat of silence and decide, in the space of a breath, which side of the seam she lived on.

She finished the alteration at midnight. It hung perfectly. She hated how well it hung.

In the morning she would carry it back to the workshop. In the morning, she would smile at Hazel across the worktable, and Hazel would look at her with those eyes that missed nothing, and Marta would discover whether a lie could survive being stitched next to the truth.

She dreamed, that night, of buttons. Thousands of them, rolling loose across a floor that stayed exactly where it was, while everything else gave way.
