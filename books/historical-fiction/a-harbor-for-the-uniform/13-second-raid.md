## 13. Second Raid

The trouble with hiding in a loft above a tailor's workshop, Hazel thought, was that you learned exactly how well you knew everyone's footsteps.

She had been up there since half past six, wedged between the bolts of surplus wool and the broken dress forms Marta called "the sisters," because they had no heads and terrible posture. She hadn't planned to be there. She had planned to be home by seven, eating bread and onions over her account book, sleeping the sleep of a woman whose conscience was heavy but her ledgers balanced. But the cipher lining was not finished — the treasury figures had to be re-sewn after Elias's corrections, and corrections took time, and time had become the one commodity in the republic that cost more than coal.

So: the loft. The lamp turned down to a coal of light. Her fingers moving through the dark by memory alone.

Below her, the workshop settled into its evening sounds. The stove ticking as it cooled. The great shears in their drawer, dreaming their metal dreams. Marta humming — off-key, always off-key, a habit so constant that when Marta stopped humming, you noticed it the way you noticed a clock stopping.

Marta stopped humming.

Hazel's needle paused mid-stitch. She listened. Boots on the cobbles outside — not one pair, not the shuffling evening drift of men going home to soup, but the crisp, coordinated percussion of many pairs walking *together*. Walking on purpose.

"Marta," Hazel breathed, though there was no chance of being heard, and every reason not to be heard at all.

The knock came anyway. Not a knock — a blow. The door shuddered on its hinges, and then it opened without anyone's permission, because permission had gone out of fashion in this city sometime around the occupation's second winter.

Hazel folded the cipher uniform into itself — once, twice, a third fold along the lines she'd practiced until the package was small enough to fit beneath a floorboard that lifted — and pressed herself flat among the wool bolts, heart hammering against the floorboards like a trapped bird.

* * *

There were six of them. She counted by sound. Six sets of boots, and one pair of heels that clicked differently — an officer's heels, polished and entitled.

"The workshop is closed," Marta said. Her voice was steady. Hazel marveled at it even as terror sealed her own throat. Steady, and only slightly too loud, the way a person is loud when they are holding a door shut from the inside with nothing but their voice.

"It was closed yesterday also," said the officer, "when we came. Yet here you are again, Frau — forgive me, I've forgotten."

"Kessler," said Marta. "Marta Kessler. And yes, here I am again. Someone must sweep the floors or the dust unions will file a complaint."

One of the soldiers laughed — a single bark, quickly strangled. Hazel would have smiled if her face remembered how. That was Marta all over: meet the wolf at the door with a joke about housekeeping, because a wolf that has to think about dust is a wolf that isn't looking under things.

"We are looking for documents," the officer said. "Papers. Correspondence. And we are looking for Hazel Crane."

The name landed in the room like a dropped spool of thread, rolling into corners.

"Crane," Marta repeated. "The Englishwoman? She went home hours ago. They keep early hours, the English. Something to do with the weather, I expect."

"Does she live far?"

"Far enough that you'd want a carriage. And supper first. You can't interrogate a seamstress hungry; she'll faint, and then where are you?"

"Frau Kessler." The officer's voice cooled several degrees. "You are amusing. I have noted it in my report. Now step aside."

Hazel heard drawers opened. Not searched — *opened*, with the violence of men who believed everything belonged to them eventually. The pattern drawers. The button cabinet. The long shelf of thread, each spool set spinning and left to wobble. A soldier found the tin of pins and upended it, and the sound of hundreds of pins scattering across the floorboards made Hazel flinch as though they'd been scattered across her skin.

"They're looking for papers," she told herself silently. "Papers burn. Papers can be denied. The uniform is under the floorboard. The uniform is safe. I am safe. Breathe."

She breathed. It did not help much, but it was better than the alternative.

* * *

They found her mending basket first.

It sat by the window where it always sat, and inside it — because Hazel had learned to leave decoys the way other people left tips — lay a week's worth of perfectly innocent work: a captain's trousers awaiting hemming, a civilian coat with a torn pocket, and a letter from her sister in Riga full of complaints about the price of eggs.

A soldier read the letter aloud, stumbling over the handwriting, and his comrades chuckled at the egg prices as though egg prices were funny, which perhaps they were, in a year when bread had begun to require references.

"This Crane," the officer said. "What is she to you?"

"A colleague," Marta said. "A quiet one. She sews, she goes home, she complains about my humming. Which is rude, but honest."

"She works late?"

"When there's work. There's always work. Half the officers in this city need their uniforms let out before Christmas dinner and taken in again by New Year's." A pause, and then Marta added, with what sounded like genuine professional grievance, "Gentlemen expand over the holidays. It's the puddings."

The strangled laugh again. Hazel closed her eyes. *Don't make them like you, Marta,* she thought wildly. *Or do. God help me, maybe do.* Because an officer who was laughing was an officer who wasn't thinking, and thinking was the enemy now.

But the officer did not laugh. "Search the loft," he said.

* * *

The ladder creaked under the first boot.

Hazel had perhaps four seconds. Four seconds in which the whole architecture of her double life compressed itself into a single question: *where?*

The floorboard with its hidden cavity was behind her — unreachable, and besides, they would find a loose board eventually; they were trained for loose boards. The wool bolts stood in ranks around her like patient sheep. The sisters — the headless dress forms — slumped in their corner with their canvas bodies and their wooden stands.

Canvas. She thought of canvas the way a drowning woman thinks of a plank.

She moved without deciding to move, silent on stocking feet, and dragged the nearest sister toward her, tipping it onto its side among the wool. With two pulls she opened the lacing at its back — the same lacing she'd sewn herself, months ago, when she'd first claimed the loft as her night workshop — and fed the folded cipher uniform into the hollow between the canvas shell and its inner frame, deep in the chest cavity where a heart would go, if dress forms had hearts. Then she laced it shut, arranged the arms, propped the sister upright against the wall with her terrible posture, and rolled herself into the gap between the wool bolts just as the soldier's head rose through the hatch.

He held a lantern. Its light swept across the rafters, across the shelves, across the ranks of wool, across the row of headless women standing in the corner like a jury that had already decided.

He looked at them for a long moment. Hazel watched him through a finger-width gap between two bolts, and she saw the exact moment the soldier's mind performed the arithmetic of a tired man near the end of a shift: *headless dummies, no heads, nothing to hide in a thing with no head.*

He kicked one anyway. The sister rocked, swayed, and righted herself with the stoicism of her kind. Inside her canvas chest, three inches from the soldier's boot, the future of a republic kept very still.

"Nothing up here," the soldier called down. "Wool and old ladies."

"Old ladies?" said the officer.

"Dress forms, sir. Headless ones."

"Then come down. We're done with the loft."

*Done.* Hazel let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since October.

But below, the officer had not finished. She heard his heels cross the floor — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a man who enjoyed being the last word in any room — and stop.

"One more matter, Frau Kessler. Your colleague, Miss Crane. The Englishwoman who keeps early hours and complains about your humming. Where does she live?"

"I couldn't say precisely. Somewhere past the canal. They all look alike, those streets."

"You could say precisely. Everyone can say precisely. Precision is simply honesty wearing its good coat." Paper rustled. "We have a witness who saw a woman leaving this workshop last Tuesday at eleven o'clock at night. Through the back door. Carrying a parcel."

"What was in the parcel?" Marta asked, and her voice was still steady, still almost light, but Hazel heard — she was quite sure she heard — the hairline crack running through it. Because Marta had seen that woman. Marta had held the back door open for her, and whispered *go, go*, and laughed afterward about how Hazel snuck about like a cat burglar with a pincushion.

"That is what we intend to find out. Along with where she lives, who she sees, and why the code-master Brandt — may he rest wherever drowned men rest — sent her letters before his unfortunate swim."

The silence below lasted three heartbeats. In those three heartbeats Hazel understood, with the cold clarity of a needle finding its mark, that this raid was not a search. It was an arrest that had missed. Someone had talked. Someone had given them Hazel's name and Marta's door, and the only reason she was breathing wool-dust in a loft instead of sitting in a bare room under that polite, cooling voice was a headless dress form and four seconds' luck.

"Marta Kessler," the officer said, "you will come with us. To answer questions. About your colleague."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will come with us anyway, and we shall describe the journey as voluntary in the paperwork. We are considerate that way."

"You're taking me in for *sweeping*?"

"We're taking you in for humming, Frau Kessler. It carries."

* * *

They took her through the front door. Hazel heard it all: the bell above the door — Marta had always meant to oil that bell — the boots on the cobbles arranging themselves, the officer saying something clipped and administrative, and then, just before the street swallowed them, Marta's voice, pitched upward as though to the loft itself, as though to Hazel directly:

"Mind the sisters! They catch cold!"

And then the door swung, the bell jingled its idiot silver greeting, and the workshop was empty.

Hazel did not move. Not for one minute, nor five, nor ten. She lay among the wool bolts with her cheek against the rough serge and listened to the building breathe without Marta in it, and the silence was a different silence than it had been an hour ago. Rooms, she discovered, could grieve.

When she finally descended the ladder, her legs shook so badly she sat down hard on the bottom rung and laughed — one thin, horrible note that echoed off the rafters and made her clamp her own hand over her mouth. Laughing. In an emptied workshop. With pins still scattered across the floor like frost.

She got up. She picked up the pins. All of them, on her knees in the lamplight, because the alternative was thinking, and thinking could wait until every pin was home in its tin.

It was while she knelt there that she saw it, caught in the gap beneath the officer's chair: a slip of paper, official cream, dropped in the haste of authority. She should not have touched it. Every rule she lived by — Elias's rules, the code-master's commandments, *trust no one, take nothing, leave no trace* — said leave it.

She read it.

It was a list. Three names, typed, with the third struck through and rewritten. The first name meant nothing to her. The second was a chandler on the quay she'd passed a hundred times. The third — the rewritten third, in fresh ink, circled twice —

was Marta Kessler.

Not as a suspect. As an *asset*. Beside the name, in the same hand, a single annotation in German: *brother's debts — cooperative since November.*

November. Before the letter. Before Ivo. Before any of it.

Hazel sat back on her heels amid the scattered pins, and the cold that moved through her had nothing to do with the season. Marta had been theirs since November. Every joke about dust and puddings, every covered absence, every *go, go* at the back door — performed, or worse than performed, *reported*. The warmth Hazel had allowed herself, the one break in her rules, the fragile human thing she had risked everything to trust—

Had it all been a ledger entry?

She looked up at the sisters standing in their corner, and at the one who carried the republic in her chest, and she understood what the morning would ask of her: the uniform still had to be finished, the cipher still had to reach Voss, and the only person who might help her now was the person most likely to destroy her.

Outside, snow began to fall on the city, soft as lint, covering every footprint equally — the guilty ones and the frightened ones alike.
