## 14. The Man Who Drowned

The watcher's name was Aldis Reiman, and Ivo knew him the way you know a scar — not because you look at it often, but because it aches when the weather turns.

They had shared a cellar in '16, two boys pressed into the resistance too young to grow beards properly, splitting a loaf that was more sawdust than flour and arguing about whether the revolution would come by spring or by summer. Aldis had voted for summer every year since. He had the optimism of a man who never intended to be present for the outcome.

Now he stood at the mouth of Fish Lane in a coat too good for his pocketbook, pretending to read a notice about herring quotas, and Ivo felt the ache of the old scar turn sharp.

*Follow him,* Elias's letter had said — no. Not Elias's letter. Hazel's discovery had rearranged everything: the letter was old, written before October, before the drowning. Someone was executing a dead man's plan. And the only living man who could be executing it was either Elias himself or the person holding Elias's leash.

Ivo pulled his cap lower and let Aldis walk.

It was the kind of morning that made the harbor honest: grey rain fine as needle-points, gulls screaming their grievances, fishwives cursing cheerfully at nobody in particular. Ivo had spent three days learning Aldis's rhythm. Mornings he took coffee at the Krambude where the occupation's junior officers gathered to complain about their postings. Afternoons he drifted toward the customs house. And twice now — twice was a pattern, three times would be an appointment — he had turned down Rope Walk toward the charitable hospice of St. Ansgar, where the harbor's broken men went to cough themselves into the next world on charity beds.

Aldis did not visit St. Ansgar. Men like Aldis did not visit hospices out of charity; charity was for people who still believed in something. But he paused at its gate each time, spoke briefly to someone inside, and left.

Today, Ivo meant to know who.

He gave Aldis half a street and followed through the fish market, past a stall where a woman was gutting cod with the serene violence of a concert pianist, and turned down Rope Walk as the rain thickened.

St. Ansgar's had been a warehouse once, then a chapel, then whatever the city needed it to be, which lately was a place to store people the way you store rope — coiled, damp, forgotten until needed. The gate was iron gone to lacework rust. A porter sat inside wrapped in three coats, reading a newspaper dated November.

"One visitor," Ivo said, and put a coin on the ledger. "For my uncle."

"Name?"

"Does your ledger have room for all of them?" Ivo asked gently. The porter looked up, weighed the coin against the effort of curiosity, and waved him through.

* * *

The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and the particular sweetness of lungs giving notice. Rows of iron beds under a ceiling of black beams. Rain came through a skylight patched with sailcloth, so the light fell soft and grey as fog itself.

Ivo walked the row slowly, nodding at men who nodded back, some asleep sitting up like horses, one reciting shipping manifests to himself in the contented voice of a man whose mind had finally found work suited to it.

And in the corner bed, by the leaking light, a man sat propped against pillows with a slate on his knees, writing.

Ivo stopped.

He had imagined this meeting a hundred times in the last weeks, and in none of those imaginings did Elias Brandt look like this. In his imagination Elias had been dead, which at least required no imagination at all. Or he had been alive and well and cunning somewhere south, laughing at them all from behind some new name. He had not been this: a thin man in a charity nightshirt, hair gone white at thirty-four, hands so changed by what they'd endured that Ivo only knew them by the way they moved — that quick, economical grace, each letter set down like a stitch that must hold.

Elias looked up.

For a moment neither of them breathed. Then Elias did something extraordinary. He smiled — small, crooked, entirely himself — and said, "You're late. You were always late. I told them you'd come by Thursday."

"Thursday," Ivo repeated. His voice came out wrong, scraped.

"It's Thursday somewhere." Elias set down the chalk. "Sit down before you fall down. You've gone the color of the wallpaper, and the wallpaper drowned years ago."

Ivo sat on the edge of the bed because his legs made the decision without consulting him. Up close the damage was worse. There was a stiffness to Elias's right side, the arm resting in his lap like cargo. His wrists bore marks healed badly, skin grown over rope-burn like bark over wire. And his eyes — his eyes were the same, and that was somehow the cruelest part, the same quick grey eyes that had once read Ivo's first cipher attempt in four seconds and said, kindly, "You've invented a code that only you can break, which makes it not a code but a diary."

"They told me you drowned," Ivo said. "In October. The pilot boat. They fished a body out at Haken—"

"They fished a body out, yes." Elias said it lightly, the way you'd describe weather. "Not mine. It was convenient for everyone that it resembled me. The occupation got to close a file. The network got to mourn cleanly. And I—" He paused, and the lightness failed him, just for a breath. "I got to stay."

"Stay." Ivo heard his own voice harden and could not stop it. Fury arrived first and pity scrambled after it, both of them shouting. "Elias. Look at yourself. What is this? Why are you here? Why are you *alive*, if this is alive?"

"Because I'm useful," Elias said simply. "Dead men can't design ciphers, and I'm very good at designing ciphers. It turns out that's the whole of my value to the occupation: my hands." He raised the ruined right hand slightly, an ironic little salute. "They keep me fed, dosed, and writing. When I write well, they let me see daylight. When I write poorly, they remind me that there are other people whose names I know, and that hospices are flammable places."

Ivo's fury, which had been aimed outward, swung around and buried itself in his own chest. *Other people.* Hazel. Marta, whoever she was. The delegation. Himself.

"The letter," Ivo said. "To Hazel Crane. Your handwriting. She verified it against her archive — nearly. It was old, Elias. Written before October. Which means you wrote it knowing—" He stopped, recalculated, and felt the floor tilt. "You wrote it *before* you were taken. You planned your own drowning."

"I planned a contingency," Elias corrected. "There's a difference. A drowning is a contingency that got promoted." He glanced toward the door, then continued, faster, quieter. "Listen to me. We have minutes, maybe less. Aldis comes at four and counts my words on the slate. Do you understand? They don't leave me alone with my own thoughts if they can help it. Everything I produce goes through him."

"Aldis." Ivo tasted the name like bad water. "He was ours. He argued about the revolution coming by summer."

"He argues about it still, I expect. For the other side." Elias's mouth twisted. "They bought him cheaply, Ivo. That's the humiliation of it. Not even expensive betrayal. A debt forgiven and a warm posting. He sold the cell in '17, didn't you ever wonder why Riga went quiet? No. Don't answer. It doesn't matter now. What matters is what he's carrying."

"The trap," Ivo said. "Hazel figured it out. The letter sets her a task — a final cipher sewn into a ceremonial uniform, proof about the treasury. She's been working it for weeks. If the whole thing is bait—"

"It's bait and it isn't." Elias held up the good hand. "That's the elegant cruelty of it, and I need you to follow every step, because we may not speak again. Yes, they used my plan. Yes, the uniform, the delegation, the cipher — all of it was designed to draw out whoever could finish my work. The occupation doesn't want the treasury scandal exposed any more than a thief wants his ledger published. They want the last of the network standing in one room holding the evidence, so they can arrest the evidence along with the hands."

"Then Hazel walks away. We burn the lining and go."

"No." Elias's voice cracked like a whip, then softened immediately, apologetic. "No. Because here is the thing I have done, lying in this bed for months with nothing but chalk and time and hatred to keep me company. I have been *writing*. They think they're getting a trap. And a trap it is — but I am a better craftsman than they will ever be, and I have built a second door into it. The cipher Hazel is sewing has layers. The outer layer proves the theft — the amounts, the dates, the accounts in Copenhagen and Hamburg where our treasury was quietly re-homed under occupation officers' cousins' names. That layer is real. I insisted it be real. A false proof would never survive scrutiny, and they needed it plausible enough to lure professionals."

"And underneath?"

Elias was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the sailcloth overhead. Somewhere down the ward the manifest-reciter reached the letter K and lingered there lovingly.

"Underneath," Elias said at last, "there is a list. Names. Collaborators — the occupation's paid friends inside what remains of our institutions. I was made to compile it. They checked my work; they're not fools. Every name is genuine. Every name would burn the right people." His eyes closed briefly. "But they also added names of their own. False ones. Good names. Names that would destroy what's left of the resistance if that list reaches sympathetic presses abroad — ministers, organizers, people in exile who would be discredited at exactly the moment the truth about the treasury needs believers. The scandal and the slander arrive together, and the slander poisons the scandal. Nobody prints a treasury exposé alongside a list that ruins the men calling for investigation. The occupation understood that better than I did, when they dictated the additions. I was too broken to refuse. I want that recorded, Ivo, because I intend to live long enough to be ashamed of it properly."

"You were a prisoner."

"I was a craftsman given bad materials," Elias said. "Which is worse, because craftsmen find ways." He opened his eyes, and there — finally — was the old fire, banked but unmistakable, the man who had taught a cellarful of teenagers that a cipher was just a promise written sideways. "So here is the message you carry back to Hazel Crane, and mark every word, because Aldis is counting mine: *finish the cipher, and cut the false thread.* The true list stands. The additions do not. She'll know how to tell them apart — the false names are stitched in a pattern that breaks the rhythm. I built the flaw in deliberately. A craftsman's signature. Tell her to read the seams like music, and listen for the bar where the beat stumbles."

"How will she know which stumble?"

"Because I taught her," Elias said, "the same as I taught you. The third rule. Say it."

"*Never let the lie sit comfortably in the pattern,*" Ivo recited. "*A forgery rests where truth would reach.*"

"There. She'll find them. It will cost her hours she doesn't have, but she'll find them." Elias's voice dropped further. "And Ivo — the extraction. Whatever you're planning for me, stop planning it."

"I haven't—"

"You have. You have your mother's face when you're scheming, and your mother schemed the way rivers flood." Elias almost smiled. "Don't. My death serves them while I'm useful and stops serving them the moment I'm not, and I am very close to not being useful anymore. The lists are compiled. Once the uniform leaves this port, I am a loose end with a memory. They'll tidy me. So don't spend your courage on a dying man in a hospice — spend it on the girl with the needle, and on getting that coat across the border. That is the whole war now. One garment."

"Elias—"

"At the end of Rope Walk there's a boy selling chestnuts," Elias said, louder now, in the flat tone of a man performing for a listener Ivo couldn't see. "Terrible chestnuts. I've told him twice. Terrible." And then, low and fast, barely moving his lips: "Four o'clock. Aldis. Go out through the chapel yard, not the gate. And Ivo — tell Hazel—" He faltered, and for a moment the code-master, the ghost, the drowned man, was just a sick man in a charity bed trying to say a simple thing. "Tell her the work was never punishment. Tell her I said that. She'll understand. She was the best student I never taught."

Down the corridor, boots. Unhurried, official, wet.

Ivo stood. His legs held, barely. He wanted to say a hundred things — about the cellar, the bread, the summers that never came — and had time for none of them. Elias picked up his chalk, bent over his slate, and became again exactly what the occupation believed him to be: a docile instrument, writing.

At the ward door Ivo looked back once. Elias didn't look up. But his chalk paused, mid-letter, one beat — the rest where the truth would reach — and then moved on.

* * *

The chapel yard was a graveyard of boat parts and one actual grave, belonging to a sexton who had evidently preferred his workplace to his home. Ivo crossed it quickly, heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't permitted it in the ward, and slipped out onto Rope Walk as the rain thickened into something serious.

Chestnut smoke drifted from the corner. The boy there caught his eye, and Ivo bought a paper twist of chestnuts he didn't want, because tradecraft was mostly superstition and he needed the ritual.

They were terrible, as advertised.

He ate them anyway, walking fast toward the tailor shop, turning the message over and over like a coin tested between fingers. *Finish the cipher. Cut the false thread. Read the seams like music.* Somewhere behind him, in a room that smelled of carbolic, a man the whole city believed dead was chalking letters for his jailers, hiding a confession in plain sight the way Hazel hid dispatches in linings — and Ivo understood suddenly that he had never once in his life met anyone braver than these quiet people who put truth where power couldn't read it.

Then he thought of Hazel's face when he told her the mission she'd bled for was also the trap she'd feared — and that the fix required unpicking weeks of work and resewing it in days, under the nose of an occupation examiner, with a returned-and-unproven Marta watching from the next table.

He walked faster.

By the time he reached the workshop's door, the rain had become a proper winter assault, the harbor had gone the color of dishwater, and somewhere across the roofs the occupation's clock tower was striking five — which meant Hazel had been alone with the uniform and her suspicions for two hours already.

He pushed open the door, shaking off the rain, and found her standing over the half-dressed mannequin with a seam-ripper in her hand and an expression he would remember for the rest of his life.

"Hazel," he began. "We have to talk about the stitching."

"Don't," she said, "tell me how to do my job. Sit down, Ivo Markun. You look like a man who's seen a ghost."

"I have," Ivo said, hanging up his wet coat. "He sends his regards."
