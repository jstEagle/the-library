## 15. What Elias Asked

The hospice smelled of carbolic and cabbage, which was fitting, Elias thought, since he had spent the better part of four months smelling of both. The bed was narrow as a coffin and nearly as comfortable, and the window — when the frost permitted — offered a view of the harbor cranes, those great patient birds that lifted cargo by day and, in his former life, secrets by night.

He had watched them for so long now that he had begun to assign them personalities. The one nearest the customs house was a pessimist; it never fully extended its arm, as though expecting to be asked to lift something heavier than it could bear. He understood the feeling entirely.

"You're doing the cranes again," Ivo said from the chair beside the bed.

"I am observing the harbor," Elias corrected. "There is a difference, though I admit it is subtle."

"You've been observing that one for twenty minutes."

"He's thinking about something. Look at him. Brooding."

Ivo did not look at him. He looked instead at Elias's hands, which lay on the blanket like two pale fish that had given up swimming. Once, those hands had written ciphers that crossed three borders inside coat linings without ever once being caught. Now they trembled if they tried to hold a spoon, and the occupation's doctor came every Tuesday with his little black bag and his little black smile and took Elias's pulse as though checking on an investment.

Four months since October. Four months since they had fished him out of the channel — not half-drowned at all, but drugged and dumped, because a drowned code-master was useful and a live one was more useful still. They had wanted him broken. They had mostly succeeded. But mostly, Elias had learned, was a word with a great deal of room left in it.

"Ivo," he said. "Tell me about her again. The seamstress."

"Hazel Crane." Ivo shifted in the chair; the wood complained. "Careful. Methodical. Keeps records you wouldn't believe — she has an archive of your dispatches going back years, filed like a librarian. She didn't trust me for three days."

"Only three? She's getting soft."

"She held a needle to my throat."

"Ah." Elias smiled at the ceiling. "That's my girl. Well. Not *my* girl. I mean that professionally."

"I know what you mean."

"Do you? Because you had that tone. Marta used to say you had tones."

"Marta," Ivo said carefully, "is currently being questioned by Captain Reiner's men, and we don't know—"

"We know enough." Elias closed his eyes. The ceiling had a water stain shaped like Estonia, or possibly like nothing at all; grief made cartographers of us all. "Ivo. Bring me the paper. It's time."

* * *

The paper was cheap, gray, the kind sold to hospices in bulk, and the pencil was a stub someone had sharpened with a knife. Elias had asked for these things days ago and Ivo had brought them without asking why, which was one of the reasons Elias had trusted him back when trusting people had still been a habit rather than a diagnosis.

He could not write. His hand refused. So he would compose, and Ivo would write, and then Elias would read it back letter by letter until it was exactly right, because a message to Hazel Crane that was anything less than exact was worse than no message at all.

"Begin," Elias said. Then he was silent for a long moment, because beginnings are difficult even for professionals, particularly this kind of beginning.

Ivo waited. He had always been good at waiting. It was his single most irritating virtue.

"'To the seamstress,'" Elias dictated finally. "'If you are reading this, then Ivo found you and you believed him, which means either that you verified the letter or that you have grown sentimental, and I have read your file notes, madam, and sentimentality was never among your faults.'"

"Her file notes?"

"I kept files on everyone in the network. Yes, including you. Yours runs to eleven pages and contains the phrase 'reliable but talks too much when nervous.'"

"That's slander."

"It's observation. Continue: 'You will have decoded the first section by now. You will also have found the second layer — the list. I am going to tell you about the list, and I ask you to hear all of it before you judge me, though I confess I have no right to ask anything of you, and less right than most.'"

Elias stopped. Outside, the pessimist crane swung its load of timber toward the customs shed, slow as a funeral procession.

"They beat it out of me in November," he said, not dictating now, just saying it, because some things needed to be said aloud once before they could be put into a cipher. "The list of names. They had my sister's address in Riga, Ivo. They had photographs of her children coming out of school. And Reiner sat there with his tea and explained, very reasonably, that I would provide names or he would provide consequences, and that either way the list would exist, because they already had a list — they only needed mine to make it credible. To make it *mine*."

Ivo said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either pity or rage, and Elias had had his fill of both.

"So I gave them names," Elias went on. "Real ones, at first — men already dead, cells already burned, names that cost nothing. I thought I was being clever. Cleverness, Hazel, if you're hearing this, is the sin I will answer for longest. Because then they showed me the rest of the plan. The treasury evidence, the ceremonial uniform, the delegation — all of it true, all of it real, everything I had built before they pulled me from the water, and threaded through it, my own second layer, my own list, expanded. New names. Living ones. Names they chose and made me encode in my own system so that any loyalist who decoded the transmission would find proof of treachery where there was none. They don't want to catch the network, Ivo. They want the network to destroy itself reading its own mail."

"And you designed the trap," Ivo said quietly. Not accusatory. Worse than accusatory — understanding.

"I designed the lock," Elias said, "and they brought their own key. Write that down. No — don't. That's just for us."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the light went thin and blue against the frosted glass. The hospice matron brought tea once and pretended not to see the paper, which was her way of participating in the resistance: she had been pretending not to see things since the occupation began, and Elias suspected her pretenses were load-bearing.

"The alteration," Elias dictated. "Here is what must be done, and here, madam, I rely on everything you ever memorized about my methods. The collaborator list is encoded in the third register — you'll have found it by its cadence, the double-shifts I use for proper names. Every name on that list after the seventh entry is false. I was made to insert them. The first seven are dead men and cost no one anything; leave them if you wish, or strike them too, but the others — Voss is clean. The woman Ansa Kolk at the wireless relay is clean. The harbormaster Lindqvist, whom your file probably marks as 'drunk but honest,' is clean. They are on my list because the occupation wants them hanging from it."

His voice was going. It always went by late afternoon now, wearing down like a pencil stub, and he had to stop and let the cough have its way with him while Ivo held the cup to his lips and the tea scalded going down and he was grateful for the pain, oddly, because pain meant the lungs still argued.

"When you can talk again," Ivo said, "there's something you should know. About how they're watching her."

"After."

"Elias—"

"*After.* A dying man gets to order his affairs, Ivo. It's in the regulations. I wrote the regulations."

Ivo almost smiled. It was a poor, crooked thing, but Elias counted it a victory and banked it.

* * *

Dusk came down over the harbor like a lid. Somewhere below, the matron was arguing with a delivery man about turnips, and the argument had the comfortable, endless quality of arguments conducted by people who had survived worse things than turnips. Elias found it steadying. Civilization, he had come to believe, was not parliaments or treaties; it was a woman defending her turnip prices in a occupied city in December, on principle, out of pure stubbornness.

"Resume," he said.

Ivo dipped the pencil stub — there was barely a thumb's width left of it — and waited.

"'Understand what you are doing,'" Elias dictated. "'If you alter the list and transmit, the occupation will know within days that their trap failed, and they will know who failed it. Every name I have cleared becomes a name they hunt twice as hard. This is not a rescue, madam. It is a correction, published at your expense. I have spent four months telling myself that the plan should die with me, that asking anyone to finish it is only recruiting another casualty. But the treasury evidence is true. The theft is real, the sums are real, and the men who signed the orders sit tonight in warm rooms giving speeches about peace. If the truth dies in that workshop with everything else, then they will have won the only war that matters — the one over what actually happened.'"

He paused. The pencil scratched nothing. Frost feathered up the window in ferns, and beyond it the crane — the pessimist, his pessimist — stood motionless against the last gray light.

"'So I ask,' he continued, 'not as your code-master, because I forfeited that office the moment I gave Reiner a single true name. I ask as a tailor asks another craftsman. I have left a flaw in the garment. You are the finest hand I ever trained, and I am asking you to take it apart and mend it, knowing the mending may be seen, knowing the seeing may cost you. That is all. No oaths. No network. Just work, and the worker.'"

Elias closed his eyes. "Last paragraph."

Ivo waited.

"'One more thing, and then I will stop taking up your evening, which I know you spend stitching, because everyone in this business stitches after dark, it's in our blood like malaria. When this is done — whatever happens, whoever pays — remember that the cipher was never mine. Codes outlive their makers. That was always the point of you, Hazel. Not the messages. The hands.'" He opened his eyes. "Sign it."

"How?"

"Not my name." Elias looked at the ceiling, at the water stain, at the country that might have been there. "Sign it the way I signed the very first dispatch she carried. She'll know. She memorized everything."

Ivo hesitated. "The needle-and-thread mark. The little running stitch under the signature."

"She'll know," Elias said again. Then, because the room had gone too solemn and solemnity was a luxury the dying couldn't afford: "And add a postscript. Tell her the cranes in this harbor are cowards except the one by the customs house, who has a philosopher's temperament, and that if the plan requires a diversion near the quay she could do worse than trust a bird with that much patience."

"You're putting a joke in a cipher transmission."

"I'm putting a *key phrase* in a cipher transmission. If she laughs when she reads it, we'll know the message arrived intact." He settled back against the pillows, exhausted in the way that was now simply the weather of his body. "Humor is authentication, Ivo. Any fool can verify a signature. Only the right person verifies a joke."

* * *

Night. The matron's lamp passed his door on its rounds; the frost thickened; somewhere across the water the occupation's searchlights swept the harbor on their schedule, punctual as conscience and half as welcome.

Ivo read the finished letter back to him twice, and Elias corrected three words — a *shall* to a *must*, because Hazel Crane would parse the difference the way a priest parses scripture — and then folded it into the lining of his own coat, the old coat, the one with the false seam under the left arm that no hospice orderly had ever thought to check, because who searches a dying man's coat?

"Tomorrow," Elias said. "She'll need to start tomorrow. The delegation sails when?"

"New Year's Eve. Six days."

"Six days." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough turned into silence, and when he could speak again he said: "Six days to unpick a nation's lies and resew them. She'll do it. She'll complain about my notation the entire time — she always thought my register shifts were inelegant, did I ever tell you? Wrote it in the margin of a dispatch once. In pencil. *Slovenly.* I framed that dispatch in my heart, Ivo. First honest criticism I'd received in a decade."

"Is there anything else?" Ivo stood, buttoning his coat, becoming again the stranger who could walk through checkpoints.

"Yes." Elias reached out, and his hand shook, and he let it shake, because there was no one left to perform steadiness for. "When you see her. Don't tell her I'm pitiful. Tell her I'm angry. Anger travels better than pity, and it's truer. I am *furious*, Ivo. At them, at myself, at the whole rotten arithmetic that trades children in Riga for names on a list. Let her sew with my fury in the seams. Fury holds a stitch beautifully."

Ivo paused at the door. "And if Reiner's men follow you out of here?"

"Then you'll lose the letter and gain a story," Elias said. "Go. The tide won't argue with you forever."

When the door closed, he lay listening to the harbor — the cranes settling, the water worrying the pilings, the far-off gramophone in the ward playing something waltz-shaped and mournful — and he thought about hands. Hazel's hands, quick and exact. Ivo's hands, writing his words in a pencil stub's worth of graphite. The matron's hands, counting turnips. All of them stitching, in their ways, at the same garment — this shabby, stubborn century — and he thought: let the thread hold. Let it hold just long enough.

Outside, the pessimist crane stood vigil over the dark water, patient as a promise, waiting for morning.
