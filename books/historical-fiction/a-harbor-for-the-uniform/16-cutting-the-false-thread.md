## 16. Cutting the False Thread

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of my thumb when I finally understood what Elias was asking me to do.

Not sew a message. Unpick one.

I sat at my worktable in the dead hours before dawn with the ceremonial coat spread open like a patient on an operating table, its lining peeled back to show the cipher I had spent eleven nights building stitch by stitch. Every knot and crossing of thread meant something. Every pin-prick in the canvas beneath it meant something else. I had learned Elias's system over three years of quiet evenings, memorizing his rules the way other women memorized psalms: *the anchor is truth, the float is mercy, the backstitch is blood.* And now his own message, carried to me by a dying man's borrowed voice, told me that buried inside the list of names — the collaborator list that could hang half the city if it reached the wrong hands — there were threads that lied.

False names. Planted by the occupation, dictated through Elias's broken ribs, designed to make the resistance devour itself the moment the cipher was decoded abroad.

I picked up my unpicker — the little curved blade we called *the confessor*, because everything confessed under it eventually — and I looked at the first false name stitched into the lining of a coat destined for Minister Voss's shoulders, and I felt something I hadn't felt since the occupation began.

Rage. Clean, cold, useful rage.

"Right," I said to the empty workshop, because Marta wasn't there to hear me and Ivo was across town arranging miracles, and a woman alone at four in the morning is permitted to talk to her needle. "Let's see what you've made me undo."

* * *

The mathematics of it were brutal.

The delegation departed on the morning ferry in six days — five now, by the time the sky went grey at the window. The coat would pass through at least two inspections before it reached Voss's cabin. And the alteration could not look like an alteration. An occupation examiner who found fresh stitching in a lining wouldn't need to decode anything; he'd simply arrest everyone whose fingers might have touched it, starting with the seamstress and working outward until he ran out of people.

So the work had to be old. That was the craft of it. A seam ages the way a face ages — you can't fake it all at once, but you can help it along.

I began by steaming. A careful pass of a hot iron over damp muslin relaxes wool thread and coaxes it into believing it has always lain where it lies now. Then the re-stitching itself, done not with my left hand as habit demanded but alternating, so no single tension signature marked the new work. Elias had taught me that too, years ago, in one of our rare spoken lessons: *"A forger changes the words. A real craftsman changes nothing but time."* He'd been talking about documents then. He had no idea he was writing instructions for his own rescue.

By seven o'clock, when the first apprentices' boots clattered in the street outside, I had unpicked and re-sewn the first false name — Alderman Krail — replacing it with the true name Ivo had brought me from Elias's hospice bed: a customs clerk named Pēteris Ozols, a man who had fed information to the resistance and been paid in threats. The lie became the truth. Somewhere in the city, Ozols was probably drinking his morning coffee, unaware that a woman with sore eyes had just rewritten him from traitor back into patriot, forty stitches at a time.

Four names remained. Four days. I laid the coat flat, covered it with the officer's greatcoat we used for hiding things in plain sight, and went to boil water for tea I didn't want.

* * *

Ivo came at midday, entering through the fitting room like a man visiting his tailor, which was precisely what he was pretending to be. He'd gotten better at pretending. In October he had moved through the workshop like a cat in a church — every bell a threat, every eye an accusation. Now he draped himself over a chair near the window with a newspaper he couldn't read in the local language, and let Marta's apprentice pin his sleeve cuffs while he talked to me in the low, quick undertone we'd perfected between us.

"The boat's arranged," he murmured, eyes on the newspaper. "A herring smack out of the north quay. The skipper owes Elias from the winter of 'seventeen. He'll take two passengers and whatever they carry, no questions, and he doesn't want payment."

"No one does something like that without wanting something."

"He wants his country back." Ivo turned a page. "It's the only currency some men deal in."

"And Elias? Can he walk?"

The pause was small, but I had learned to hear Ivo's pauses the way I heard dropped stitches — by what was missing rather than what was there.

"He can be carried," Ivo said finally. "There's a stretcher involved. It looks undignified. He'll hate it."

"Good. Let him hate something. Hatred keeps a man's temperature up." I bit a thread free and smiled down at the cuff I was finishing. "When?"

"The night after the delegation sails. While every officer in the port is busy watching the ferry instead of the hospice." His voice tightened. "Which means your coat has to be perfect before it boards, because after that night there will be no second chances for any of us. They'll know everything. They'll burn everything."

"They already intend to burn the workshop. I heard the inspector telling the landlord." I said it lightly, the way you report weather. Ivo's head came up sharply.

"You didn't tell me that."

"There wasn't anything you could do about it, and I needed sleep more than I needed sympathy."

"Hazel." He set down the newspaper. Under the shop-boy disguise he looked tired in a way that went past the body — the tiredness of a man carrying a dying friend's last wish in a wet paper bag through a city full of knives. "You should come with us. On the boat. Both nights are the same night for a reason. You finish the coat, we take Elias, and you get on that smack and you never see this harbor again."

"I have a pattern book," I said. "Three years of records. Names, dates, routes, payments. If the workshop burns and I'm on a boat, that book burns with everything else, or worse, it gets found in the ashes and read by the wrong people."

"So bring the book."

"The book is the size of a psalter and the occupation searches ferries with tweezers." I tied off the cuff thread and looked at him properly. "Ivo. I've thought about this every night for a month. I know exactly how heavy the book is and exactly how light a smuggler's boat rides. I will hand the coat to Voss's valet, I will walk home, and I will leave this city the way I came into it — through the seams, slowly, when nobody's looking. There's a fishing family in the estuary who owe Marta's cousin. It's arranged. It's fine."

"It's fine," he repeated, without conviction.

"It's stitched twice," I said, "which is more than I can say for most things."

The apprentice finished his cuffs and Ivo stood, dropped coins on the counter with theatrical generosity, and paused beside my worktable just long enough to murmur: "Two days for the remaining names. The skipper sails regardless."

"Then the skipper should trust me," I said, "because I don't sail at all unless this coat is right."

He almost smiled. It was the closest either of us came to tenderness that week, and honestly, it was enough.

* * *

Marta came back on Thursday.

I heard her before I saw her — the particular drag in her step on the stairs, the one she'd had since the questioning, as though her shoes had gained weight in the cells below the police headquarters. She appeared in the doorway of the cutting room with her hair pinned carelessly and a bruise, faintly yellowed, at the edge of her jaw where the collar of a coat usually hid it.

Neither of us said anything for a moment. Around us the workshop hummed — apprentices chattering about the delegation, steam hissing, the eternal rattle of the cutting shears — and in the middle of it Marta and I regarded each other like two chess players resuming a game after someone had knocked the board off the table.

"You're thinner," she said at last.

"You're bruised."

"They were very thorough." She hung her coat on the peg — deliberately, I noticed, on the peg farthest from mine — and rolled up her sleeves. "They asked me about you for nine hours. I told them you were dull, pious, and hopeless at cards. Two of those were lies."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, but her voice cracked in the middle of the gesture, and for a moment she was just a tired woman of fifty-three whose brother owed money to men who broke jaws. "Don't thank me and don't apologize and don't explain anything, because I don't want to know. Do you understand? As long as I don't know, I'm safe. As long as I don't know, my brother is safe. Whatever you keep in that loft, whatever you stitch after hours — I saw nothing, I know nothing, and if they ask me again I will tell them the same three sentences until they get bored."

"That's not how they work. They don't get bored."

"Then we'll both find out how stubborn I am." She took up her shears and a length of grey suiting and began to chalk a pattern with hands that shook only slightly. "Now move over. You've got that delegation coat half-finished and I can see from here the collar canvas is puckering. Honestly, Hazel. Nine hours in a cell and I come back to amateur hour."

I moved over. We worked side by side through the afternoon, and it was almost — *almost* — like the old months, before the letter, before the raids. But there was a new silence in her, a carefulness, and once, when the floorwalker passed behind us, Marta's shoulders climbed toward her ears in a way they never had before. She covered for me again when the examiner came sniffing through that Friday. She laughed at his jokes. She showed him the honest coats.

And when he left, she didn't look at me at all, and I understood that whatever had been taken from her in those nine hours, it wasn't her loyalty. It was her certainty. She was helping me now the way you hold a railing on a dark staircase — not because you know what's at the bottom, but because letting go feels worse.

I decided that was enough. Love that isn't certain is still love. Ask any stitch.

* * *

That night, alone, I cut the second false thread.

This one was harder. The name was General Lindqvist's adjutant — a man named Rask — and Elias's true replacement for him was a name I recognized. Frau Ilse Behrend, the flower seller on Cathedral Square, who had sold violets to half the officers' wives and passed their gossip to our network in the language of bouquets. *White roses mean the meeting is moved. Forget-me-nots mean someone is following you.* She was sixty-eight years old and had knees like bad weather, and the occupation had written her into a death sentence to protect a colonel who took bribes.

I unpicked Rask. My eyes burned. The candle guttered and I trimmed the wick with my nail scissors because getting up to fetch the proper snuffer would cost thirty seconds and I had stopped having thirty seconds to spare somewhere around Tuesday.

Somewhere in the middle of re-stitching Ilse Behrend's name — forty-two stitches, alternating hands, steam-pressed, aged with a rub of tea-water and chalk dust — I caught myself humming. An old song my mother sang over the washboard. Something about a sailor and a girl and a ring lost in the harbor. My mother had hummed it while mending other women's clothes in a kitchen smaller than my fitting room, and I had sworn at sixteen that I would never live like that — bent over cloth in a cold room, working for people who'd never learn my name.

And here I was. Bent over cloth. In a cold room. Working so that a republic I'd never been allowed citizenship in might, someday, learn the truth — even if it never learned my name.

I laughed, quietly, so as not to wake the needle. There was a version of this where I was a fool. Where all of it — the cipher, the coat, Elias dying by inches in a hospice while his captors wore his mind like a stolen coat themselves — was just vanity dressed up as justice. But my mother's hands had known what mine knew: that mending is not submission. That a garment tells the truth about the body inside it, whether the body wants it told or not. That was the whole of my politics, really, and I supposed I'd inherited it along with the thimbles.

I pressed the seam. Held it to the light. Turned it. Pressed it again.

Ilse Behrend, restored to life in wool and silk, lay against Minister Voss's shoulder blade, and she would cross the water in the morning fog with the rest of the evidence, and somewhere in a foreign pressroom a typesetter who didn't know her name would set her testimony in ink, and that was as close to a monument as any of us were going to get.

Three names undone. Two remained.

* * *

On the fourth night, the trouble arrived wearing good manners.

I was deep in the fourth name — the hardest, because the false thread ran directly beneath a section of Elias's original cipher that could not be disturbed — when I heard the street door. Not the knock. Just the door, opening quietly, the way people enter when they don't want to be announced.

I killed the candle with my palm and slid the coat under the officer's greatcoat in one motion I'd rehearsed until it took less than four seconds, and I was standing in the dark with my heart going like a treadle machine when a match flared across the room.

Marta's face appeared above it, cupping the flame.

She looked at me. I looked at her. The match burned down toward her fingers and neither of us spoke, and in that little trembling circle of light I watched her make a choice — watched her eyes go from my face to the greatcoat on the table and back again — and I understood that she knew exactly what she was seeing, and had known for longer than she'd admitted, and that she had climbed these stairs at midnight anyway.

"You're doing it tonight?" she whispered.

"Marta—"

"The ferry leaves Monday." She struck a second match from the first and lit the lamp, turning the wick low, low, low. "I counted the names you've changed. Don't look like that — I press that coat every evening, I'm not blind, I'm only pretending to be. There are five foreign names in that lining. There were five yesterday and there are five today, but two of them are different, and I can count." She set the matches down with a small, decisive click. "My brother's debts are paid. Someone paid them. Three days ago, anonymously, through a lawyer who smelled of government offices. So either your friends bought my silence, or theirs did, and I've spent two nights deciding which, and I still don't know."

The lamp hissed softly. My mouth had gone dry.

"But here's what I do know," Marta said, and she untied her apron with steady hands and folded it over the chair back, and rolled up her sleeves. "I know that coat leaves Monday, and I know you have two names left and one pair of hands, and I know that in nineteen years in this trade I have never once seen anyone press a lapel roll correctly under time pressure." She pulled her sewing stool to the table and sat, and held out her palm for the needle. "So. Show me which threads are the liars, Hazel Crane. And then never tell me another thing, ever, for the rest of my life."

For a long moment I couldn't speak. Nineteen years of rules — *trust no one, trust no one, the network survives on silence* — stood in me like a row of pins. And across the table Marta waited with her palm open and her jaw bruised and her brother saved by somebody's money, offering the only currency she had left.

I put the needle in her hand.

"Anchor is truth," I said. "Float is mercy. Backstitch is blood. And Marta — if anyone asks, you're hopeless at cards."

"I'm excellent at cards," she said, threading the needle in the lamplight. "But God help them if they ever ask me about linings."

We worked until the windows went grey. Two names became one; one became none; the coat grew older and truer under four hands instead of two, and somewhere in the small hours Marta began to hum — my mother's sailor song, though she couldn't have known it, though I never told her — and I put my head down over the wool and let my eyes sting and kept stitching.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the quarter-hour, counting us down toward Monday. And it was only when the first pale light found the finished lapel that I remembered what Ivo had said, and went cold all over.

The extraction was tomorrow night. The same night the ferry sailed.

And I had promised Ivo there would be no loose threads.
