## 17. The Fitting

The morning of the final fitting arrived the way bad weather arrives over the harbor — slowly at first, then all at once, and with no possibility of arguing it back out to sea.

Hazel had been awake since four. She had not meant to be; sleep had simply abandoned her sometime after three, sliding away like a thread pulled through cloth until there was nothing left to hold. She lay in the dark of her narrow room above the workshop and listened to the wind worrying at the chimney, and she thought about linings.

Specifically, one lining. The one inside Minister Voss's ceremonial coat, where forty-one characters of a corrected cipher now lived in pin-pricks so fine that only a fingertip trained by six years of Elias Brandt's discipline could read them — or so Hazel prayed, lying there in the dark, cataloguing every stitch like a woman counting sheep who had learned that sheep could hang her.

The alteration had taken eleven hours. Eleven hours of unpicking the false names Elias had been forced to weave in and resewing the truth in their place, while Ivo kept watch below with a poker he insisted was for the stove and everyone knew was for something else entirely. Marta had sat with her through the worst stretch, holding the lamp steady without being asked, saying nothing, which from Marta was itself a kind of eloquence.

Now the coat hung on the form near the north window, grey wool broadcloth, silver braid at the collar, looking for all the world like exactly what it appeared to be: the dress uniform of a dying republic's last minister of trade, destined for a peace delegation that would sail on Thursday whether anyone believed in peace or not.

"It's staring at me," Marta said, coming up the stairs with two cups of chicory coffee. She set one on Hazel's bench without ceremony. "That coat. It has an opinion about us."

"Everything in this shop has an opinion," Hazel said. "The pressing machine alone is a gossip."

Marta snorted into her cup — a small, real laugh, the first Hazel had heard from her since they'd brought her back from questioning nine days ago. She'd returned changed, quieter, with a new carefulness in her hands, as though she were handling herself the way she handled fragile fabric. But she had come back. She had covered for Hazel once already during the inspection scare, and last week, when Officer Brandl had made his rounds and paused too long at the cutting table, it was Marta who'd stepped between him and the fitting room with a bolt of defective serge and a ten-minute complaint about moths.

Ten minutes. Hazel would remember those ten minutes for the rest of her life.

"The delegation arrives at ten," Hazel said. "The examiner arrives with them."

"I heard." Marta looked at the coat again. "Kessler himself?"

"Kessler himself." Herr Kessler, the occupation's textile examiner, a man whose reputation preceded him the way a funeral bell precedes a coffin. He could date a bolt of cloth by smell. He had once identified a smuggled dispatch hidden in a hatband from across a room — or so the story went, and stories like that were usually true, because men like Kessler didn't need exaggeration.

"And you're certain the lining will pass?"

Hazel took a sip of coffee and found it, as always, terrible, and was oddly comforted by that. Some things held. "I'm certain I did everything a person can do. Beyond that, we're in the hands of God and Marta Vītola."

"Don't put my name next to God's," Marta said. "He's got enough trouble."

* * *

They came at half past ten, twenty minutes late, which Hazel chose to interpret as nerves rather than contempt: Minister Voss, thin as a winter branch and twice as bent; his aide, young Lieutenant Auseklis, carrying the minister's gloves as though they were state documents, which in a sense they were; and behind them both, unhurried, gloved, smelling faintly of good tobacco and bad intentions — Examiner Kessler.

"Herr Examiner," Hazel said, with a bow calibrated to the exact degree the occupation required. Too deep was mockery. Too shallow was defiance. Six years of practice had given her the measurement down to the millimeter.

"Fräulein Crane." Kessler removed one glove finger by finger, taking his time about it, letting the room know that time belonged to him today. "You have the garment ready."

"The garment is ready."

"Then let us see whether it is *only* a garment."

Minister Voss was led behind the curtain to change, and Hazel busied herself with pins and chalk while the sound of him dressing came faintly through the canvas — the rustle of a man putting on his country. When he emerged, the coat fit beautifully. Of course it did. She had measured him twice, once officially and once with her eyes, and a seamstress's eyes do not err.

"You've done fine work," Voss said quietly, turning before the long mirror. His gaze met hers in the glass, and for one unguarded second there was something in it — a question, perhaps, or a farewell. He was an old contact of the network, careful as a man walking on river ice, but he knew what workshops like this one carried in their seams. "The republic thanks you for your craftsmanship."

"The republic," Hazel said, "has always paid its bills late, but it pays."

A ghost of a smile crossed the old man's face and was gone before Kessler turned around.

And then Kessler turned around.

"May I?" He said it the way a man says *may I* when the answer has never once mattered. He crossed to the coat's second suit — the traveling frock that would go under it, innocuous, honest — first, running his fingers along the seams, checking the tension of the stitching, tilting the collar to the light. Hazel stood very still and thought about nothing, which is the hardest sewing of all.

The frock passed. Kessler grunted, a small bureaucratic sound of disappointment, and moved to the ceremonial coat.

Hazel felt Marta move somewhere behind her. Not toward the door. Toward the pressing table.

Kessler began at the shoulders. His hands were extraordinary, she had to admit that — dry, patient, thorough, the hands of a man who loved cloth more than he had ever loved any living thing. He worked down the left sleeve, then the right. He examined the braid. He lifted the hem and studied the fell of it against the light, and Hazel watched him and breathed and did not breathe and watched him some more.

Then he turned the coat partially inside out at the shoulder to look at the lining.

Time went strange. It did that sometimes, Hazel had learned; terror slowed the world down like a clock dropped in honey. She saw everything with terrible clarity: the grain of the examining table, a chalk mark on the floor shaped like a bird, the small muscle ticking in Kessler's jaw, and her own reflection doubled in the mirror — herself, and beyond the curtain, the pale shape of Minister Voss, who had gone very still, who understood, who was watching.

Kessler's fingers found the lining seam under the left arm. The altered seam. Her seam. Eleven hours of work condensed into a single point of contact between his fingertip and her future.

"There is a—" he began.

"Herr Examiner!" Marta's voice rang across the workshop, bright and aggrieved and absolutely perfect. She came around the pressing table holding the officer's greatcoat — the finished one, the one belonging to Captain Rezek of the garrison, the one Hazel had hidden things inside during the first raid and which had since become, through sheer repetition, a kind of talisman. "Forgive me, forgive me, but if you are inspecting coats, you must inspect *this* one first, because I have told Fräulein Crane for three weeks that the pressing on the right lapel is wrong, and she tells me it is the steam, and I tell her the steam is innocent—"

"Marta." Hazel injected warning into the name, the exact note of an employer embarrassed by a chatty subordinate.

"No, no, let the examiner decide." Marta thrust the greatcoat forward with the confidence of a woman presenting evidence in court. "If our work passes his inspection, then Fräulein Crane owes me an apology and a bottle of the good gin. If it does not pass, I owe her nothing, and I shall say so loudly and often."

For one suspended heartbeat, Kessler looked at Marta the way a cat looks at a dog that has unexpectedly begun to sing. Then, because vanity is universal among men who believe themselves infallible, he released the ceremonial coat — *released it*, his fingers leaving the altered seam like a wave retreating from shore — and took the greatcoat instead.

"This pressing," he said, examining the lapel, "is acceptable."

"*Acceptable?*" Marta's voice climbed into regions previously unmapped. "Herr Examiner, with respect, that lapel has a roll in it like a drunkard's walk. Look at how it breaks—"

"It breaks correctly."

"It does *not*—"

"Marta!" Hazel seized her by the elbow, mortified, furious, grateful beyond the capacity of any language she spoke. "Enough. You'll apologize to the examiner."

"I'll apologize when the steam apologizes to me."

There was a silence. And then — Hazel saw it happen, saw it arrive and could do nothing whatsoever about it — a sound escaped Kessler. Small, rusty, involuntary. Something adjacent to laughter, from a man whom rumor said had not laughed since the annexation. He pressed his lips together immediately, as though recapturing a prisoner, but it had happened. Everyone had seen it happen.

"You have," he said to Marta, replacing one glove, "an insubordinate tongue."

"Yes, Herr Examiner. It runs in the family. My mother had one too. They shot her opinions in 'nineteen, but the tongue survived."

Another silence, longer, colder, with edges. Marta stood in the middle of it with her chin up, and Hazel understood suddenly what this was — not recklessness, not cover, but testimony. A woman telling an examiner, in front of witnesses, in the grammar of a joke, exactly what she thought of him and his occupation, and daring him to make something of it. Because a man who arrested a seamstress for insolence about a lapel would have to explain why, and explanations were the one thing the occupation hated more than insolence.

Kessler finished with the greatcoat. He returned to the ceremonial uniform, completed his circuit of it in ninety seconds flat — briskly now, the spell of his own thoroughness broken — and pronounced it satisfactory.

"The delegation departs Thursday," he said, buttoning his glove. "The garment will be delivered Wednesday evening to the quay warehouse. Sealed transport. Do not wrap it in anything sentimental." At the door he paused, and looked back at Hazel with an expression she would spend years decoding. "Your colleague is correct about the steam, by the way. Your boiler runs two degrees cold. It affects the nap." And he left.

Nobody moved for a full minute. Through the window they watched the black motorcar pull away, and Lieutenant Auseklis handed the minister back his gloves, and the ordinary gray day resumed its ordinary gray business.

Then Marta sat down on the cutting table, quite suddenly, as though her legs had submitted their resignation.

"Gin," she said. "You owe me gin."

"Marta." Hazel's voice cracked on the second syllable, which she pretended hadn't happened. "Why did you do that?"

Marta looked at her for a long moment, and whatever Hazel saw in her face, it wasn't fear anymore. It was something decided. "Because they asked me questions in that room for four days," she said, "and I answered them, and I hated every answer I gave. And because my brother's debts are paid now — that was the price, don't pretend you don't know — and a debt that's paid is a debt that's *mine* again. What I do with myself afterward belongs to nobody but me." She shrugged, elaborately casual, ruined completely by the tremor in her hands. "Also the lapel really does have a roll in it. I stand by that."

Hazel laughed — a short, wet, astonished laugh — and then did the thing her rules forbade and her heart had been voting for since October. She embraced her. Briefly, awkwardly, over the top of the pressing table, two seamstresses and a greatcoat, while Minister Voss emerged from behind the curtain dressed once more in his plain civilian sorrow and pretended very hard to be interested in a box of buttons.

* * *

Ivo came at dusk, slipping in through the yard door with the ease of a man who had stopped pretending to be a stranger weeks ago. He listened to Hazel's account of the fitting with his arms folded, and when she got to the part about Marta and the lapel, he made a noise that might have been a laugh trying to escape through a locked door.

"She insulted Kessler. To his face. About steam."

"Two degrees cold," Hazel said. "It affects the nap."

"He's not wrong about that," Ivo admitted. "The man knows textiles. That's the horror of it — give the occupation a hundred Kesslers and they wouldn't need soldiers." He unwound his scarf, and beneath the scarf's shadow Hazel saw what exhaustion had done to him: the hollowed cheeks, the bruised skin under the eyes, the hands that wouldn't quite stay still. The extraction plan for Elias was moving, he said. Moving the way ice moves before it breaks. Thursday night, the same tide that carried the delegation out would carry a small boat to a rendezvous that depended on eleven separate people not betraying each other, and Ivo trusted perhaps four of them, and he was including Hazel and himself in the count.

"And Elias?" Hazel asked quietly. "Is he strong enough?"

Ivo was quiet for a moment. "He asked me yesterday if you'd finished the rework. I told him yes. He closed his eyes and said—" Ivo stopped, and started again. "He said, *tell her the false threads always show, in the end. Cloth remembers.*"

Cloth remembers. Hazel looked across the workshop at the ceremonial coat hanging in the lamplight, patient and beautiful and full of ghosts, and felt the truth of it settle into her bones. Every lie sewn into a nation, every name falsely given, every stolen coin — it all showed eventually, the way a bad seam shows, puckering the surface no matter how carefully it's pressed.

"Wednesday evening," she said. "Sealed transport. Quay warehouse."

"That's the schedule."

"Then we have one day to make sure the most inspected coat in the Baltic stays boring." She reached up and touched the collar, the silver braid cool under her fingertips. "One day, Ivo. After that it walks out of here wearing the whole truth, and we never touch it again."

"One day," Ivo agreed.

Neither of them said what both were thinking, which was that Thursday also carried other cargo — a hospice bed, a harbor gate, a watcher with Elias's cell in his memory and a price list in his pocket. The coat might get through. Everything else was weather.

From the corner, Marta looked up from her mending, met Hazel's eye, and held up her needle meaningfully, as if to say: *whatever's coming, hand it to me, I'll hold it steady.*

Outside, the harbor fog was coming in off the water, thick and white and full of muffled sounds — a ship's bell, footsteps that stopped too soon, and somewhere far off, the low horn of the delegation's ship testing its voice for the journey it would make in two days' time, out past the breakwater, out past the occupation's reach, carrying a republic's confession stitched into its lining like a prayer into a shroud.
