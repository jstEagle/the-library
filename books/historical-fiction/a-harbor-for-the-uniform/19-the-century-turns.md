## 19. The Century Turns

The proclamation went up at nine in the morning, pasted to the door of the customs house and, by ten o'clock, to the door of our own shop, as though the occupation had decided we deserved the courtesy of reading our own obituary on site.

*By order of the Provisional Authority of Occupation, all commercial premises on Harbor Street are closed effective the thirty-first of December. Owners may collect inventory until six o'clock. Thereafter the buildings pass to military administration.*

"Inventory," Marta read aloud over my shoulder, her breath fogging the glass. "As if we were a warehouse of boots."

"We are a warehouse of boots," I said. "Three crates in back, if I remember."

"Of *bad* boots." She sniffed. "Even our ruin is shoddy."

I might have laughed — there was something almost festive about it, the empire finally admitting what we had all known since autumn, that it intended to sweep the street clean of us like so much sawdust — except that above us, folded into tissue paper in the fitting room, hung a ceremonial uniform with the truth of a nation sewn into its left breast lining. And at midnight, that uniform was going onto a train.

* * *

We had planned it for a week, and every plan had a hole in it shaped like a person.

The delegation departed at 12:40 a.m., the first of January, on a special train under white flags — the last diplomatic gesture of a republic that would not live to see the year named after it. The uniforms had been fitted, inspected once by an examiner who had nearly undone everything and been saved by Marta's quick tongue and quicker hands. The corrected cipher was in place; Elias's false names cut out thread by thread, the true list stitched where no iron would press and no finger would think to look.

But a garment is only half a message. The other half was the relay.

"There's a wireless set in the harbor office," Ivo had told me two nights before, drawing on the back of a pattern sheet in his cramped, slanting hand. "Coastal station, run by occupation signals men, but the operator on night duty is a boy named Kessler who owes me from Riga. At midnight he changes shift alone. Eleven minutes when the room is his. Enough to send maybe four hundred characters if my handwriting doesn't make him laugh."

"And after eleven minutes?"

"After eleven minutes," Ivo said mildly, "we are no longer in the harbor office."

That was the whole plan. That was everything. A coat on a train and a boy at a key, and between them, threaded through the dark like a basting stitch, the hope that someone across the water would be listening.

* * *

New Year's Eve came down gray and cold, with a wind off the harbor that rattled the shutters like a creditor at the door. We spent the daylight doing what the proclamation demanded: taking inventory, boxing spools, stacking patterns — while underneath the honest work, the dishonest work continued at double speed.

Because here was the thing Ivo and I had understood in the same moment, standing over the proclamation like mourners over a grave: the closure was our gift. The occupation wanted the shop emptied, swept, its papers burned or carted away. Which meant that tonight, of all nights, nobody would question a seamstress burning scraps.

"You're going to burn the archive?" Marta asked me mid-afternoon, watching me feed pages into the stove. Not the pattern book — that lived against my ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a second heartbeat. But the working records, the measurement logs, five years of Elias's teaching notes disguised as tailoring memoranda.

"I'm going to burn some of it," I said. "A girl must keep her hands busy."

"Hazel." She lowered her voice and came closer, close enough that I could see she hadn't slept — none of us had slept since they took her, since she came back three days later quieter and watchful, loyal in a way neither of us could afford to test. "Whatever you're doing tonight. If it needs a second pair of hands."

I looked at her. She looked at the stove. Somewhere outside, a patrol went by, boots striking cobbles in that unhurried rhythm of men who owned the street and knew it.

"Marta," I said carefully, "if I ever needed a second pair of hands, I'd want them to belong to someone whose stitching I trust. Yours is the best in this shop."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can give you while the walls have ears."

She held my gaze a long moment, then nodded once, sharply, and turned back to the crates. But as she went, she said, "The stove draws badly on the left side. Pages catch there and don't burn through. Use the right."

Which was, in its way, more of an answer than mine had been.

* * *

At dusk Ivo came in through the alley door with snow in his hair and a look on his face that I had learned to read the way I read fabric: this weave has a flaw somewhere.

"Well?" I said.

"Elias is on the boat." He stamped the snow off his boots, and his voice was steady but his hands, hanging at his sides, were fists. "Janek moved him at noon. He's in the hold, wrapped in sacking like cargo. He asked—" Ivo stopped. Started again. "He asked whether the list was clean. Whether every false name was out."

"Every one. I checked it twice against his own key. Then a third time because I didn't trust the first two."

"He'll want to know that."

"He knows. You'll tell him." I touched Ivo's arm, and he flinched — just slightly, just enough — and I understood without being told that telling Elias anything had become its own kind of wound. "Ivo. When this is done, when the train goes, you take him out of that harbor and you don't stop until the water belongs to someone else."

"That's the intention." He managed half a smile. "You know what Elias says about intentions."

"He says intentions are what cowards sew instead of actions."

"He says intentions are the basting stitches of history. You pull them out later and nobody remembers they were there." Ivo shrugged out of his coat and dropped onto the cutting bench, suddenly looking every one of his tired years. "Hazel. The wireless plan has changed."

My stomach did what stomachs do. "Kessler?"

"Kessler is fine. Kessler is better than fine, he's practically patriotic. No — the change is timing. The delegation's departure moved up twenty minutes. Train leaves at 12:20. Which means the uniform goes into the baggage car by 12:10, which means the harbor road will be thick with officers and lantern light exactly when we need to walk through it."

"Then we don't walk. We're already there."

He turned and looked at me properly, and I watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I had already done, alone, at four that morning, lying in the dark with the pattern book under my pillow.

"No," he said.

"The uniform needs a final pressing before it travels. That's not sentiment, Ivo, that's craft — linen creases in tissue paper, and a crease across the breast panel sits exactly where the lining—"

"*No.*"

"—and the only steam iron in this district that's still plugged in is in this shop, and I am the only person in this city who knows what that lining looks like when it's right. So either the coat leaves this shop imperfect, or I carry it to the train myself, pressed, finished, inspected by these eyes—" I tapped my own face, "—and handed to Voss's valet with a curtsy and a complaint about the cold."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the shutter and the stove ticking as it cooled.

"You've already packed your bag," he said at last. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it behind the fitting-room curtain, no doubt — my good boots beside it, my mother's shawl on top.

"I packed a bag," I corrected. "Whether I wear those boots home or onto a boat depends entirely on how the evening goes. That's called flexibility. Elias taught me that too — lesson fourteen, *a seamstress plans for alterations*."

"He never taught you lesson fourteen."

"He taught me everything. You just weren't in the room."

And there it was — the thing that had grown between us over these weeks without either of us naming it, the strange siblinghood of people who have inherited the same dead man's work. Ivo laughed, short and helpless, and put his hand briefly over mine on the cutting bench.

"All right," he said. "Together, then. But we do it my way: you press the coat, I carry it, and you stay behind me like a shadow with opinions."

"I've never stayed behind anyone in my life."

"So I've noticed. God help us both."

* * *

The last hours of the old century passed in the smell of steam and scorched wool.

I pressed the ceremonial uniform one final time at eleven o'clock, the iron moving in long slow strokes while Marta held the shoulders taut and neither of us spoke, because some silences are a kind of prayer. The lining lay flat and innocent. The cipher slept inside it — treasury figures, dates, ship manifests, the true list of names, Elias's arithmetic of theft rendered in silk thread at eight stitches to the centimeter. Anyone who knew how to look would find a nation's evidence. Anyone who didn't would find excellent needlework.

"Voss will wear it to the signing," Marta said quietly. "In front of all of them. The men who did it, wearing the proof of it on their own ceremonial occasion."

"That was always Elias's joke," I said. "He liked irony with a good drape."

At half past eleven we heard the lorries come. Not for us — for the printing house two doors down, whose presses had been silent since November anyway, but whose type and paper the occupation wanted counted and carried off before the year died. Soldiers swarmed the street with lanterns, their breath smoking, singing something tuneless and triumphant. Through the window I watched them load crates, and I thought: *you are carrying away empty boxes while the real freight walks out on a man's shoulders at midnight.*

"They're celebrating," Marta observed.

"They're counting."

"Same thing, for people like that."

Ivo emerged from the back room dressed as a delivery man — borrowed cap, borrowed coat, the ceremonial uniform in a plain canvas carrier over his shoulder, looking for all the world like a tailor's errand boy running late for a fitting. Which, in fairness, he was.

"The quay at 12:15," he said. "If the wireless fails, the coat still carries everything. If the coat fails—" he paused, and I supplied it:

"The wireless still sends it. Two threads. Either holds."

"Either holds," he agreed. And then, softer, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: "Elias says — he wrote it down, because he said if he tried to say it aloud he'd get it wrong — *tell Hazel the stitches were always straight. Tell her the republic was lucky in its hands.*"

I looked at the floor. I am not ashamed of it; some moments require a floor.

"Tell him," I said finally, "that lesson fifteen is gratitude. And I invented it myself, just now, so he'd better learn it."

* * *

Marta walked us to the alley door.

The street beyond was lantern-light and song and soldiers, but the alley itself lay in darkness, snow squeaking underfoot, and in that pocket of black between the shop and the world, she took my hand — hers freezing, mine not much warmer — and held it harder than politeness allowed.

"When they burned your papers today," she said, "the ones from October. The interrogation transcripts. They didn't burn well. The right side of the stove draws beautifully, but you fed the left."

I went very still.

"I read what I could before the flames got it," Marta said. "Enough." Her voice didn't waver. It cost her; I could feel it costing her, in the grip of her hand. "They made you a promise, those men. They promised you'd be forgotten if you talked. And you sat in that room and told them nothing, and they let you go anyway, and you came back here and kept sewing. So whatever happens tonight — whatever you're carrying out of this city in that bag — I want you to know I know. And I'm glad," and here her voice did crack, just once, like ice under a boot, "I'm glad it was me covering for you, and not someone else."

"Marta—"

"Don't. Don't ruin it with a speech. Go. Press your coat. Catch your train. Come back someday when there's a country again, and buy me dinner somewhere expensive."

"There won't be anywhere expensive. The whole economy's ruined."

"Then buy me two dinners at a cheap place." She pushed me toward the dark. "Go. Both of you. And Hazel —" I turned at the mouth of the alley. Lantern light caught half her face; the other half was already winter. "The stove. When you're done with the shop. Leave nothing that draws."

* * *

The harbor at midnight was a held breath.

Snow falling fat and slow through the lamplight. The delegation's train breathing steam at the platform, carriages lit gold, officers stamping and saluting, a band playing something national that everyone pretended not to recognize. And threading through it all, invisible, ordinary: a delivery man with a canvas carrier, and a woman with a sewing basket, walking toward the harbor office as though the cold were merely weather.

The wireless room sat above the customs shed, one lit window, a chimney puffing. Ivo knocked the pattern he'd arranged with Kessler — three short, pause, two long — and the door opened on a boy of perhaps nineteen, pale, sleepless, terrified and thrilled in equal measure, who looked at us the way novices look at relics.

"You're really him," Kessler breathed. "Riga. The Baltic route. Janek used to say you once sent a dispatch through a funeral wreath."

"A lily wreath," Ivo said gravely. "It mattered that it was lilies. Is the room clear?"

"Oberleutnant Brandt's relief comes at 12:12. He's drunk downstairs. Everyone's drunk downstairs. It's New Year's." Kessler said it as though reporting enemy movements.

Inside, the wireless set gleamed under its brass fittings, patient as a loom. Ivo stripped off his coat, cracked his knuckles like a pianist, and unrolled the message — the corrected cipher, encoded twice over, compressed to its leanest form. Four hundred characters. Eleven minutes. One century ending outside the frosted window while inside, a boy tuned a dial and a man who had buried his best friend alive began to send.

I stood at the window with the sewing basket on my hip and kept watch, and below me the train waited, and somewhere in its baggage car a canvas carrier held a coat that would cross the border by morning wearing a republic's stolen soul in its seams.

The key began to click. Fast, clean, confident — Ivo's fear converted directly into speed. Outside, the band struck up again. Somewhere a whistle blew, long and low, the kind of whistle that means departure.

"Two minutes early," Kessler whispered, stricken. "They're moving the train—"

"The coat's aboard," Ivo said, not lifting his fingers from the key. "The coat was always going to be aboard. Keep the relay clear."

Click, click, click. The message poured out into the frozen air, over the water, toward whatever listening ear waited across the strait. I found I had my hand pressed flat against the cold glass, as if I could push the signal along, baste it through the night the way I'd baste a hem — hold it true, hold it flat, don't let it pucker.

The last group went out at 12:09. Ivo sat back. For a moment none of us moved. Then Kessler, with the solemnity of a boy performing a sacrament, logged the transmission in the station book as *routine traffic, coastal weather advisory*, and shut the set down, and the three of us stood in the little brass-lit room while outside, the train began at last to move, pulling the old century's lies out of the station and the new century's truth out of the country, both bound for the border.

"It's done," Ivo said. He sounded surprised, the way a man sounds when a fever breaks. "Hazel. It's actually done."

"It's done," I agreed. "Now comes the part where we leave."

We were halfway down the customs shed stairs when I saw him — a figure at the edge of the lantern light by the platform gate, hatless in the snow, not watching the train but watching the harbor office, watching *us*, with the patient posture of a man who has waited a long time for exactly this. Even at forty meters, even in falling snow, I recognized the set of the shoulders.

The watcher from the hospice. The collaborator. Elias's own man.

And he had seen us.

He raised one hand — not waving. Pointing. Behind him, at the far end of the quay, torches were moving toward Harbor Street, toward the row of shuttered shops, toward ours, and they were moving quickly, and they were not there to count inventory.

"Hazel," Ivo said quietly. "Walk. Don't run. Walk toward the boat."

Below us the train gathered speed. Ahead of us the watcher smiled through the snowfall. And behind us, up Harbor Street, the first orange glow of something burning climbed into the new century's sky.
