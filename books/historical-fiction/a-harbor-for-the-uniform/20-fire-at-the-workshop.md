## 20. Fire at the Workshop

The order came on the last morning of the year, delivered not by an officer but by a boy of perhaps fourteen in an occupation courier's cap too big for him, who thrust an envelope at Marta and pedaled off before she could even scowl at him properly. The envelope bore the seal of the Provisional Administration for Public Order — a phrase Hazel had long considered the finest joke the occupation ever told, since it contained no provision, no administration, and no public order worth naming.

Marta read it twice. Then she carried it to Hazel at the cutting table and set it down between them as though it were something dead.

"They're closing us," she said. "Effective tonight. The premises are to be 'cleared and secured' by the Bureau of Records." She said *Bureau of Records* the way one says a diagnosis.

Hazel took the letter and read it herself, though she had already understood from Marta's face. *All materials, patterns, ledgers, and garments remaining on the premises are subject to destruction or confiscation as instruments of irregular correspondence.* Instruments of irregular correspondence. She had spent four years hiding state secrets in trouser linings, and it seemed almost insulting that the occupation had finally, accidentally, written down the truth of her trade.

"They mean to burn it," Marta said quietly. "Everything."

"Of course they do." Hazel folded the letter into precise quarters. "A fire explains a great many things. Missing records. Missing people. A very tidy way to end a story." She looked up. "It's also," she added, "the best thing that has ever happened to us."

Marta stared at her the way she always did when Hazel said something that would have gotten a saner woman committed. "Explain how a fire is a gift, because I'm standing right here freezing and I don't see it."

"Smoke, Marta. Confusion. Every officer in this district will be watching flames instead of watching the quay. And every scrap of paper they burn is a scrap they can't read." Hazel was already moving, pulling her workbasket toward the loft stairs. "We have until nightfall. I need you to be slow."

"I'm always slow."

"Slower than that. Glacially, magnificently slow. When they come to inventory, you will show them everything — *everything*, Marta, hold nothing back, be so helpful it hurts. The more cooperative you are, the longer their lists take, and the less they'll look at the woman carrying out a coat."

Marta's jaw set in a way Hazel had learned, over these hard weeks, to read correctly. It was the set that meant *yes*. "And if they ask where you've gone?"

"Tell them I ran when I heard about the fire order. Cowardice is very believable in me; I've spent years cultivating it."

"You have not cultivated anything except posture," Marta said, but there was something underneath it, rough and warm, that neither of them had time to name properly. "Go. Get your things. I'll mind the shop."

Hazel climbed to the loft and stood a moment in the cold gray light under the eaves, among the bolts of cloth and the smell of chalk and wool that had been the whole architecture of her adult life. Four years of nights up here with a needle and a memorized cipher, four years of being no one, of stitching silence into every seam. Tomorrow there would be nothing here but ash and a plausible official report.

She did not cry. There wasn't time, and besides — she had learned this much — grief keeps better when pressed flat, like good cloth, and can be taken out later without wrinkling.

She packed.

* * *

The pattern book came first.

It was an unremarkable object: a fat ledger bound in scuffed maroon leather, its spine re-sewn twice, its corners worn round from years of hands. To any examiner it held only what its pages plainly showed — measurements, alterations, dates of delivery, sketches of collars and cuffs, notes in three different hands about a minister who required extra room across the shoulders and a colonel who lied about his waistline by two full inches. Hazel had kept it faithfully because faithful record-keeping was the best disguise ever invented. No one suspects a bookkeeping woman.

But threaded through it, invisible unless you knew, was everything else. The pin-prick conventions Elias Brandt had taught her, mapped onto margin holes. The numbering system keyed to button counts. On the inside back cover, beneath a pasted-down swatch of navy broadcloth, the master key to the entire network's correspondence, sewn — actually sewn, in thread finer than hair — into the pasteboard itself.

Elias was gone now. Shot at the quay eleven days ago, covering Ivo's escape, and the harbor had taken the ledger pages he'd been carrying and scattered them like gulls. But the harbor had never seen Hazel Crane's pattern book. Nobody had. Not even Ivo knew quite what it held; he knew only that she refused to leave it behind, and he'd had the sense, bless him, not to press the point. He had said once, early on, that her attachment to it reminded him of a priest and his breviary. She had said it reminded *her* of a woman and her evidence, and he'd laughed, which was when she'd begun to suspect she might forgive him for arriving in her life like a lit match.

The second thing was the coat.

It hung in the back of the finished-goods cabinet, wrapped in linen, tagged with a false ticket marking it as a private commission, unclaimed. Inside its lining — restitched three times over the past week, each pass more careful than the last — lay the corrected cipher. The treasury proof, complete, the collaborator list purged of Elias's coerced lies, the whole confession of a stolen republic rendered in silk thread and patience. It was, Hazel thought as she lifted it out, the finest garment she had ever made and would never deliver to anyone who could wear it openly. A coat full of testimony. A coat that would outlive the country it accused.

She put the pattern book in a canvas satchel, wrapped the coat around it — evidence cushioned by evidence, which struck her as the most sensible packing she'd ever done — and paused at the little window of the loft to look down into the yard.

Two occupation lorries were already parked outside the workshop gate. Soldiers moved about with the unhurried confidence of men doing paperwork with fire. A sergeant with a clipboard consulted with a lieutenant whose greatcoat was better cut than his judgment. And Marta was out there, coatless in the cold on purpose, arms folded, arguing.

Hazel could not hear the words, but she could read the performance. Marta was pointing at shelves, at bins, at the pressing room, walking the lieutenant through the inventory with the tireless thoroughness of a woman who has found her calling late in life. Every so often she stopped him to correct a count, or to insist a particular shelf be listed separately from its neighbors, and the lieutenant's shoulders sagged a little more each time. Somewhere in the middle of it she produced a tin of sweets — where had she even gotten sweets? — and offered them around with such aggressive hospitality that two soldiers visibly reconsidered their careers.

Hazel smiled despite everything. Trust, she had learned this winter, was not a thing you decided once. It was a thing you renewed daily, like bread, like thread. Marta had come back after the questioning changed — quieter, watchful, with a bruise she didn't discuss and a brother whose debts the occupation had used as a leash. Hazel could not promise her safety. She could only promise her this: whatever happened next, Marta's exit was already arranged. Ivo had seen to it through the smuggler, two berths on the boat, no names given. Whether Marta chose to take hers was her own stitch to place.

Downstairs, through the floorboards, Hazel heard the front bell ring and Marta's voice rise, bright and bureaucratic: "*Absolutely* we'll need a second form for the pressing equipment, Lieutenant, the last audit flagged it—" God love her. The fire might take the building, but it would not take Marta's talent for making officials suffer.

* * *

Ivo arrived at half past three, coming through the yard gate with a coal merchant's sack over his shoulder and a face that said he'd already been questioned once today and had enjoyed it about as much as usual.

"Back street's crawling," he said, low, once he was inside the mending room with the door shut. "They've posted a man at the corner of the chandlery. Not watching the shop — watching who leaves it. Which means somebody upstairs still thinks there's something here worth taking out."

"There is," Hazel said. "It's wrapped in linen in the cabinet."

He looked at the satchel, then at her, and something in his face went carefully blank, which was his version of shouting. "You're going out the front."

"I'm going out the front dressed as exactly what I am: a dismissed seamstress carrying her personal effects, which is permitted under section nine of the closure notice, which I made Marta read aloud twice so I could quote it." She pulled her shawl tight and settled the coat-wrapped bundle against her hip like a fussy infant. "The man at the chandlery is watching for couriers, conspirators, anyone moving quickly or looking guilty. So I will move slowly, look bored, and complain about my wages to anyone who asks."

"That's — " Ivo began, and stopped, and rubbed his jaw. "That's either brilliant or insane."

"The two run on adjacent threads. You learn that in tailoring." She looked at him steadily. "You go out the back, through the tannery yard, like we planned. Take the boat route. I'll meet you at the third arch of the customs quay at dusk, and if I'm not there by full dark—"

"I wait anyway."

"You don't. You sail. That was the agreement, Ivo."

"And I'm amending it," he said, in the tone of a man who had recently watched Elias Brandt die on wet stones and had no further interest in agreements that ended with him leaving people behind. He said it gently, though. Gently, and with the stubbornness of a mule wearing a diplomat's manners. "Full dark, third arch. After that I get creative. Ask anyone; I'm famously creative."

There was no time to argue, and anyway — she admitted it to herself in the space of one breath — some stubborn animal part of her didn't want to win the argument. "Half past six," she conceded. "Not full dark. Half past six."

"Half past six," he agreed, as though she'd granted him a duchy.

Then, because the world was ending in a small way that afternoon, and because some things ought to be said while buildings still stood to hear them, he touched the edge of her shawl — just the edge, just briefly — and said, "Whatever happens on that quay, Hazel: it was the best work I ever saw. All of it. Every seam."

"Flatterer," she said, in a voice that wobbled precisely once. "Go. Through the tannery. Mind the dog."

"What dog?"

"The tannery acquired a dog in November. Nobody knows whose. It hates hats."

He left. She counted to sixty, adjusted the bundle on her hip, picked up the pattern book, and walked out the front door into the smoke-colored afternoon.

* * *

The yard smelled of coal oil already; they'd laid the fires early, waiting only for the final signature. The lieutenant with the clipboard stood near the gate with his list, and Marta was beside him, mid-performance, pointing out — Hazel could hardly believe it — the exact location of the owner's private storeroom, "so your men don't waste time searching the wrong cellar, Lieutenant, there was a theft in 'sixteen, you understand, one can't be too careful."

The soldier at the gate stepped halfway into her path. "Name?"

"Hazel Crane. Seamstress. Dismissed as of this afternoon, per the closure notice." She hefted the satchel wearily. "Personal effects. Section nine."

"Open it."

Inside the satchel, visible to any casual glance: a thimble. Two spools of thread. A half-eaten heel of bread wrapped in wax paper. A woman's spare stockings, darned. And beneath all of it, folded flat and unremarkable, a man's overcoat in dark wool, ticketed *unclaimed commission, balance owing* — the most boring object in the Baltic.

She opened the satchel and let him look. He looked the way soldiers look at sewing supplies, which is to say briefly and with relief. Only the lieutenant glanced over, and Marta — magnificent, shameless Marta — chose that exact moment to raise her voice: "Lieutenant, if you're signing for the boiler, someone must sign for the boiler *separately*, I cannot tell you the trouble the last man had—" and the lieutenant turned away from Hazel with the gratitude of a drowning man thrown a rope made of paperwork.

"Go on, then," said the soldier at the gate.

She went. Slowly. Boredly. Down the lane, past the chandlery, past the watcher at the corner, who gave her the glance one gives a tired middle-aged woman carrying sewing, which is to say none at all. Her heart behaved scandalously, hammering like an apprentice loose in a pin barrel, but her feet kept their pace, and her face kept its expression of a woman thinking mostly about dinner and slightly about her back pain.

Behind her, at some point — she never learned the exact minute, and would always regret not knowing — the workshop caught light.

She heard it before she saw it: a low roar, then shouts, then the peculiar cheerful crackle of a fire that has been professionally encouraged. She turned at the mouth of Fish Lane, and there it was — her whole world standing up in flame, the loft window glowing orange, sparks climbing into the gray sky like a flock of startled birds. Smoke rolled down the street thick and sweet-smelling with burnt wool and sizing. People came running from three directions, and soldiers shouted at them to keep back, and somewhere a church bell began ringing for reasons that had nothing to do with the hour.

Hazel stood in the crowd and let it burn.

It should have been unbearable. Some of it was — the sight of the big windows bursting, the thought of the cutting table where she'd taught herself Elias's first cipher by candlelight, the pressing room that smelled forever of steam and Marta's terrible singing. But underneath the grief ran something harder and stranger, a cold clean current: *they think they're erasing something.* They were burning a building and calling it history. They believed that fire was stronger than thread.

Poor fools. Fire couldn't read a lining. Fire had no idea what a needle remembers.

Beside her in the crowd, a voice muttered through clenched teeth, pitched for her alone: "Third window, second row. Don't look yet."

Marta. Standing shoulder to shoulder with her in the smoke, eyes forward, hands jammed in her pockets like any grieving neighbor. "Your man in the loft window saw me signal the tannery boy. Ivo's clear — went out with the coal sacks twenty minutes ago, before the fire even started, the smug devil. My berth's arranged. I'm meant to decide by tomorrow." A pause. The firelight moved on her face. "I'm deciding tonight."

"Marta—"

"Don't." Rough, and quiet. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't make a speech, Hazel Crane, I haven't got the time and neither have you. Just — " Her jaw worked. The bruise from November was nearly faded; Hazel could see where it had been. "Just make sure the coat gets where it's going. Make sure it *fits*. That's all. Everything else, we'll settle on the water."

Then a soldier shouted at the crowd to disperse, and Marta melted backward into it, and was gone — toward the harbor, toward the decision, toward whatever she would become when this city finally let her go.

Hazel turned east, toward the customs quay, toward the third arch and half past six, the satchel against her hip growing heavier with every step, though nothing had been added to it. The smoke followed her for two streets and then the wind took it, out over the black water, where the century was turning whether anyone approved or not.

At the mouth of the quay she stopped once, briefly, and looked back at the glow above the rooftops — the workshop burning, tidy and total, exactly as ordered. Let them write their report. Let them call it cleared and secured.

Somewhere under her arm, stitched into a dead man's design and a living woman's patience, the truth was walking out of the city on two tired legs.

And at the third arch, in the shadow where the lamplight gave up, a figure detached itself from the stones and raised one hand — and Hazel's breath caught, because he was not alone.

There was a second figure beside Ivo. Small. Wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin with the hood up.

And she knew, before the hood fell back, exactly who it was.
