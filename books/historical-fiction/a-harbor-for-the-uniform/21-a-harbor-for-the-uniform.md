## 21. A Harbor for the Uniform

The harbor at dawn in January smelled of tar and herring and the particular cold that gets into wool and stays there like a grudge. Hazel stood at the edge of the quay with the coat over her arm and thought, absurdly, of her mother's kitchen — how her mother had always said a woman should never leave a house without checking the stove twice.

She had checked every seam three times. That would have to do.

"You're smiling," Ivo said beside her. He had the ledger pages sewn into his own waistcoat now, a precaution she had insisted on after the quay. "People don't usually smile at funerals."

"This isn't a funeral."

"No?"

"It's a delivery." She shifted the coat's weight. Beneath the lining, flat against her palm through the wool, she could feel the faint ridges of the corrected cipher — thirty-one rows of pin-pricked code, each one unpicking a lie the occupation had stitched across the republic's books. The false names were gone. Elias's last instruction, honored to the last thread. What remained was only truth, which weighed less than lies, though it had taken far longer to make.

Ivo looked out at the water. The smuggler's boat — a blunt-hulled coaster named *Vera* with paint peeling off her in long gray ribbons — lay at anchor past the breakwater, waiting for the pilot boat that would bring her in under pretense of taking on ballast. The fog was doing its work, lying over the harbor like a sheet drawn up to the chin.

"Minister Voss crossed at four this morning," Ivo said. "Klaas sent word. The delegation's train went through the checkpoint without so much as a look inside the trunks. The examiner who nearly found us on Thursday signed the transit papers himself." He paused. "He signed them with a hand that shook, Klaas says. Apparently he hasn't slept since the fitting."

"Good," Hazel said. "Let him lose sleep. It's the least he can contribute."

That earned her one of Ivo's rare laughs — a short, startled sound, as if he'd forgotten he owned it and found it still in working order. She had learned, these six weeks, to count his laughs the way she counted stitches. There hadn't been many. But there had been enough.

* * *

They waited by the fish sheds, where the smell covered everything and no one lingered who didn't have to. Ivo paced. Hazel sat on an overturned crate and worked, because her hands did not know how to be idle — a mending job for one of the shed men, a torn elbow, nothing. Just sewing. Just the oldest and most innocent thing in the world, and therefore the safest thing to be seen doing.

"You know what strikes me," Ivo said, on his fourth turn of the pacing. "All those weeks in the workshop, I watched you thread needles without looking. I thought: there's a woman who could pick a lock blindfolded. And all the while you were doing something harder."

"Sewing?"

"Lying beautifully. With your whole self. Every straight seam, every honest hem." He stopped in front of her. "I've carried dispatches through two borders and a war. I never once had to make the thing I was carrying look like ordinary life."

"That's the trick of it," Hazel said, snipping the thread and folding the mended sleeve. "A secret hidden in a strongbox says *steal me*. A secret hidden in a coat collar says *mind the cold, sir*. Nobody robs a coat collar."

"My mother said something similar about bread," Ivo said. "Hiding things in loaves. Though hers was mostly hiding the bread from my father."

For a moment they were just two people laughing quietly in a fish shed, and Hazel let herself have it — the smallness of it, the warmth. Marta's face came to her then, unbidden: Marta standing in the smoke yesterday with her chin up and her brother's debt in her pocket and her loyalty finally, irrevocably, spent where she chose to spend it. *Go*, Marta had said, shoving the pattern book into Hazel's arms while the occupation's fire detail looked the other way at a bribe's worth of distance. *And don't you dare write to me. Not once. Not ever.*

Hazel intended to honor that completely, and also to write to her within the year, because some instructions existed precisely to be broken carefully.

"She'll be all right," Ivo said, reading her silence the way he'd learned to. "Klaas vouched for her with the harbor people. She has work on the herring boats until spring. By summer nobody will remember she was ever a seamstress."

"Marta will remember."

"Yes," Ivo said. "But remembering is different from being caught by it. That's a lesson Elias taught me." His voice went careful around the name, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. They had buried Elias two days ago — or rather, Klaas's people had, in the plot behind the sailors' chapel, under a stone that read only *BRANDT, MARINER*. No dates. Dates invited questions. Hazel had sewn a single row of pin-pricks into the hem of the shroud's lining, the first eight letters of his real name, and if that was sentimentality then she was content to be sentimental. A man who spent his life making sure messages survived deserved one message addressed only to him.

"The boat's coming in," Ivo said.

* * *

The *Vera* came alongside the old coal quay where the cranes stood rusting like herons. Her captain was a broad woman named Sigrid with forearms like mooring rope and the unimpressed expression of someone who had been woken before dawn for less money than the danger warranted.

"You're the luggage," she said. Not a question.

"We're the passengers," Ivo said.

"You're the luggage," Sigrid repeated, "because passengers ask questions and luggage keeps quiet. Ballast goes aboard in twenty minutes. You go aboard with it. You stay below until we're past the lightship. If anyone boards — customs, patrol, anybody — you're a pair of machinists going to Rostock for work. I've got papers. Don't improve them by talking." She looked at Hazel's coat bundle. "That the famous coat?"

"It's a coat," Hazel said.

"It's a coat worth more than my boat, from what I hear, so keep it dry." Sigrid spat expertly over the quay edge. "Border crossing's at noon. The delegation's train crosses ahead of us — big show, flags on the engine, the occupiers standing at attention saluting men whose country they've already picked clean. Poetic, if you like poetry. I prefer cargo manifests myself."

"And after the border?" Hazel asked.

Sigrid looked at her for a long moment, and something behind the flinty face shifted — recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional. "After the border, madam, there are foreign presses. That's all I know and all I want to know. My part ends at a dock in Lübeck where a man with a briefcase waits. He pays me. I buy paint. The world improves or it doesn't, but it does it somewhere else." She jerked her thumb at the gangplank. "Aboard. And mind the third step, it's rotten."

The third step was indeed rotten. Hazel noted it with professional disapproval — a woman who kept a workshop's accounts balanced down to the half-crown could not look at loose carpentry without pain — and stepped over it.

* * *

Below decks, the *Vera* smelled of fish oil and lamp black, and the ballast bags rose around them like sleeping animals. Hazel found a space between two of them, set the coat across her knees, and discovered that her hands, given nothing urgent to do for the first time in weeks, had begun to shake.

Not fear. Something after fear. The body settling its accounts.

Ivo crouched opposite her, striking a match to light the small lantern they'd been permitted. "Cold?" he asked.

"Tired."

"You've been tired for six weeks."

"I've been *useful* for six weeks. It's different." She flexed her fingers, watching them tremble, and found it almost funny. "You spend years teaching your hands to be steady, and the moment the work is done they fall apart like apprentices."

"Mine did that in October," Ivo said. "After the harbor. After Elias." He set the lantern between them, and its light moved gently with the swell. "I sat in a doorway off the Hafenstrasse for three hours and couldn't make my hands hold a cigarette. Then a boy came along selling roasted chestnuts, and buying chestnuts turned out to be something my hands could do. So I bought chestnuts. Sixteen of them. I ate eleven and gave five to a stray dog who looked like he'd been having a worse day than mine."

"What happened to the dog?"

"I don't know. I like to think he's doing well. Possibly he's in the treasury scandal too. Everyone else seems to be."

Hazel laughed — really laughed, the first time since the workshop burned, and the sound surprised her coming out, low and cracked and unmistakably alive. The trembling in her hands eased, not gone but companionable now, like an old colleague sitting down beside her.

Above them, boots crossed the deck. Voices — Sigrid's, and another, clipped, official. Both women went still. Hazel's hand found the coat and rested on it, lightly, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping child. The voices moved on. The gangplank creaked. Somewhere above, the *Vera* took on her ballast, and the hull settled deeper into the water with a sigh, as if the whole ship were exhaling.

Then the engines woke beneath them — a shudder first, then a rhythm, patient and enormous, like a heart deciding to keep going.

"We're moving," Ivo said unnecessarily.

"I noticed."

"Old habit. Announcing things. Elias hated it. Said a courier who narrates is a courier who gets shot."

"Then we'll both be quiet."

They were quiet for perhaps ninety seconds.

"Hazel." Ivo's voice had changed. She looked up. In the lantern light his face was stripped of its usual guardedness, and she saw underneath it the young man he must have been before — the one who'd joined a cell with Elias Brandt because the world needed fixing and someone had to hold the needle. "When we dock at Lübeck. When the coat goes to the man with the briefcase. After that — I don't actually know what comes after that. For either of us. I've been running this mission so hard I never planned the part where it ends."

"There's tea," Hazel said. "There's always tea, somewhere, in any country. And work. Someone will always need a seamstress. Half the world walks around with buttons hanging by a thread and no idea how close they are to ruin." She smoothed the wool over her knees. "I thought I might open a shop. Small. One window. A sign that says alterations, and means exactly that, and also doesn't."

Ivo studied her for a moment. "You'd take apprentices."

"One or two. Carefully chosen. Slow learners needn't apply, but quick learners with steady hands—" she shrugged— "the trade has to pass on somehow. Everything I know lives in my fingers, and my fingers won't last forever."

"And the person who helps you carry the sewing machines," Ivo said, with the elaborate casualness of a man walking across ice, "would that position be filled?"

Hazel looked at him. Six weeks ago he'd been a stranger with a dead man's letter and too much certainty in his coat pockets. Now he was the only living soul besides herself who knew every stitch of what that coat contained — who had sat with her through the raids, the grief, the reworking of thirty-one rows by lamplight while the century turned outside the shuttered windows. Some partnerships were made in offices with seals and wax. Hers had been made with a thimble and a shared enemy and eleven chestnuts' worth of kindness.

"The position," she said gravely, "requires references."

"I can produce a drowned man."

"He'll do."

* * *

The lightship passed at half past eleven, a squat iron sentinel blinking its slow eye, and no one boarded, and no one shouted, and the *Vera* slid out past the mole into the open gray water where the republic's harbor lights fell away behind them one by one — the customs house, the chapel, the black skeleton of the workshop still smoking faintly on the hill like a candle burnt to its socket.

Hazel stood in the stern — against orders, briefly, until Sigrid decided looking back was harmless once you were past the lightship — and watched the city that had made her shrink to a line, then a smudge, then nothing. She did not cry. She had expected to. Instead she felt something quieter and stranger settle over her, like snow falling on a finished garment: the particular peace of a thing completed well.

Somewhere south of them, a train was carrying Minister Voss and his doomed, dignified delegation across a border that no longer governed them. Sewn into the ceremonial uniform of a republic that officially did not exist hung the proof of everything done to it — the thefts, the names, the arithmetic of plunder — riding in a coat collar past soldiers saluting their own crime. No court had ordered this evidence produced. No judge had summoned it. It had been measured, cut, pinned, and pressed into being by a seamstress and a courier and a dying man's stubborn ghost, and by morning it would be spread on a desk in a neutral capital, and by spring it would be ink, and by summer it would be resignations, and none of it — not one thread of it — would ever pass through a courtroom at all.

Justice, Hazel thought, watching the wake unspool behind them like a seam across gray cloth, was simply the truth delivered on time. Everything else was tailoring.

She went below. Ivo had made tea on the little spirit stove, badly — it was mostly hot water with ambitions — and he handed her a tin cup and sat beside her among the ballast bags, and the coat lay safe between them, and the *Vera* pushed north and west through the winter sea.

"Tell me something," Ivo said, after a while. "The code-master's rules. You memorized them all. Trust no one, verify everything, leave no trace. Which one do you break first, in the new shop?"

Hazel blew on her tea and considered it properly, the way she considered everything.

"None of them," she said. "But I'm adding a new rule at the top. Rule zero." She smiled into the steam. "*Feed the courier.*"

Outside, the gulls wheeled and cried over waters that belonged, for the moment at least, to no occupying army at all. And far to the east, in a border station hung with the wrong flags, a uniform case was being carried down from a train by a tired diplomat who did not yet know what he was carrying — only that it was heavy, and that he had promised a seamstress to deliver it intact.

He would keep his promise. The presses were waiting.

So, though neither of them knew it yet, was the spring.
