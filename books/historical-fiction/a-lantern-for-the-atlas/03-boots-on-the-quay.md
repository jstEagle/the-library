## 3. Boots on the Quay

The gulls woke him before the drums did.

Lior Oyelaran had lived above the harbor long enough to know its every voice — the groan of hulls against pilings, the fishwives' dawn argument, the bell on the customs house that rang only when a ship ran aground. He knew which sounds meant weather and which meant money and which meant nothing at all. So when he rose from his narrow bed in the room behind the studio, still smelling of last night's ink, and heard boots — not scattered, not drunken, but *unified*, hundreds of them striking the cobbles in a single rhythm — he stood at the window for a full minute before he allowed himself to understand what he was hearing.

They came up from the naval yard along the quay road, four abreast, gray-green in the light that had not yet decided to be morning. Their column filled the street the way water fills a channel, without hurry, without any doubt that it belonged there. Behind them came supply wagons, their canvas covers lashed tight, and behind those a staff car with mud to its axles that could not have come from Vellamare's clean streets. On the car's fender, wrapped around the grille like a scarf, hung a banner of black and iron-red.

Lior counted. It was an old habit; Aldric had taught it to him without ever meaning to. *A mapmaker who cannot count windows is no mapmaker at all,* the old man used to say, tapping Lior's knuckles with a ruler whenever the boy sketched a terrace with one door too many or too few. So Lior counted: three platoons, perhaps a hundred and forty men visible, more beyond the bend by the salt warehouses. Two officers mounted. One clerk's cart, and the sight of it did something worse to him than the rifles did — rifles were honest things; a clerk's cart was a promise of paperwork.

He watched them take the town.

That is the phrase that would stay with him afterward, though he did not yet know it: not *invade*, not *occupy*, but *take*, the way one takes possession of a house left empty, or a ledger left open. The soldiers posted men at the harbor mouth and at each of the three streets that climbed from the quay into the old town. They did not point their weapons at anyone. There was no one awake enough to point them at, except the fishermen already out on the water, and the soldiers ignored the boats entirely, which frightened Lior more than any bayonet might have. A regime that ignores your fishing fleet has already decided what your fishing fleet is worth.

By full light they had papered the customs house, the guildhall doors, the fountain post in Fishmarket Square, and the shuttered face of the old theater. Lior went down among the first of the neighbors to gather, because a man who stays at his window alone is a man who has already admitted something to himself. He pulled on the coat with the ink-stained cuffs — he never owned a coat without ink-stained cuffs; Aldric used to say you could read his apprentices' histories off their sleeves like tide tables — and he joined the crowd at the fountain post with his hands in his pockets and his face arranged into the expression he had practiced for twenty years: attentive, mild, slightly dull.

*Read everything. Remember everything. Agree with nothing.* Aldric's third lesson, delivered the week after the soldiers of the previous regime carried him away. Lior had repeated it to himself so often it had worn smooth as a river stone, and he found it now beneath his fear, the way a hand finds a familiar banister in the dark.

The order was pasted in three layers, official script over official script, but the newest layer was crisp and the seal at its foot was fresh wax, and so Lior read it as it was meant to be read — slowly, twice, while the man beside him moved his lips through the words.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL MILITARY ADMINISTRATION OF THE COASTAL DISTRICT:

1. The port city of Vellamare, its harbor, quays, warehouses, workshops, and all structures therein, are hereby requisitioned to the service of the State.

2. All civic records, guild registers, commercial ledgers, parish rolls, and documents of residence are declared property of the State and shall be surrendered within seventy-two hours for audit.

3. All residents shall present themselves for registration, together with all papers of identity, ownership, and status, at the Guildhall, beginning tomorrow at the eighth hour.

4. Movement beyond the harbor chain is prohibited without written permit.

5. Non-compliance will be treated as sabotage against the war economy of the State.

Lior read the fifth clause twice more than the others. Around him the square filled with the particular silence of people reading the same terrible arithmetic: *seventy-two hours; all ledgers; all residents.* Old Ysolt from the rope-walk said aloud what everyone was thinking, in the flat voice she used for announcing deaths. "Every family name in Vellamare is written down somewhere. And now they want the books."

"They've always wanted the books," said her son. "It's the only thing they've ever wanted."

"Hush," said Ysolt, and looked at the soldiers at the corner, and hushed her own son with a hand on his arm, and the crowd absorbed the exchange and went back to reading.

Lior said nothing at all. Twenty years of saying nothing at all had built him a kind of architecture, and he retreated into it now: the studio above, the atlas on its bench, the final coastline inked not nine days ago, the margins he had only begun to suspect held more than sea-marks. He thought about the word *audit*. He thought about clause two the way a surgeon thinks about a wound he has just discovered under clean bandages. Every ledger in Vellamare. The guild register. The harbor master's rolls. The parish books with their baptisms and marriages and burials, three generations deep. Names, dates, debts, inheritances — the whole sedimentary record of a town that had spent centuries quietly helping people disappear seaward.

And somewhere beneath his floorboards — not yet, not this morning, but soon, very soon — an atlas whose margins remembered every soul Aldric Vane had ever smuggled out by night.

He turned for home and nearly walked into Tomas.

Tomas Requena kept the chandlery two doors down from the studio, a broad, cheerful man with sawdust permanently in his beard and a voice calibrated for shouting across water. He had known Lior since boyhood; he had carried Aldric's coffin with four other neighbors when the regime of that era finally returned what was left of the old mapmaker, and he had never once asked Lior what the old man had died refusing to give up. That restraint was the truest friendship Lior had ever been shown, and he repaid it now by catching Tomas's sleeve a half-second too late.

"Read it," Tomas said, jerking his chin at the fountain post. His voice was already too loud. "Go on. Read me the part where my chandlery belongs to them. Read me the part where my father's name — my *father's*, who built that shop plank by plank in 'ninety-one — goes into some clerk's cart down there." He pointed at the staff car, at the clerk's cart behind it, at the whole gray-green machinery of the morning. "Seventy-two hours to hand over every book I own. Books! Since when does a chandler keep dangerous books?"

"Since always," Lior said quietly. "Come inside, Tomas. Have coffee."

"Coffee!" But he let himself be turned, let himself be steered toward the studio door, because underneath the outrage he was frightened — Lior could feel it in the tremor of the arm under his hand, a fine constant vibration like a struck string that would not finish dying away. "You're calm," Tomas said as they climbed the stairs. "Twenty years you've been calm. Don't you ever get tired of being calm?"

"Constantly."

"Then say something. Say it loud, where they can hear it. Half the trouble in this town is that everyone learned to whisper from your master's funeral onward, and look what whispering bought us. Look what's marching up our quay."

In the studio, with the shutters half-closed against the new day, Lior measured coffee into the pot with steady hands and did not answer, and the not-answering made Tomas worse.

"I'm not ashamed of it," Tomas announced to the rafters. "My grandfather helped row people out. Everyone's grandfather helped row people out; it's the one thing Vellamare was ever good at. And if some clerk in a gray-green coat wants to read that out of my ledger — my *sales ledger*, God help us, half of it's tar prices — then let him. Let them come and read it. Let them stand in my shop and hear me say—"

"Tomas." Lior set the coffee down. He heard his own voice come out low and even, the voice he used on difficult clients, the voice Aldric had used on him once, only once, the night before the arrest. "The soldiers outside are not listening to you. The clerk in the cart is. Everything shouted in this town for the next seventy-two hours will end up transcribed in somebody's neat hand, with your name at the top of the page. So sit. Drink. Be loud later, when loud costs less."

For a moment he thought it had worked. Tomas sat. Tomas drank. And then, because Tomas had carried a coffin at twenty-three and had never entirely forgiven himself for how little the carrying accomplished, he put the cup down with great gentleness and said, "No," and stood, and went back down the stairs into the square, and raised the voice calibrated for shouting across water.

Lior reached the window in time to see most of it.

Tomas planted himself in front of the fountain post, in front of the layered orders, and addressed the soldiers at the corner first, then the growing crowd, then the town entire. He spoke of his grandfather and the winter nights and the boats that went out darkened. He spoke of the chandlery and his father's planks. He spoke well — that was the horror of it; he spoke better than Lior had ever heard him, and every sentence landed on the cobbles like a coin too valuable to pick up. The crowd grew. Someone began to cheer. An officer detached himself from the corner with the unhurried air of a man crossing a room to close a window against rain.

What happened next took less time than the telling of it. Two soldiers took Tomas's arms. A third gathered the man's cap from where it fell. Tomas did not struggle — that, too, was horrible; he simply stopped speaking mid-word, as though someone had lifted the needle from a record, and turned his head once toward the crowd, toward the window where Lior stood, though of course he could not have seen Lior through the half-closed shutters, and yet Lior felt the look pass over him like the shadow of a bird.

They put him in the clerk's cart.

The crowd stayed exactly where it was and became, collectively, extremely interested in the fountain. Ysolt studied the order again as if she had found an error in it. Her son examined his boot heels. In less than a minute the square contained no witnesses at all, only citizens going about their morning, and Lior understood, watching from behind the slats, that he was looking at twenty years of his own life reflected back at him — the practiced mildness, the arranged face, the whole architecture of silence — and that it had never once been courage. It had been contagion. Aldric had caught it, and passed it to him, and now the whole town had it in the blood, this genius for looking elsewhere, and the men in gray-green coats had marched five hundred miles to harvest precisely that.

He stayed at the window until the cart rolled away up the coast road, until the drums faded, until the gulls reclaimed the quay. Then he crossed the studio to the bench where the atlas lay closed under its linen cloth, and he stood over it without touching it, the way one stands over a sleeping child one is about to wake into bad news.

*All records. All ledgers. Seventy-two hours.*

His master's handwriting was in that book. His master's secret, whatever it was, was in that book — and Lior's own hand was in it too, twenty years of coastlines and soundings, the last coastline finished nine days ago by a fool who had believed the world would grant him one quiet season to admire a finished thing.

Below, in the square, a soldier hammered a nail into the fountain post and pinned up a second notice, smaller, in red-bordered stock. Lior could not read it from the window. He would go down and read it, of course; he would read it slowly, twice, with his face arranged. But he already knew its shape. Notices like that always had the same shape: *registration begins tomorrow at the eighth hour. Bring your papers. Bring your books.*

He blew out the lamp he had never lit, sat down at the bench, and lifted the linen cloth from the atlas — because whatever the next seventy-two hours demanded, it would demand it of a man who knew exactly what he was trying to save.
