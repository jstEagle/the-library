## 4. The Ledger of Names

The soldiers came for the ledger on the third day, and they came with a clerk.

Lior had imagined many things since the boots first rang out over the cobbles at dawn — imagined bayonets through the studio door, imagined fire, imagined himself dragged into the street the way Tomas had been dragged. What he had not imagined was a thin man in a grey officer's coat carrying a leather portfolio and a bottle of iron-gall ink, as though he had come to Vellamare to do accounts.

In a sense, he had.

Captain Reiss — the name went before him like a smell — arrived at the Cartographers' Guild a little after nine, when the light through the harbor fog was still the color of pewter. Two soldiers flanked him with rifles slung casually, the way a farmer might carry a scythe between rows. Reiss himself carried nothing but the portfolio and an expression of mild, professional patience, the patience of a man who knew that everything in the world would eventually be sorted, filed, and stamped, and who could afford to wait.

"The guild ledger," he said, without greeting. He spoke the coastal dialect with an inland flatness, each vowel pressed flat under something heavier. "All registers of residence, trade licenses, property rolls, and family records. You are Master Oyelaran? The guild's senior member?"

"Acting master," Lior said. "Since Aldric Vane's death."

"Yes." Reiss opened his portfolio and made a small note. The pen scratch was very fine, very precise. Lior found himself watching it the way one watches a spider descend. "The atlas is also state property, I am told. Charts, surveys, coastal drawings. Where are these kept?"

"Gone," Lior said, and heard his own voice come out level, unhurried, the voice of a man discussing weather. Twenty years of practice lived in that voice. "The old master sold most of it before he died. The rest was scattered among buyers abroad. You'd have to write to three countries."

Reiss looked up from his notes. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a fish in deep water, and they rested on Lior with no particular suspicion and no particular trust — simply inventory. "You will make me a list of those buyers."

"I can try. My memory of correspondence twenty years old is imperfect."

"Try anyway." He closed the portfolio with a soft clap. "The ledger, please."

* * *

It sat where it had always sat: on the oak stand beneath the guild's great window, a book so old its spine had been re-sewn twice, its boards recovered in green leather gone black at the corners from two generations of salt-damp hands. Every family in Vellamare lived inside it. Not their stories — the ledger did not trouble itself with stories — but their names, their trades, their births and marriages and debts, entered in the careful hands of successive guild secretaries going back past living memory. Lior's own name was in it, entered by Aldric's predecessor in a year before Lior's father was born: *Oyelaran, L., apprentice to A. Vane, mapmaker, lodgings above the shop, unmarried.*

Aldric's name was there too. *Vane, A., master cartographer.* And beneath it, in Aldric's own hand added years later in the status column, in that small dry script Lior knew better than his own heartbeat: *no heirs. The work is the heir.*

He had read that line a thousand times. He read it now as the soldier lifted the book off its stand, and something in his chest went tight and cold, because he understood — in the way one understands a wave has broken only after the water is already over one's boots — that the work was not the heir anymore. The work was evidence. The names were not memory anymore. They were inventory.

"Careful," Lior heard himself say. "The binding is older than the town charter."

The soldier did not care. Captain Reiss cared slightly more, or rather, he cared about condition in the way of a man assessing an asset. He turned the ledger over, weighed it, opened it to the middle and ran one finger down a column of names without appearing to read any of them.

"Good," he said. "Complete?"

"Every family. As required by the guild charter."

"Then every family is now known to us." He said it without triumph, without cruelty, even. That was the worst of it, Lior thought later, lying awake in the dark above the shop. There was no cruelty in it at all. It was said the way a mason says *then every wall is measured*. The horror of it was that it was true, and had always been true, and had simply been harmless until someone decided to use it.

They carried the ledger out into the street. Lior followed, because no one told him not to, and stood on the guild steps and watched.

* * *

They had set up tables in the market square. Three trestle tables under a canvas awning, and behind them, clerks — not soldiers, actual clerks, brought inland men with ink-stained cuffs and sleeve garters, men who might have been doing this same work in some customs house in the capital all their lives and had merely been transferred, the way one transfers a filing cabinet. The requisitioned ledger lay open before the head clerk. Beside it, a fresh register, new paper, army-issue, with columns already ruled in.

Lior stood at the edge of the square among the other townspeople, who had gathered the way people gather at a drowning — too far back to help, too ashamed to leave.

They called the families forward by district. Harborside first.

"Ferrand, Odile. Smuggler — the word used was *maritime transporter*, unlicensed — vessel Corvane, one hull, condition fair."

Odile herself stood in the crowd maybe ten feet from him, arms folded, face unreadable as driftwood. Lior did not look at her. She did not look at him. But he felt the stillness of her, the particular stillness of a woman memorizing the layout of a room she intends to rob.

"Pellam, Anselm. Fisherman. Boat confiscated. Status—" The clerk paused, consulted a list that had come from somewhere else, from someone who had never seen Anselm Pellam's face, and read out a word. Lior didn't catch it at first. Then he did, and it went through him like a fishhook.

*Restricted.*

Anselm stepped forward. "Restricted from what?" he asked, reasonably enough, the way a man asks which net tore. "I've fished this water thirty years."

"Next," said the clerk.

"Restricted from what? My wife is buried up at Saint-Aubin, my daughters—"

Two soldiers moved toward him, unhurried, and Anselm's voice climbed, and Lior watched the arithmetic of the thing happen on the clerk's face — not anger, not fear, just recalculation, one entry amended in real time. The pen scratched. Whatever column Anselm had been in, he was moved to another, and the moving of him took less than four seconds and cost the clerk no visible effort at all.

They led Anselm away to be held for questioning about his tone. His daughters stood in the crowd with their hands over their mouths.

That was how the morning went. Name after name after name. Lior knew them all. That was the thing that ground against him like sand in a joint — he knew them all, knew which of them had coughs, whose husbands were dead, which children were promised to which trades, and here it all came apart into columns. *Permitted to remain. Restricted. Under review.* Three columns, ruled neat and straight, and human lives decanted into them like grain graded at market, some for milling and some, presumably, for burning because it would not keep.

A woman ahead of him — Madame Cousse, the rope-walker's widow, seventy if she was a day — was sorted into *Under review* because her late husband's brother had once sailed for the northern ports and no one could establish whether letters had passed between them. Letters. The clerk asked her if she had received letters. She said she received the almanac. The pen paused; then she too was led aside, gently, almost courteously, to sit on a bench under guard while her fate was referred upward to someone who had also never seen her face.

Lior stood in the grey light with his fists in his pockets and understood that he was watching the town die without a single drop of blood being spilled. This was how it was done now. Not fire — fire was crude, fire left ashes and martyrs and songs. This was erasure by column. A name moved from one side of a ruled line to another, and a family's whole horizon changed, and the hand that held the pen never trembled because the hand had nothing personal in it at all. It was worse than hatred. Hatred at least saw you.

"Oyelaran, Lior," the clerk called, near midday. "Mapmaker. Skilled classification pending."

Skilled classification pending. Lior walked forward when beckoned, gave his name again, his age, his trade, answered four questions about the guild's holdings with the truth carefully trimmed around its edges, and watched his own self become a line of entries in a stranger's handwriting. Somewhere in that fresh register, *Lior Oyelaran* ceased to be a man who kept silences and became six words in a column. He felt the strange vertigo of it — grief and relief tangled together. The line did not know about the attic. The line did not know what he had spent twenty years learning to hide. A line could not suspect. A line was safe precisely because it was empty.

He was dismissed. He walked home through streets gone quiet as held breath.

* * *

That night he took up the floorboards.

The studio above the shop had been built in a hurry a century ago and repaired ever since by men who preferred patching to rebuilding, and beneath the section where Aldric's drafting table had always stood there was a cavity between the joists — dry, dark, smelling of cedar shavings and old glue — that Aldric himself had shown Lior once, years ago, with a look that had needed no translation. *There are things a mapmaker keeps that are not maps,* the old man had said, or had seemed to say; Lior could no longer be sure he hadn't invented the words afterward, the way one invents a dead friend's last advice. Either way, the cavity existed, and Aldric had used it, and Lior had kept the habit alive without quite admitting to himself what he stored there or why.

Now he admitted it.

He worked by candlelight with the shutters latched, though the curfew patrol passed twice below and he sat motionless both times with his heart going like a gull in a storm. The atlas came down from its shelf wrapped in its linen cloth. Twenty years of his life. Aldric's coastlines, Aldric's soundings, Aldric's tide-tables copied from logs now burned or scattered — and beneath the ink, in margins thin as breath, the code Lior had only begun to understand, the record of everyone the old man had ever smuggled out to sea. If Reiss found it, the code could be broken by anyone patient enough; codes always could. But first Reiss would have to find it, and Reiss believed the atlas was scattered across three countries, because Lior had told him so with a steady voice and a face trained by two decades of funerals, taxes, and hard winters to show nothing.

He wrapped the atlas in oilcloth, then in the linen again, then bound it with cord the way one binds a parcel of ordinary charts. He lifted the board — it came up easier than he remembered, as though the house itself had been waiting — and laid the parcel in the cavity among the cedar shavings, on top of the small tin box that held what remained of Aldric's private papers. Then the board went back, and a rug went over the board, and the drafting table went onto the rug, and Lior sat down at the table and put his palms flat on its surface as though taking an oath.

Below him, in the shop, the great press stood silent. Out in the square, he knew, the clerks were probably still working by lamplight, feeding the town through their three columns. *Permitted. Restricted. Review.* Somewhere east, trains were already being scheduled for the third column; he did not know this yet, but some animal layer of him felt it coming, the way the harbor felt a storm two days out, in the ache of ropes and the behavior of the gulls.

One thought stayed with him as he pinched out the candle, and it was neither noble nor comforting. It was simply this: a ledger is only paper, and paper can be forged, altered, drowned, burned, or rewritten by anyone with a steady hand. The regime had confiscated the town's names believing that possession was power.

But Lior Oyelaran had spent twenty years learning what ink could be made to say.

He lay down in the dark above the hidden atlas and did not sleep, and toward morning he began, without deciding to, to plan.
