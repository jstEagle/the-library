## 5. Sister's Daughter

The coach from Harrowmere took eleven days, and for nine of them Maren Oyelaran Vasseur had been rehearsing what she would say to her brother.

Not the greeting — the greeting was easy enough to imagine, a stiff embrace on the quay or in the doorway of the map shop, his hands ink-stained as ever, his eyes going first to Isolde and then, reluctantly, to her. She had rehearsed the greeting only once, on the second night, when the coach had stopped at a relay station and she had watched her daughter sleep sitting upright against the window with ash still ground into the creases of her knuckles from the burning.

No — what Maren rehearsed was the quarrel. The old one. The one that had opened between them like a crack in ice twenty years ago and never properly closed, only widened by winters of letters neither wrote.

*You could have warned me,* she would say. *You knew what was coming. You always knew — he told you everything, didn't he, your precious master? You sat up there in your attic with your coastlines and your compasses while the rest of us stood in the market square listening to proclamations, and you said nothing.*

And Lior — she knew him well enough, even now, even after all this silence — Lior would look at her with those patient, maddening eyes of his and say nothing back. That was his answer to everything. Silence. Twenty years of it. Their father had called it steadiness; their mother had called it wisdom; Maren had come to call it cowardice dressed in careful hand-lettering.

She watched the road unspool below the coach window as they came down out of the hills toward the coast. The land changed color as it flattened — the dry gold of the inland provinces giving way to a deeper green, then to the gray-green salt scrub of the shore country. And then, past a last shoulder of pine, there it was: Vellamare, cupped in its bay like water held in two hands, the harbor crowded even at this distance with masts, the town climbing its hillside in tiers of pale stone and red tile, and above it all the guildhall clock tower with its green copper dome gone black in places with age.

Isolde woke when the road began to descend. She did not stretch or yawn the way children do; fourteen-year-olds who have burned their papers do not waste movements on comfort. She simply opened her eyes and looked at the town with an expression Maren could not read, and after a moment she asked, quietly, "Will he know us?"

"He'll know you." Maren heard how her own voice caught on it. "He hasn't seen you since you were four."

"Then will he *recognize* me?"

Maren looked at her daughter — the long face, already losing its childhood roundness; the dark brows that were pure Oyelaran; the watchfulness that was not inherited from anyone but grown in her like a pearl grows around grit. "Yes," Maren said. "He'll recognize himself in you. That's worse."

Isolde almost smiled. It was the first thing resembling a smile in nine days, and Maren treasured it and hated herself for the smallness of what she could give her daughter now — a joke, a view of a harbor, a brother's house that might not even be safe.

Because that was the truth she had not put into words yet, not even to herself, not fully: she did not know if Vellamare was refuge or trap. Inland, the provinces had been falling province by province, each new decree arriving stamped and officious and slightly worse than the last — travel permits, work registrations, the lists, always the lists. Harrowmere had been slow about it, complacent, the way a body is slow to feel frostbite. But slow was not the same as safe. Slow was just a longer fuse. And when the men in gray had come to the tannery district asking for Edouard Vasseur by name — when Edouard had kissed her forehead in the kitchen and walked out to meet them so they wouldn't come inside — Maren had understood that the fuse was lit, and that the coast, whatever else it was, at least had ships.

So she had burned their papers. Both sets, hers and Isolde's, in the iron stove at three in the morning, feeding them in piece by piece so no single flame would flare too high against the window. Birth certificates. Marriage lines. The little leather folder with Isolde's school marks. Her own mother's letters, because the letters bore the family name and the family name was becoming a weight rather than a gift. The paper curled and went orange and then black, and Isolde had stood beside her in her nightgown holding the poker, and neither of them had cried, and afterward the whole house had smelled faintly of burnt ink for days, and Maren had thought: *this is what my brother's whole life smells like.*

They traveled without documents. It should have been impossible. It was nearly impossible. They paid a carter, then a fish-dealer, then a woman who ran a laundry van between towns, riding among bundles of other people's linens, and twice they hid in root cellars while inspections passed overhead like weather. Maren learned in those nine days that fear has stages, the way grief does — first it is a storm, then it is a climate, and finally it is simply the air you breathe, unnoticed until you try to speak and find your voice is shaking.

Now, looking down at Vellamare, she breathed that air and tried to find her brother's face in her memory and found it strangely difficult. Twenty years. He had been twenty-two when she left, newly bound to Aldric Vane, full of the quiet certainty of someone who has found his one true thing. He had walked her to the coach with a parcel of dried figs and a drawing he'd made of the harbor as a wedding gift — the bay in ink so fine it looked woven, every mast accounted for — and she had thought, boarding, *he will visit, we will write.* And then the letters had thinned, and then stopped, and then the world had turned over twice, and here she was, coming down the hill with her dead husband's name sewn into her coat lining and her daughter's future folded into nothing but hope and a burned-paper smell she couldn't wash out of anything.

*You could have warned me,* she rehearsed again, silently, as the coach rattled through the gate in the old sea wall. But the words felt thinner now, this close. Words always did, at the moment of use.

* * *

They found the map shop by asking, which Maren hated doing — asking strangers things, being visible — but the streets of the lower town had been rearranged by decades she hadn't witnessed, and the shop was no longer where she remembered it near the fish market. A boy selling chestnuts directed them uphill, past the customs house, past a chandler's hung with coils of rope like sleeping animals, to a narrow street where the buildings leaned toward each other across the gap like old conspirators.

The shop was there. *VANE & OYELARAN, CHART-SELLERS AND MAPMAKERS,* said the sign, and beneath the lettering someone had painted, long ago and half worn away, a small compass rose with eight points. The windows were shuttered. Maren's heart performed a cold, quick maneuver before she saw why — a card wired behind the glass, handwritten: *GONE TO THE GUILDHALL ON REQUISITION BUSINESS. BACK BY EVENING. KNOCK LOUDLY.*

"Requisition business," Isolde read aloud, testing the word. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Maren said slowly, "that I may be right about everything."

But she knocked loudly anyway, and after a long minute there were footsteps, and the door opened on lamplight and on her brother.

He was forty-two and looked older, the way coastal men do, weathered around the eyes; his hair had gone iron-gray at the temples and his sleeves were rolled and spotted with ink, exactly as she had known they would be, exactly as they had been at twenty-two. For a moment he simply looked at her, and the lamplight moved, and twenty years fell away and then reassembled themselves differently, and Maren found that all her rehearsals had evaporated and left only the raw fact of him.

"Lior," she said.

"Maren." His voice was lower than she remembered. Then he looked past her, down, and his whole face changed — some door opening behind his eyes that she remembered from their childhood, the rare one, the one that made him beautiful. "Isolde."

"I'm told you're my uncle," Isolde said. Fourteen years old and standing straight as a mast. "I don't remember you."

"No," Lior said softly. "You wouldn't." He stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. Please. Quickly."

The *quickly* snagged on Maren like a fishhook. She followed him inside, and Isolde pulled the door shut behind them, and in the close warm smell of paper and lamp oil and sea salt, Maren said, "Why quickly?"

Lior busied himself lighting a second lamp, and she watched his hands — steady hands, always steady — and noticed that they were not entirely steady tonight. "There are soldiers in the town," he said. "They came yesterday at dawn. They've posted requisition orders on the customs house. The town, the records, the ledgers. Everything is to be counted." He set the lamp down. "Everything and everyone."

The word *everyone* landed in Maren's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it struck bottom.

"So we've run from the fire into the smoke," she said.

"I don't know what you've run into yet." He looked at her more closely then, really looked, taking inventory the way he must take inventory of everything — she could see it happen, the draftsman's eye cataloguing the wear on her shoes, the burn-scarred edge of Isolde's cuff, the way Maren kept her left arm curved slightly inward, guarding the coat lining where a name was sewn. Something in his expression shut like a drawer. "Your papers—"

"Burned," Maren said. Flatly. Daring him to flinch. "Nine days ago. All of them. We came across the provinces in a laundry van and slept in cellars, and we have nothing that says who we are, and now you tell me the army is collecting names." She heard her voice rise and could not stop it; nine days of held breath were escaping all at once. "So you tell me, brother. You're the one who knows things. You've always known things. What happens to people with no names in a town where the army is counting?"

The silence that followed was the old silence, the family silence, and it fit between them like a key in a lock, and Maren hated it and was grateful for it in equal measure.

"There's a room upstairs," Lior said finally. "Behind the chart drawers. You can't see it unless you know. Isolde—" He hesitated, and something crossed his face, some calculation or memory. "Isolde, you'll stay close to the house for now. Both of you. The soldiers aren't stopping people yet, but they will be."

"You mean they're not sorting us yet," Maren said. "But they will be."

"I mean I need a day to learn what's happening. Odile Ferrand runs boats out of the east quay; if it comes to leaving, there are ways—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man steps back from a ledge.

"Ways," Maren repeated. "Of course. Ways. There were ways twenty years ago too, weren't there? When your master was smuggling people out in the bottoms of chart crates. Everyone knew except me. Even Father knew, I think, toward the end. Everyone knew but the sister who'd married inland and gotten comfortable." She was saying too much; she could hear herself saying too much, the resentment coming up like floodwater through floorboards. "Twenty years, Lior. Twenty years and maybe six letters. I buried Mother with a letter from you that was four sentences long. I read it at the graveside. Four sentences. I used to wonder if you were ashamed of us — the tanner's daughter's life, the ordinary husband, the child. Or if you'd simply forgotten we existed up there with your maps."

Lior received all of it without moving. That was the worst part. He stood in the lamplight with his ink-stained hands loose at his sides and let her empty twenty years onto the floor of his shop, and when she finished, breathing hard, he said only:

"Aldric died because he wouldn't give up names."

The sentence dropped the temperature of the room.

"The regime before this one wanted his records," Lior went on, quietly. "The routes, the manifests, the families who'd gone out by night fishing boats. He refused. They took him away and he didn't come back, and the atlas survived because I hid it and said nothing, and I have spent twenty years finishing it and saying nothing, and I will tell you honestly, Maren, I don't know yet whether the saying-nothing has protected anyone or only myself. I've asked myself that question every night for twenty years and I still don't know." His eyes were wet, or seemed so in the lamplight; with Lior you could never be certain, the man kept his own weather. "But you are here now, and Isolde is here now, and whatever comes through that door, it goes through me first. That's the only sentence I have that's longer than four words. It's yours."

Maren stood in the paper-smelling dimness with her heart hammering and her grievances suddenly embarrassed, like actors whose play has been interrupted by news of a war. She wanted to hold onto the anger — anger was familiar, anger was a coat she'd worn so long it fit better than any dress — but her daughter was watching her from beside the chart drawers with those dark Oyelaran brows, and the coat suddenly weighed a great deal.

"We haven't eaten since morning," she said, which was not forgiveness, but was at least a change of subject, and they both knew it, and let it stand.

* * *

Later — after bread and hard cheese and sardines packed in oil, after Isolde had fallen asleep in a chair drawn up by the cold stove and been carried, deadweight, up the hidden stair — Maren lay awake in the narrow room behind the chart drawers and listened to the harbor sounds through the wall: the hawser-creak, the lap of water, a bell buoy somewhere out in the dark keeping its idiot faithful time.

The room was barely a room. Shelves of rolled charts pressed close on two sides; a cot and a washstand filled the rest. But there was a window, high and narrow, and by craning on the pillow she could see a slice of the night sky and the very top of the guildhall clock tower, and once, briefly, the glow of lanterns moving along the quay in a pattern that had no purpose she could recognize — patrols, probably. Counting.

She thought about Edouard walking out to meet the men in gray. She thought about how he had straightened his collar first, because Edouard straightened his collar for everything, funerals and festivals and arrest. She thought: *he would have liked Lior. Two quiet men. They'd have sat in this shop for hours saying nothing together and enjoying it enormously.* Grief arrived then, punctual as the bell buoy, and she let it, because there was no one to see and because refusing it cost more than receiving it.

And underneath the grief, quieter, a thought she had been circling for nine days finally landed:

*You brought her to the water because the water leaves. That was the whole plan. Burn everything and bring her to the place with ships.* But plans made in terror were like charts drawn from memory — mostly right in the large features, fatally wrong in the details, and the details were where people drowned.

She must have drifted, because she woke to lamplight under the door and voices below — low, urgent, her brother's and another voice, a woman's, roughened like rope. Maren rose without putting her boots on and eased the door open a finger's width.

In the shop below, Lior stood with a stranger: a broad-shouldered woman in a salt-cracked oilskin, hair cut short and practical, a face that had decided long ago what it thought of the world. Charts were spread on the counter between them, and money — no, Maren corrected herself, looking harder — the woman was pushing something across the counter that was not quite money, and Lior's face wore an expression Maren recognized from their childhood: the look he got when the arithmetic was bad.

"—three berths," the woman was saying, "and not one more, and not one day later. Morning tide. After that, Corvane sails light or not at all, because I don't sail into a net."

"And the price?" Lior asked.

The woman smiled without any pleasure in it whatsoever. "Oh, Vane's boy. You know what the price is. It's the thing you love best."

Maren drew back from the door, her heart beating hard and fast, and stood in the dark of the hidden room listening to her daughter breathe.

Morning tide. Three berths. She looked at the sleeping shape of Isolde in the cot, and at the lamplight leaking under the door, and she understood with perfect clarity that the arithmetic her brother was doing downstairs had her family's name in it — all of their names, in columns she could not yet see.

She sat down on the cot's edge in the dark to wait for dawn, and to decide, before anyone decided for her, what she was willing to spend.
