## 6. Columns and Categories

The summons came on the third morning after the boots, delivered not by a soldier but by a boy in a grey coat too big for him, who thrust a printed slip into Lior's hand at the door and walked on without meeting his eyes. The paper was cheap, the ink already feathering at the edges where the harbor damp had found it. *All persons residing within the district of Vellamare will present themselves for registration at the Guildhall between the hours of eight and four. Failure to comply constitutes obstruction of state requisition.* Below that, a stamp — a wheel inside a wreath, pressed slightly crooked, as if whoever had carved it had been hurried or careless or both.

Lior stood in the doorway a long while, the slip trembling faintly in his hand, and watched the town move toward the guildhall like water finding a drain. Old women with shawls pinned tight. The fishmonger's boys carrying their father's ledgers wrapped in sacking. A family from Tanner's Row with everything they owned in two baskets, though no one had told them to bring anything. They went because the paper said to go, and because twenty generations of living under someone else's law had taught Vellamare that you survived by appearing at the appointed hour with your hands visible and your mouth shut.

He had learned that lesson twice over. Once from his mother, who had died with her silences intact. And once from Aldric Vane, who had taught him that a man could keep more than one silence at a time — the one that protected him, and the one that protected whatever he carried.

Lior locked the studio, tucked the key into the false seam of his boot heel where it had lived for years out of habit rather than fear, and joined the slow river of his neighbors flowing up Cooper's Lane.

* * *

The guildhall had been Vellamare's pride for two hundred years. Its stones had been laid by the first chartmakers' brotherhood; its great arched windows had been glazed with panes donated, so the story went, by every captain who had ever come home alive from the Teeth — the reef line that guarded the outer roads like a row of broken teeth indeed. In the hall's rafters hung the old banners of the pilot guilds, faded to ghosts of blue and gold. Lior had been presented there at nineteen, his apprentice's certificate signed before the assembled masters, Aldric's hand heavy and warm on his shoulder.

Now the banners were gone. In their place hung a single banner of black cloth bearing the wheel-and-wreath, and beneath the arched windows stood long trestle tables arranged in a horseshoe, each manned by a clerk with an army ledger open before him, each ledger ruled into columns as straight and merciless as gun barrels.

The hall smelled of wet wool, ink, and something else beneath — the sourness of several hundred people afraid in the same room, breathing the same air. It was a smell Lior would know again for the rest of his life, though he did not know it yet.

"Name," said the clerk at the second table, when Lior reached the head of the queue.

"Lior Oyelaran."

"Trade."

"Mapmaker. Draftsman." He hesitated half a breath. "Journeyman of the Cartographers' Guild."

The clerk's pen stopped. He was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the flat, incurious gaze of someone whose work required him never to look up, and now he looked up. "Guild?"

"The Vellamare Cartographers' Guild. Registered journeyman, twelve years certified. My master—" Lior stopped himself. Saying Aldric's name aloud felt like setting a lamp in a window for a sniper. "I was apprenticed to the guild's late master."

Something shifted behind the clerk's flatness. Not interest exactly — recognition, the way a man recognizes the shape of a tool he has been sent to find. He made a small mark in the margin of his ledger, a mark Lior could not read upside down, and said, "Stand aside. To the left. Someone will call you."

"To the left" was a stretch of bare floor near the base of the master's dais, where perhaps thirty people had already been set apart: the harbormaster's assistants, two pilots, the keeper of the tide tables, an old woman who turned out to be the guild's registrar of seals. The useful ones, Lior thought. The ones whose hands knew things. He watched the columns swallow his neighbors — name, household, trade, status — and understood, with a nausea that rose slowly from his stomach to his throat, what the columns were for.

Every person in Vellamare was becoming a line. And lines, he knew better than almost anyone alive, could be drawn, erased, redrawn. Whoever held the pen held the town.

Across the hall he saw Tomas Rell being processed at the fourth table — Tomas, who had spent three days in custody for arguing with a sergeant, released overnight and summoned here like everyone else, his face bruised yellow-green along the jaw, his hands shaking so badly the clerk had to ask him twice to hold them flat on the table. Their eyes met across the room. Tomas looked away first. That frightened Lior more than the soldiers did. Tomas had argued with armed men in the street without blinking; now he could not hold a neighbor's glance.

* * *

They called him just past noon.

Not by the clerks. A captain descended from the dais — Captain Reiss, whom Lior had seen only once before, on the quay the morning the troops landed, a narrow man with spectacles and a voice so quiet the whole quay had strained to hear it. Reiss consulted a paper, then studied Lior's face with the particular attention of a man comparing an original against a copy.

"Oyelaran. Journeyman." He pronounced the guild title the way another man might say *suspect*. "You trained under Vane."

It was not a question, which meant someone had already given him the answer. Lior kept his face in the neutral arrangement he had practiced for twenty years — attentive, modest, empty. "Yes, Captain."

"Aldric Vane refused to surrender guild property to the previous administration. You'll know the story. Everyone in this town tells it like scripture." Reiss's tone suggested he found scripture tiresome. "Where are the guild's charts and records now?"

"Gone, Captain. When the old master died, most of the working collection passed to the Guild Conclave in the capital. What remained here — working drafts, practice sheets, spoiled plates — I sold off over the years. Paper keeps poorly this close to the water. Damp, mold, rats." He let a small, rueful shrug carry the rest: *you know how it is, you've seen the coast, things rot.*

Reiss regarded him for a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then: "You can draw."

"I can draw, Captain."

"Coastal surveys. Soundings, bearings, approaches. The army requires accurate charts of this shoreline within the month." Reiss said it the way he seemed to say everything — quietly, precisely, as though the words themselves were orders already executed. "You'll work here, in the map room they've established upstairs. You'll be paid from requisition funds. You will be fed." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Men with skills eat whether or not they cooperate. But cooperation is remembered, Oyelaran. So is the other thing."

Lior bowed his head. It was the safest motion available, and he hated how easily it came.

"You start tomorrow at seven." Reiss turned away, then paused, and added without looking back: "Vane was a fool. Talented men need not be."

* * *

That night Lior did not sleep. He sat in the attic studio with one candle lit low behind its tin shutter, listening to the garrison patrol pass below — their footsteps had a rhythm now, three nights running, and he had begun to measure time by them the way sailors measured it by bells.

On the bench before him lay the atlas.

He had taken it up intending to work — the final coastline still wanted its last pass of ink, the little archipelago south of the Salt Reach, and his hand needed work the way lungs needed air. But his hand would not steady. Every time he dipped the pen he saw instead the trestle tables, the columns, the ruled spaces waiting to be filled, and heard Reiss's quiet voice assigning him to the map room: *soundings, bearings, approaches.*

Approaches. The word sat in him like a swallowed hook. Approaches meant where ships could come in. Where ships could come in meant where ships could be caught, or driven onto the Teeth, or prevented from leaving at all. The charts he was being ordered to make were not maps. They were nets. And he was the one being handed the twine.

Aldric had died rather than put his knowledge into the hands of men like these. Twenty years ago — Lior had been twenty-four, furious, uncomprehending, convinced the old man's stubbornness was vanity. *It's only paper, Master. Ink and paper. Give them the charts, keep the guild.* And Aldric had looked at him with those pale, sea-worn eyes and said, in a voice worn down to gravel: *Boy, a coastline is never only a coastline. Draw it wrong and ships die honestly. Draw it true for the wrong hands and they die lying down.* Then he had burned what he could not hide, hidden what he could not burn, and waited, with terrible patience, for the knock that finally came.

Lior had inherited the patience. He had spent two decades believing he had escaped inheriting the choice. Now the choice had arrived anyway, wearing spectacles and speaking softly, offering him bread in exchange for the same betrayal.

He thought of the columns in the hall. Name, household, trade, status. He thought of how a line could be drawn — and how a line could be drawn *wrong*. Deliberately, invisibly wrong, in ways only a trained eye would ever find, and only if it knew where to look.

His own craft, turned inward against itself. It was a dangerous thought and a beautiful one, and he sat with it until the candle guttered, turning it over the way Aldric had taught him to turn a problem of survey: from every bearing, patiently, until the true position resolved.

There were ways. He knew there were ways. A sounding off by two fathoms in a channel no local pilot needed a chart to navigate. A bearing line drawn from a landmark that had crumbled into the sea a decade past. A shoal marked a cable's length east of truth — enough to ground a landing barge, not enough to catch any eye that didn't know these waters intimately. Errors indistinguishable from honest error, unless you had sailed these coasts yourself, and the men giving the orders had not; they trusted the instrument, and the instrument was him.

It was sabotage dressed as obedience. It was survival shaped like resistance. And it was also, he understood with cold clarity, a road that ran only one direction: once he began drawing false charts, he could never draw a true one for these men again, and if they discovered it — when they discovered it — there would be no explaining, no pleading, no column marked *cooperated*. There would be a different list entirely.

Somewhere below, the patrol passed again. Lior snuffed the candle and sat in the dark with the atlas under his palms, feeling through the vellum the ghost of Aldric's margins, all those coded marks recording every soul the old man had smuggled past exactly such men as these. The atlas had been waiting twenty years for a purpose. Perhaps this was the beginning of it.

Tomorrow at seven he would climb to the map room. He would sharpen his pencils and unroll the military surveys and become, to every watching eye, the cooperative journeyman. And in the quiet privacy of his own hand — in the pressure of a nib, the weight of a line, choices invisible to anyone who could not read a drawing the way Aldric had taught him to read one — he would begin.

He slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of columns, and woke to the summons of a whistle, and climbed the guildhall stairs into a room that had once been the pilots' reading chamber and was now hung with coastal grids, where Captain Reiss waited beside a table laid out with the army's existing charts — crude, outdated, drawn by men who had clearly never smelled the salt they were describing.

"These are inadequate," Reiss said, tapping the worst of them. "Yours will be better." He set a fresh sheet of the army's finest paper before Lior, and a pen, and a pot of ink black as tar. "Begin with the approaches to Vellamare harbor."

Lior took up the pen. His hand, he noted distantly, was perfectly steady — twenty years of practiced calm holding true even now, especially now. He touched the nib to the paper and drew the first line of the new chart: the coastline, true and clean and beautiful, exactly as he knew it.

And as he worked, keeping his eyes down and his face empty, he began — very quietly, one measurement at a time — to plan where the errors would go.
