## 7. A Berth Costs What It Costs

The Salt Cellar did not look like the kind of place where passages out of Vellamare were bought and sold. That was rather the point of it.

It sat wedged between a chandler's storehouse and a net-mender's shed on the lowest street of the fish quarter, a low-beamed tavern with a crooked sign of a barrel and a salt-cured cod nailed above the door for luck — or perhaps as a warning. Inside, the air was thick with tar smoke and fried onions, and the lamplight came through glass so old it turned everything the color of weak tea. Lior had not been inside since he was nineteen years old, when Aldric Vane had sent him there with a sealed packet and strict instructions not to speak to anyone but the woman behind the cask of dark rum at the back.

*You will know her by her hands,* the old man had said. *And if she is not there, come home, burn nothing, say nothing.*

She was there tonight.

Odile Ferrand sat alone at the corner table beneath the salted cod, as though the intervening twenty-three years had been a long afternoon and she had merely stepped out to check the tide. She was smaller than Lior remembered — or perhaps his memory had built her up into something mythic, the woman who could move a family across three hundred miles of hostile water in a herring boat without waking the customs men. Her hair was iron-grey now, cropped close as a sailor's, and her face was a map itself: deep channels around the mouth, weather-lines across the brow, two pale scars near the left ear that might have been knife-work or rigging-work or both. But her eyes were unchanged. They were the grey-green of the sea an hour before a storm, and they took in Lior from the doorway to the table in a single unhurried pass, the way a pilot reads water.

"You've grown," she said. "Sit down."

He sat. A girl of perhaps twelve brought him a cup of something hot that was either coffee or a lie about coffee, and melted away before he could thank her. Around them the tavern murmured its evening murmur — dice, gossip about the requisition orders, a man singing half a sea-song before his wife elbowed him quiet. Nobody looked at their table. In Vellamare, in this week above all weeks, nobody ever looked at the corner table under the cod.

"Twenty-three years," Lior said quietly. "I thought you'd be dead, or rich, or both."

"Dead, no. Rich, never — rich people get audited." Odile turned her cup slowly on the wood, not drinking. "I heard about Aldric. I'm sorry. He was the only honest man who ever paid me, which is a rarer thing than honesty."

"He paid you?"

"Twice a year, for eleven years. You didn't know?" Something flickered across her face — surprise, then a kind of rueful amusement at her own slip. "No. Of course you didn't. The old man kept his compartments separate the way a ship keeps its holds. Water in one, grain in another, and God help the man who drills through the wrong bulkhead."

Lior's hands tightened around the cup. Eleven years. Two payments a year. He had lived in that house, slept under those roof beams, mixed that man's inks, and had known nothing — known nothing the way one knows nothing in a town full of silences, until silence felt like the natural state of air.

"The atlas," he said. "He was moving people out. With the charts."

"With the charts, past the charts, because of the charts. Doesn't matter now." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register sailors use when the wind changes. "What matters is what's happening now, and how much of it you already understand. So let's not waste each other's time. You came looking for me. Say what you're looking for."

"Passage. Out. On the ship leaving on the morning tide."

"Corvane." She said the name flatly, testing it like bad coin. "Cargo ship, registered out of Marrow Bay, carrying salt fish and timber west. She takes on a few passengers when the price is right and the passengers are quiet. She sails with the dawn tide tomorrow morning, and after that—" Odile spread her hands, a small, terrible gesture encompassing the harbor, the gunboats riding at anchor beyond the mole, the new flags hanging limp over the customs house. "After that, there is no after that. Not for a while. Maybe not ever. The navy's closing the port to civilian traffic as soon as the census ledger is finished being processed. Everyone still in Vellamare when the Corvane sails belongs to the state, on paper and otherwise."

"How many berths?"

"How many do you need?"

"Three."

Odile did not answer immediately. She drank at last, a slow swallow, watching him over the rim of the cup. Outside, somewhere down the quay, boots struck stone in unison — a patrol passing — and the tavern's murmur dipped and rose again like water over a submerged rock.

"Three is possible," she said finally. "Three is expensive. And before we talk numbers, you should understand what you're buying, because there are people in this room right now who would pay double what you can pay and I'm telling them the same thing: the Corvane is not a ferry. She's a leaky forty-year-old tramp steamer with a captain who owes me more than he wants to and engines held together with prayer and wire. If anything goes wrong at the mole, if the harbor watch decides to board her on a whim, if the weather turns — my guarantees end at the gangplank. What I sell you is a place on the deck and a captain who has been paid to sail. What happens after that belongs to the sea and to whoever wins whatever war is coming. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Good. Then here are the numbers, and I want you to listen all the way through before your face does anything stupid."

She laid it out plainly, the way Aldric must have taught her to lay out a coastline: features first, hazards second. Three berths on the Corvane. One hundred and eighty crowns per head for the passage itself — that part was almost honest freight, she said, the captain's cut for risking his hull. Then the harbor fee, because nothing moved past the chain boom without the harbor master's palm being greased, and the harbor master had recently acquired superiors whose palms were wider. Then the paper fee — transit endorsements, exit stamps, the little inked permissions that turned three human beings from contraband into cargo. Paper, Odile said, had tripled in price since the soldiers arrived, as paper does in every country where it starts to matter more than people.

"Four hundred and ten crowns each," she said. "Twelve hundred and thirty for the three of you. Half now, half before boarding. And that's the friendship rate, Lior Oyelaran, priced against what I owed your master and what he paid me. Anyone else walks in here asking for three berths tomorrow, I tell them fifteen hundred and sleep fine."

Lior had already done the arithmetic. He had done it walking down through the fish quarter, doing it again and again hoping the number would shrink with repetition, the way a child checks a cupboard twice for bread. He owned the house on Lantern Row outright — Aldric had left it to him, along with the studio, the tools, the copperplate press, and twenty years of unfinished work. The house might bring four hundred crowns in a normal market. This was not a normal market; this was a market where everyone was selling and no one was buying, where soldiers set the prices of everything including departure. His savings — the tin box beneath the floorboards beside the atlas — held two hundred and sixty-four crowns, counted four times, the product of two decades of careful commissions and a lifetime of wanting nothing.

Even if he sold the house tonight — and who would buy? Who could buy? Every buyer in Vellamare was standing in the same queue for the same three ships —

Even then, even stripped to the walls, he did not have twelve hundred and thirty crowns. He did not have half of it.

"I have two hundred and sixty-four crowns," he said. "And a house no one will buy, and a craft that the army has just decided it owns the output of."

Odile nodded slowly, unsurprised, and he understood that she had known his arithmetic before he had, that she had probably priced the conversation before he walked through the door.

"Then we have a problem," she said. "Or rather, you have a problem, and I have a curiosity. Because here's what interests me, mapmaker. You're a draftsman. Good one — I've seen guild work with your master's corrections in the margins, and someone with a very steady hand has been finishing them. The army noticed too, I hear. They pulled you into the guildhall to draw their coasts." Her eyes narrowed a fraction, not unkindly. "A man in your position has options most of this town doesn't. Reiss feeds his draftsmen. Keeps them warm. Why does a man with a protected pencil need three berths off a smuggler's rust-bucket at criminal rates?"

Lior weighed the truth against the habit of twenty years. The habit said: reveal nothing, confirm nothing, let silence do the work. But silence, he was learning, had a price now too, and it was rising faster than paper.

"Because protected pencils get sharpened into other things," he said. "Because I've seen the columns in that ledger, Mistress Ferrand, and I know what the third column means, and I know my sister's name is going into it. Because a man who draws their coastlines for them this year draws them from a prison desk next year, once they've taken what they need." He paused. "And because my master spent eleven years paying you to move people out from under the last lot of men who liked lists. I'm told the trade runs in families. I'm hoping it runs in apprentices too."

For a long moment Odile Ferrand said nothing at all. The tavern noise washed around them. Somewhere behind the bar, a cork popped and a cheer went up over a card game. Under the table, Lior realized, his knee had been bouncing, and he stilled it.

Then she smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman confirming that a chart she'd been handed matched the water in front of her.

"Aldric trained you well and lied to you thoroughly," she said. "Both at once, which takes talent. Yes — the trade runs. And yes, I'll take your two hundred and sixty-four crowns, and yes, I'll put your names on my list for the Corvane, provisionally. Provisional, do you understand? Ink that can be washed out. Because the money doesn't close this bargain, and we both knew that before you sat down."

"What closes it?"

"That depends." She turned her cup again, and now she was choosing her words with visible care, a pilot feeling for the channel marker in fog. "There's a man. Abroad — across the strait, in a city where such things are still bought openly. A collector. Wealthy, obsessive, and sentimental in the way collectors are, which is to say he pays obscene sums for objects with stories attached. For months he's been asking after one object in particular. An atlas of the Vellamare coast. Complete, original, in the hand of Aldric Vane. He's offered — and I have this on good authority — passage for six, plus money, plus letters of credit that would spring a dozen families from this harbor. Six berths, Lior. For a book."

The bottom seemed to tilt gently beneath Lior's chair, the way the floor of the studio did sometimes when he stood up too fast after hours bent over the drafting table.

Six berths. Three for Maren, Isolde, and himself, with three to spare — three more names off Odile's list, three more families out from under the third column. All of it sitting in his attic wrapped in oilcloth, twenty years of his life lying in the dark between the joists, waiting to be traded like salt fish.

"It isn't finished," he heard himself say, and hated how thin his voice sounded. "The western approaches aren't surveyed to final scale. The soundings stop at Cape Merren. There are—"

"Lior." Odile's voice was gentle in a way that made it worse. "The man isn't buying a navigational instrument. He's buying a dead man's masterpiece and a living story, and he'll pay for the privilege of owning the incompleteness, because incompleteness means the hand that made it is gone. That's the whole economy of relics. Ask any church."

"And you'd take a commission on that."

"I'd take my commission on anything that floats. That's the business." She leaned back, and the lamplight caught the old scars at her ear. "But hear the rest, because the rest is the part that should worry you. The collector isn't the only one who knows the atlas exists. Reiss knows there was an atlas. It's in the old seizure warrants — the ones from the first regime, the file your master died refusing. When the army processes a town, they read its history first. Reiss will have read that Aldric Vane died with an unfinished coastal atlas unaccounted for, and men like Reiss do not leave 'unaccounted for' alone for long. Which means every day that book sits under your floorboards, it gets heavier. Right now it's worth six lives abroad. In a week it may be worth a firing squad at home. In two weeks it may be ash in the yard, worth nothing to anyone. The window is closing, mapmaker. It always closes."

Lior sat with it. Around them the tavern went on with its small human noises, and outside the boots passed again on the quay, and beyond the black windows the harbor lights of the gunboats burned steady and patient, the way lights burn on things that intend to stay.

Two hundred and sixty-four crowns. Twelve hundred and thirty crowns. Six berths for a book. A book that was also, in its margins, in its faint coded traceries of dots and dashes he had only begun to decipher, a record of every soul Aldric Vane had ever smuggled to freedom — a list that would hang a great many people, living and dead, if it fell into the wrong hands and was read correctly. A book that was a masterpiece, a cargo manifest, and a confession, all sewn into one binding.

His master had died rather than surrender it. And now the apprentice sat in a salt-stained tavern learning that surrendering it might be the only way to honor what it was for.

"When would you need it?" he asked.

"Money and atlas both, before midnight tomorrow. Berths confirmed by the second bell. Boarding at the coal dock, not the main quay, an hour before the tide." Odile pushed back her chair and stood, suddenly all brisk motion, a woman with a list longer than the harbor chain. At her full height she barely reached his shoulder, and yet for a moment she seemed to fill the whole low room. "You don't have to decide tonight. You have until midnight tomorrow. But decide alone, mapmaker, and decide fast, and don't tell anyone what the atlas is worth — not your sister, not the girl, not the priest, not me twice. A price spoken aloud in this town travels faster than you can row."

She dropped coins on the table for the coffee, paused, and looked down at him with something that was almost — almost — the expression Aldric used to wear when Lior got a coastline right on the third attempt.

"For what it's worth," Odile Ferrand said, "your master faced the same arithmetic once and solved it differently. He kept the atlas and paid with everything else. Maybe that was right for him. But he was an old man with an old book, and you—" her eyes flicked toward the door, toward the town above the fish quarter where a house waited with three names in it, "—you're not."

Then she was gone, out into the dark, and Lior sat alone under the salted cod with a cold cup and a number that refused to become smaller no matter how many times he divided it.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the sea wall, because the short ways were patrolled now and because he wanted — needed — a few minutes with the water before he climbed the stairs to the attic and took the oilcloth bundle down from between the joists.

The tide was turning. He could feel it in the slack water below the wall, that brief confused hour when the sea seems to hold its breath between decisions. Beyond the mole, the Corvane rode at her moorings, a blunt black shape with one working light, and beyond her the gunboats, and beyond them the open dark where, if everything was paid and everything held, a ship would turn west tomorrow with his niece aboard.

Two hundred and sixty-four crowns. A house. A life's work. Twenty years of a dead man's silences, bound in calfskin.

He stopped at the gap in the sea wall where the steps ran down to the low-tide rocks, and he stood there doing the arithmetic one final time, though the sum had not changed and would not change, because arithmetic was the one honest thing left in Vellamare.

The atlas could buy six berths. The atlas could also, in the wrong reading, kill every name written in its margins. And tomorrow morning he was expected in the guildhall, at Captain Reiss's own table, with his pens and his steady hand, drawing the coast for the men who would decide — column by column — who in this town was allowed to remain a person on paper.

Unless he saw, first, exactly which signatures and seals moved a departure permit. Unless he learned the machinery from the inside while his hands drew their charts.

Lior looked at the black water a moment longer, at the lanterns of the fishing boats coming in late, small and stubborn against the dark, and he thought: *my master paid with everything else.* 

He turned up Lantern Row toward the house and the attic and the oilcloth bundle, and began composing, in his head, the most carefully inaccurate maps of his life.
