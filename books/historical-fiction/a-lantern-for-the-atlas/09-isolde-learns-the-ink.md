## 9. Isolde Learns the Ink

The attic smelled of linseed oil and old cedar, and to Isolde it was the finest smell in the world.

She had been told, in the particular tone adults used when they meant *never*, that she was not to go up there. Her uncle had said it gently enough — he said most things gently — but Maren had repeated it with the flat finality of a woman who had burned her own papers and did not intend to burn her daughter's future on a child's curiosity. The attic was Lior's. The attic was work. The attic was where he disappeared for hours and came down with ink on his fingers and nothing in his eyes.

Which was, naturally, precisely why Isolde went up.

It was the third morning after their arrival in Vellamare, and the house was quiet in the way houses were quiet when everyone was pretending not to be afraid. Downstairs, her mother was mending a shirt that did not need mending, working the needle as if each stitch might stitch something larger back together. Uncle Lior had gone out before dawn — she had heard his boots on the stairs, careful even on steps he'd climbed for twenty years, as if the house itself were listening now. Soldiers listened. Everyone had learned to speak as if soldiers were listening, even when they weren't.

Especially when they weren't.

The attic door was not locked. It was only shut, and shut was a word Isolde had never much respected. She climbed the narrow stair on tiptoe anyway, out of some half-formed courtesy to the silence, and pushed the door with two fingers.

Light fell through the dormer window in a long dusty blade, catching motes that turned slowly, like snow that couldn't decide to fall. Slopes of roof pressed close on either side. And everywhere — everywhere — paper.

Reams of it stacked against the rafters. Rolls of it in ceramic jars like scrolls in a tomb. Sheets drying on lines strung beneath the beams, ghost-pale, translucent where the light touched them. A great table dominated the room's center, scarred and burnished by decades of elbows, its surface a palimpsest of knife-cuts and ink-stains and one deep scorch mark near the corner that Isolde immediately decided had a story and resolved to learn it.

And along the far wall, in a case whose doors hung open because there was no longer any reason in Vellamare to lock anything away from soldiers, lay the atlas.

She knew what it was before she touched it. She had grown up on the edges of her uncle's reputation the way coastal towns grew up on the edge of the sea — aware of it constantly, rarely looking directly at it. *Aldric Vane's atlas*, her mother said sometimes, in the voice reserved for dead men and unfinished arguments. Twenty years in the making and twenty more in the finishing, and now here it was, open on a stand, its pages breathing faintly in the draft from the window.

Isolde approached the way one approaches a sleeping animal.

Up close, the map resolved into a world. The coastline of Vellamare curved across the page in a line so confident it seemed less drawn than remembered, every inlet and shoal annotated in a hand small and precise and utterly certain of itself. Depth soundings clustered along the harbor approaches like flocks of birds. Tide tables ran down the margin in columns so regular they might have been set in type.

But it was the margins themselves that caught her — because the margins were wrong.

She had been raised on maps. Her father, before, had kept an atlas of the inland provinces on a lectern in the parlor, and Isolde had spent whole rainy afternoons tracing roads with one finger, learning the country the way other children learned prayers. She knew how margins behaved. They held compass roses and scale bars, the cartographer's name and the year, maybe a decorative sea-serpent if the mapmaker had a sense of humor. They were frame, not picture. Edge, not content.

These margins were full of marks that meant nothing at all.

Tiny strokes beside certain place-names — a dot above, a dash below. Small circles, some open, some filled. Little clusters of ticks arranged in threes and fives. Here and there a letter that seemed to have wandered in from another alphabet entirely, or perhaps from no alphabet at all: a character like a leaning gate, one like a fishhook, one like a bird's footprint in wet sand. They were scattered with such casualness that a careless eye would take them for smudges, or tests of the pen, or the idle doodling of a man thinking about something else.

A careless eye. Isolde stood very still, and understood, with the cold bright clarity that would come to feel like an inheritance, that this was exactly the point. These marks had been made to be dismissed. Whoever had drawn them had wanted them invisible in plain sight — legible only to someone who already suspected that maps could say more than coastlines.

Her uncle hadn't seen them. She was almost sure he hadn't; he spoke of the atlas as a coastline, a life's work of measurement, something to be finished and delivered. If he had known the margins were speaking, he would not stroke his chin over them at night. He would not look ill.

Unless he did know, and looked ill for another reason entirely. Isolde filed this possibility away. Fourteen years of being talked around had taught her that adults kept secrets the way rivers kept silt — invisibly, and always moving somewhere.

She pulled the stool from the drafting table, dragged it to the stand, and sat down in Aldric Vane's light.

* * *

Decipherment, she would later understand, was mostly patience wearing the mask of inspiration. But at fourteen, on that attic morning, it felt like a door swinging open onto a lit room.

She started with what any sensible person would start with: repetition. She took a blank sheet from the stack — her uncle would notice eventually, but paper hoarded against the end of the world could spare one sheet for the beginning of another — and copied the marginal marks in order of appearance, page by page, until patterns surfaced the way land surfaces at low tide.

The dots and dashes appeared only beside names of places. Never towns — she checked twice — but coves, rocks, points of land, the kind of featureless geography that existed mostly to be avoided. Except someone had marked them. Someone had cared about Gullskerry Rock and the Hollows below Raven Point and a dozen nameless bends of shore.

The circles appeared beside dates. Tides, probably — the atlas was thick with tide tables. Open circles on some days, filled on others. She counted. Open circles clustered around the dark of the moon. Filled ones around the full.

*You can only move at night,* Isolde thought, her heart suddenly loud in the quiet attic, *and you can only move without lights on a moonless night.*

She didn't know yet what was being moved. But the shape of the sentence assembled itself in her chest before her mind caught up, the way you know a room is occupied before you see anyone in it.

The ticks came next, and nearly stopped her cold, because the ticks were arithmetic. Clusters of three and five and seven, and once — beside an entry from eleven years back — a cluster of nineteen, which was too many for tides and too many for weather. Nineteen of anything, smuggled anywhere, was people. She wrote the number on her sheet and looked at it for a long time. Nineteen someones, out through the dark water, past whatever waited offshore, on a night with no moon, guided by a man who drew maps so beautiful that no one ever thought to read between their lines.

The strange letters were hardest, and she saved them for last the way you save the sweet part of a meal. There weren't many of them — a dozen distinct characters scattered across twenty years of pages — but they recurred, and recurrence was a lever. She found one standing alone at the head of a sequence of tick-marks, appearing again before another sequence three years later, and again before a third. A name? A ship? A greeting? She copied it: a vertical stroke crossed by two short diagonals, like a fence post with birds.

Then, near the back of the atlas, on a page devoted to the treacherous shoals off Cormorant Spit, she found the same character followed by four more she'd catalogued, and beneath them, in ordinary letters so faint they were nearly embossed rather than written, a single word:

*Lantern.*

Isolde sat back. The dust motes turned. Somewhere below, her mother's needle paused and resumed.

It wasn't a key. It wasn't even a sentence. But it was a voice — proof that the marks were language, that the silence had grammar, that a dead man had been talking the whole time to whoever was patient enough to listen. And the word itself rang in her like a struck bell, though she couldn't have said why. *Lantern.* Something you carried into the dark so others could follow. Something you lit in a window so the lost could find the harbor.

She worked until her neck ached and the blade of light from the dormer swung around and began instead to pour. She filled both sides of her sheet with columns of marks and guesses and small triumphant checkmarks. She had, she calculated, perhaps a tenth of the code — the counting-marks, most of the moon-circles, three of the twelve letters, and one word that felt less like a translation than a summons.

Footsteps on the stair made her spine straighten.

Lior Oyelaran came through the doorway carrying a parcel under one arm and the particular stillness of a man returning from the requisitioned guildhall, and he stopped as though he'd walked into glass.

His niece sat at his master's atlas with ink on her fingers and sheets covered in Aldric Vane's own cipher spread before her — organized, cross-referenced, partially solved.

"You're supposed to be downstairs," he said at last, which was remarkable, Isolde thought, given everything else he might have said.

"The door wasn't locked."

"It's never locked." He set the parcel down very carefully, as if his hands needed a task. "That's — I never thought it needed to be."

"Because nobody listens to margins." She turned the sheet toward him before he could stop her, before she herself had decided whether to stop. That was the thing about her, the thing her mother despaired of and her father had loved: Isolde moved faster than her own caution. "Uncle Lior. He hid people. In these pages. Counts of them, and the nights they left, and which coves — open circles are new-moon tides, you can't run a boat dark otherwise — and there's a word, here, he wrote it under the shoals at Cormorant Spit, and I think it matters."

Lior crossed the attic slowly. He looked at her sheet for a long moment, and Isolde watched his face do something complicated: grief arriving late for a man who had died twenty years ago, fear arriving early for a danger not yet named, and underneath both, unmistakable even to a girl, a fierce and startled joy.

"I've had this atlas in my hands for twenty years," he said quietly. "Twenty years, Isolde. I've inked its coastlines and corrected its soundings and slept beneath it. It took me four years to notice the marks at all, and I still read them like a stranger sounding out words in the dark." His finger came down beside her tally of the tick-clusters, stopping on the nineteen. "How long?"

"Since midmorning." She shrugged, and then, because lying to him felt suddenly wrong in a way lying to her mother never had, added: "I like puzzles. Papa used to say I'd take apart a clock just to hear it confess."

Something moved through her uncle's face at her father's name — she saw it land and be set aside, a debt deferred — and then he pulled the second stool to the stand and sat down beside her, and the two of them fit into the dormer light together, shoulder to shoulder, the way apprentices and masters had sat at that table for longer than either of them knew.

"This character," he said, tapping the fence-post-with-birds. "I've seen it forty times. I assumed it was a monogram."

"So did I, at first. But look — it opens every group of counts. Every single one. You don't open a list with a signature, you open it with—" She hesitated, hearing the word arrive from wherever such words arrived from. "*With* a name. The name of whoever took them out. It's not a monogram, it's a heading. Like a chapter title." She looked up. "I think it says *Corvane*."

The silence that followed was of a different weight than the attic's usual silence. Lior had gone very still, in the way of a man listening to water find a crack in a hull.

"Where did you hear that name?" he asked, and his voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of the silences her mother had raged about all those years.

"I haven't heard it anywhere," Isolde said honestly. "I read it."

He stared at her, and then he laughed — a short, unsteady sound with no amusement in it and a strange amount of wonder, the laugh of a man discovering that a fire he thought long banked had been burning underground the whole time, waiting. He put his hand briefly, hard, on top of her head, rumpling hair she'd spent a quarter hour arranging, and Isolde let him, because she understood that it was not really her hair he was touching.

"Aldric," he murmured to the atlas, to the dead man whose hand lay open before them, "you impossible old fox."

"Who is Corvane?" Isolde asked. "And why does the name make you look like that?"

Lior didn't answer at once. Outside the dormer, far down toward the harbor, a bell began to ring — not the tide bell, not the guild bell, but the quick urgent triple-stroke of the customs house, the one that meant a ship had come in unscheduled and unsanctioned, and men were moving on the quay.

Her uncle rose, and all the softness had gone out of him, replaced by something swift and decisive that Isolde recognized with a thrill she didn't fully understand, because she had felt its twin rising in her own chest all morning.

"Gather your sheet," he said. "All of it. Nothing you've written leaves this room — do you hear me? Not in your pocket, not under your mattress, nowhere a soldier would think to look first." He was already folding the atlas closed, his hands sure and hurried. "From tonight, you'll work up here with me. There's a great deal left to decipher, and it seems—" the ghost of the laugh returned, wry and grieving at once, "—it seems I've been doing it too slowly."

Downstairs, the front door opened and her mother's voice rose, sharp with alarm, calling both their names.

"And Isolde," Lior said, pausing at the head of the stair, the light behind him turning him half to silhouette. "Whatever happens next — and something is happening next, I can feel it the way you feel weather in a bad tooth — remember what you found today. Not the code. The fact that you found it. There will come a day soon when they tell you that you're too young to matter, too young to be dangerous, too young to be worth anything at all." His eyes held hers, gray and steady and suddenly terribly tired and somehow burning anyway. "You are not. You never were. Now come — your mother's calling, and we mustn't give her reason to add lock-making to her worries."

He went down into the noise of the house. Isolde folded her cipher-sheet into eighths and slid it inside the lining of her collar, where paper could lie against skin and pretend to be nothing, and followed him — past the sleeping atlas, past the dust motes still turning in the dying light, down toward whatever the bells at the harbor wanted from them all.
