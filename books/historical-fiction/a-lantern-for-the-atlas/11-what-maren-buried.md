## 11. What Maren Buried

The bread was going stale in its cloth, and Maren could not stop looking at it.

Three days in her brother's house and she had learned the rhythms of the place the way she had once learned the tides of Vellamare as a girl: the creak of the third stair when Lior came up from the shop below, the particular quality of his silence on nights when he worked late — a silence with weight to it, like a man carrying water — the way Isolde's breathing changed when she dreamed of running, which she did most nights now, her legs twitching beneath the blanket as though even in sleep she were trying to put distance between herself and the road behind them.

Maren sat at the kitchen table with the loaf between her hands and understood, finally, that she could not eat it. Not because she wasn't hungry. She was hungry in the way that women who feed children first are always hungry, a low constant ache she had carried so long it felt less like hunger than like weather. But every time she lifted the bread, she saw Henric's hands tearing a loaf exactly like it in their kitchen in Aldenmoor, six weeks ago, on the morning before the grey cars came.

*You'll eat when I'm gone,* he had said, laughing, because Henric laughed at everything, because that was how he survived his own father's funeral and the bad harvests and the first notices nailed to the church door. *You'll eat enough for both of us and you'll get fat and ugly and I'll come home and not know you.*

He had not come home.

She set the loaf down. Outside, the gulls were quarrelling over something on the quay, and beneath their cries she could hear the boots of the patrol making its slow circuit — left, left, left — a rhythm that had already become ordinary, which frightened her more than anything else that had happened since they arrived. Fear wore grooves into a person, and after only three days she could feel hers forming, the sound of boots settling into the same groove as rain on the roof, as wind in the shutters, as all the sounds a body stopped hearing because hearing them cost too much.

Isolde was out — Lior had let her go to the fish market with old Sera from the corner house, and Maren had watched from the window until the two small figures disappeared among the barrels, and then had watched another hour for the soldiers who did not appear. Lior was upstairs in the attic, doing whatever it was he did with his papers and his ink, behind a door he had never once invited her through, in twenty years.

And that — that was where her mind went whenever she let it wander. To the door. To the years.

She had rehearsed this conversation for the whole journey from Aldenmoor. On the train, in the coal yard where they'd hidden for two days, walking the last forty kilometres on roads where she taught Isolde to call her *auntie* instead of *mama*, just in case. She had rehearsed it in the voice of a woman who had been wronged, because that was the voice she knew best. *You vanished into your maps while I buried Mother alone. You chose an old man's ghosts over your own family.* Twenty years of grievance, worn smooth as a river stone, carried in her pocket like a talisman.

But grievances are heavy things to carry when your legs are shaking, and somewhere on the second day of walking she had found she could no longer lift it.

Now she climbed the stairs.

* * *

The attic smelled of ink and linseed oil and dust warmed by the single lamp, and it smelled — though she would not have admitted this under torture — like their father's workshop, like childhood, like the years before everything. Lior sat bent over a frame of stretched paper, his pen moving in strokes so fine they seemed less drawn than grown, the way frost grows on glass. He did not look up. He never looked up. It used to enrage her; now she saw it for what it was, a man holding himself together by the thread of the work.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

His pen paused above the paper. A bead of ink gathered at its tip, trembling, and she watched him tilt the nib back toward the well with the care of a surgeon, saving the drop. Only then did he raise his eyes.

"Where's Issy?"

"At the market with Sera." She pulled the stool from under the chart table and sat, uninvited, because if she waited to be invited she would stand in this doorway until she died. "This isn't about her."

"It's usually about her," Lior said, but gently, and closed the ledger in front of him, giving her the whole of his attention, which was more than he had given anyone in her memory.

Maren looked at her hands. They were her mother's hands, broad-knuckled, cracked at the cuticles, and she thought about how their mother had died waiting for a letter from Lior that came eleven days too late, and how Maren had written him that letter herself, cold and precise as a requisition order, and how he had answered it with a page of grief so naked she had burned it unread because anger was easier than that.

"I'm going to say it all," she said, "and you're going to let me finish, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I've spent six weeks losing my nerve on the road and I can't afford it anymore."

Lior nodded once.

"Henric is dead." The words came out flat, and she made herself give them their weight. "Or I believe he's dead. They took him in the night, the fifth of September. Grey car, four men, no warrant shown. The neighbour saw. He didn't fight them — Henric never fought anything, that was the joke, that was the whole of him, he'd apologize to furniture he walked into. And they took him anyway." Her voice caught on the furniture, absurdly, and she pressed her knuckles against her mouth for a moment. "There's been no letter. No record. In Aldenmoor there's a woman down the lane whose husband was taken the same week, and she got a slip of paper eight weeks later saying he had died of pneumonia in custody. Eight weeks, for a piece of paper. So either Henric is dead or he is in a place where death is the merciful outcome, and I have decided to believe the first thing because the second thing would keep me awake in a way I cannot afford with a child to move across a country."

Lior's hands had gone still on the desk. She could see he wanted to speak. She held up one finger, and — to his credit, to whatever the old mapmaker had drilled into him about silence — he waited.

"Here is the part I did not tell you. Here is the part I buried in the garden behind the house along with the silver and the photographs." She made herself look at him. "They didn't take Henric because of anything he did. They took him because of what I signed."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below, a shutter banged in the wind off the harbour.

"There was a man," she said. "A clerk, in our town hall. After the new orders came — you've seen the orders here, they're the same everywhere, the same paper, the same stamps, they must print ten thousand copies in some factory somewhere — after the orders came, everyone had to declare their associations. Who they voted for, ten years ago. Who they drank with. Whether they'd ever said anything against the government, and everyone had, everyone says things, that's what talking *is*." She heard her own voice rising and dragged it down. "I went in with Henric's name on my declaration, of course, because we were married, and this clerk — young, polite, terribly polite, the polite ones are always the worst — he looked at Henric's trade union card and he looked at me and he smiled and he said, *Frau, your husband's brother emigrated in 'thirty-four. Did you know that?*"

She had known. Everyone had known. Henric's brother Tomas— no, not Tomas, that was the neighbour here, the names kept colliding in her head — Henric's brother Aldous had walked over the mountain pass into Valcourt in 'thirty-four with a suitcase and never sent so much as a postcard, because sending postcards could get a family visited.

"And the clerk said, *that makes your household unreliable by association,* and he said it kindly, he offered me the way out the way a butcher offers a clean cut, and he put a paper in front of me and said, *sign here, confirming that you denounce your brother-in-law's actions and affirm your loyalty, and your household will be reclassified as cooperative. Decline, and the file goes upstairs.*" Her hands had turned to fists in her lap without her noticing. "It was one signature. One line of ink. I told myself it meant nothing, it was a formality, everyone signed, the priest signed, the schoolteacher signed, half the town signed things that week. And three weeks later they took Henric anyway, because the list I saved us from was not the list that mattered, or because a cooperative wife is useful in ways I don't want to imagine, or because none of it has ever had anything to do with reason. I don't know why. That's the part that eats me, Lior. Not the guilt. The not-knowing."

"Maren—"

"*I'm not finished.*" It came out harder than she intended, and she saw him flinch, actually flinch, her big quiet brother who faced down soldiers with a clerk's face, and something in her chest tore along an old seam. "No. Let me finish. Because here is the thing I came to say, and it's not the confession. The confession is nothing, the confession is one signature, thousands of people signed. Here is the thing."

She stood, because sitting made her feel like a defendant, and paced the narrow aisle between the chart tables, her hand trailing along the edge of one, feeling the fine grain of the wood worn smooth by decades of a dead man's elbows.

"For twenty years I hated you. I want you to understand that I did hate you, really, properly, the way only family can hate. You stayed in this attic with your ink while I buried our mother. You wrote letters that took a year to answer and said nothing in them. When the first purges came through the inland provinces and people whispered about escape routes and sea passages, do you know what I thought? I thought, *Lior knows. Lior has always known. The mapmaker's sister could have had a route out of anywhere, and instead we got Christmas cards with tide tables printed on the back.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "Tide tables. I used to show them to Henric. Look, my brother sends me the moon's phases, how charming, how useless."

Lior had risen from his stool without her noticing. He stood by the lamp now, and the light caught the lines of his face, and she saw for the first time how much of their father lived in it, and how tired.

"You thought I chose the maps over you," he said quietly.

"I thought you chose cowardice dressed up as craft."

"And now?"

Maren stopped pacing. This was the moment the whole road had been walking toward, and now that it arrived she found she had no rehearsal for it at all. She looked at the atlas lying closed on the table — she had seen it only glances, always, the way you glance at a shrine — and she thought about what she had seen these past three days. Her brother measuring flour for porridge with a cartographer's exactness. Her brother teaching Isolde, casually, between mouthfuls, the difference between a harbour and a roadstead, filling the child's head the way he filled paper, stroke by patient invisible stroke.

"Now I think I never once asked what staying cost," she said. "I counted the letters you didn't send. I never counted the ones you couldn't. I sat in Aldenmoor judging your silences, and then a polite man put a paper in front of me and I signed it in four seconds, and I have spent every hour since learning exactly how loud a silence has to be before it becomes the only honest thing left." Her voice broke and she let it. "You weren't silent because you didn't love us. You were silent because everything else was a lie, and you couldn't bear to say the lies out loud. And I signed my name to a lie because they asked nicely, and I have hated myself for it every single day, and I needed you to know that before—" 

She stopped.

"Before what?" Lior asked.

Before the soldiers come for me too. Before they match my signature to my file and find that Maren Oyelaran Vasseur, née the mapmaker's daughter, is already marked, already unreliable, already a name on a list with her husband's beside it. Before the berth on whatever ship Odile Ferrand can bribe out of this dying port has to be divided three ways among people one of whom is hunted twice over.

"Before it matters," she said. "That's all. Before it matters."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Lior crossed the attic and did the thing he had not done since they were children — he took her face in his ink-stained hands, thumbs at her cheekbones, and looked at her the way he looked at a coastline he was rendering for the first time, with total attention, missing nothing.

"The signature doesn't make you theirs," he said. "Do you hear me? A name coerced onto paper is not your soul. They think ink is power because ink is all they understand." Something moved behind his eyes then, quick and strange — not comfort but calculation, or perhaps the two had become the same thing in him long ago. "Maren. When you signed that paper. Was your name spelled correctly?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The clerk. The polite one. Did he write your name himself, or did you sign beneath your own particulars? Was the spelling right? Your mother's name — Oyelaran — they always misspell it. Always."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because everything matters," he said, "in a ledger. Every entry, every column, every crossed t. A file with an error in it is a file that can be challenged. A file that can be challenged is a file that can be delayed. And in this country, right now, a delay is worth more than gold." His hands dropped from her face and he turned to the shelf where he kept his tools, pulling down a magnifying loupe and a stick of sealing wax as though preparing for surgery. "Tell me everything you remember about that paper. Everything. The stamp, the serial number, the colour of the ribbon on the registry seal. Leave nothing out."

Maren stared at him, and slowly, horribly, wonderfully, began to understand. "Lior. My file is in Aldenmoor. Two hundred miles inland. There's no—"

"There are duplicate registries. There are always duplicate registries; I've spent a week watching Captain Reiss work, and that man trusts no one, least of all his own clerks." He was already clearing space on the table, laying out a fresh sheet of paper with the reverence other men laid out linen. "Which means your name may already be sitting in the guildhall downstairs, in a column, in a book. Which means—"

He stopped. The colour left his face all at once, like tide going out.

"Lior? What is it?"

"That's why Reiss summoned me for tomorrow," he said slowly. "Not the charts. He said he wanted my help with *discrepancies*. Discrepancies in the residency ledger." He looked up at her, and she saw fear in her brother's face for the first time since the grey cars had come into their lives — real fear, naked, the fear of a man whose luck has run further than his cunning. "Maren. If your file crossed with the coastal registry — if your married name rang any bell at all — then he didn't summon me to draw charts. He summoned me to see whether I'd flinch at your name."

The lamp hissed. Down on the quay, the boots went on beating their slow drumbeat, left, left, left, and Maren stood in her father's old attic holding her brother's terrified gaze, and understood that the confession she had carried two hundred miles was only the first stone of what she had buried — and that the rest of it was about to be dug up by Captain Reiss himself.

* * *

They worked until the candle guttered.

Not on the atlas — Lior would not touch the atlas, not tonight, not with his hands still unsteady — but on memory. He made her rebuild the denunciation paper out of thin air, line by line, the way he might reconstruct a coastline from a drowned pilot's recollection. The clerk's rank insignia. The date stamp, upper right, smudged. The serial prefix — *she was almost certain* it began with a K, K for the provincial registry, and Lior wrote it down and circled it twice. The exact wording of the loyalty clause, which she repeated three times until it flowed smooth as a prayer, and hated herself a little more with each repetition.

"You're memorizing it," she said finally. "Why am I memorizing it?"

"Because paper burns," Lior said, trimming the wick with his fingernail. "Memory has to be taken. And they're running out of men willing to take things from people like us — there aren't enough prisons, Maren, that's the secret nobody says aloud. There have never been enough prisons. That's why they use ledgers. A ledger imprisons a thousand names for the price of one clerk." He blew out the guttering candle, and in the sudden dark his voice came steady and close. "So tomorrow, when I sit across from Captain Reiss, you will be at the market with Isolde. You will buy fish. You will haggle. You will be the most boring widow in Vellamare. And whatever happens in that guildhall—"

"What happens in that guildhall?"

A pause. In the darkness, she heard him folding paper — the small precise sounds of a man putting his tools away in their proper places, preparing for a night he did not expect to survive cleanly.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we find out whether he's read your name yet. And if he has—" Another pause, longer, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet in a way that frightened her far more than shouting would have. "If he has, then Odile Ferrand's three berths just became the only arithmetic that matters. And I need you to swear to me, right now, in the dark, that whatever I decide about those berths — you won't argue with me in front of Isolde. Whatever I decide."

Maren stood in her brother's attic with her buried confession finally exhumed and lying open between them like a grave robbed in the night, and she opened her mouth to promise him anything, everything —

— and heard, below them, the front door open, and small feet on the stairs, and Isolde's voice, breathless and bright with discovery, calling up through the dark:

"Uncle Lior? Uncle Lior, I need to show you something. I figured out what the little marks mean."
