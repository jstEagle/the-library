## 12. Reiss Reads the Margins

The summons came at the hour when Vellamare's gulls went quiet, that gray seam between afternoon and evening when the light off the water turned the color of old pewter. A corporal stood in the doorway of the drafting room with a slip of paper and an expression of studied indifference, the look of a man who had learned not to read what he carried.

"Captain Reiss requests the draftsman Oyelaran at the guildhall. Seven o'clock."

*Requests.* The word sat strangely on a man's tongue when it meant *orders*, but Lior had learned long ago — first from Aldric, then from the years after — that regimes loved their euphemisms the way cartographers loved their conventions: north always up, water always blue, violence always called something gentler.

"I'll come now," Lior said, setting down his pen with care, as though the line he was drawing mattered more than the summons. It did not matter, of course. Nothing he drew for them mattered; that was rather the point. But the corporal watched him the way soldiers watch men who have been summoned, and so Lior let his hands be steady and his face be the mild, slightly dull face of a craftsman thinking about ink.

He walked through streets that had learned to empty themselves. Three weeks of occupation had taught Vellamare a new grammar: shutters closing at the sound of boots, conversations dying mid-sentence on corners, women crossing to the far side of the quay rather than pass within speaking distance of a patrol. The town had always been a place of noise — fish-cries, church bells, the argumentative clatter of the morning market — and now its silence had a texture, a weight, like fog pressing against glass.

Lior counted windows as he walked. He could not help it. Aldric had trained him to count everything — bell towers, chimneys, mooring posts, the precise number of steps between the harbor master's office and the customs shed — because a mapmaker's first discipline was noticing what others overlooked. And so he noticed that the guildhall's upper windows were lit earlier than usual, and that two soldiers stood at its door who had not been there yesterday, and that one of them was holding, wrapped in canvas, something flat and rectangular and roughly the size of a folio.

His heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery against him, turning over once like a boat coming about.

*The atlas is under the floorboards,* he reminded himself. *The atlas has been under the floorboards since the day they marched in.* What lay in the guildhall then — what lay in anyone's hands — could only be copies. Charts he had drawn for them. Charts he had *altered* for them.

But the margins. He thought of the margins.

---

The requisitioned guildhall had been the Cartographers' Guild for a hundred and forty years, and Captain Reiss had occupied it the way mold occupies bread — thoroughly, quietly, without altering the shape of anything except what it fed upon. The great reading room still held its long tables and its brass lamps and its smell of size and paper. But where the guild's seal had been, the state's eagle hung instead, and where the masters' portraits had lined the walls there were only paler rectangles, the paint around each frame protected from fading while the wall itself darkened. Someone had taken the portraits down. Lior wondered if they'd been burned or merely stored, and found he could not decide which answer frightened him more.

Reiss was at the head table, and he was not alone, and this too Lior noticed at once: the captain had arranged the room. Two lamps instead of the usual one, positioned so that whoever sat opposite him would sit facing the light. A single chair placed at a careful distance. On the table before him, laid out with a curator's precision, were documents.

"Lior Oyelaran." Reiss said the name correctly — all four syllables, the accent on the second, which almost no one outside Vellamare managed — and gestured to the chair. "Please. Sit. You've been working late these past weeks. I hope the hours aren't unreasonable."

"They're necessary," Lior said. "Charts don't draw themselves."

"No." Reiss smiled faintly. "They require a hand."

He was a small man, Reiss, narrow through the shoulders, with the pale indoor complexion of a clerk and hands that were surprisingly fine-boned, a scholar's hands wearing a soldier's cuffs. When he had first arrived, Lior had made the error — the comfortable, dangerous error — of filing him among the functionaries, the men who processed the world into columns because they could not bear it whole. Three weeks of working beneath him had begun to correct that file. Reiss read everything twice. Reiss asked questions whose answers he already possessed. And Reiss, tonight, had spread documents across a table like a man laying out a hand of cards he intended to play slowly.

"You know these waters better than any living man," Reiss said. "That's why you're here. So I want you to look at something as a professional, not as a subject of the state. Consider it a consultation." He turned the topmost document and slid it across the table. "Tell me what you see."

It was a chart. Not one of Lior's military charts — an older thing, browned at the edges, salvaged from somewhere in the guild's archive. A coastal survey from forty years ago, unsigned. Lior bent over it and gave it the honest attention of a craftsman, because the safest ground in any interrogation was expertise.

"Pre-war survey. Shallow draft. The soundings are in fathoms here, converted later — see the overwriting? Someone updated it by hand rather than redrawing. Lazy work, or hurried work." He tapped a smudge near the harbor mouth. "And this correction is wrong. There's a shoal there the original surveyor missed. We call it the Widow's Tooth. It's eaten three hulls in my lifetime."

"Yes," Reiss said softly. "I know. I've read your deposition on the harbor approaches. Very thorough." He drew the chart back. "You see how much a document can tell us about the man who touched it? You looked at it for eleven seconds and told me its age, its provenance, its errors, and its local name. Now." He set the chart aside and placed a second item on the table, and this time Lior's heart did not turn over; it stopped altogether, briefly, like a clock between ticks.

It was a page. A single leaf, removed from a bound volume, and Lior knew it the way a father knows a child's cry in a crowd of children. It was from Aldric Vane's atlas — one of the coastal leaves, the stretch of shore south of the point, the one with the lighthouse rendered in Aldric's peculiar cross-hatching that no apprentice had ever been permitted to touch.

But it was not the original. Lior's eye went to it and confirmed in a single sweep what he already knew: it was one of his own copies, the fair copies he had made for the Guild presentation, the ones he had kept in the studio cabinet because the atlas itself slept beneath the floorboards. His own hand. Which meant Reiss had searched the studio, or had cause to search it, or had someone searching it still.

"The old man's work," Reiss said. "Magnificent. I've been studying it." He smoothed the leaf flat with those fine-boned fingers. "There is a tradition in your guild, I understand, of marginal annotation. Corrections, observations, sailing notes. Very common in the older atlases." He turned the leaf toward the lamp, tilting it so the raking light crossed the paper's surface. "And here, along the eastern margin — do you see? Faint marks. Pinpricks. Tiny characters, almost invisible unless the light comes from the side. Your master was thorough even in his secrets."

Lior looked. He made himself look with a craftsman's eye rather than a condemned man's, because the difference between those two gazes might be the difference between walking out of this room and not walking out of it. The pinpricks were there — of course they were there; they were on every leaf — and beside them, in ink so dilute it seemed a stain rather than writing, ran the small coded figures Aldric had devised. Numbers that were not depths. Letters that were not names. A ledger written along the edge of the world, recording every soul the old man had smuggled out to sea while the previous regime hunted them through the very streets whose outlines he was drawing.

Twenty years, and Lior had only deciphered fragments. Isolde, in three nights, had cracked more of it than he had managed in two decades — the girl read the code the way other children read adventure serials, greedily, joyfully. That was why the pages had gone back beneath the floorboards, deeper this time, behind the loose stone where the studio's foundation met the cliff.

"That would be the old man's habit," Lior said. "He annotated everything. Tides, weather, complaints about his tea. Half of it's gibberish — private shorthand. He didn't trust apprentices with his methods until they'd earned them, and I never earned that part."

"Mm," said Reiss.

The silence stretched. Lior had learned silences the way sailors learn weathers, and he knew this one: it was the silence of a man waiting to see whether you would fill it. Twenty years ago, when the soldiers had come for Aldric, they had filled every silence themselves — shouted questions, slammed tables, demanded answers — and the old man had given them nothing but courtesy and blankness, and it had cost him his life, and it had saved the atlas. Lior had stood in the stairwell of this same building — no, not this building, the old guildhouse, since burned — and listened to his master refuse, politely, endlessly, to fill the silence.

He did not fill it now.

"You knew him for twenty years," Reiss said at last. "Apprentice at fourteen. That's a long apprenticeship."

"He was slow to delegate."

"And loyal to him?"

"He was my master."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's the only answer I have that's true," Lior said mildly, "so I'll keep giving it."

Something flickered across Reiss's face — irritation, or perhaps its cousin, respect. He reached beneath the table and brought up a folder, thick, tied with the regulation gray cord, and Lior's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world, because he recognized the folder. It was the guild's own binding style. It was a file compiled from the guild's own records.

"In 1917," Reiss said, opening it, "your master petitioned the harbormaster's office for permission to conduct a private hydrographic survey of the southern coves. Permission granted. In 1918, he requested exemption from the wartime blackout ordinances for his studio lantern — claimed he worked by night. Granted. In 1919, he purchased, through a chandler, quantities of lamp oil wildly disproportionate to a workshop of its size. Also noted: between 1917 and 1920, eleven vessels left this port on nights of poor visibility carrying passenger manifests that do not match their cargo declarations. Eleven vessels. Forty-three souls unaccounted for in the customs rolls. Forty-three people who appear nowhere in the departure records, and yet —" he tapped the browned chart again — "appear, I am increasingly confident, in these margins. Names, or substitutes for names. Dates, or substitutes for dates. Your master recorded them, Master Oyelaran. Every escape. Every hull. Every night he moved them out under the nose of the authorities who trusted him precisely because he was the man who made their maps."

"He was an eccentric old man who liked working at night," Lior said. "Half Vellamare will tell you his lantern burned till dawn. It's why I work late myself. Habit inherited."

"Forty-three souls."

"Fisherfolk miscounted by lazy clerks, more likely. Customs rolls in this port were never accurate. Ask any harbormaster."

"I intend to." Reiss closed the folder, gently, the way one closes a book one intends to reopen. "Here is what puzzles me. The regime that preceded us wanted this atlas. They wanted it badly enough to kill for it. Why would a mapmaker die for a book of coastlines?" He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his eyes, and Lior saw in them the thing he had been dreading since the day the boots came ashore: not cruelty, which can be waited out, but curiosity, which cannot. "Coastlines don't die for themselves. But a record — a record of treason, a ledger of the disappeared, proof that the great Aldric Vane spent his final years running a smuggling operation out of his own drafting table — that, a man might burn for. Or kill for." He folded his hands. "Where is the atlas, Master Oyelaran?"

"Gone," Lior said. It was even true, in the way that mattered least and most. "Scattered. After his death I sold what the guild didn't claim. Charts go to collectors. Leaves get broken up. It's the fate of every atlas eventually — no one keeps a dead man's coastlines whole."

"Sold to whom?"

"Brokers. Dealers. One went to a collector in the capital — I never learned his name; the dealer handled it. You'd know better than I how hard it is to trace a buyer who pays in cash."

"I imagine it's very hard," Reiss agreed pleasantly. "For someone who doesn't know where to look."

* *

 *

The interview lasted another hour, and Lior emerged from it the way a diver emerges from deep water — alive, and astonished at the fact, and carrying the pressure of the deep in his joints. Reiss had circled and circled: the night-work, the oil purchases, the eleven vessels, the identity of Aldric's correspondents, the whereabouts of the guild's older records, the question — asked three times in three different shapes, which meant it was the question — of whether Lior himself had ever helped move anyone out by sea.

To all of it, the same mild surface. *I was a boy. I mixed inks and swept shavings. My master shared his methods, not his business.* It was not even entirely a lie, which was the secret of good lying: Aldric had indeed shared his methods and concealed his business, and Lior had genuinely spent twenty years not knowing how much the old man had hidden inside the hiding.

But twice, Reiss had caught him. Twice, small moments when the current pulled and Lior had to fight not to show the strain. Once when Reiss mentioned the chandler's name — Bostan — and Lior realized with cold horror that Bostan's grandson still kept the chandlery, and that the family had helped Odile Ferrand's people for two generations, and that his face must not change, and he could not be certain it hadn't. Once when Reiss said, almost idly, near the end: *"Your sister arrived recently, I believe. From inland. Without papers."* And Lior had answered, "She lost them on the road; the provinces are chaos," and heard his own voice remain perfectly level, and hated how practiced it sounded even to himself.

Now he walked home through the dark with the sentries' lanterns swinging behind him, and the fear arrived properly, the way it does only after the danger has passed — flooding in, filling every hollow. Because Reiss had the leaf. Reiss had the folder. Reiss had forty-three souls and eleven ships and a theory, and theories were worse than evidence, because evidence could be disputed but a theory only needed time.

Time. That was the currency now, more than coin, more than charts. Time until Reiss found the floorboards or didn't. Time until the Corvane sailed on the morning tide after next — two tides, only two. Time until Odile's price came due and Lior had to decide what a life's work weighed measured against a berth.

He let himself into the studio and stood a moment in the darkness, listening. The house breathed its familiar breaths — Maren moving below, the small creak of Isolde's attic-nest above, the sea keeping its own counsel beyond the wall. Everything ordinary. Everything, for a few more hours, intact.

Then he struck no light, and knelt, and lifted the loose stone, and put his hand into the dark cavity where the atlas slept wrapped in oilcloth — and his hand found cloth, and found boards, and found the shape of the folio.

And found, wedged beside it, a scrap of paper that had not been there before. He knew every object in that cavity the way he knew his own teeth. This was new. In the faint gray light from the window he made out a child's careful capitals:

*HE ASKED THE CHANDLER'S BOY ABOUT YOU TODAY. I HEARD HIM. WE SHOULD GO SOONER.*

Isolde had known. Fourteen years old, and she had heard the net drawing in before her uncle had, and she had crawled down here in the dark to leave him a message in the one place she knew he would look.

Lior sat back on his heels in the cold, holding the note, and understood that the cat-and-mouse was nearly played. Reiss was not circling anymore. Reiss was closing.

Two tides. Perhaps fewer.
