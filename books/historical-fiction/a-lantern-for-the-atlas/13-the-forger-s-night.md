## 13. The Forger's Night

Lior had not closed the shutters of the attic studio since the soldiers came, but tonight he drew them tight for the first time in twenty years. The candle he lit was a stub, half-burned, the kind that gave off more smoke than light — and that was the point. Light could be seen from the quay. Smoke could be smelled by a patrol walking the lane below. Aldric Vane had taught him everything about ink and paper and the patience of lines; only now, with the army's boots echoing through Vellamare's streets at all hours, did Lior understand why the old man had also taught him to work in darkness.

He laid out the materials on the drafting table like a surgeon laying out instruments: three sheets of transit stock taken from the guildhall's own stores — paper was never forged, Aldric had said, paper was *found* — a dish of iron-gall ink mixed thin, a second dish mixed with lampblack to match the ledger clerk's cheaper hand, two pens cut from different quills, pumice powder, a bone folder, and the razor he used for scraping vellum. Beside these, wrapped in a linen cloth that still smelled faintly of the sea, lay the pages he had cut from the requisitioned ledger three days ago, when Captain Reiss's clerk had looked away for exactly the length of time Lior had calculated it would take a man to sneeze twice and pour himself wine.

The pages held eleven names.

He had chosen them over many sleepless nights, and choosing had been worse than any forgery he would ever commit. There were families who could not run — old Marta Bren with her swollen legs, the Kessler brothers who refused on principle to abandon their boats. There were families too visible to move, watched since the first day. And there were those whose names sat in the wrong column through nothing but a clerk's fatigue: marked *essential labor* when they should have been marked *permitted departure*, or worse, marked with the small black cross that meant *hold for review* — which everyone now understood to mean the trains east.

Eleven names. Eleven chances to unmake what the ledger had made of them.

The floorboard creaked behind him, the third one from the stair, and he did not turn around because he already knew the weight of the step. Isolde came into the candlelight with her hair braided tight against her skull the way Maren did it for long journeys, and she carried the inkstone under her arm as though it were a kitten she'd rescued.

"I couldn't sleep," she said. "I heard you moving up here."

"You were supposed to be asleep."

"Aunt Maren's supposed to be asleep too. She's sitting at the kitchen table staring at the wall." Isolde set down the inkstone with exaggerated care, the way he himself set things down, and he felt something twist in his chest — pride and fear arriving together, inseparable, like the two inks in their dishes. "I want to help."

Lior studied her across the table. Fourteen years old. In the lamplight of ordinary evenings she was all elbows and questions, forever pulling books from his shelves, forever asking *why does the coastline curve there, why do you hold the pen so*. But tonight her face had gone quiet and older, the way faces in Vellamare had been going quiet and older for weeks, children aging in months.

"If you help," he said slowly, "you are not a child watching anymore. You understand that. If they ever trace any of this —"

"They'll trace it to both of us or neither." She pulled the second stool to the table and sat. "Tomas said Reiss doesn't care about children. Tomas is wrong. I've seen the lists. Children are just smaller entries."

He wanted to tell her to go back downstairs. He wanted it so badly that his hands shook, and then he saw that hers did not, and he understood that the argument was already lost — had perhaps been lost the day she found the atlas in the attic and read Aldric's margins faster than he ever had.

"Then we do it properly," he said. "Aldric's way. Every forgery begins with surrender."

"Surrender?"

"We give up our own hand. Completely." He turned the transit stock toward her and weighted its corners. "Look at this. A clerk named Duval wrote the original entries. He presses hard on downstrokes, lifts on curves, dots his i's low and to the left. I am not going to write like Lior Oyelaran pretending to be Duval. That is how forgers hang themselves. I am going to stop being myself for four hours and become a tired man with bad penmanship and a wine habit."

Isolde leaned close, her braid slipping forward. "And the line-weight? You always say line-weight is a fingerprint."

"It is. So we steal his fingerprint too." He showed her the razor, the pumice, the bone folder. "We lift his genuine strokes where we can and float new ones between them. Where we can't, we age the paper over the mend with smoke and tea so the fibers forget they were ever separated. Paper has a memory, Isolde. Our whole art is teaching it to remember differently."

She was silent for a moment, and then she said, in a voice very unlike a child's: "Master Vane did this for people. In the margins. Didn't he?"

"He did."

"Then we're not forging," she said, picking up the spare pen and turning it in her fingers as if weighing it. "We're continuing."

* * *

Isolde had thought forgery would feel like stealing.

It did not. It felt like drawing coastlines — which was to say it felt like the only honest thing left in Vellamare.

She practiced Duval's letters forty times before Lior let her touch the real page. Downstroke heavy, curve lifted, the i dotted low and crooked-left as though the man had written ten thousand entries and hated every one of them. By the twentieth attempt her wrist ached in an unfamiliar way, learning a stranger's body through his handwriting, and by the fortieth Lior grunted — the smallest sound, barely a syllable — and she understood she had passed some examination she hadn't known she was taking.

"The third name down," Lior said. "Sela Marchetti. Marked *hold for review* on the fourth of October. We're changing the date to the twenty-ninth and the classification to *permitted, harbor pass issued*. Harbor passes went out on the thirtieth, so the entry must look like it was written the day before."

"Why the twenty-ninth and not the thirtieth?"

"Because clerks prepare the day's papers the evening before. If you forge a document dated the same hour it was needed, that's a lie told at the moment of inspection. If you date it the night prior, it's a lie told in the natural order of a working day." He glanced at her. "Never forge the present tense. The present tense is where suspicion lives."

She wrote it. Sela Marchetti's name transformed itself under her pen, the black cross erased with pumice so gently the paper's surface barely whispered, replaced by the flat bureaucratic phrase that meant *free*. Her hand did not shake. She noticed that with a strange, floating surprise — she had expected terror to live in her fingers, and instead her fingers were calm, almost happy, doing the thing they were made for while the terror stood off to one side like a chaperone, watching, unable to interfere.

"Lior." She kept her eyes on the page. "The woman with the bread stall. Frau Ostrow. She's not on our list."

"She can't be. Her son is conscripted into the garrison. If she disappears, they'll tear the quarter apart looking for her."

"But she's marked with a cross. Like the others. She's going on a train."

"Yes."

The word sat between them, enormous and immovable. Isolde pressed her pen down harder than she meant to and a blot bloomed across the practice sheet, ugly as a wound. She stared at it. Eleven names, saved out of hundreds. Three berths on the *Corvane*, bought with an atlas. And everywhere else, columns and crosses and a captain with neat handwriting compiling his lists.

"That's the arithmetic," Lior said quietly. "It doesn't resolve. It never resolves. You do what your hands can reach tonight, and tomorrow you reach again, and you don't let the arithmetic make you stop reaching. Your master learned that from Aldric. Now you're learning it from me."

"You're not my master yet," Isolde said, and was relieved to see the corner of his mouth move.

"No," he agreed. "Not yet. But you've been my apprentice since the night you climbed those stairs without permission and read a dead man's code in an hour. I'm merely catching up to the paperwork."

They worked. The candle burned down in its dish. Outside, a patrol passed along the lane, boots striking cobbles in fours, and they froze without being told — Lior's hand flat over the pages, Isolde's breath held until her chest burned — and then the boots faded toward the quay and the pen touched paper again, downstroke heavy, curve lifted, the i dotted low and to the left, a dead man's habits resurrected to buy living people out of columns.

Near midnight her aunt came up with bread and watered wine, set the tray down without a word, and stood looking at the table for a long moment. Isolde saw her mother's face do something complicated — grief and fury and a terrible pride all fighting for the same ground — and then Maren reached out and straightened a bottle of ink that wasn't even crooked, and went back down the stairs.

"She knows," Isolde said.

"She's known since the night she arrived," Lior said. "She burned her own papers to get here. She knows exactly what this work costs."

* * *

By the third hour Lior's shoulders had locked into stone and his eyes kept sliding out of focus, and he understood that the night's true enemy was not Reiss but exhaustion, because a tired forger grew careless in ways no amount of skill could repair. He made himself stop after each page, stand, roll his neck, drink the watered wine Isolde had brought, and look at the finished work as an examiner would look at it.

Page one: seven names amended. He held it to the candle, tilted it, tilted it back. The pumiced patches vanished under the tea-staining. Duval's ghost walked comfortably across the page. Good.

Page two: four names. Also good — except.

He bent closer. The fourth entry. Old Piotr Havel, the ferryman. The date amendment was clean, the classification clean, the little flourish Duval used to close an entry reproduced perfectly. But beneath the entry, where the original page had been handled by many thumbs over many years, there was a faint shadow of handling that their fresh pumicing had smoothed away. A patch of paper too clean in a field of dirt. Invisible to a tired eye. Not invisible to a suspicious one, and Reiss was nothing but suspicion wearing a well-pressed uniform.

"Isolde. Look at this patch. What do you see?"

She leaned in, her face nearly touching the page, and he watched her mind move — he could see it moving, the same way he'd once watched Aldric's move, the same hunger for the hidden logic of surfaces. "It's too clean," she said. "Everyone touches the ledger. This spot hasn't been touched enough."

"How do we fix it?"

She was quiet a moment. Then she got up, went to the shelf where he kept his reference folios, and pulled down the worn volume of Aldric's coastal studies — not the atlas, never the atlas, that stayed under the floorboard — and flipped through it with dirty fingers until she found what she wanted. "Your master's hands. Look." She showed him the margin of a chart of the northern headlands, gray with decades of thumbprints. "Oil. A little on the fingertips, press lightly, build it up in layers. Real grime isn't applied, it accumulates. So we accumulate it."

Lior looked at her for a long time.

"What?" she said, defensive suddenly, fourteen again for a single heartbeat.

"Nothing," he said. "That's exactly what he would have done. Exactly. I'd have scraped and restained and gotten it wrong. You went and found how dirt actually behaves." He took the folio from her, carefully, and set it down. "Isolde. When this is over — whatever 'over' means after this year — I am going to teach you everything I know. All of it. The projections, the surveying, the code, all of it. I want you to know that now. I want you to have heard it plainly, once, from me, in words."

Her eyes went wide and bright in the candlelight, and she blinked fast and said only, "Then you'd better survive to do it," and bent back over the page, and neither of them said anything else about it, because some promises are too large for the hours they're made in.

They oiled the thumbprints. They aged the mends. They re-inked the binding thread holes where pages had been removed from the ledger and would need to look as though they'd never been anywhere else. Toward the coldest hour of the night, when the candle had become a coin of blue flame drowning in its own wax, Isolde fell asleep with her cheek on her folded arm, mid-stroke, the pen still between her fingers, guarding the last unfinished name even in sleep.

Lior finished it for her — Piotr Havel, ferryman, thirty years on the crossing, permitted departure — and then sat for a while simply watching her breathe, this child of his sister's stubborn blood and some fire he didn't have a name for, and he thought: *Aldric, look at her. Look what you started in a margin, sixty years ago, in a hand so small you probably believed no one would ever find it.*

He wrapped the pages in oilcloth. He slid them into the false bottom of the map case that hung, in plain view, among forty other map cases on the studio wall, because the best hiding place in an occupied town was inside the occupier's own categories. Then he pinched out the candle, and in the dark he heard the bells of the harbor chapel begin to count the hour —

and stopped counting with them, because below the bells, thin and wrong, came another sound: knocking. Not at his door. At Maren's. Three knocks, a pause, three more — the rhythm Odile Ferrand had taught him meant *the net is closing, burn nothing, open nothing.*

Downstairs, Maren was already moving.
