## 14. Tomas Returns Broken

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Lior knew the sound of Tomas's knuckles the way he knew the grain of his own drafting table — three quick raps, impatient, a fisherman's greeting that had never once in forty years waited for an answer before the door swung open. This was different. A single tap, soft as a moth against glass, and then nothing, and then another.

He set down his pen. The chart he had been correcting — Reiss's coast charts, the ones he was slowly poisoning with errors small enough to pass a clerk's eye and large enough, God willing, to put a gunboat on a sandbar — lay open beneath the lamp, its false depths gleaming wet. He covered it with a sheet of blotting paper out of habit, though habit had stopped protecting anyone weeks ago.

Tomas stood on the landing like a man who had forgotten what doors were for. He was thinner — Lior had expected thin, everyone came back from the guildhall cells thinner — but it was more than that. It was as if someone had reached inside him and turned down a lamp. His eyes moved to Lior's face, past it, around it, cataloguing exits that weren't there.

"Tomas." Lior stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. I have soup—"

"I'm not staying." But he came in anyway, because his legs made the decision for him, folding him onto the bench by the window where he'd sat a thousand evenings mending nets while Lior worked and Aldric Vane — dead twenty years now, or thirty, time had gone strange since the soldiers came — argued cheerfully about coastlines with no one at all.

Lior ladled the soup anyway and set it within reach. Tomas looked at it the way you look at something belonging to a stranger.

"They let me go," Tomas said. "Tuesday last, they let me go, but I couldn't—" His hand made a vague gesture toward the harbor, the town, the whole geography of Vellamare. "I went to my sister's first. In Cailleville. I walked most of it."

"Cailleville is eleven leagues."

"I know how far it is." There was the old irritation, surfacing for one moment like a fish under ice, and Lior felt an absurd surge of relief at the sight of it — *there you are, there's still someone under there* — before it sank again.

The light through the window was failing. Lior lit the second lamp, and the room filled up with warm yellow shadow, and Tomas flinched at the match-strike so violently that the bowl rattled on its saucer.

They sat without speaking while the town's evening noises came through the walls: boots somewhere below, the creak of the requisitioned cranes, a woman calling a child home before curfew in a voice carefully stripped of everything but volume.

"You were taken for arguing," Lior said at last. "At the quay. Eleven days ago."

"Twelve." Tomas's mouth twitched. "I told the sergeant the requisition order was theft. That the harbor belonged to the families who'd fished it for two hundred years. I said it loud." He almost smiled. "You remember. You were standing right there, doing your statue impression, and Maren pulled your sleeve and you didn't say a word."

"I remember."

"And you were right to keep quiet. You were right and I was wrong, and I want you to understand that I know it now, because I paid twelve days to learn it, and what I learned isn't worth what it cost, and I need you to listen to me anyway." He turned on the bench then, fully, and looked at Lior with both eyes, and whatever lived behind them made Lior's stomach drop before a single word arrived. "Reiss isn't sorting us into columns so some of us can leave. That's the story. That's what the notices say — emigration permits, orderly departure, resettlement. But that's not what the list is for."

Lior kept very still. Twenty years of keeping still. "What list?"

"The unreliables." Tomas said the word flatly, without quotation marks, the way the guards had said it, the way it had been ground smooth of all meaning before being handed to him like a stone to carry. "There's a ledger — a different one, not the family ledger, this one's Reiss's own hand, I saw it through a door that should've been shut. Names. Not everyone's. Just certain names. Men who argued. Priests who read the wrong things aloud. The Ferrand woman's cousins, half her crew. Anyone who's ever been to sea without papers, anyone with kin abroad, anyone who asked questions twice." His hands had begun to move, turning over each other, the way they did when he talked about weather too bad to sail in. "And next to each name, a mark. A little cross. And next to the crosses, a word I only saw once before a guard shoved me along. *Ostwärts.*"

The word sat in the room between them. Eastward.

"A train," Tomas said. "Not a ship. Ships go where people choose to go. They're putting the unreliables on a train east, and nobody comes back from east, Lior. Nobody. The gendarme who processed my release — he was young, he still had a face — I asked him about my cousin Pell, taken in spring, same charge, arguing. And he looked at me the way you look when you step on something soft in the dark, and he said, 'Pell who?' Twelve days I was in there and I can tell you the cell block has four hundred names chalked on its wall, four hundred men and women scratching their own names so somebody would know they'd existed, and every week a transport goes east and the wall gets shorter, and the chalk—" His voice did something terrible then, cracked sideways, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth until it passed.

Lior poured him wine he hadn't asked for. Tomas drank it like water, all of it, and held out the cup.

Outside, the searchlights had begun their slow rotation over the harbor, sweeping the water where the *Corvane* would — if Odile Ferrand's promises meant anything, and Lior had staked three lives on the belief that they might — take on passengers at morning tide two days hence.

"How many marks on the list?" Lior heard himself ask.

"Hundreds. Maybe more. It grows every day. And here's the thing—" Tomas leaned forward, and the lamplight found the hollows of his cheeks, the new architecture of his skull. "Here's the thing I came to tell you, the thing I walked eleven leagues to tell you, so hear it. Your name is spoken in that place. Not marked yet — not crossed — but spoken. The clerks know there's a draftsman in Vellamare who draws better than their own surveyors. Reiss knows. Reiss knows everything about you, Lior. Where you sleep. Who visits. He knows your sister came from inland with a girl and no luggage worth mentioning. He knows you spent an hour talking to Odile Ferrand on the fish steps, and he knows what she is." 

"He doesn't know about—"

"He doesn't have to *know*," Tomas said. "That's the genius of it, don't you see? He doesn't need proof anymore. Suspicion is enough to earn a cross. Proof takes clerks and time and honest witnesses, and suspicion takes nothing but a man with a pencil and a list." He laughed — one syllable, dry as bone. "A pencil. We're all just lines in his book now. I used to think it was the guns we should fear. It's not the guns. It's the pencils."

* * *

After Tomas left — refusing the second bowl, refusing the blanket, refusing everything except the promise that Lior would not walk alone after curfew — Lior climbed to the attic studio and stood in the dark among the smell of ink and linseed oil and the ghost of Aldric Vane's pipe smoke, which twenty years had not managed to evict.

Time had collapsed. That was the truth of it, the thing Tomas's visit had shown him plain. He had thought he was living in days — Monday's errand, Tuesday's summons, the tide on Thursday morning. But the soldiers had folded all of it together, past and future, into a single crushing present. Aldric's death was happening now. The escapes recorded in the margins were happening now. The train east had already left, was always leaving, its whistle sounding somewhere under every hour. And Thursday's tide — Isolde's berth, Maren's fury, the atlas wrapped in oilcloth — that too was already upon him, already receding, already memory.

He struck the lamp and unrolled the military charts across the table, the ones due on Reiss's desk tomorrow noon. His sabotage lay there in careful millimeters: a shoal shifted two cables north off Pointe d'Aurel, a channel depth understated by a fathom where the patrol cutters liked to run. Small treasons. Pencil-line treasons. Enough to embarrass, perhaps enough to strand, nowhere near enough to stop anything — and he understood tonight, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that none of it mattered against trains. Charts could wreck ships. Nothing he could draw would derail what was coming east.

But the margins could do something else.

He fetched the atlas from beneath the floorboard — his knees knew the exact angle of the loose plank now, the way they once knew Aldric's tread overhead — and opened it to the pages where the old man's coded record ran along the edges of his beautiful coastlines: dots and dashes and tiny altered flourishes, decades of smuggled souls written invisibly beside the safe anchorages that had received them. Lior had spent months learning to read it. Isolde had needed an afternoon.

If Reiss was building a list of the unreliable, then the atlas was the counter-list. Every name in those margins had escaped once. Every anchorage, every tide-window, every signal arrangement Aldric had recorded in his secret hand was still true, or true enough, if there was anyone left with the nerve to use it. The atlas wasn't just cargo to buy berths. It was a manual. And manuals could be copied.

Lior took fresh paper from the drawer — the good rag stock, Aldric's stock, hidden years ago against exactly this kind of night — and began, by lamplight, with the harbor at Vellamare.

He worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. Twice he heard boots in the street below and put out the flame without deciding to, his body moving ahead of his mind, twenty years of practiced silence operating him like a machine operates its levers. When the boots passed, he relit and continued.

Near dawn, copying the third sheet, he noticed something he had never seen in all his years with the atlas. At the bottom of the page bearing the Cailleville coast — Tomas's sister's coast, eleven leagues east — Aldric's marginal code ended mid-word. And beneath the broken cipher, in the old man's ordinary hand, small enough to hide under a thumbnail:

*If they come for the ledgers, the coast will not save you. Go inland. Go high. The mountains have no harbors, and that is their mercy.*

Lior sat back. Outside, the sky over the harbor was going the gray of unsized paper, and somewhere below, the morning bell rang the hour, and two days had become one.

A train east. A ship west. And between them, a fourteen-year-old girl asleep in the next room who could read Aldric's hand faster than grief itself.

He blew out the lamp and went down to wake them early. There was arithmetic to finish, and the tide would not wait, and neither — he understood now — would the pencils.
