## 16. The Guildhall Burns

The news came to Lior the way most true things came to Vellamare that autumn — not by proclamation, but by whisper, passed along the quay in fragments, wrapped in the language of weather. *There's smoke inland.* *The bells are ringing wrong.* *Someone says it's the guildhall.*

He was at his drafting table when Tomas's wife, Sera, appeared at the studio door without knocking, her shawl clutched at her throat like a rope she'd been pulling on all day.

"They're saying it's burning," she said. "The guildhall. The whole roof."

Lior set down his pen with exaggerated care, as if sudden movement might frighten away what he was hearing. "Who's saying?"

"Everyone. Half the town is walking up the hill to look." Sera's eyes were wet and bright, and beneath the tears something Lior hadn't seen in months: a wild, disbelieving joy. "Lior — they're saying the ledger burned with it."

*The ledger.* Every name in Vellamare, every family, every column and category Reiss had ruled across its pages with his neat bureaucratic hand. The book that decided who could leave and who belonged to the state. The book into which Lior had smuggled forged alterations over three sleepless nights while Isolde held the candle steady and never once flinched.

"The whole thing?" he asked, and hated how his voice shook on the second word.

"So they say."

Maren looked up from the corner where she'd been mending Isolde's coat, needle suspended mid-stitch. Isolde herself had gone very still by the window, a map of the harbor spread under her fingers, though she pretended to be studying the tide tables.

"We shouldn't celebrate," Maren said quietly. "Not yet. Not until we know."

But her needle didn't move either.

* * *

They went up the hill with the rest of Vellamare, because not going would have been more suspicious than going. The crowd moved through the streets like water finding its level — shopkeepers and fishwives, old men leaning on younger arms, children hoisted onto shoulders for a better view. No one spoke above a murmur, but the murmur itself had a strange music to it, a low sustained note Lior had not heard in the town since before the soldiers came.

The guildhall stood at the crest of the hill, a gray stone building that had housed the Cartographers' Guild for a hundred and forty years, where Aldric Vane had once served as master and Lior had been apprenticed at nine years old. Now its windows were black mouths and smoke poured from the eastern wing in great slow coils, white against the afternoon sky, smelling of pitch and burnt paper.

Soldiers had cordoned off the square, but the cordon was porous; officers shouted at civilians to stand back without much conviction, their attention split between the fire and each other. Lior pressed into the press of bodies and listened.

An argument between two lieutenants, close enough to catch in pieces. "—started in the records room, that's certain—" "—no watchman posted, I told them, I *told*—" "—the fire brigade took forty minutes to come, forty minutes, who ordered that—"

Forty minutes. In Vellamare, where every house kept buckets by the door out of habit older than anyone living. Forty minutes was not an accident. Forty minutes was a sentence someone had written and carried out.

A woman near him — Lior didn't know her face, which meant she'd come from one of the outlying lanes — was crying openly, but the tears were of a particular kind, the kind that come when grief and relief arrive together and cannot be told apart. "My brother's name," she whispered to no one. "It was in there. They can't send him east now. They don't know his name anymore."

Lior felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there.

Because it was true, wasn't it? That was the arithmetic of it. Without the ledger, Reiss had nothing but memory and rumor. Three hundred families, sorted into columns — *may leave, may remain, transport east* — and now the sorting burned to ash along with the paper it lived on. Somewhere in the crowd, dozens of people were realizing the same thing at the same moment, and Lior could feel it pass through the mass of bodies like warmth through a wall: *we are unrecorded. We are free.*

He knew better. He was sorry for it, and he knew better.

* * *

He saw Reiss from across the square.

The captain stood apart from his own officers, at the edge of the cordon, perfectly still, watching the fire with the expression of a man reading a document rather than watching a disaster. He had removed his cap. His thin hair moved slightly in the heat-drawn wind. He did not shout at anyone. He did not confer. He simply watched, and the stillness of him was worse than all the shouting in the world.

As Lior looked, Reiss turned his head — slowly, without urgency, the way a man turns a page — and his gaze crossed the crowd and stopped.

On Lior.

There was no recognition in it, no accusation, nothing so legible as suspicion. It rested on Lior the way it might have rested on a lamppost or a gull. But it stayed a moment too long, and then Reiss put his cap back on, and turned back to the fire, and Lior understood that whatever had just happened had happened entirely inside himself.

*Maren,* he thought. *Isolde. Go home. Don't run. Walk.*

He found them by the fountain at the square's edge, Maren holding Isolde's shoulder with both hands. He said nothing. He took Isolde's hand, and the three of them walked down the hill at the pace of people with nowhere urgent to be, and only when they'd turned twice, and were sure of no eyes behind them, did Maren exhale.

"He looked at you," she said.

"He looked at everyone."

"No," Maren said. "He looked at you."

* * *

That evening the town allowed itself its celebration in the only way left to it: privately, behind shutters, in lowered voices. Lior sat in the attic studio with a cup of the barley coffee Maren had made, listening to the distant sounds of a town that believed itself delivered. Someone two streets over had begun to sing — an old harbor song, wordless melody drifting up through the dark — and then stopped abruptly, as if remembering itself.

Isolde sat cross-legged on the floor with the atlas open before her, working through Aldric's margin-code with the patient ferocity she brought to everything. She'd cracked another symbol that afternoon — the little hook that meant *family, four persons, departed from the northern quay* — and she was transcribing her findings into a notebook she kept hidden inside a hollowed-out loaf.

"You're not celebrating," she said, not looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"That's what you do instead of celebrating."

"It's what I do instead of dying," Lior said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Isolde looked up then, and her face in the lamplight was fourteen years old and far too serious. "I'm sorry. Come here. Look at this with me."

She climbed onto the bench beside him, and he turned the atlas toward the light, and for a few minutes the work saved them both — the coastlines, the soundings, the beautiful secret alphabet marching along the margins, twenty years of the dead man's hand and the girl's living one meeting in the middle.

Then footsteps on the stair.

Three sets. Unhurried. The particular unhurried tread of men who know no one will ask them to account for where they go.

Lior closed the atlas with one hand and slid it beneath the floorboard with the other, and had time to straighten his shirt before the knock came — though it wasn't really a knock, more the flat announcement of knuckles against wood, once.

Captain Reiss entered without waiting.

He was not in uniform, or not fully — the tunic open at the collar, the belt hanging loose, a man off duty except that he was never off duty. Two soldiers remained at the top of the stairs, visible past his shoulders, looking anywhere but into the room. Reiss himself carried a small leather folio under one arm, and he took in the attic the way he took in everything: completely, silently, his gaze traveling across the drafting table, the lamp, the racks of pens, the smell of ink, the girl standing too close to her uncle.

"Lior Oyelaran," he said. Not a question. He knew the name; he'd written it himself in a column six weeks ago. "Forgive the hour. I find I don't sleep well when public property is on fire."

"Terrible thing," Lior said. "The guildhall. My master helped raise part of that building."

"Yes. Your master." Reiss stepped further into the room. He touched nothing, which Lior recognized as its own kind of menace; a man who touches nothing is a man who doesn't need to. "Aldric Vane. The old traitor. Tell me, draftsman — when you cleaned the atlas for presentation to the Guild, some years ago, did you ever wonder why your master kept such poor margins?"

Lior let exactly one breath pass. "Poor margins?"

"Faint marks. Little hooks and strokes, in a hand different from the charts themselves. I have spent the last several evenings with a magnifying glass and a very patient lamp." Reiss's voice never rose; it never needed to. It was the voice of a clerk reading aloud. "Most of them mean nothing, I think. But some are arranged in groups. Groups of four. Groups of five. Dates, perhaps. Quay numbers, perhaps. And always beside the dates of ships that sailed from this harbor carrying more souls than their manifests admitted."

The lamp hissed softly. Downstairs, Maren had gone silent — Lior could feel her silence rising through the floorboards like cold air.

"I wouldn't know," Lior said. "I inherited the atlas. I finished it. Margins weren't my concern."

"No," Reiss agreed pleasantly. "Yours were the line-weights."

And there it was. Lior kept his face still, kept it the smooth blank vellum he had trained it to be for twenty years, and inside himself he heard the small terrible sound of a lock turning.

"Fire is a curious instrument," Reiss went on, moving toward the window, looking down into the street as if the conversation were an afterthought. "It destroys, but it also illuminates. Everything it touches, it reveals. Do you know what I learned tonight, watching my records burn?"

"That you should have posted a watchman?"

Something flickered at the corner of Reiss's mouth — not quite a smile; the ghost of appreciation, quickly dismissed. "What I learned," he said, "is who did *not* seem surprised."

He turned from the window. "You walked up the hill with the rest of them, Oyelaran. I saw you. And your face — I am told I have a good eye for faces, it's why they keep me at paperwork — your face was doing sums. Everyone else in that square was grieving or rejoicing. You were checking figures." He lifted the leather folio slightly, almost apologetically. "So tonight I begin a new ledger. A private one. Names only I hold. And I find I already have one entry in it, written in advance, awaiting confirmation."

"Captain—"

"You'll want to say I'm mistaken. Everyone does. You may even be right." Reiss moved to the door, paused, and looked back — and for the first time Lior saw something almost human in the man's face, something tired and cold and endlessly patient. "Here is what comforts me, draftsman. Paper burns. But a man who can draw a coastline from memory — a man who has memorized every quay, every sounding, every ship that ever left this harbor secretly — such a man *is* the ledger. And ledgers, unlike paper, do not burn so easily."

He knocked once on the doorframe as he left, a mirror of his arrival. The footsteps descended. The outer door closed.

For a long moment Lior did not move. Then Isolde's hand found his sleeve — small, steady, steady as it had been holding the candle through three nights of forgery.

"Uncle," she whispered. "Does he know about the atlas?"

Lior looked down at the floorboard beneath which twenty years of his life lay hidden, and beyond it, at the dark window, and beyond that, at the sea where a ship waited on the morning tide with fewer berths than names.

"Not yet," he said. "But he's close enough to smell the ink."

Downstairs, faintly, the singing began again — and stopped again — and in the silence after it, Lior could hear, clearly, the sound of the net drawing tight.
