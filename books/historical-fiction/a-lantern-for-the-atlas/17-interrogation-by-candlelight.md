## 17. Interrogation by Candlelight

They came for him an hour before dawn, when the fog off the harbor was thickest and the lamps along the quay burned as small yellow coins drowned in milk.

Lior had expected it since the fire. He had slept badly but slept — a habit learned in the attic years, when Master Vane's coughing kept half the street awake and Lior learned to rest in the gaps between sounds. So when the boots came up his stairs two at a time, he was already dressed, already sitting at his worktable with a cold cup of chicory before him, arranging his face into the mild blankness of a man interrupted at nothing.

There were four of them. Only one spoke: a sergeant with a voice like wet gravel, who read no charge and offered no courtesy, only said, "Captain wants you," as though Lior were a document to be filed.

Maren stood in the doorway of her room in her nightdress, Isolde's hand clutched white-knuckled in hers. Lior met his sister's eyes and gave her the smallest possible nod — the same nod he had given her across their mother's deathbed, across twenty years of quarrels and reconciliations — *I am not afraid, do not be afraid on my account.* It was a lie, but it was a well-made one, and he had never yet delivered shoddy work.

"Pack his brushes," the sergeant said, almost as an afterthought. "He'll want them."

That was the detail that told him everything. They did not bring men's brushes back from interrogations unless they intended to keep the man working.

* * *

The requisitioned guildhall had been built by men who believed in light — tall arched windows, a ceiling painted with the constellations of the southern trade routes, marble floors that rang like bells underfoot. The army had papered over the windows with grey sheeting, so that the great chamber where the guild once held its exhibitions now existed in permanent dusk, lit only by the clerks' green-shaded lamps and, tonight, by candles.

Reiss worked by candlelight. Lior noticed this first, before the table, before the guards, before the folder lying closed at the captain's elbow. Two tallow candles in brass sticks, their flames perfectly still in the sealed air. Reiss sat behind a plain deal table that had replaced the guildmaster's carved oak — the oak presumably burned or shipped east — and he looked up from a stack of papers with the expression of a man who had been expecting a delivery.

"Master Oyelaran." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. You'll forgive the theatricals." A thin gesture at the candles. "The generator failed this afternoon and the requisition office cannot seem to find lamp oil for a building full of traitors. But I find I prefer it. Candlelight is honest. It shows what lamplight flatters."

Lior sat. He placed his hands flat on his knees and let his breathing settle into its lowest register. Twenty years ago, in this same building, Aldric Vane had taught him to grind ink slow and steady no matter what noise came through the floorboards below. The lesson had been about ink. It had also not been about ink.

"You know why you're here," Reiss said.

"I assume it concerns the fire."

"The fire." Reiss smiled faintly, as if tasting the word and finding it underripe. "The fire concerns me less than you might think. Buildings burn. Ledgers burn. Paper is cheap; that has always been the tragedy and the salvation of our profession." He opened the folder at last, unhurried, and turned it so its contents faced Lior. "No. What concerns me is this."

Transit documents. Six of them, fanned like a card sharp's hand. Lior recognized every one — the papers he and Isolde had made by candlelight three nights ago, the names reclassified, the columns rewritten, six families moved from the column marked *Detain* into the column marked *Permit*. His stomach did not lurch. He had spent the hours since the fire preparing his stomach for exactly this, and preparation held.

"Do you see anything familiar?" Reiss asked.

"They look like transit documents."

"They do. Very good ones. Better than good — better than my own chancery produces, frankly, which is itself an indictment of my chancery." Reiss drew one from the fan and held it up between two fingers, angling it toward the candleflame. The light passed through the paper and Lior saw the watermark bloom there, ghost-pale, the guild's old anchor-and-compass seal that they had reproduced from memory. "But documents are like people, Master Oyelaran. Under sufficient light, they betray their parentage. Look here." His finger traced the long horizontal stroke of the border rule. "This line. Do you see how it swells? Begins thin, thickens through the middle third, thins again at the terminus. Pressure distributed like a bow drawn and released. My forgers — and I have employed professional forgers, men who learned their trade in prisons in the north — draw such lines mechanically. Even pressure, even speed. Dead lines. This line is *alive*. Someone drew it the way a musician plays a phrase, with intention, with breath."

He set the document down and folded his hands.

"I have spent eleven days studying the charts you drew for my office. Eleven days of your coastlines. Every chart carries this same hand in its rules and borders — the swelling stroke, the breathing line. And your guild registration file contains fourteen specimens of your draftsmanship going back to 1921." Reiss leaned forward slightly, and the candlelight climbed his face, hollowing his cheeks, turning his eyes into two dark wells. "You made these papers, Master Oyelaran. There is no other hand in Vellamare that could have. So we may dispense with the question of *whether*, and proceed to the questions that interest me. *For whom?* And *how many?*"

The silence that followed belonged to Lior, and he occupied it the way a man occupies a house he has lived in all his life.

He let it stretch — one breath, three, five — long enough that a lesser interrogator would have filled it. Reiss did not. That, too, Lior had prepared for. He watched the flames bend minutely in some current of air too slight to feel, and he thought of Aldric Vane on the last night of the old man's life, sitting in this very city while soldiers of the previous regime demanded the atlas, and how Aldric had answered them. Not with defiance — defiance was a luxury that got apprentices buried. With nothing. With the patient, maddening, absolute silence of a man who has decided that his words are worth more than his comfort, and who will spend neither cheaply.

"You're mistaken," Lior said at last, mildly.

"Am I."

"These lines weren't drawn by me." He said it without heat, without haste, the tone of a craftsman correcting a technical error — not defending himself but defending the *work*, which was easier, which was true in a way he could inhabit. "The pressure profile you describe is characteristic, yes. But you've misread the direction of the swell. In my hand the thickening falls in the final third of the stroke, not the middle. I favor a German nib ground to a left oblique. Whoever made these used a straight-cut point and hesitated mid-stroke — you can see the ink pool where the hand paused." He reached out, slowly enough that the guard by the door shifted his weight, and turned one document toward himself. "Here. See the bloom? A draftsman who draws eight hours a day doesn't hesitate on a border rule. This was someone careful but unpracticed. Learning."

He watched Reiss watch him, and understood that he was doing something terrible and magnificent simultaneously: using the truth of his expertise to construct a lie about its application. Every word was accurate. Every word was armor.

"Someone learning," Reiss repeated softly. "From whom?"

"There are many hands in a port city."

"There are indeed." Reiss rose, crossed to the shuttered window, and stood with his back to Lior — a calculated posture, Lior knew, offering the illusion that the prisoner might relax, might speak to the room rather than the man. "I want to tell you something about myself, Master Oyelaran, because I believe in fair exchanges. Before the consolidation, I was an archivist. Land registries, northern provinces. Eleven years cataloguing deeds and boundaries. Do you know what I learned in eleven years of land registries?" He turned. "That paper outlives intention. A man sells a field intending one thing; fifty years later the deed means something else entirely; a hundred years later it is simply *fact*. Paper is the most patient material ever devised. It waits. It accumulates. And eventually—" he tapped the folder "—it testifies."

"That's very elegant," Lior said. "I'd have drawn it differently."

Something flickered across Reiss's face — not anger. Interest. He came back to the table and sat, and now the performance fell away like a coat, and what remained was colder and more dangerous: a competent man tired of competence wasted on theater.

"Here is the exchange," Reiss said. "You give me the network. The smuggler Ferrand, her contacts on the Corvane, whoever fed you the names and columns, wherever your forged stock originates. You give me these, and by morning tide you walk out of this building a free man with a work permit for any coastal posting you choose. Not a prisoner. A *draftsman of record*. Your sister and her daughter remain untouched — I give you my word, entered into this file, witnessed." He touched the folder. "Or you give me nothing, and I enter your name in a different column. You've seen my columns, Master Oyelaran. You redraw them often enough."

Lior looked at the candles. Their flames stood perfectly upright, twin blades of yellow, and in them he saw reflected — tiny, inverted — the grey sheeting over the windows, the deal table, the whole dim machinery of the state that had come to sort his town like cargo.

He thought about Maren's face in the doorway. About Isolde's hand steady on the pen three nights ago, the girl's tongue caught between her teeth exactly as Aldric's had been, drawing the breathing line. About Odile Ferrand saying, *a berth costs what it costs,* and about the single berth remaining, and about the arithmetic of love that no column could hold.

And beneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, he felt the thing he had carried for twenty years rise up and clothe him: the silence. Not emptiness — never emptiness — but a silence *full* of names. Aldric's margins. The families who had gone down to the boats in the dark decades ago, whose names the old man had coded in flourishes and dot-rules because paper must testify but must also protect. Lior had learned silence the way other men learn a trade, stroke by stroke, year by year, until it was not an absence in him but a structure, load-bearing, weatherproof. They could burn the studio. They could burn the atlas. They could put him on a train east. The silence would hold.

"My sister," Lior said carefully, "has committed no offense that I'm aware of. She arrived three weeks ago with her daughter. Her papers were in order."

"Were they." Reiss's voice was silk over stone. "Her husband's file crossed my desk in the autumn. Her name appears in a denunciation signed in Harrowmere province. You didn't know that?" He watched Lior with genuine curiosity, and Lior gave him nothing — gave him the mild, faintly puzzled face of a man hearing gossip about strangers. Maren had confessed it herself, weeping, weeks ago; the knowledge had long since calcified into something Reiss could not excavate.

"I barely know my sister," Lior said. "We were estranged. You'd know that from the neighbors' statements. Everyone in Vellamare knows we were estranged."

"Everyone in Vellamare knows a great many convenient things." Reiss exhaled through his nose. Then, abruptly, he changed registers — leaned back, rubbed his eyes, and for a moment looked merely like a tired archivist in a cold room. "Do you know what exhausts me about this posting, Master Oyelaran? Not the resistance. Resistance is legible. Men throw bombs, I arrest men. No — what exhausts me is *craftsmanship in the wrong place*. You people love your little city, your little guild, your beautiful useless maps, and you defend it all not with weapons but with *penmanship*. And penmanship, unlike bombs, leaves no smoke. Only evidence. Evidence I must chase stroke by stroke through eleven days of coastline." He picked up one of the transit papers again and regarded the breathing line with something perilously close to admiration. "It's a lovely hand. Whoever taught you — Vane, I assume — taught you superbly. And it will hang you."

"It wasn't my hand," Lior said again, in exactly the same tone.

Reiss studied him for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, dry, without humor. "You know, I interviewed a fisherman last month. Four hours. He gave me nothing. Not silence — worse than silence. He gave me *weather*. Tides, winds, the price of herring in 1933. I broke three men that week and could not break one fisherman, because there was nothing in him to break. He had already decided what he was willing to lose, and once a man has decided that, Master Oyelaran, he is a closed book written in a cipher I don't possess." He gathered the papers back into their folder with precise, unhurried movements. "I had hoped you would be more legible than the fisherman. You're not. You're worse. You're a mapmaker — you know exactly where every line in yourself runs."

* * *

But Reiss was not finished, and Lior had known he would not be, and this was the part Lior had rehearsed hardest — not the refusal, which cost him nothing now, but the gift, which had to look like collapse.

Reiss opened a drawer and produced a second folder, thinner, dog-eared. "Before you decide, consider this. We recovered materials from your studio during the search following the fire. Chart stock. Inks. A quantity of prepared paper bearing pre-printed border rules — unfinished documents, in effect. Blank instruments waiting to become anything." He laid a photograph on the table: Lior's own workroom, ransacked, drawers gutted. "The inventory is incomplete. By my count, several sheets of finished stock and at least one bound notebook are unaccounted for. Where are they?"

And there it was — the door, held open exactly wide enough.

Lior let his composure crack. Not much; a man of his age and training would not shatter. But he let the fatigue show, the gray dawn-hour sag of a man who has been awake too long and afraid too long, and he let a silence pass that could be read either as loyalty or as exhaustion, and then he said, quietly:

"If I tell you where they are — the materials, the cache — does that buy my sister's name out of your columns?"

Reiss went very still. It was the stillness of a fisherman feeling the line go taut.

"That depends on what you tell me."

"The old boathouse on the salt marsh road. Past the ruined customs post. There's a false floor under the tar barrel — Vane built it, years ago, before either of us understood how bad things would get. Stock, inks, a notebook of route memoranda. I haven't been near it since the occupation; I was too frightened to move it and too frightened to leave it, so I left it." He heard his own voice roughen, and did not correct it, because the roughness was real — grief always was, whatever it pretended to be about. "Take it. Take all of it. I want my sister's name clean. That's the trade."

Reiss wrote it down in a small black notebook, unhurried, each letter placed like a stone. When he finished he did not look up.

"You understand that this confession is not absolution."

"I understand that it's the truth."

"It is a truth," Reiss agreed mildly, capping his pen. "One of several available to you, I suspect. But it will be verified within the hour, and if it verifies, your sister's file will note your cooperation." He rose. The interview was ending; the candles had burned down to thumb-height puddles, and somewhere above them the painted constellations of the southern trades wheeled invisibly in the dark. "Sergeant will take you home. Consider our earlier conversation ongoing, Master Oyelaran. Networks have a way of surfacing. When one surfaces in your hand, you'll want to be the first to bring it to me — for your sister's sake. For the girl's."

At the door, Lior stopped. He did not turn around.

"The boathouse floor," he said, "is rotten in the northwest corner. Send a heavy man."

Then the sergeant's hand was on his shoulder, and the corridor swallowed him, and behind him the two candles guttered and righted themselves and went on burning down toward nothing.

* * *

The walk home took twelve minutes. Lior spent all of them composing his face.

The boathouse was real — Vane had built the hiding place, though what lay beneath the tar barrel now was a crate of rotted netting, a dead gull Lior had planted at midnight two nights ago, and forty sheets of chart stock printed with deliberately corrupted coordinates that would poison any military chart drawn from them. Let Reiss's cartographers inherit a coastline that did not exist. Let the regime's ships hunt for shoals that were open water and sail confidently into water that was not. The decoy had cost him three nights of forgery and one nearly unbearable hour crouched in the marsh reeds; it would buy him, he hoped, days. Perhaps a week. Long enough.

Because Tomas's warning rang in him still, tolling like a cracked bell: *not a ship, a train.* The list of unreliables. The transport east. Time had collapsed to the width of a few tides.

Maren was awake when he came in — of course she was — standing at the window with the oilcloth-wrapped bundle that was no longer the atlas, had not been the atlas for two days, holding it against her chest like a second child. Isolde slept in the corner, curled around her satchel the way she had slept every night since the quay, one hand outside the blanket, pen-callused, ready.

"Well?" Maren whispered.

"He took the bait." Lior hung his coat on its nail with hands that were finally, honestly, shaking. "The boathouse. By morning he'll have his soldiers knee-deep in marsh mud hauling up dead gulls and poisoned charts, and he'll be so busy deciding whether I betrayed the network or saved it that he won't watch the water." He looked at the bundle in his sister's arms — the code key, the blank stock, the margin-cipher pressed flat and innocent among Reiss's own confiscated supplies, smuggled out of the studio inside an army crate, hidden in the enemy's pocket like a lantern in a wolf's belly. "Which means tomorrow night is the last night anything can move. Odile sails on the morning tide after next."

"And the berth?"

Lior did not answer. Through the gap in the shuttered window, far off, he could hear the sea working at the harbor wall, patient as paper, keeping its own ledger of everyone who had ever left by night — and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that Reiss's men would be counting heads on that quay, and that the arithmetic had grown crueler overnight.

Somewhere across the dark city, in the requisitioned guildhall, Captain Reiss blew out his candles and opened the black notebook, and wrote a single line beneath the boathouse entry: *Cooperates too smoothly. Verify twice.*
