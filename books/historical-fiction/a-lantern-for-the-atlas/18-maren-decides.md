## 18. Maren Decides

Maren heard it first from the fishwife on the corner, who had heard it from a boy who ran messages for the garrison, and by the time the story reached her third mouth it had already grown teeth. *The mapmaker's brother talked. The mapmaker's brother is broken.* She stood in the doorway of Lior's house with a basket of bread she had not asked for and did not want, and let the words settle into her like stones into a well.

Broken. She turned the word over as she climbed the stairs to the attic, because there were kinds of broken and she knew them all now, had catalogued them these past weeks the way her brother catalogued coastlines — the breaking that came from fists, the breaking that came from fear, and the worst kind, the slow breaking that came from loving someone in a town full of soldiers. She had done her own breaking quietly, alone, in the dark of this attic where she slept now with her daughter one floor below the maps that might kill them all.

Lior was at the worktable when she came up, and she saw it immediately, the way you see a storm front over water before you can name it weather. His hands were steady — his hands were always steady, God help him, even now — but they lay flat on the table as if he had set them down and forgotten how to lift them. There was a bruise along his jaw going yellow-green at the edges, days old, that he had told her was a fall on the stairs. His left eye still carried a faint swell. And around him the room had the particular emptiness of a place where something had been removed: no atlas on the table, no tools laid out for the morning's work, nothing.

"They took you again," she said. Not a question. "After Tomas came back. After we knew about Reiss's list."

Lior did not look up. "It's handled."

"Handled." She set the basket down harder than she meant to. "Your face is handled. Your hands are shaking where they're pressed flat so I won't see them shake. Lior. Look at me."

He looked at her then, and what she saw in his face undid something in her chest that she suspected would never be mended properly. It wasn't pain. Pain she could have borne, could have raged against, could have shouted at until the rafters rang. It was the absence of pain — a flatness, a smoothness, like a coastline drawn by someone who has decided the harbors are no longer worth charting. Twenty years of silence had taught him to make his face into blank paper, and last night, or the night before, someone had finally written on it.

"Reiss showed me my own line-weight," Lior said quietly. "On the transit papers. He's known for some time, I think. He offered me the network in exchange for walking out of the guildhall a free man." A pause, and Maren watched him choose honesty the way other men chose weapons — deliberately, knowing the cost. "I gave him a decoy cache instead. An empty cellar on the Salt Row. He'll find it empty tomorrow and know I lied, and he doesn't care, because it was never about the cache."

"Then what is it about?"

"Holding me." He said it simply, the way he said everything now, all the terrible arithmetic reduced to its plainest terms. "I'm to be kept regardless. There's a transport forming. East. For the unreliables — the ones whose names survived the fire, the ones Reiss marked himself. My name will be on it whether I talk or not. Whether I confess or not. I stopped being useful and started being evidence, and evidence goes where evidence goes."

Maren sat down across from him because her legs informed her they were finished standing. The candle between them guttered in a draft from the shutters and neither of them moved to shield it.

"When?" she asked.

"Odile says the Corvane sails on the morning tide. Day after tomorrow."

"And the berth?"

"One berth." He folded his hands. "After the atlas went to the buyer abroad, after Odile took her price and paid her crew, one berth remains. I've been doing sums since they brought me back. Maren—"

"Don't," she said.

"You have a mark against your name. You signed their paper, under duress, but signed, and Reiss keeps duplicates of everything, and if you stay—"

"I said don't." Her voice came out low and strange to her own ears, and she realized her hands had closed into fists on the table's edge, knuckles white, and that she was leaning toward him across the candle like a woman across a bargaining table. "You sit there with your face beaten soft and your name on a train east, and you are going to do sums at me. You are going to count yourself out first, aren't you. That's why your hands are flat on the table. You've already decided it's between Isolde and me, and you've already decided it isn't you, and you think you're being noble."

"Maren—"

"Our father taught us both to read charts," she said, and her voice cracked on *father*, and she let it crack, let him hear it. "He taught you ink and he taught me arithmetic, and I am telling you your sums are wrong. If you stay, you die on a train or in a cell or against a wall in some yard at dawn, and everything you know dies with you, and everything Aldric bled into those margins dies with you, and then Reiss wins not just our town but the *knowledge*, the whole inheritance, twenty years of your life spent keeping a flame alive so that it could go out in a rail yard? No. No, Lior. Listen to me. I have been silent in this family long enough. I burned my papers and I ran and I signed their filthy denunciation because they had my husband's name in one column and my daughter's in another and I was given a pen and no choice, and I have hated myself for it every hour since, and I will not hate myself for this too. This one thing I will do right."

He was watching her now with something that was almost fear, this brother who had faced down an army clerk without blinking, and she understood suddenly that he had never once seen her ferocious. He had seen her grieving, resentful, distant, proud. He had never seen what she looked like when the last soft thing was stripped away.

"The berth belongs to Isolde," Maren said.

"She's fourteen."

"She's fourteen, and she deciphered Aldric's code in an afternoon while you needed years, and she forged papers with a steadier hand than yours, and she carried the atlas wrapped in oilcloth past a checkpoint without breathing hard. She is the next apprentice whether anyone names her so or not. She is the atlas now, Lior. You said it yourself — the idea survives the man. Well. Then let the man go down and the idea sail." Her throat closed and she pushed through it. "And I am her mother. Whatever else I've lost — her father, my name, my town, every scrap of respect I once had for myself — I have not lost the right to say where my child goes when the ships are leaving. That right is mine. Not yours. Yours."

For a long moment neither spoke. The candle steadied. Somewhere below, in the street, boots passed, unhurried, four men or six, the sound Vellamare had learned to sleep through and would never sleep through again.

"You'd stay here," Lior said at last. "With me. Knowing what's coming."

"I know exactly what's coming." She reached across the table and took one of his flat, steady hands and unfolded it, and held it, and felt the tremor he had hidden all evening finally arrive where it could be felt and not seen. "I buried a husband already. Do you imagine a train frightens me more than that did? I stayed beside him for three days while the fever took him apart, and I would have stayed three years. Staying is the one thing I have ever been good at, little brother. Let me be good at it one more time."

"It isn't sacrifice if it's just surrender," Lior said hoarsely.

"It isn't surrender." She smiled, and felt the smile come out wrong and fierce and true. "It's a decision. The first free decision I've made since they put that pen in my hand. They took everything else by force or fraud; this I give, and giving it, I take something back. Do you understand? Every column in that ledger, every category they invented to sort us — mother, widow, unreliable, denouncer — none of it survives what I'm about to do. On the quay, when the time comes, I will lift my daughter onto that ship myself, and I will tell her it is not abandonment, it is commissioning, and she will believe me because I will make it true. And you—" her grip tightened on his hand, "—you will get on that ship behind her, because I am not spending my daughter's whole life carrying the atlas alone when her uncle lives and breathes and draws. Both of you go. That is the shape of it. That is final."

"Odile said one berth."

"Odile said one berth *at the price she named*. Prices change. Captains change their minds. You are the man who forged half the town's transit papers in a single night — forge the arithmetic too. Find a way. Bribe a mate, sweeten Odile, stow her in the chart room among the oilcloth, I don't care how, but you will both be on the tide, and I will stand on the quay and watch you go, and it will be the proudest hour of my ruined life." She was crying now, she realized, without any of it reaching her voice, tears simply falling as she spoke, as if grief and resolve had finally agreed to share the same body. "I argued with you for twenty years over your silences. I resented every year you stayed here ink-stained and dutiful while I was inland raising a child alone. I said cruel things. Some of them I meant. And the whole time, you were building the only boat out of this town that ever mattered. So the war between us ends tonight, on my side of it, however you carry yours. I decide. She goes. You go. I stay. And if you argue with me, Lior Oyelaran, I will tell Isolde every embarrassing story from your childhood, including the one about the harbor eel, and I will tell it loudly, on the quay, in front of soldiers."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh in a better year, wet and broken, and his hand turned under hers and gripped back, and that was the treaty, unsigned and unwitnessed, sealed in candle smoke in an attic above a conquered town.

* * *

She found Isolde after midnight, sitting cross-legged on her pallet with the code key — Lior's copy, smuggled and recopied in a child's careful hand — propped against her knees, mouthing the cipher symbols to herself the way other children mouthed prayers. In the small light of a shuttered lantern she looked, for a moment, exactly like her uncle at that age: the same stubborn brow, the same hunger in the eyes when the shapes began to mean something.

Maren sat down beside her without asking. Isolde glanced up, wary — she had learned wariness young, these past months — but did not close the book.

"Read to me," Maren said. "Whatever it is. Read it aloud."

Isolde hesitated, then translated, haltingly, a passage from the margins: names, dates, tides. People who had escaped by sea thirty years ago, recorded in invisible sympathy by a dead man who had refused to burn his own conscience. Maren listened with her arm around her daughter and thought: *this is what they cannot burn. Not paper. This.*

"Mother? What's wrong?"

"Nothing is wrong." She smoothed the girl's hair, feeling the skull beneath it, warm and alive and utterly unaware. "Something is right, for once. Something is very right, and it's going to stay right, and I need you to promise me you'll remember this room when the sea gets rough. Attics, and candles, and people who loved you enough to quarrel about you."

"Quarrel about me?"

"Tomorrow," Maren said. "Sleep now."

* * *

At dawn she went to find Odile Ferrand herself.

The smuggler was mending net outside the Anchor's Rest, hands moving with the bored competence of a woman who had heard every plea a drowning town could produce. She looked up as Maren approached, and her eyes — quick, grey, unfooled — went over Maren's face and read the whole night there.

"You've decided," Odile said.

"The berth is Isolde's. And Lior goes with her. Both, or I start telling people what you charge, Ferrand, and who you charge it to, and how much of it stays in Vellamare."

Odile's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "The captain named one berth."

"The captain isn't sailing the ship. You are." Maren crouched to the older woman's level, forearms on knees, close enough to smell tar and salt and the iron underneath. "My brother paid your price. He gave up the work of his life so strangers could flee this town, and now the army means to put him on a cattle car east, and you're going to stand there and count berths at me? Find the second berth. Squeeze the mate, lie to the harbormaster, sell your own boots. I don't care. But hear me, Odile Ferrand, and hear me clearly: if my brother sets foot on that quay and watches that ship leave without him, I will spend whatever hours I have left making sure every smuggler from here to the Straits knows that Ferrand's word costs nothing and holds less. Your trade runs on trust. So does mine."

The net-mending had stopped. Odile studied her for a long moment, and Maren met it without flinching, thinking absurdly of her father teaching her sums with dried beans on a kitchen table: *count everything twice, girl, and then count what can't be counted.*

"You argue like your brother draws," Odile said at last. "Every line load-bearing." She resumed her mending, eyes on the knots. "There's a berth in the aft hold where the survey crates go. Cramped, dark, smells of bilge. The mate owes me and hates Reiss. It'll cost — not coin. I'll want the code key copied before you sail. All of it."

"You'll have it."

"Then it's done. Girl in the cabin, brother in the hold." Odile pulled a knot tight and finally looked up, and something in her face had softened by a degree, no more. "And you? Where does the fierce sister stand while they sail?"

Maren rose. Down the street, the garrison drums were beating the morning muster, and beyond the rooftops the harbor lay grey and waiting, the Corvane's masts visible above the warehouse line like bare trees before spring.

"On the quay," she said. "Where mothers stand."
