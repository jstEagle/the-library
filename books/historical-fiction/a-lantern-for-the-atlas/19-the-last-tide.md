## 19. The Last Tide

The harbor clock had not struck four when Lior woke, though he had not truly slept. He lay in the dark of the studio with his coat still on, listening to the sound that had kept him company all night: the sea, working at the pilings below the cliff road, patient as a man counting coins. Somewhere out in the black water the *Corvane* rode at anchor beyond the mole, her riding lights burning low, and somewhere in the town Captain Reiss was awake too — Lior was certain of it — sitting in the requisitioned guildhall with his duplicate ledger and his lists, waiting for the morning tide the way a spider waits without hurrying.

He rose and did not light a lamp. Twenty years of working this attic by feel had taught him where everything lived: the oilcloth folded on the third shelf, the waxed thread, the knife with the bone handle that Aldric Vane had used to trim vellum before Lior was born. He wrapped the atlas slowly, turning it as he went so that every board of it took its layer of cloth, the way one swaddles a child against cold. It weighed more than a book had any right to weigh. It always had. Tonight it felt like carrying a sleeping man.

Maren was already downstairs. She stood at the window of the front room with her shawl drawn tight, watching the searchlights sweep the rooftops, and she did not turn when he came down with the bundle under his arm.

"Isolde's dressed," she said. "She hasn't slept either. She's been sitting on the edge of the bed since two o'clock, waiting for us to tell her it was time."

"Then it's time."

They walked through streets that no longer belonged to them. That was the strangest thing, Lior thought — how quickly a town could become a foreign country inside its own skin. The shuttered bakery where Tomas had sold bread for thirty years now bore a stenciled number. The fountain in the Fishmarket Square had been drained because standing water gathered crowds, someone had explained, as if water were a kind of assembly. Boots rang on the cobbles ahead of them and behind them, always just out of sight, keeping them between patrols like beads on a string.

At the mouth of Quay Lane a searchlight caught them, held them, let them go. Maren's hand found his sleeve and gripped it.

"They're checking papers at the customs shed," she whispered. "Odile said the east gate. She said the east gate would be open."

"Odile says many things. Some of them are even true."

"Don't." Maren's voice cracked on it, quiet as it was. "Not tonight. Tonight I need you to believe her, because I can't."

The quay opened before them like a wound. Searchlights crossed and recrossed it in long white blades; soldiers moved in pairs along the mole; and everywhere, pressed into the shadows between the warehouses, were people — dozens of them, hundreds, it seemed, families crouched around bundles, an old man holding a birdcage with the cloth still over it, a woman walking a crying child up and down the stones in small circles, everyone silent or nearly silent, because silence had become the only citizenship left to them. Beyond the mole, riding lights. Beyond the riding lights, black water. Beyond the black water — Lior made himself think it plainly — the world.

The *Corvane* was taking people aboard by twos and threes, at the far end of the quay where the customs shed threw its shadow. A soldier there checked names against a list, and beside him Odile Ferrand stood with her hands in her coat pockets, wearing the expression of a woman supervising cargo she had already been paid for. When she saw Lior she did not wave. She looked past him, counted something, looked back. Then she tilted her chin toward the shed's blind side, once.

They crossed the open stones fast. Lior kept his body between the searchlights and the bundle under his arm, an instinct so old it seemed to come from his bones rather than his mind. At the shadow line Odile met them, and up close her face was grey with a tiredness that had nothing to do with sleep.

"One berth," she said, without greeting. "I told you what the buyer paid for the atlas. Three berths, I said, and I got you three, and then the navy requisitioned two of them at gunpoint an hour ago, and I am telling you now, Oyelaran, so that you hear it from me and not from the empty deck: there is one berth left on my ship."

"I know," Lior said. "You sent word."

"And your sister knows, and the girl knows, and none of you has settled it, because none of you will say it aloud." Odile's eyes went from him to Maren and back. "So I'll say it. One berth. Choose before the tide does it for you. The mate gives me until the first bell, and after that I fill it from the crowd behind me, because I am not sailing to a free port with empty bunks while these people drown here. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every minute costs someone."

"We understand," Maren said.

"Then understand faster." And Odile turned away to shout softly at a man with a handcart, already elsewhere, already spent.

Isolde was waiting where they had arranged, in the lee of the net-loft, and the sight of her undid something in Lior that he had been holding shut for three days. Fourteen years old, in a coat too big for her — his old coat, the one with the ink stains at the cuff that no amount of washing would ever lift — with the family bundle at her feet and her chin up the way Aldric used to raise his chin when a difficult customer tried to haggle over a chart. She was trying to look unafraid. She was failing, and she knew she was failing, and she lifted her chin higher anyway.

"You have it?" she asked. Her voice came out steady. He loved her for the effort more than the result.

Lior knelt and unwrapped the top folds of the oilcloth, just enough. The atlas showed its spine in the dark, the boards scarred and mended, the title tooled in gold that had gone dull twenty years ago. Isolde put her hand flat on the cover the way you'd touch the flank of a horse you were about to trust your life to.

"It's heavier than I remembered," she said.

"That's the names. Paper doesn't weigh much. Names do."

She almost smiled. "Page ninety-one is loose at the gutter. I re-sewed it Tuesday with the waxed thread, the way you showed me. If anyone opens it they'll find the stitching is older than the rest — I rubbed it with tea and bent the holes with the awl so it looks worn." She stopped. In the sweep of a distant searchlight her face went white and resolute at once. "Uncle Lior. Tell me you checked my sewing."

"I checked your sewing. It's better than mine was at fourteen. It's nearly better than mine is now."

"Nearly," she repeated, savoring it, hoarding it, a last coin of ordinary life to carry across the water. Then the smile went out like a lamp, and she looked past him to where her mother stood apart, facing the sea. "Which one of us?"

"Isolde—"

"No." She rounded on him, and for a moment she was not fourteen at all but some older, harder thing wearing her face. "Don't do the thing where you decide things in silence and we find out afterward. Not tonight. Mama signed their paper — she told us, she told us everything, she carried that shame here in her own hands — and you stayed twenty years and learned to keep quiet and neither of you will ever choose yourselves. So it has to be said out loud, tonight, on this quay, or it never gets said." Her breath shook. "Take me or take her. But say which."

From the customs shed came the sound of voices rising — a man protesting, a rifle butt knocking wood, the particular hush that fell over a crowd when violence stood up among it. The first bell did not ring yet, but Lior could feel it coming, gathering in the dark the way the tide gathers under a hull.

"Maren," he said quietly. "Come here."

His sister came. She had heard everything; she had been built to hear everything, his sister, all her life, the way some people are built to see in the dark. She stood before them with the wind pulling at her shawl and the searchlights making her shadow long and thin on the stones, and she said, before any of them could speak:

"It's Isolde."

"Maren—" Lior began.

"It's Isolde, and I will stand here and say it until the bell rings, and if you put yourself on that ship instead of her I will walk up to that soldier at the shed and give him my name and my daughter's name both, and you know I will, because I have already betrayed everyone I love once, and I know exactly how it's done." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about Maren; she never raised her voice; she simply became impossible to move, like a door grown into its frame. "Twenty years ago you gave your whole life to an old man's work and I called you a coward for it, and I was wrong, and I have had months to learn how wrong. But I will not be wrong twice. The work goes with her. The names go with her. You taught her the code yourself — she reads Aldric's margins better than you do, you've said so, you've said it with such pride you didn't hear what you were saying. She isn't our child to keep, Lior. She's the lantern. You carry the lantern toward the light or you don't, and I am telling you — I, who buried myself alive in the inland provinces and burned my own name off my own papers — I am telling you how it ends if you don't."

The tide was turning. He could hear it change below the quay, the long suck and shift of it, the sea collecting its fee.

Lior looked at his niece. She was crying now, silently, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as if tears were an error in drafting she refused to leave on the page. And he understood, with the cold clarity that had come to him over Reiss's charts and never left, that Maren was right, and that being right cost precisely everything, and that there was no version of this night in which he did not spend himself. There had never been. The atlas had cost him Maren once, twenty years of silences, a life lived in an attic. It was fitting, in the way grief is fitting, that it should cost him the rest.

"All right," he said.

Isolde made a sound that was half sob and half nothing at all.

"All right," he said again, and knelt, and finished wrapping the atlas, and bound it with the waxed thread, and tied it across her chest under the big coat, snug against her heart where a soldier's patting hands would find only a girl's thin ribs and a schoolgirl's bundle of clothes. He worked with his fingers and did not let them shake. Later there would be time to shake. There was no later he could count on, actually, and so he shook anyway, a little, and let her see it, because she deserved to know that courage was not the absence of trembling but the work done while trembling.

"Listen to me." He took her face in both hands. "The buyer is a man named Anselm Rouch, at the sign of the Blue Meridian, in Corvane's home port — Odile will give you the street. Show him the margins first, not the maps. Any fool buys maps. He's paying for the margins. And the code key —" he pressed a folded square of paper into her palm, small as a stamp — "you memorize it tonight and you burn it tomorrow, and after that it lives nowhere but in you. You are the key now. Do you understand? Not the paper. You."

"Yes," she whispered.

"And when you get there — when you're safe, when the fear has gone off you like a fever — you finish it. Page one-forty is blank. The last coastline. Aldric left it blank on purpose, he told me once: a map is never finished, it's only abandoned. Don't abandon it. Finish it. Put the new harbors in. Put the new names in the margins, in his code, the ones who escape after us, because there will be ones after us, Isolde, there are always ones after us —" His voice broke and he hated it and let it break. "And write our names. Yours, your mother's, mine. Write them where the fools can't read them and the free ones can."

"I will," she said. "I swear it on the ink."

On the ink. Their old oath, hers and his, from the attic, from the candle-lit nights of forging and fear. He laughed — a broken, wet, astonished laugh — and pulled her to him, and felt her arms go around him hard enough to hurt, and thought: this is what I kept. This. Not the atlas. This.

The bell rang.

Odile materialized out of the dark the way she always did, took Isolde's bundle in one hand and her elbow in the other, and said, briskly, cruelly kindly, "Walk, don't run, look bored, you're a clerk's daughter going aboard with dispatches, and don't look back — no, listen to me, girl, don't look back, looking back is how they remember faces." She glanced at Lior over the child's head, and something passed between them that had no words in any language spoken above a whisper: a debt acknowledged, a debt refused, a debt that would outlive them both. "Rouch. Blue Meridian. Margins first."

Then Isolde was walking away down the quay between crates and coils of rope, small and straight-backed under the too-big coat, and Lior stood with Maren's hand crushing his arm, and did not look back either, because his niece had been given an order and someone in this family had to keep discipline even now.

They heard the mate call a name that wasn't hers. They heard boots on a gangplank. They heard — faintly, terribly, wonderfully — nothing else at all.

The tide turned fully in the hour before dawn, and the *Corvane* took it.

They watched from the end of the breakwater, where the patrol had swept past and would not return for a quarter hour — Odile's quarter hour, bought and paid for. The ship cast off in stages, fenders in, lines coiled, her engine muttering like an old man disagreeing with the weather. A searchlight found her and slid along her hull, and Lior's whole body clenched, and the light passed on, finding nothing but a tramp steamer with a legal manifest and a bored crew, carrying, among its listed cargo of salt fish and machine parts, one fourteen-year-old girl with a nation's memory strapped to her chest.

Maren stood beside him with her hands folded at her waist, very straight, watching the riding lights shrink. She did not weep. He understood that she had done her weeping in advance, all of it, over years, in inland rooms he had never seen, and that what remained was a kind of flinty calm he recognized from mirrors.

"She'll finish page one-forty," Maren said.

"She'll finish it better than I would have."

"Everything about this is worse than it should be," Maren said, "and she is better than we were. Both things are true. I've decided to live inside both."

The *Corvane* cleared the mole. For a moment she hung against the greying east, black on pearl, her funnel smoke bending seaward — and then the light strengthened by a single degree, the way dawn does, grudgingly, and she was gone, absorbed into the horizon the way a name is absorbed into a margin.

The searchlights clicked off one by one, suddenly pointless, suddenly theatrical. The quay filled with grey morning and gulls and the ordinary sounds of a port waking to a day that belonged to soldiers. Somewhere behind them in the town, Lior knew, Reiss was reading his duplicate ledger, running his finger down columns, arriving — perhaps this morning, perhaps the next — at the name Oyelaran, draftsman, unreliable, and placing it in the column that led east to trains instead of west to ships.

Maren took his hand. Her grip was dry and strong and final.

"Well," she said. "We stay."

"We stay," Lior said.

And below them, against the pilings, the sea went on working, patient as ever, carrying on its long gray back one small departing light and all the names it could hold — and Lior Oyelaran, master of blank paper, turned from the water and walked back into the burning town with his sister at his side and nothing left to lose except exactly the things that could not be taken: the method, the memory, the code, the craft. Somewhere out on that water a girl was unwrapping oilcloth. Somewhere ahead of all of them, a lantern waited to be lit.

Behind them, at the head of Quay Lane, boots were already ringing on the cobbles, coming their way.
