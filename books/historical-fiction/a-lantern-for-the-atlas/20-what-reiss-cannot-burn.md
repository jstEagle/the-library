## 20. What Reiss Cannot Burn

They came for him at the hour when the gulls went quiet.

Lior had expected it since the night the guildhall burned — had felt it in his bones the way a mapmaker feels a storm three days out at sea, in the ache of old scars and the smell of the air. When the knock came, two soldiers and a sergeant with a clipboard, he was already dressed. He had slept in his clothes. On the table beside the cold stove lay a cup of tea gone grey and bitter, untouched since midnight, when he had finally admitted to himself that sleep would not come and that there was no more work left to pretend at.

"Lior Oyelaran." The sergeant did not make it a question. "You are to accompany us. Captain Reiss's orders."

"And my sister?" Lior asked. "My niece?"

"The women remain under watch. You are charged under Article Fourteen — falsification of state records, obstruction of a lawful requisition, and harboring materials belonging to the Crown." The sergeant read it flatly, as though reciting a shopping list, and perhaps to him that was all it was. "You may bring nothing."

"I understand," Lior said.

And he did understand — better than the sergeant could ever know. Because the thing Reiss wanted most in this world, the thing that had kept the captain pacing his candlelit office these past two nights, was not in this house. It had not been in this house for eleven days.

* * *

The walk down through Vellamare was a funeral procession without a body. Lior had lived forty-six years in these streets and had never seen them so empty. Shutters closed like eyes. A cat sitting on a wall with the stillness of something that had learned what boots meant. At the corner by the chandler's, where Tomas used to mend nets and complain about the price of tar, the wall bore the pale rectangle of a torn-down requisition order, and beneath it someone had scratched, small and desperate, a single word in the local dialect: *stay*. Whether it was a plea or an instruction, Lior could not tell, and he thought that was rather the point of everything now.

The harbor opened before him like a wound that would not close. The Corvane was four days gone, swallowed by the morning fog on the last tide, and the quay where she had lain stood bare and scoured, as if the sea itself were ashamed of what it had failed to carry away. A troop transport sat at the far berth instead, gray and graceless, its crane swinging crates of requisitioned goods into its hold — the town's copper, the town's clocks, the town's ledgers, packed in straw like corpses prepared for shipping.

At the head of the quay stood the guildhall, and Lior stopped walking despite the sergeant's hand between his shoulder blades, because the guildhall was a ruin.

The fire had taken the roof and half the eastern wall. Charred beams jutted against the pale sky like the ribs of some beached leviathan. Soot blackened the stone in great weeping streaks. Soldiers picked through the wreckage with long-handled rakes, sorting ash into piles, and the smell of burnt paper still hung over everything — that particular sweet-sour stink of ink and vellum and a hundred years of records surrendering to flame. Somewhere in those piles, if one believed the official account, lay the ashes of every family name in Vellamare: the births, the marriages, the debts, the departures. All of it rendered into identical gray powder, beyond sorting, beyond reading, beyond use.

Reiss stood at the edge of the ruin, hands clasped behind his back, watching the rakes work. He turned when they brought Lior near, and his face wore the expression Lior had come to know best over these weeks — the patient, courteous face of a man who believed that everything, given time, could be filed.

"Master Oyelaran." A small nod, almost respectful. "You'll want to see this, I think. Before we proceed."

He gestured toward the ashes, and Lior understood that this was the morning's theater: here is your work, here is your town's memory, here is what disobedience buys. Reiss wanted him to see the ledger burned. Wanted him to understand that the regime's memory was now the only memory, and that it had survived the fire because it had never been in the building at all.

"It's a shame," Lior said quietly. "That ledger was fine work. Good rag paper. It would have lasted another century."

Reiss's eyes narrowed a fraction. There it was again — that flicker of the man beneath the clerk, the sharp mind that had decoded Aldric's margins once already, that had held a forged transit paper to the light and known whose hand had made it. "Yes," Reiss said. "A shame. Though I suspect you take more comfort from it than grief."

"I take what's given."

* * *

The charge sheet was read to him in a room above the customs house, a room Lior knew well because he had drawn its windows once, years ago, for a survey of the harbor approaches commissioned by men who no longer existed. The walls had maps on them now — his own coast charts, corrected, annotated, the ones he had drafted under Reiss's eye with errors folded into them like seeds into bread. He looked at them while the sergeant read, and found it difficult to feel afraid of them anymore. They were lies wearing his linework, but lies have short lives when the truth has good handwriting.

He listened to the charges with the calm of a man hearing a weather report about a place he has already left.

Falsification of state records. The forged transit papers — Isolde's papers, Maren's, the neighbors' whose entries he had reclassified in the ledger before the fire. Obstruction. Harboring Crown property. That last one meant the atlas, of course. Reiss believed the atlas had gone to sea in oilcloth wrapped around a fourteen-year-old girl, and he was right, and he could prove nothing, and it was eating him alive. Lior could see it in the way the captain's fingers drummed once, twice, against the tabletop before going still.

"You will be detained pending tribunal," Reiss concluded. "The studio is forfeit. It will be searched thoroughly and then disposed of."

"Disposed of," Lior repeated.

"Burned. Tomorrow morning." Reiss said it watching him, watching for the flinch, and Lior gave him none — though inside, where no officer could reach, something twisted and tore. The attic where he had inked the final coastline after twenty years. The south window where the light came in gold at dusk and made every coastline look like a promise. The smell of gum arabic and lampblack and Aldric's pipe smoke that had soaked into the beams so deeply it would never leave, even if the beams themselves did.

"Of course," Lior said. "Paper burns easily. It's one of paper's virtues. It means a thing can travel light."

Something moved behind Reiss's eyes — suspicion reaching for a shape it couldn't quite hold. "Take him down."

* * *

They held him in a cell in the customs house cellar, which at least had a view — a slit of window at ground level, six inches of glass, through which he could see the feet of passing soldiers and, if he pressed his cheek to the cool stone, a sliver of the harbor and the sky above the breakwater. He had spent twenty years learning patience in an attic; a cell was only an attic with worse light.

Maren came the second day, escorted, permitted ten minutes across a table. She looked as though she hadn't slept since the Corvane sailed, and her hands, always so capable, lay flat on the wood as if she didn't trust them.

"They're saying tomorrow," she said. "The studio. Tomas heard it from a guard who owed him money."

"I know."

"Lior—" Her voice caught, and he watched her master it, watched her do what their mother used to do, press everything down behind the teeth and speak around it. "Isolde is at sea. Whatever happens to this house, to us—"

"She's carrying the atlas." He said it softly, so the guard by the door couldn't catch it. "Every name. Every route. Aldric's code, and mine now too. As long as that ship stays afloat, Vellamare remembers itself."

Maren's eyes were bright and furious and wet. "And you?"

"Me," Lior said, "I'm exactly where I need to be."

She didn't understand it — he saw that she didn't — and there was no time to explain, and some of it he could not have explained anyway, because it rested on a gamble he had made eleven days ago and would not know the outcome of until it was almost too late to matter. So he gripped his sister's hand across the table, hard, once, the way they had gripped each other as children crossing the shingle beach, and said only: "Watch the captain's supply wagons, when they come. And say nothing to anyone. Especially nothing clever."

"That's the first sensible thing you've ever told me."

"It won't be the last. Ten minutes is nearly up. Go home. Keep the kettle on."

* * *

On the third night, they let him have a candle.

It was Reiss's doing, Lior suspected — a small courtesy, or a test, or both. The guard set a stub of tallow on the floor of the cell, lit it, and withdrew, and Lior sat looking at the little flame for a long time. Then he took off his left boot, and from inside the tongue of it, where he had sewn a pocket with sail-thread during his last free evening, he drew a strip of paper no wider than his thumb.

He unrolled it carefully. In the candlelight, the tiny characters marched down the strip like ants: Aldric's code key, copied in Lior's own miniature hand — the substitution cipher, the tide-tables offset, the directional marks that turned ordinary margin flourishes into names and dates and destinations. Twenty years of escapes recorded in what any censor would read as decorative pen-work. And below Aldric's key, in fresh ink, the additions Lior himself had devised: three new symbols, three new rules. Enough for the next hand. Enough for whoever came after.

He had memorized it long ago, of course. Every line of it lived behind his eyes, in the same vaulted dark where he kept his master's voice and the sound of the south window and the weight of the atlas in his arms. Burning the paper would burn nothing that mattered.

But paper was not the point. Paper was never the point.

The point was the other thing. The thing hidden in plain sight, the way Aldric had taught him that all true hiding is done in full view — *the best concealment, boy, is a thing nobody thinks to look at twice.*

Three days before the arrest, when the summons had still been a rumor and the burning still a possibility he refused to name aloud, Lior had walked into Captain Reiss's own chart room carrying a wooden case under his arm. The guards had waved him through; he was the guild's draftsman, the army's hired hand, a man whose comings and goings were furniture. Inside the case, among the reams of chart stock requisitioned for military surveys, he had placed two dozen sheets of blank paper — heavy rag stock, the good kind, watermarked with nothing, waiting to become anything — and beneath the false bottom of the case, folded to the thickness of a thumbnail, the original code key in Aldric's own cramped, unmistakable hand.

Then he had stacked the case, neatly, with the others, in the corner of Reiss's chart room where supplies were kept, and dusted his hands, and gone home to wait.

Let them search his attic. Let them burn it. Let them sift the ashes of everything he loved and find nothing but charcoal and pipe-smoke and the ghost of a man who had died refusing. The key to every name, every escape, every future forgery — it sat now in a government store-room, stamped with an army inventory number, catalogued as *chart stock, assorted, 24 sheets*, guarded by the very men who wanted it destroyed. If the tribunal confiscated his effects, the case would go with them. If the army moved its offices, the case would move too. Wherever Reiss went, the seed went with him, carried in the enemy's own luggage, watered by the enemy's own bureaucracy.

Aldric would have laughed until he coughed.

* * *

They burned the studio on a Thursday, in the rain.

Lior watched from the customs house yard, flanked by guards, while the soldiers did their work with kerosene and efficiency. The flames fought the wet and won slowly, sullenly, sending up steam-white smoke that smelled of pitch and hot varnish and, faintly, impossibly, of pipe tobacco — twenty years of Aldric Vane rising off the beams in a final exhalation. The south window went first, bursting outward in a bloom of glittering drops, and Lior thought of all the dusks it had framed, all the gold light on unfinished coastlines, and he did not look away. He owed it that much. You watched a thing die properly or you had no right to remember it.

The neighbors were made to stand and watch too — he could see them along the quay, a thin line of hollow-eyed people, Maren among them with her arm through Tomas's. Rain ran down their faces and no one could have said how much of the wet was weather.

When the roof fell, the soldiers cheered. Someone played a tin whistle. It had the cheerful ugliness of all their ceremonies.

Lior stood in the rain and felt, beneath the grief, something harder and quieter settling into place — the feeling he had first known at nineteen, standing over Aldric's body, understanding that the old man had lost everything except the one thing that mattered, and that losing everything else had been the price of keeping it. The studio was ash. The atlas was at sea. The names were safe in a girl's oilcloth bundle and in the memories of everyone on that quay, and the key — the living, generative heart of it all — slept in an army crate, waiting.

*Burn the map,* Aldric used to say, tapping ash from his pipe. *The territory keeps its shape.*

* * *

They came for him again at dusk, and this time it was Reiss himself who unlocked the cell door, dismissing the guard with a flick of two fingers. He carried a lantern and a folder, and he set neither down. For a long moment he simply looked at Lior, and the look was different from all the previous looks — less the clerk, more the hunter who has run his quarry to earth and found the earth strangely empty.

"Your studio is gone," Reiss said.

"So I hear. It rained; I hope the smoke was tolerable."

"We found nothing." The words came out clipped, each one laid down like a card he resented playing. "No drafts. No notes. No correspondence. No trace of the atlas's preparation, no working copies, no cipher material. Twenty years of work, Master Oyelaran, and not one scrap of evidence that you ever lifted a pen."

"Perhaps I never did. Perhaps I only watched the sea."

"Don't." Reiss stepped closer, and the lantern threw his shadow huge and wavering up the cellar wall. "I have held your hand in my sight for weeks. I know your line-weight the way you know your own pulse. And I know a man who hides everything is a man who has hidden something *everywhere*." He paused, and his voice dropped, and for the first time Lior heard something beneath the certainty — not doubt exactly, but its first cousin. "Tomorrow you go to the tribunal. You will likely go east after that, on the transport, whatever the verdict. Most men would bargain tonight. Name the smuggler Ferrand. Tell me where the girl's ship will make landfall. Buy yourself a warm cell instead of a cold grave."

Lior rose slowly, joints stiff from stone floors, and met the captain's eyes across the little cell, and twenty years of practiced silence rose in him like a tide — the silence he had learned over a dying man, the silence he had kept through interrogations and inspections and every gray dawn since, silence not as emptiness but as architecture, load-bearing, built to hold the weight of everything he would not say.

"Captain," he said mildly, "you've searched my life and found it clean. Take the compliment."

For a moment Reiss said nothing at all. Then he turned to go, and at the doorway he stopped, without turning around.

"There's a supply inventory to reconcile before the transport east," he said. "Chart stock, mostly. Assorted lots from the guildhall salvage and the requisition stores. It moves with us when we go." A beat. "I mention it because you notice things, Oyelaran. It's your trade. I wonder what you'd notice, counting sheets."

The lantern light swung away up the stairs, taking the captain's shadow with it, and Lior stood alone in the dark with his heart beating slow and enormous, understanding two things at once: that Reiss knew — or half-knew, or suspected with that terrible clerical instinct of his — and that the game had changed. The captain was not offering a bargain. He was setting a trap with the bait of his own suspicion, or perhaps, in some buried chamber of himself that would never survive daylight, extending a hand.

Either way, the case was in motion. Either way, within days, the key and the blank stock would travel east in a government wagon, past checkpoints, into the interior, toward whatever waited there — and somewhere behind it, at sea, a fourteen-year-old girl was opening an oilcloth bundle by lamplight, learning her grandfather-master's alphabet, adding names to the margins in a steady young hand.

Lior sat back down on the cot and smiled in the dark, and it was not a defeated smile.

*Count the sheets,* he thought. *Oh, Captain. I intend to.*
