## 22. A Lantern for the Atlas

The harbor at Port Solenne never fully slept, but it dreamed differently in the hour before dawn — a slow breathing of water against stone, the creak of moored hulls, the far-off clank of a buoy bell that had been tolling since before Isolde Oyelaran was born and would go on tolling long after everyone who heard it now was gone. She climbed the tower stairs with the lantern unlit in her hand, as she did every morning of her life now, and paused on the landing where the window framed the sea.

Eleven years since Vellamare. Eleven years since the Corvane slipped out on the morning tide with a fourteen-year-old girl pressed against its rail, clutching an oilcloth bundle to her chest as though it were a sibling, watching two figures grow small on the quay — her uncle straight-backed even in defeat, her mother's hand raised in a wave that never lowered until the searchlights swallowed them both. Eleven years, and she could still summon the smell of that quay: tar, salt, diesel, fear. Fear had a smell. She had learned it young, the way other children learned the smell of bread baking.

She reached the lantern room and set the lamp down on its brass cradle. The keeper's post had come to her strangely, by way of a widow named Madame Corbeau who had taken in half the port's orphans during the war years and who had looked at Isolde's ink-stained fingers and said, *You'll want paper, not pity.* But the harbor needed tending too, and there was poetry in it that Isolde had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't stood where she stood now: every ship that came into Port Solenne came in guided by light she lit. Ships carrying people. Ships like the one that had carried her.

She struck the match and touched it to the wick, and the great lens caught the flame and threw it outward, a blade of gold sweeping across the water, over the breakwater, out to where the fishing fleet was already a scatter of dark shapes.

Then she went downstairs, past the keeper's cot and the coiled rope, to the small room at the tower's base where the printing press waited under its dust sheet, and where the atlas lay open on the table, finished at last.

* * *

It had taken nine years to complete.

She had not known, when the Corvane docked at Port Solenne and she stepped ashore with Aldric Vane's charts strapped to her back like wings, how much of the work remained. The atlas was magnificent and it was incomplete. Coastlines ended mid-stroke. Islands floated without soundings. And everywhere, in the margins, ran her uncle's handwriting in Aldric's code — names, dates, tide-tables of human lives, entries that stopped abruptly in the winter of 1938 as though the pen itself had been arrested.

For the first year she had simply copied. She rented a room above a chandler's shop, hung oilcloth over the window when she worked, and reproduced Lior's unfinished maps line by line, teaching herself his hand until her own disappeared inside it. The guild here — the Free Cartographers' Confraternity of Port Solenne, three old men and a woman who met above a tavern and argued about projections with a passion other men reserved for football — had examined her samples and gone very quiet, and then the eldest, Master Anselme, had said: *Who taught you this?* And she had said, *My uncle. He is in prison,* and the room had changed around her, the way rooms do when grief is recognized and given a chair.

They taught her what she lacked: the mathematics of latitude, the printing trade, the law of what could be published and when and under whose name. She taught them something too. She showed them the margins. She showed them the code — Aldric Vane's cipher, which she had cracked at fourteen in an attic in Vellamare while soldiers drilled in the square below, because a child reads secrets the way water finds cracks, without effort, without permission.

*These are people,* she told them, laying out the pages. *Every name. Everyone my master smuggled out by sea, twenty years before me. He hid them where no censor would think to look — in the decoration.*

Master Anselme had run his thumb along a margin, along the tiny stylized waves and compass roses that were not decoration at all but alphabet, and had wept without any change in his expression, tears simply falling from an otherwise composed face. Then he said: *Then the book must carry both names. The dead man who began it and the living man who gave everything for it. And yours, child. Yours as well. A book like this needs a hand that will keep adding.*

Isolde had refused her own name for six years. It was not modesty; it was arithmetic. Three names meant three people for the authorities to hunt, and she had spent her first years in Port Solenne learning what happened to families whose names appeared together in official files. Only after the amnesty, only after the last of Reiss's generation had been swept from the ministries in the general reckoning that followed the war — only then had she allowed the title page to read:

**A LANTERN FOR THE ATLAS**
*Being the Coastal Charts of the Western Reach*
*by Aldric Vane, completed by Lior Oyelaran*
*with additions by Isolde Oyelaran*

Three names, one book. Her uncle would have hated it and her mother would have loved it, and both of those certainties were among the things that kept her working through nights when the ink froze in the well.

* * *

The proofs came back from the printer on a Tuesday, smelling of solvent and hot metal, and Isolde spread them across the tower table while gulls screamed their opinions through the open door.

Forty-one charts. Every coastline from Vellamare to the southern capes, corrected — she had spent three seasons aboard herring boats and mail steamers verifying depths and hazards, redrawing shoals that had shifted, striking through channels that no longer existed. And threaded through all of it, printed now in honest ink instead of hidden cipher, the register of names: eleven hundred and forty-two people whom Aldric Vane had carried out of darkness in the holds and cabins and fish-crates of borrowed boats, each with a date, a port of embarkation, and — where she had found them — a note of where their lives had gone afterward. *Marta Ellsen, 1931, now a schoolmistress in Haverdal. The Ferro brothers, 1934, fishermen still, their sons' names added below.* The margins had become what they had always secretly been: a ledger of the saved.

And beneath it, on the final page, a new section, printed in smaller type, titled plainly: **Additions.** Blank ruled lines, hundreds of them, waiting.

Anselme had argued against it. *You invite the censors of every future regime to finish your book for you.* Isolde had held up the blank page to the light. *That's exactly why it's there. A finished atlas can be confiscated. An unfinished one has to be continued, and continuation means someone has to keep writing, and someone writing is someone watching the water.* She had learned that from Lior, though she'd learned it without knowing she was learning it — in the attic, in the candlelight of the Forger's Night, in the terrible arithmetic of berths and names. Knowledge kept is knowledge buried. Knowledge shared is a lantern.

The press in the tower was small, bought secondhand from a newspaper that had folded, and it could not print the full edition alone. That work belonged to the Confraternity's shop across the harbor, and the first bound copies were due off it within the month. But Isolde liked to print single sheets herself, proof after proof, reading each one as if for the first time, hunting errors the way Reiss once hunted her uncle's telltale line-weight. She had inherited his eye whether she wished it or not. Some mornings she caught her own reflection in the window glass bent over the table, pen in hand, brow furrowed, and saw not herself at all but a thin dark-haired man in a mapmaker's apron, and had to stand up and walk to the window and look at the sea until the ghost agreed to be her again.

Letters came to the tower now. That was the thing she had not predicted — that publishing the atlas would make it a kind of lighthouse in its own right, and the desperate would steer by it. They wrote from camps, from transit hotels, from inland towns where new governments with new ledgers were sorting populations into columns again, because it seemed the twentieth century intended to practice that particular craft until it mastered it. They wrote asking: *Is there a route? Is there a boat? Is my father's name in your margins? Was anyone saved from my village?*

Isolde answered every letter. She answered them at the tower table with the lantern burning overhead, and she used Aldric's code for the dangerous replies — routes and contacts disguised as corrections to the charts themselves, errata slips that were anything but. The censors of three nations had examined sample copies of *A Lantern for the Atlas* and found nothing but exemplary cartography. Let them look. Her masters had built hiding places into beauty, and beauty was the one cargo no inspector ever weighed properly.

This morning there were two letters. One was from a woman in the eastern provinces whose grandmother appeared in the register — Marta Ellsen's daughter — thanking her in four careful sentences and enclosing, wrapped in wax paper, a pressed flower from a garden that no longer existed. The other was forwarded through three hands, unsigned, written in a cipher Isolde knew better than her own signature.

She decoded it standing at the window, watching a gray passenger steamer come up the channel toward the harbor mouth, riding low, too low for cargo, which meant people. The message read: *Vellamare harbor reopened to civilian traffic spring next year. Old quarter largely stands. The studio address is known to friends. Someone should come and see what survived.*

Someone should come.

She read it five times. Through the glass, the steamer sounded its horn, one long mournful note, and the buoy bell answered, and below her on the quay she could see the harbor officials moving toward the gangway, and the crowd that always gathered now when a refugee ship made port — wives, priests, Confraternity members with blankets and bread, and always a few people simply standing with their hats off, as if the arriving strangers were a procession and decency required witnesses.

Her mother and uncle had stayed behind so that she could be on that deck. In eleven years she had built a life worth the price of the ticket: the tower, the press, the book, the letters, the hundred small rescues routed through chart-errata. She had told herself that this was her side of the bargain — that she had become the continuation they purchased. But a bargain is not the same as a grave, and no one had ever shown her one. No one had ever told her what happened on the quay after the Corvane passed out of sight. Maren might have lived. Lior might have lived. Twenty years of practiced silence had gotten him through one interrogation; men had survived prisons and worse and walked out gray and older into a changed country. Or the train east had taken them, as it took Tomas, as it took so many whose names she had entered in the Additions with a dagger-cross beside them and a prayer she didn't believe in and recited anyway.

She had never gone back. Not when the war ended, not when the letters began saying *it is safe now*, not even to search. Because going back meant learning, and not-knowing had been its own strange mercy, a room she could close the door on. She was thirty-two years old and she had crossed oceans of ink and she had never once crossed that particular water.

The steamer eased against the quay. Down below, the gangway came down, and the first passengers descended into the noise and the blankets and the light — a family, Isolde saw, a whole family, which was rare and therefore miraculous; parents and three children blinking in the sun like creatures raised from some deep place.

She looked at the decoded letter in her hand. *Someone should come and see what survived.*

She thought of the attic in Vellamare, the smell of size and cedar oil, the floorboard that lifted, the atlas sleeping underneath while boots marched in the street. She thought of Aldric Vane, whom she had never met, whose hand she knew more intimately than the faces of most living people. She thought of her uncle's last lesson, given not in words but in the act of wrapping oilcloth around the book and pressing it into her arms on a freezing quay: *the idea survives the man.* He had said it aloud once, actually — the night of the forging, candle-guttering, her small steady hands doing work no child should do — he had said, *Remember this feeling, Isolde. This is what the ink is for.*

She set the letter on the table beside the open atlas. Then she took a fresh sheet of the good rag stock, trimmed it to the margin-size of the register, and uncapped the iron-gall ink.

At the top of the page, in her own hand — not Aldric's cipher, not Lior's disguise, but plain upright letters any child could read — she wrote the heading: **Additions, Volume Two.** Below it, the first entry:

*Spring, 1949. Departure: Port Solenne. Destination: Vellamare. Passenger: one.*

She blew the ink dry, cut the entry free with the chart knife, and slid it into the margin of the atlas where it belonged — between the saved and the not-yet-saved, between the dead man's code and the living sea. Then she banked the lantern for the day, took up her coat and the oilcloth satchel with the proof sheets and the coded letter inside it, and started down the tower stairs, two at a time, toward the quay, toward the ticket office, toward the water.

Below her, the buoy bell rang on, marking the harbor's patient heart, and the morning tide was already turning.
