## 1. The Weight of Sorts

My father says a compositor can tell a man's whole character by the state of his type case. A sloppy man's em-quads are scattered like spilled teeth. A vain man keeps his italics too clean from disuse. And a frightened man—my father did not say what a frightened man's case looks like, but I suspect he was looking at mine when he decided to leave it unsaid.

It was the third of October, 1899, and in the Vane Type Foundry we were cutting letters by lamplight because the gas had been turned down at the meter again. My father claimed it was the city throttling supply to the river districts. Tam claimed it was rats gnawing the pipes. I suspected my father of turning it down himself, to teach us economy, which is the sort of lesson that costs more than it teaches.

"Again," Aldous Vane said, holding his palm flat under the lamp so the light came up through his fingers and made them look carved from wax. "What goes in the case?"

"The case holds what cannot be replaced."

"And what cannot be replaced?"

I set down my graver and looked at him across the cutting bench. He was sixty-one years old and had hands like a pair of well-worn composing sticks, all edges and purpose, and he was asking me—for the ninth evening running—which of our lives would fit into a single traveling case.

"Mother," I said.

He blinked. It was not the answer on his list. His list, delivered with the dry cadence of a man reciting inventory, ran: the punchcutter's gravers, the specimen book of 1871, the matrix of the Vane ampersand, which no other foundry in Vellamark possessed and which he called, without irony, the family crest. Tam's list ran: a slingshot, a knife, a compass with a cracked face, and—when pressed—the cat, whom Tam had named Sorts, because my father let him name things only when he thought the joke would go over a child's head. Mother had offered no list at all. She had simply begun, quietly, wearing her good coat around the house as though rehearsing for a departure she refused to name.

"Mother does not fit in a case," my father said.

"No," I agreed. "But she is not replaceable, and you asked."

For a moment something moved behind his face like a line of loose type shifting when the case tilts. Then he picked up his graver again. "Sentiment," he said, "does not cut steel."

"No," I said. "But it cuts everything else."

* * *

You should understand what a foundry smells like at night, because I will be describing it a great deal and you ought to be prepared: hot lead and machine oil and the faint vinegary bite of the ink we tested our casts against, and under all of it the cold stone smell of a building that has stood on the Vellamark quay since before anyone now living was born. The presses slept in the back room like patient cattle. The racks of matrices stood in their cases along the wall, thousands upon thousands of small brass wombs waiting to give birth to the alphabet. My grandfather had bought the building with money earned printing hymnals, which my father considered an embarrassment and my mother considered proof that God has a sense of humor.

I was cutting a lowercase g in fourteen point, working from my father's drawing, because the old g punches were wearing soft and a worn g makes every word look slightly ashamed of itself. This is the work I loved and love still, though I could not then have told you why. There is a pleasure in it that borders on the devotional. You take a piece of steel no bigger than a fingernail and you convince it, stroke by stroke, to hold a letter. The letter exists nowhere until you make it exist. Then it exists ten thousand times, on bills and broadsheets and wedding invitations and the cheap penny dreadfuls Tam traded at school, and none of those readers will ever know your name, and that is the correct arrangement of things. The work matters. The worker does not. My father taught me this as theology.

"Hazel." He had come around the bench to inspect my progress, which meant standing close enough that I could feel him deciding whether to praise me. "Your counter is drifting left."

"It's following the bowl."

"It's following your hand, which is tired. Rest it."

"I'm fine."

"You are seventeen years old and you are fine the way a bridge is fine. Everyone says so right up until the day it isn't." He took the graver from me—not snatching, never snatching; my father handled tools and daughters with the same deliberate courtesy—and turned it in the lamplight. "Show me your wrist."

I showed him my wrist. He pressed his thumb into the tendon and watched my face. I gave him nothing, having learned years ago that complaint is currency and I preferred to save mine.

"You'll want to marry a printer," he said, releasing me. "Or a doctor. Someone whose trade ruins hands, so yours won't look strange beside them."

"That's your advice. That's the sum of paternal wisdom."

"My wisdom is in the case behind you. Four hundred matrices and a graver set older than the Republic. The rest is just talk." He hung the graver on its board, each tool in its silhouette like a saint in a niche. "The census men come Thursday."

And there it was. Nine evenings of drills about packing cases, and he had finally said the word aloud.

"I know," I said. "There's a notice on the bridge."

"There are notices everywhere. On the bridge, at the Custom House, pasted inside the pissoir on Tanner's Row, which tells you what the government thinks of where such announcements belong." He lowered himself onto the stool with the small grunt that had arrived sometime after his fiftieth year and never left. "A man named Kettle. Ambrose Kettle. He counts households in the river wards. He'll want names, ages, trades, and papers."

"We have papers."

"We have papers," my father said, in exactly the tone he used when a customer asked whether our type was guaranteed against wear.

I let it lie. In the Vane household we had a long tradition of letting certain things lie, the way you leave a line of type undisturbed overnight because touching it might spill it. My mother's papers were among these things. They existed. They were signed by a parish clerk in a town upriver whose name my mother pronounced with a slight hesitation, the hesitation of a woman reading a word in a language she learned late. I had been eleven when I first noticed the hesitation. I was seventeen now, and I had built a small room in my mind where such observations went to live, and I kept the door shut, and I believed—this is the part that shames me, how completely I believed—that a shut door was the same thing as safety.

"What does he do if the papers are wanting?" I asked.

My father looked at the lamp instead of at me. "Asks questions. Writes things in a ledger. Ledgers travel upward. That is all a ledger ever does."

"And then?"

"And then," he said, "the government knows one more true thing about us, and knowing things is what governments do instead of loving people. Cut your g, Hazel. The counter is still drifting."

* * *

I walked home from the bridge that night the long way, which is to say I walked home from the bridge by way of standing on the bridge for the better part of an hour, and I will tell you why.

The notices had gone up that morning. I had seen men from the Registrar's office paste them to the stone piers of the lantern tower, four sheets on each pier, squared and leveled with a string line, because even tyranny in Vellamark was tidy. The paper was good stock—heavier than newsprint, lighter than bond—and the type was machine-set, soulless, a grotesque face with no warmth in it whatsoever, the kind of lettering that looks like it has already counted you and found the total disappointing.

NOTICE OF GENERAL ENUMERATION, the sheets read. ALL PERSONS RESIDENT WITHIN THE CITY AND LIBERTIES OF VELLAMARK SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR COUNTING AND VERIFICATION OF STATUS. PERSONS UNABLE TO ESTABLISH RIGHT OF RESIDENCE WILL BE DESIGNATED FOR RELOCATION.

Relocation. What a gentle word. As though the government were a helpful uncle offering to move your furniture.

I stood at the rail in the wind coming off the river and read the notice perhaps forty times, and here is the thing I most remember: I was not alone. The bridge at dusk was never empty—it was the city's spine, everyone crossing it sooner or later—but that evening the traffic had changed its character. People stopped. People read. And then they did the thing that I think of now, all these years later, as the first symptom of the sickness that autumn: they glanced sideways to see who else was reading.

An old woman with a market basket read the notice and then looked quickly at a young clerk reading the same notice, and the clerk looked away, and neither of them said anything, because the notice did not ask anything yet, but it would, and everyone understood that the looking-away had already begun. A fish porter spat over the rail, elaborately casual. Two students from the Academy read the sheet and laughed at some joke between them, and their laughter had a tin sound, the sound of laughter doing manual labor.

And beneath us the river ran brown and patient toward the sea, and the barges lay at their moorings with their tarps lashed down, and the lantern in the tower overhead had just been lit by the lamplighter—a boy, these days; they'd dismissed the old man who'd done it for thirty years—and its light swung out over the water in that slow golden sweep that I had fallen asleep to every night of my life.

I want to be honest with you, since honesty is the one luxury I can still afford: I did not feel dread that evening. Not exactly. Dread is a big animal and it hadn't arrived yet. What I felt was smaller and meaner: a tightening in the chest, a sense of the world narrowing like a tunnel, the feeling you get when the tide goes out further than it should and you understand, without being told, that somewhere upstream something has dammed the water. I felt the future getting smaller. That's all. That's where it starts—not with soldiers, not with chalk on doors, but with a woman glancing sideways from a posted notice to see who is watching her read it.

A hand touched my elbow and I nearly went over the rail.

"Saints, Hazel, it's only me." Tam, twelve years old and constructed entirely of elbows and opinions, breathless from running the quay stairs. "Father sent me. He says come home, the g can wait."

"The g cannot wait. The g is a matter of civic pride."

"He also says—" Tam paused, and I saw him arrange his face into the particular gravity children put on when they've been given a message above their pay grade and intend to deliver it correctly. "He says to tell you the Thursday man is coming, and mother wants the good tablecloth off the table before he arrives, because—" he frowned, working the logic, "—because we're not the kind of house that owns a good tablecloth."

We walked home together along the quay, Tam skipping onto every bollard, and behind us the lantern swept its light across the water, and ahead of us our windows were dark because my father had turned down the gas again, teaching us economy, teaching us what things cost.

At the door my mother was waiting with a lamp held high, and her face in that light was calm and kind and utterly unreadable, the way a well-cut counter is unreadable—the space shaped so precisely that you see straight through it to whatever stands beyond.

"Come in, both of you," Ilse Vane said. "It's cold on the river."

And it was cold on the river. But that is not why she called us in. I knew that even then, somewhere beneath the shut door. My mother had been watching the bridge herself. My mother had been watching a great many things.

That night I dreamed of type cases, thousands of them, each one holding a single letter, and I went down the rows looking for the case that held my own name, and every drawer I opened was empty except for one small steel g, drifting left of center, ashamed of itself.

Thursday was coming. The Thursday man was coming. And in the rack behind the cutting bench, wrapped in oilcloth where no census taker would think to look, lay the matrix of the Vane ampersand—the family crest, my father called it, and he was wrong, or rather he was wronger than he knew. An ampersand means *and*. It means *together*. It is the only piece of type whose whole purpose is to refuse to choose.

We would need it sooner than any of us imagined.
