## 2. Questions in a Ledger

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept ledger, and I will defend that pleasure against anyone who calls it small. A ledger does not lie, flatter, or weep at inconvenient moments. It simply opens itself to you and says: *here is the world, counted.* Most people cannot bear to be counted. That is precisely why counting them matters.

I had been a census taker for eleven months as of that gray Tuesday in March, and in eleven months I had learned three things about the citizens of Vellamark. First, they lie about their ages — downward if they are women, upward if they are men applying for work permits. Second, they lie about their incomes, always downward, with the sincerity of parishioners reciting creeds they half believe. Third, and this is the one that pays my wages eventually, they lie about who lives under their roofs.

Uncle Ambrose sleeping in the attic. Cousin Marta "just visiting" for the ninth consecutive year. The lodger who has no name at all until pressed, at which point he produces one from somewhere, damp and folded, like a coin from behind a child's ear.

My superior, Undersecretary Brann of the Office of Enumeration, says that the census is not an instrument of punishment but a mirror held up to the nation. He says this while polishing his spectacles, which he polishes whenever he is about to ruin someone. I have come to understand that the census is indeed a mirror, but it is also a sieve, and the government holds both ends.

On the morning in question I carried my ledger, my ink bottle wrapped in flannel against the cold, and a list of forty-one households on Foundry Row and the streets feeding into it. The Vane foundry was the fourth house on my route. I remember the weather being the sort that can't decide between rain and sleet, which is Vellamark's native tongue, spoken fluently by the sky perhaps eight months of the year.

* * *

The house above the foundry smelled of hot metal and lamp oil and, faintly, of bread. A girl answered the door — sixteen or seventeen, sleeves rolled, her forearms smudged to the elbow with something dark that was either ink or soot. She had the look of someone interrupted mid-thought, and the further look of someone deciding whether to be annoyed about it.

"Ambrose Kettle," I said, presenting my credentials, which were a folded card and a brass badge that tarnished if you looked at it wrong. "Office of Enumeration. I'm here to conduct the household count."

"We've been counted before," she said.

"Every ten years. And every ten years the country changes its mind about what it wants to know." I stepped past her into the hall before she could decide whether to admit me, which was not rudeness on my part but efficiency, and efficiency, I have found, reads as authority. "You are?"

"Hazel Vane."

"Daughter of Aldous Vane, type founder."

"You know the house already."

"I know all the houses, Miss Vane. Knowing the houses is the entire profession." I opened my ledger on the hall table without asking leave. There is a school of thought that says one should ask permission before writing down a family's particulars. That school produces incomplete ledgers.

The father came down the stairs wiping his hands on a leather apron, and I will grant him this: he did not perform the little theater of surprise most householders stage when the census arrives. No fumbling for spectacles, no "oh, is it March already." Aldous Vane looked at me the way a man looks at weather he has already read in his bones.

"Ambrose Kettle," I said again, because introductions are like type: set once, reused endlessly. "Household count. Names, ages, places of birth, occupations, and — new this year — documentation of residence status."

"Documentation," he repeated.

"The Residence Verification Schedule. Form R-9." I produced a copy and laid it beside the ledger. "Each adult member of the household confirms their right of abode. Birth record, marriage record, naturalization papers, parish registry — any one suffices."

I said it lightly. That is the trick of it. You say *any one suffices* in the tone of a man discussing umbrella options, and people relax, and relaxed people produce documents, and documents produce truth, and truth, depending on its contents, produces either a tick in column four or a letter to the district office that ruins everyone at the table.

Aldous Vane did not relax. But neither did he flinch, and I noted that too. Not-flinching is its own kind of information.

"There's myself," he said. "My wife, Ilse. My daughter Hazel, whom you've met. My son Tam, aged twelve."

"Four souls."

"Four souls."

"And your wife — Ilse. Family name?"

The pause was brief. Brief enough to miss if you were the sort of census taker who thinks his job begins and ends with pencil marks. I am not that sort. I once waited out a silversmith for ninety full seconds while he decided whether his "nephew" existed, and I would wait out Aldous Vane too, though he rallied faster than the silversmith had.

"She was born across the river," he said. "Ilse Marren. Of Harrow Bend."

"Harrow Bend." I wrote it. My pen moved smoothly; I have never been one of those scribblers who makes a performance of hesitation. "Parish of record?"

"I couldn't say which parish. We married in Saint Ferrel's, here in the city. Eighteen years ago now."

"Saint Ferrel's keeps good books. I'll want the date."

He gave me the date. He gave it without reaching for a Bible or a framed certificate, which told me either that he carried his wedding day in his head where better men carry it, or that there was no paper in the house to consult. Both possibilities interested me.

* * *

The interview proper took place at the kitchen table, because the parlor was full of type cases and the father seemed reluctant to have me among them, which again I noted. People guard the rooms that hold their secrets; the rest of the house they'll show you freely.

Hazel Vane brought tea without being asked. She poured four cups and gave one to a boy who materialized from somewhere — Tam, presumably, twelve years old and built entirely of elbows and attention, the kind of child who watches a stranger's hands instead of his face. Children know what the adults are afraid of before the adults do. They watch the hands.

I filled in the columns. Aldous Vane, born Vellamark, fifty-one years of age (he said forty-nine; the ledger of the Guild of Typefounders, which I keep a copy of for exactly these occasions, says otherwise, but a man's vanity about five years is not worth a letter to anyone). Occupation: master founder. Right of abode: guild registration, twenty-six years standing, ironclad. I ticked him with genuine satisfaction. A man with a guild number behind him is a rock in the stream; everything else washes past him.

Hazel Vane, born Vellamark, seventeen. Dependent, no separate abode claim. Tick. The boy, Tam — tick, dependent, no claims, no complications. Children are the easy arithmetic of the census. It is the mothers who make the sums go strange.

"And Mrs. Vane," I said. "If she's at home, I'll need to speak with her directly. The schedule requires confirmation from each adult in her own words."

"She's resting," Hazel said.

"She's out," Tam said, at the same moment, and then the two of them performed the delicate sibling maneuver of pretending they hadn't contradicted each other.

"Resting out," I suggested, and wrote nothing, and let the silence do its work. Silence is the cheapest tool in the enumerator's kit and the sharpest. I let it sit on the table among the teacups for a good while. The father studied his tea. The daughter studied me with a frankness that would have unsettled a younger version of myself — she had the gaze of someone cataloguing *me*, which is not a thing civilians are supposed to do. The boy watched my pen.

"She'll confirm the particulars when she returns," Aldous Vane said finally. "Everything I've given you stands for the household."

"Of course it does," I said pleasantly. "But the form wants her mark. And her parish of birth, confirmed. Harrow Bend falls under the diocese of Saint Aubin's, you know. Their records burned in the flood year — 'eighty-one — so if she was registered there, we may need a secondary attestation. A baptismal record, perhaps. Her parents' names. Something of that order."

I said all this in the same light tone, the umbrella-tone, and I watched it land. Hazel Vane's cup stopped halfway to the table — a small arrest, a fraction of a second, then completed as though nothing had happened. The father's face did nothing at all, which was more telling than anything it could have done. Only the boy kept looking at my pen, and I began to suspect he was looking at it because he understood, better than his elders, that the pen was the weapon in the room.

"I'll fetch whatever's needed," Aldous Vane said.

"No hurry," I said. "The district office collects my book at month's end." I closed the ledger. "Thank you for your hospitality, Miss Vane. The tea was excellent."

It had been mediocre tea. But courtesy costs nothing and buys cooperation, and cooperation, unlike courtesy, has a market value.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I set it down plainly because a man ought to be able to read his own account without flinching, even if he flinches anyway.

I went to Saint Ferrel's.

Not because the schedule required it. The schedule required only that the Vanes produce documentation by month's end, and nine households in ten would have produced something by then — a certificate, an affidavit, a neighbor's oath. But I had an hour before my next appointment on Cooper's Lane, and Saint Ferrel's was six minutes' walk from the Vanes' door, and I have never in my life walked past an archive without going in. Curiosity is the occupational disease of enumerators. Brann calls it thoroughness and rewards it, which tells you something about Brann.

The verger at Saint Ferrel's owed me a favor dating back to the autumn, when I had overlooked the fact that his nephew was counted in two parishes at once. Favors of that kind are the true currency of Vellamark; the crown mints money, but favors spend better. He fetched the marriage register without asking why I wanted it, which is why I continue to extend him credit.

Saint Ferrel's, 18th of June, 1881: Aldous Vane, typefounder, of this parish, to Ilse Marren, spinster, of — and here the register grew interesting — of no fixed parish, attested per special license.

Special licenses are not common. They exist for the impatient, the notorious, and the untraceable. A bishop grants one when the ordinary channels won't hold, and the ordinary channels won't hold when there is nothing on the far side of them. I copied the entry into my notebook in my best hand, which is very good indeed — a census taker whose hand cannot be trusted is a eunuch in his trade — and then I sat in the pew by the vestry door and did some arithmetic.

Ilse Marren, married eighteen years, mother of two citizen-born children, wife of a guildmaster with twenty-six years' standing. If her papers were sound, producing them would cost the family nothing. If her papers were sound, Aldous Vane would have sent his daughter running for them the moment I said *parish of record*, the way a man with money in the bank responds to a creditor.

Instead: a pause. A contradiction between siblings. A daughter's teacup arrested midair.

And beneath it all, the thing I had known since the government first posted its notices on the bridge and called it enumeration rather than expulsion: that in Vellamark this spring, a missing page is not a clerical matter. It is a sentence. The Residence Acts do not distinguish between the forged document and the lost one, the never-issued one and the deliberately destroyed one. Paper is blood now. Everyone in the city knows it, and everyone pretends not to, the way a congregation pretends not to notice which families have stopped coming to church.

I could report the discrepancy that afternoon. A line in my ledger — *residence documentation outstanding, wife of subject; refer to district office* — and the machinery would take it up. The district office refers to the provincial office; the provincial office refers to the Bureau of Settlement; the Bureau of Settlement sends men who do not bring tea or ask pleasant questions. I had seen their work in the Salt Quarter in February. Chalk marks on doors, furniture on pavements, a woman's piano sold at auction because no one claimed it and no one dared.

One line, in my neat hand, and the Vane household would enter that machinery.

And I — well. Let me be honest, since honesty costs me nothing on this page. Brann had said, not three weeks earlier, over the spectacles-polishing, that the office needed cases. Exemplary cases. Households handled correctly, thoroughly, with the rigor that distinguishes mere counting from true enumeration. *Deliver me one clean example, Kettle, and there's a desk with a window in it for you by summer.* A desk with a window. I have worked eleven months at a desk in a corridor where the gaslight comes and goes according to the mood of the building's plumbing. I want that window the way other men want women or salvation.

The Vane discrepancy was exactly the kind of case Brann meant. A guildmaster's household — respectable enough to matter, vulnerable enough to fall. A foreign-born wife with a special-license marriage and no parish roots. Textbook.

So why did my pen hover?

* * *

I finished my route that day. Forty-one households; thirty-eight clean; two with discrepancies of the ordinary kind (a boarder unregistered, a widow claiming her dead husband's guild rights), which I reported without ceremony, because they were ordinary, and reporting ordinary things is merely work. The fourth discrepancy sat in my ledger like a stone in a shoe.

That evening I sat in my rooms above the chandler's shop on Tanner's Wynd and looked at the line I had written:

*Vane, Ilse, b. Harrow Bend (unverified). Parish of record: none found. Marriage: St. Ferrel's, special lic., 18.vi.1881. Documentation outstanding.*

Below it, in the margin, where the instructions say discrepancies requiring referral should be marked with a red cross, my pen had rested, and the ink had dried on the nib, and I had written nothing.

I am not a sentimental man. Sentiment in an enumerator is like water in a watch: it stops the mechanism. I have reported weavers with infant children. I reported the Salt Quarter. I slept afterward, which I mention as evidence of sound constitution.

But there is a difference, I told myself that night, between a discrepancy and a proof. What I had was a pause, a contradiction, an absence of paper. Absence is not evidence; absence is only a hole shaped like a question. Perhaps Ilse Marren's records burned at Saint Aubin's in the flood year, exactly as the registers did. Perhaps she was born on the water, to barge folk, whom no parish ever bothered to christen. Perhaps a dozen innocent things. The law says the burden rests with the household to prove otherwise — but the law says many things, and the men who wrote the Residence Acts do not live in corridors with unreliable gaslight.

And yet.

And yet a man does not marry across a special license if his bride's papers are in order. A special license is a bridge built in a hurry, at night, over water you don't want examined. Aldous Vane knew, in 1881, that his bride could not pass through the ordinary doors, and he paid for a way around them, and for eighteen years the way around held, and now the government has moved the river.

I turned the ledger page, then turned it back. Then I did the thing I had not done on any previous evening of my eleven months. I blotted the line carefully, closed the book, and put it in the drawer — unmarked. Unmarked entries are due to the district office at month's end regardless; omission requires only that I fail to add the red cross. A failure to add a cross is not a crime. It is barely even a decision. It is a pen that ran dry. These distinctions matter to men in my position; our whole trade is the distinction between what is written and what is merely true.

Why? I ask myself now, with the advantage of hindsight, which flatters everyone. Call it conscience, if you like the word. I prefer to call it valuation. A discrepancy reported is worth a cross in a column — worth nothing, spent instantly, forgotten by Friday. A discrepancy withheld is worth something else entirely. It is a debt the household owes without knowing it owes. And debts of that kind, in a city sorting itself into the kept and the cast out, appreciate.

I tell myself that is all it was: patience, prudence, the enumerator's equivalent of letting a field ripen. I almost believe it. There was a moment, though — putting out my lamp, hearing the chandler's dog below complain at the sleet — when another thought crossed the ledger of my mind, unbidden, in a hand less neat than mine own:

*She has children. One of them watches pens the way I watch faces.*

I did not write that down anywhere. Some entries, once made, cannot be blotted.

The month ends in nineteen days. The book goes to the district office whether my pen runs dry or not, and Brann expects his exemplary case by summer, and somewhere on Foundry Row a girl with ink to her elbows is already wondering what I wrote in that ledger — I could see her wondering as I left, could see her adding me to some inventory of her own.

Nineteen days. A hole shaped like a question, sitting in a government book, waiting for someone to fill it — with a red cross, or with something else entirely.

I have not yet decided which.
