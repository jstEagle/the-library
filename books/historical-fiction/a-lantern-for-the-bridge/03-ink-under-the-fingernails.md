## 3. Ink Under the Fingernails

My father says a compositor can read a man's soul in his letterforms. Everyone's hand has habits, he told me when I was nine and learning to justify my first line of type—the way an *f* leans into its neighbor like a gossip, the way certain people cross their *t*s with a flourish that means vanity and others with a slash that means hurry. He said it as craft wisdom. He did not know he was handing me a burglar's toolkit.

I thought about that on the third night after the census taker came, sitting in the foundry with one lamp lit and a stolen census form flattened under the composing stone, practicing how to be my father.

* * *

Let me explain what a census form is, for those lucky enough never to have held one. It arrives on stiff government paper, watermarked with the seal of the Ministry of Records—a bridge over a river, naturally, because Vellamark's own bridge was too good an emblem to waste on anything so honest. The form asks your name, your trade, your parish of baptism, the names and parishes of everyone sleeping under your roof. It asks these questions in a typeface chosen by someone who clearly hated both readers and compositors: a condensed gothic with serifs like fishhooks.

And at the bottom it asks—this is the part they think is clever—for the signature of the head of household, attesting that all entries are true.

Aldous Vane had signed our form three days ago, in front of Ambrose Kettle, whose pen scratched like a beetle in a drawer and whose eyes went from the ledger to my mother's face and back again one time too many. My father signed the way he signs everything, the way he signed my birthday cards and the delivery notes for lead shipments: a hard downstroke, a looped *A* like a snail shell tipped sideways, the final *e* trailing off as though he'd already lost interest in his own name. It is not a beautiful hand. It is a *working* hand, ink-stained and impatient, and I have watched it since before I could walk.

Which meant that when I sat down at midnight with a fresh form—one Kettle had left behind by accident, or perhaps not, though I chose not to think about that—I knew exactly what I had to make my hand forget.

Because here is the trouble. Our form was true when my father signed it, mostly. Hazel, daughter of Aldous and Ilse Vane. Tam, son of same. Trade: typefounder. Parish: Saint Ferrell's-by-the-Weir, where I was baptized at six weeks old in water so cold I apparently screamed the sermon short. All of that could survive any scrutiny Kettle cared to apply.

It was the fourth line that would hang us. *Ilse Vane, née Ostermann. Parish of origin: Saint Anselm's, Marrowfield.*

Marrowfield is across the river. Across the river is another country now, or will be officially come spring, once the Boundary Act finishes digesting. And the new census regulations—posted on the bridge in letters tall enough to read from a barge—are very clear about persons whose origins lie beyond the line. Persons of transriverine origin are subject to review. Review, in the ministry's vocabulary, is a word with teeth at both ends.

My mother came to Vellamark twenty-six years ago with two trunks and an accent she spent a decade sanding down to nothing. She married my father, raised us speaking river-Vellamarkish with barely a trace, cooked our food and mended our clothes and paid her taxes and had not set foot across the water in nineteen years. None of it mattered. The form didn't ask whether you belonged here. It asked where you were *from*, as if a woman could be unwound back to her starting point like thread off a spool.

So there I sat, at half past midnight, with a blank duplicate form and my father's handwriting living in my fingers, trying to decide how far a daughter should go.

The honest answer, of course, was nowhere. The sensible answer was nowhere. I want it recorded that I weighed those answers properly, that I sat with them the way you sit with a line of type that won't justify no matter how you space it, turning the problem over, looking for the flaw in your own reasoning rather than the world's.

Then I dipped the pen.

* * *

The first attempt was a disaster. I wrote *Ilse Vane* in my father's style but at my own speed, and speed betrayed me—my natural slant crept in, a little more forward-leaning than his, a girl hurrying where a man ambled. I burned it in the stove and felt, absurdly, like an arsonist of the self.

The second attempt I made slowly, thinking through each letter before setting it down. Downstroke. Shell-shaped *A*. Don't finish the *e*, abandon it mid-breath. It looked better. It looked almost right. It also looked like exactly what it was: a forgery assembled letter by letter by someone who understood construction but not rhythm. Anyone who'd watched my father sign a hundred delivery notes would feel something wrong, the way you feel a floorboard give slightly underfoot.

What I needed was not to draw his letters. I needed to *think* in them.

This sounds mystical, but it isn't; it's muscle and memory. My father's hand is tired. That's the secret of it. Every letter he writes is a letter written by a man who has been writing all day, whose wrist wants the shortest possible path from one character to the next. So I stopped trying to write beautifully and started trying to write *wearily*. I let my hand droop. I let the connections between letters sag. On the fifth sheet, somewhere around midnight becoming one o'clock, my wrist learned the trick of his fatigue, and the name *Ilse Vane* appeared on the page looking like it had been written by a man signing off on a shipment of twelve-point lead, already thinking about his supper.

I stared at it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the river a barge horn moaned, low and lonesome, the sound this city makes in its sleep.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about doing wrong: it doesn't feel like falling. It feels like flying. There was a moment—I'm ashamed of it now, or I would be if I were a better person—when I looked at that forged name and felt *proud*. Not relieved, not frightened. Proud, the way I feel when I lock up a forme perfectly, when the forme lifts off the stone whole and every piece of type holds its breath together. It was good work. That was the horror of it, hiding underneath the exhilaration like a stone under snow: I had done a wicked thing *well*, and the well-done-ness was singing louder than the wickedness.

I told myself the lie I suspect every forger tells: it wasn't really lying, only correcting. The form asked where my mother was from, and the answer Saint Ferrell's was nearly true—she'd lived in this parish longer than some people had been alive. Wasn't a place your history, if your history had been spent entirely in one spot? Weren't we allowed to choose? I built the argument carefully, brick by brick, the way my father builds a wall of type, and then I noticed what I was doing—justifying—and had to laugh, alone, at one in the morning, at my own cleverness, which is either a sign of health or the opposite.

I practiced the rest of the form too. *Aldous Vane, typefounder.* *Hazel Vane.* *Tammas Vane, aged eleven.* I wrote my own name in my father's hand and it was the strangest thing I've ever seen, like meeting myself in a distorted mirror at the fairground—recognizable, but wrong in ways I couldn't fix because I didn't know which distortions were mine and which were his. I wrote my brother's name and hesitated over it. Tam. Eleven years old, all elbows and schemes, currently asleep upstairs dreaming God knew what dreams about coal chutes and river rats. Whatever I wrote on this page would follow him the way a smell follows smoke.

I finished the practice sheets, burned four of the five, and kept the best one folded inside my apron pocket, which was itself a decision I hadn't consciously made. You don't keep a rehearsal unless you intend a performance.

That was when the floor creaked behind me.

* * *

I want to say I reacted with cool presence of mind. What actually happened is that I went off the stool like a scalded cat, crammed the folded paper deeper into my pocket, and knocked my elbow against the type case hard enough to rattle every sort in its compartment—a sound, in a silent foundry, like the building clearing its throat.

"Hazel?" said the dark.

Tam stood at the bottom of the stairs in his nightshirt, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog, holding the stub of a candle that had gone out sometime during his descent. His feet were bare. His expression was the particular expression of a boy who has woken at night, heard nothing, and come downstairs anyway because sleep is for people without imaginations.

"What are you doing," he said. Not a question. In our family, "what are you doing" spoken in that flat tone means *I saw something and I am going to stand here until you explain it.*

"Couldn't sleep," I said. "Thought I'd clean the cases."

He looked at the cases, which were immaculate, because I had cleaned them that afternoon to have something to do with my hands while my brain chewed on its problem. He looked at the lamp. He looked at the stove, where the ashes of four census forms lay still faintly warm, and—this is my brother, remember, eleven years old and sharp as a burin—he looked at the stove longest of all.

"You're burning paper," he said, "at one in the morning."

"Rubbish."

"You're burning rubbish in the stove instead of the bin, which Father says wastes heat, and you hate wasting anything."

I have never wanted to strangle and admire a person simultaneously before. It's an educational sensation.

"Go back to bed, Tam."

"No." He padded over to the stove and peered into it, and I genuinely considered shoving him, which would have been a new low even for this household's current standards. But the ash had done its work; paper burns to gray lace, unrecognizable, and whatever fragments survived were indistinguishable from the old delivery notes and spoiled proofs we burn every week. He straightened up, disappointed, and then his eye caught the composing stone, where I had—idiot, fool, amateur—left the practice sheet face-up. The one I'd decided to keep. The one bearing five signatures of *Aldous Vane* in descending quality, like a portrait of a man aging in fast motion, above the single line written in his tired-hand mastery: *Ilse Vane.*

Tam read it. I watched him read it. I watched the exact moment comprehension arrived, because his face did something complicated—you could see the machinery working, the small gears of a child's mind engaging a much larger gear it hadn't known the machine contained.

"That's Mum's name," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"And that's Dad's writing."

"It's a copy of Dad's writing."

"Why?"

And there it was, the whole cliff edge of the thing, and me standing at the top of it deciding whether to push him off or jump myself. Because *why* has answers of increasing sizes. Why: because Kettle noticed something. Why: because the notice on the bridge says people like Mum get reviewed, and reviewed people disappear onto barges. Why: because I mean to rewrite our family onto that form so thoroughly that no clerk in Vellamark will ever find the seam.

I gave him the medium-sized answer, which was already more than he should have carried at eleven.

"The census man had questions about Mum. I'm making sure next time there aren't any."

Tam absorbed this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then, because he is our father's son and our mother's too: "Can I help?"

"*No.*"

"I could keep watch. I hear everything. Mrs. Petch next door says I've got ears like a bat."

"You have ears like a boy who eavesdrops, which is different." I took him by the shoulders. He was getting tall; I resented it. "Tam. Listen to me. What I was doing tonight—that's not a game and it's not a joke. If anyone ever found out, it wouldn't just be me. It would be all of us. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

He nodded, solemn as Sunday. Then: "Does Mum know?"

The question went through me like a splinter driven deep, because the answer was no, and the shape of that no was ugly. I was forging my mother's identity without my mother's knowledge—rewriting her origins, deciding what she'd been and where she'd come from, all to protect her, all without asking her. There was a version of that which was love. There was another version which was just another person telling Ilse Vane what she was.

"She'll know," I said finally, "if it becomes necessary. Until then, the fewer people carrying it, the safer."

"Two people are carrying it now," Tam pointed out.

"Which is why you're going to swear silence."

"On the lantern?"

Our family's most binding oath. We keep a small brass lantern on the mantel, older than any of us, the kind that hangs on bridges to mark the safe channel. When we were small and fought, Father would make us swear on the lantern, because—as he put it—a light that guides other people home must never be used to guide anyone astray. It's sentimental nonsense, honestly, brass and glass and a candle, but oaths don't care about their objects. They care about the people swearing them.

"On the lantern," Tam said, and stuck out his pinky finger with tremendous ceremony, and we swore, and the oath hung in the dark of the foundry between the type cases and the press like a third worker who'd clocked on early.

Then he yawned enormously, ruining the solemnity completely, and said, "Your copy of Dad's writing is good, but you lift the pen too high between words. He drags it. Like this—" and he scribbled a connection in the ash-dust on the stove lid with his fingertip, a dragging line, and I swear to you the boy had it exactly right, because he'd spent years watching the same hand I had, from a lower angle, closer to the table.

"Go to bed," I said, "before I decide you know too much."

"Too late," said Tam, and thumped upstairs, leaving me alone with the lamp and the ash and the folded paper in my pocket suddenly feeling heavier, as though complicity had weight, as though a secret shared were a case of type distributed across two shoulders instead of one.

* * *

I didn't sleep after that. I sat until the lamp burned low and the window over the sink went from black to the color of dirty pewter, and I thought about what I'd become in the course of a single night. A printer's daughter learns early that every act of setting type is an act of authority: someone decides what the words say, and everyone else believes them. That power had always belonged, in my imagination, to other people—editors, ministers, the ministry itself, whoever set the notices nailed to the bridge. Tonight I had reached over and taken a piece of it, small and hot, like a coal from the fire, and closed my fist around it.

It hurt. That surprised me most. I'd expected fear, expected guilt—had both, plenty—but underneath them ran a steady ache, grief almost, for the girl who went to bed honest and got up a counterfeiter. She'd been a decent girl. She'd believed the forms meant what they said, that truth was armor, that if you kept your accounts clean the clerks would pass you by. I'd buried her sometime around the second practice sheet and hadn't even stopped to mark the grave.

But the city was filling up with people being sorted, and sorting requires paperwork, and paperwork is only type on paper, and type on paper is my trade, my inheritance, my father's whole life pressed into mine. If the century intended to end with documents deciding who lived where, then perhaps it was fitting that a document-maker's daughter should learn to fight with the same weapons.

I banked the lamp. I washed my hands at the sink, and the ink came away from my fingers slowly, staining the water gray-blue, settling under my nails where it always settled, the permanent record of the family trade. Except that now the stain meant something different. Now it was evidence.

Upstairs, dawn was considering the rooftops. Out on the bridge, I knew, the notices fluttered in their frames, and sooner or later a man named Ambrose Kettle would come back up Foundry Lane with his beetle-scratch pen and his ledger and his questions, and when he did, he would find a form waiting for him so smooth, so weary-handed, so perfectly ordinary, that he'd never feel the floorboard give.

Unless, of course, he'd already counted what wasn't in any parish book—and unless he'd come back not to check our answers, but to tell us what they'd cost.
