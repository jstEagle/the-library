## 5. A Berth Is Promised

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept ledger that I suspect only men in my profession can properly appreciate. Other men take their satisfaction from wine, from women, from the thunder of their own opinions. I take mine from a column of figures that adds up, from an index card filed in its correct place, from the small, dry click of a discrepancy falling into line with three other discrepancies to make a pattern.

The Vane household had made a pattern.

I had not reported it yet. Let me be precise about that, because precision is the whole of my character and I will not have it said otherwise, even here, even to myself: I had *not* reported it. The page existed—my neat entry, dated the fourteenth of October, noting that one Ilse Vane, listed wife of Aldous Vane, typefounder, appeared in no parish register in Vellamark nor in any of the river parishes upstream where she claimed her girlhood. The ink was dry. The observation was made. But an observation and a report are two different animals, and I had kept the first leashed away from the second for eleven days, which for me was roughly equivalent to another man keeping a loaded pistol in his coat pocket and telling himself he'd merely forgotten it was there.

I told myself I was being thorough. Thoroughness required verification. A good census man did not fling half-checked discrepancies at his superiors; he confirmed them, cross-referenced them, built them into something a magistrate could act upon without embarrassment. That was the story I told myself on the tram, on the stairs of the Registry Office, at my desk with its government-issue lamp and its view of nothing but the registry's interior courtyard, where a caretaker swept the same leaves every morning as though the leaves conspired against him.

On the twelfth day, the story stopped being necessary.

"Kettle." Deputy Registrar Hobb stood in the aisle between the desks with a folder under his arm, and when Hobb carried a folder under his arm rather than clutching it, it meant promotion or dismissal, and I had not been dismissed yet, so. "My office."

Hobb's office smelled of pipe smoke and floor wax, the twin perfumes of authority in Vellamark. He had a window—an actual window, overlooking the bridge, though from this angle you saw mostly its underside, the great iron ribs of it, like looking up into the belly of some beached whale that the city had decided to call architecture. He sat, I stood, because standing is what one does in that office unless invited otherwise, and he let me stand long enough to remember my place before he spoke.

"You've flagged forty-one discrepancies this quarter," he said. "The next best man has nineteen."

"I count what's there, sir."

"Mm." He opened the folder. "Most men count what's convenient. Or what they're paid to count." He looked up at me over the folder's edge, and there was something almost companionable in it, which unsettled me more than hostility would have. "You know what happens to thorough men in this office, Kettle? Usually?"

"Transferred to the salt warehouses, sir."

"They're transferred to the salt warehouses," he agreed, "because thoroughness is only useful to a government when it's pointed at something. Unpointed thoroughness is just... friction." He slid a single sheet across the desk toward me. "So we're going to point yours."

I looked down. It was an internal memorandum, circular letterhead, the sort of document whose entire purpose is to exist in triplicate so that no one is responsible for anything. The Ministry of Interior Reordering, it announced, desired exemplary prosecutions in the matter of falsified residency papers before the year's end. One such case, well-documented and cleanly prosecuted, would reflect credit upon the district office. Upon the officer responsible, the word *credit* seemed to glow faintly, the way certain words do when they are doing work they were not designed for.

"We need an example," Hobb said. "Not a flood of them—a flood looks like chaos, and chaos reflects upward. One clean case. A family of decent standing, respectable trade, sympathetic faces. The kind of people the others look at and think, *there but for honest paperwork go I.*" He tapped the desk once. "You've been sitting on something for twelve days. Don't look surprised; you've requested the Vane parish records twice through channels, and channels talk. What is it?"

And here is the thing about ambition, which I understood in that moment the way a man understands water only after he has begun to drown in it: it does not feel like greed. It feels like relief. All those days of carrying the discrepancy alone, the weight of knowing and not-knowing-what-to-do-with-the-knowing, and now a superior was reaching out his hand to take half of it, and my first feeling—not my second, not my third, my *first*—was gratitude.

"Aldous Vane," I said. "Typefounder, Bridge Street. Wife registered as Ilse Vane, born Kessler, of Marrow Parish upstream. No Ilse Kessler exists in Marrow Parish. No Kessler family at all, in fact, within the years she claims. Either she was never born or she has invented herself, and the forms do not provide for the second possibility." I paused. "The children are registered. Hazel and Tamuel Vane, born in the city, records clean."

"And the husband?"

"Papers in order. Which suggests the wife's papers were arranged after arrival, by someone with access to a press and a casual relationship with official typefaces."

Hobb smiled. It was not a warm smile but it was an efficient one. "A printer forging documents. The newspapers will enjoy the irony. Deliver me the file complete by month's end—witness statements, the forged instrument itself if you can obtain it, the whole anatomy—and I will see your name attached to the case in the report. There's a berth-assignment post opening for the evacuation administration. Someone will have to decide who goes on the last barge." He leaned back. "Someone meticulous."

I walked back to my desk past forty other clerks bent over their columns, each of them counting what was there, and I sat down, and I found that my hands were entirely steady, and this frightened me more than shaking would have. A man's hands should shake a little, the first time he agrees to feed a family into a machine in exchange for advancement. Steady hands meant the agreement had been reached somewhere deeper than the part of me that was supposed to be consulted.

* * *

I went to Bridge Street that evening, off the books, out of uniform, in the grey civilian coat I kept for occasions requiring a face rather than an office.

The foundry occupied the ground floor of a narrow building wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing I could identify—birdcages, perhaps, or instruments for measuring guilt. Lamplight came through the workroom windows in the particular amber of oil burning over lead, and inside I could see figures moving among the type cases: the father, broad and slow as a piece of furniture; the boy, quick, everywhere at once; and the daughter.

The daughter.

I had seen her once before, briefly, on the day of the census visit, and had recorded her in my notes as *elder child, female, approx. 19, assists in trade.* That was the bureaucratic sentence. The living woman was another thing. She had ink to the second knuckle of both hands—it never fully comes off, that ink, it lives in the whorls—and she moved through the foundry the way a bellringer moves through a belfry, touching things not because they needed touching but because she knew them all by name.

She answered my knock herself, and when she saw me her face did something interesting. Not fear exactly. Recognition, followed by a rapid recalculation, followed by a decision to show me neither.

"Mr. Kettle." She stepped back to admit me, which surprised me; most people leave census men on the doorstep, as if hospitality might be contagious. "We don't have anything new to count."

"No. This isn't counting." I removed my hat, because manners cost nothing and buy more than they should. "May we speak privately?"

"My father's in the back with the casting bench. My brother's under a table somewhere. Privacy on Bridge Street is a theoretical discipline." But she led me to the front office anyway, a cramped room smelling of paper and solvent, and closed the door on the clatter of the workshop, and turned to face me with her arms folded, waiting, the way one waits for weather.

I had rehearsed a speech on the tram. It had been a good speech, with a shape to it—sympathy first, then the threat held at arm's length like something unpleasant on tongs, then the offer, framed as generosity. Standing in front of her, I heard the speech in my head and found it cheap. So I set it aside, which I tell myself was respect, though it may simply have been that she would have seen through it in four seconds and I could not afford to be seen through.

"Your mother's name appears in no parish record anywhere on the river," I said. "I wrote that down eleven days ago. In eleven days I have not filed it. Do you understand what that means?"

Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, fast and cold, and I confess it gave me a small unworthy thrill to watch it, the way a chess player thrills when the opponent turns out to play properly. She understood immediately. Most people require the machinery explained. She simply looked at the gears.

"It means either you're a lazy clerk," she said, "or you're holding it for something."

"Or both."

"Which is it?"

"The distinction matters less than the price," I said. "Miss Vane, I'm going to be blunt, because bluntness is the only currency you appear to accept. Your mother does not legally exist. When that fact reaches the file upstairs—and it will reach the file, the only question is when—your family enters the removal schedule. Removal means the barges, and the barges mean whatever the barges mean, and nobody downstream has ever sent back a letter describing it fondly. That is the future as it currently stands. I am offering you a different future. Narrower. But different."

"How much narrower?"

"One page narrower." I watched her. "If a certain page in my working notes were to become illegible—if, say, an ink spill, a clerical error, an unfortunate accident of the tea cup were to render the entry unusable—your household would remain, on paper, exactly what it appears to be: a typefounder, his wife, two children, all born where the forms say they were born. The census would pass over you. The reordering would pass over you. You would keep your name, your street, your foundry, your—" I gestured vaguely at everything, the walls, the lamplight, the smell of hot metal, "—your life."

She was quiet for a moment. Through the door I could hear the boy laughing at something, a bright unguarded sound, the kind of laugh that exists only in houses that do not yet know what is coming for them.

"And the price," she said. Not a question. She knew there was a price; girls who grow up around presses know that nothing prints for free.

"There are expenses associated with misplacing official records," I said. "Risks. If the loss is discovered, the loss falls on me alone, and I am not a man of independent means. I would need compensation proportionate to the risk."

"Money."

"Money is one form."

Her chin came up slightly. "What are the other forms?"

Here is where I should tell you that I named a figure and haggled and behaved like the corrupt functionary of the popular imagination, rubbing his hands, etc. The truth was less picturesque and worse. I named no figure. I told her I would send word of the amount and the manner of payment within the week, and I watched relief begin to settle onto her face—the relief of a person who believes she has been offered a fine and paid it, who thinks the transaction is a wound that heals—and I did not correct her. Because the money was real, and I wanted it, but the money was also the smaller half of the arrangement, and I judged—I correctly—that a girl clever enough to spot the gears would pay the coin gladly if the alternative was spelled out plainly, and so I left the alternative unspelled, sitting between us in that paper-smelling room like a guest who had not yet been introduced.

I put on my hat. At the door I paused, because there was one more thing, and I have never been able to leave a document unsigned, so to speak.

"Miss Vane. A word of professional advice, free of charge." I turned. "Whatever your mother's true name is, wherever she truly comes from—you should burn anything that says so. Letters, papers, keepsakes with writing on them. The Registry has begun secondary sweeps of households already counted. Old correspondence is exactly the sort of thing a junior examiner finds and makes a reputation on." I settled the hat. "Consider it a courtesy between people who both understand what paper can do."

She stared at me, and I saw her arrive at the understanding I had intended: that I knew there *was* something to burn. That my offer of mercy came pre-loaded with knowledge of her guilt, and that every kindness I extended would henceforth be a reminder of the knife folded away in my pocket.

"Good evening, Mr. Kettle," she said, in a voice with no temperature at all.

"Good evening."

* * *

The walk home took me along the bridge, because it always does; the bridge is the spine of Vellamark and all roads in this city are ribs bending toward it. Dusk had come down. The lamps were being lit—one of them, above the eastern pier, burns in a little iron lantern-house that has been there longer than the Registry, longer than the census, longer probably than the idea of Vellamark—and below, on the quay, the registration queue had thinned to its evening stragglers, stamping their feet against the cold, holding their papers flat against their chests like men holding wounds.

I stopped midway across and looked down at them, and I performed upon myself the small experiment I sometimes perform, which is to imagine being a better man. If I were a better man, I thought, I would have gone up those stairs to Hobb tomorrow and resigned, or confessed, or done whatever it is better men do instead of climbing. If I were a better man, the Vanes' fate would not now be a currency in my pocket, spending itself in small denominations against my future.

But then—and here is the thought that turned me homeward, and I record it honestly because honesty is the one luxury I have always allowed myself—then I remembered the memorandum. *Exemplary prosecution.* I remembered Hobb saying *someone meticulous.* I pictured the berth-assignment office: a desk with a window, actual lists passing under actual hands, my hands, deciding which names went down to the quay and which stayed in their beds. And I felt the same steadiness in my hands that had frightened me in the afternoon, except that by now I had stopped frightening easily. Power, I discovered, tastes less like wine than like bread—unremarkable, daily, necessary. Once you have eaten, you cannot imagine having not eaten.

The lantern over the eastern pier caught my eye one last time. It burned steady, indifferent, the way lights have burned over rivers since before anyone thought to ask permission. Somebody kept it lit, I supposed. Some keeper climbed those stairs every dusk with oil and a taper and asked nothing of the light except that it continue.

I found myself thinking, with a small cold pleasure, how useful such a position must be—to see everything on the water and be seen by none of it.

Then I went home, and sharpened a pencil, and began drafting the witness questions I would need for the Vane file. Month's end, Hobb had said. There was time. There was always time; time was the one resource the desperate had too much of and I had learned to buy at discount.

I did not yet know, that night, that the elder Miss Vane was already at her father's type cases practicing his hand by lamplight, or that paper cut both ways, or that the page I meant to lose could be lost twice.

But the reader may believe me when I say this much: I had met her eyes, and I had seen the gears turn, and somewhere beneath the ledger-keeping part of me—the part that adds, and files, and clicks shut—a quieter clerk had entered a note he did not read aloud:

*This one counts back.*
