## 6. What Can Be Carried

My father owned three thousand pounds of type and could not name a single one of his mother's recipes.

I learned this on a Tuesday in October, when the inventory began. The census notices had been up on the bridge for six weeks by then, and the household had passed through the stages of grief the way a river passes through a lock—slowly, with much groaning of timber and no possibility of going backward. Denial had been brief; my father had simply read the proclamation twice, said "nonsense," and gone back to cutting a new fount of Clarendon. Anger had lasted longer, aimed mostly at the government, the census takers, the bridge itself for having the poor taste to host the notices. Bargaining had taken the form of letters written to cousins downriver who never wrote back.

And now we had arrived at acceptance, which in our family took the form of sorting.

"Three cases," Papa announced, setting them on the floor of the workroom like three small coffins. "One each. We choose tonight."

It was evening. The lamp over the composing stone burned low and amber, and outside the window Vellamark was doing its autumn impression—rain coming off the river sideways, gulls complaining, the gas lamps along the quay stuttering like nervous witnesses. Tam sat cross-legged on a type cabinet with a pencil behind his ear, ready to record our decisions as though this were a game, which I suppose to a twelve-year-old it partly was.

"I'll go first," Papa said, and went directly to the cases.

Not the furniture. Not the portraits of grandparents whose faces had faded to the color of weak tea. Not the silver, which Mama eyed with the expression of a woman performing long division. He went to the racks of type—Caslon and Baskerville, the fat-faced Didots, the little drawers of ornaments shaped like fleurons and pointing hands—and stood before them the way other men stand before their children.

"These come with us."

"Papa." I kept my voice level. I had been practicing level voices for weeks. "The barge has weight limits. They weighed the trunks at the quay yesterday. A man's trunk was turned away because it held books."

"Then he packed them badly."

"A case of ten-point Bodoni weighs more than every book he owns."

"Then the Bodoni matters more."

I looked at him. He looked at the type. Somewhere below us the foundry's great press stood silent under its sheet, patient as a sleeping ox, and I felt the old familiar ache of loving a room full of metal more than most people love each other.

"Papa, you cannot carry three thousand pounds of letters onto an evacuation barge."

"Two hundred pounds," he said. "A selection. The essentials."

"The essentials," Mama repeated from the doorway. She had come up with the teapot and the particular stillness she wore when she intended to win something. "Which letters are essential, Aldous? The vowels? All words need vowels. Or perhaps just your own initials, so that whatever they print downriver is secretly about you?"

Tam snorted. Papa gave her the look of a man betrayed by his own household.

"You're both missing the point entirely. This is not *metal*. This is—" He stopped, and I watched him reach for the word the way a man reaches for a rung in the dark. "This is four generations of Vane hands. My grandfather cut these matrices. Your names are in there, girls' names, boy's name—every letter needed to spell every one of us. You want me to leave that on a dock in Vellamark for soldiers to melt into bullets?"

"No," I said. "I want you to leave it for someone else to print with."

The room went quiet the way a press goes quiet—not gradually, but all at once, with a finality.

"That's not how it works," Papa said. "You don't leave a foundry to strangers. A foundry dies with its family or it doesn't die at all."

"Then it will die," I said, "and we will not."

* * *

I should explain what the type meant to him, because I understood it even then, even while I argued against it, and understanding a man's madness does not make it less mad but it does make it harder to hate.

Aldous Vane had inherited this foundry from his father, who had inherited it from his, and in all that time the family had survived plagues, fires, two recessions, and one scandal involving a bishop and a pamphlet nobody was supposed to have printed. Through all of it the type had remained. When Mama's first child—a sister I never met, born and gone inside a single week—died, Papa set her name in the smallest pearl type he had and printed one card, and then distributed the type back into its case, letter by letter, because that was how you kept a person: not in lead, he said, but in the capacity to say her name again whenever you needed to.

He believed, genuinely, that a family was a fount of type. That as long as the sorts existed, any sentence could be set again. Any loss could be recomposed.

And here is the thing about arguments with a man like that: you are not arguing about luggage. You are arguing about whether souls live in objects or in people, and only one of those can fit on a barge.

* * *

We inventoried until midnight.

Mama's case filled first and filled sensibly: linens, the good wool, photographs, her mother's ring sewn into a coat hem, a tin of coffee she guarded like contraband. She moved through the house with the efficiency of a woman who had already done this once in her life—I knew she had crossed a border at nineteen with one case, though she never spoke of what she'd left, and the burned letters were months away yet, and I didn't know, that night, that she was already rehearsing departure in ways none of us suspected. She only said, folding a tablecloth into a shape no tablecloth naturally takes, "In my family we learned that you carry the needle and thread and let the rest be re-sewn."

Tam's case was a scandal of nonsense: a compass, three marbles, a frog skeleton, a tin of bootblack for reasons he declined to explain, and a folded map of the city's drains that I pretended not to see and privately resolved to confiscate later, or possibly never, because a boy who maps sewers is either a nuisance or a genius and I hadn't yet determined which.

Then there was Papa's case, which contained forty pounds of type by midnight and climbing.

"Papa. Look at this objectively."

"I am looking at it objectively. It's forty pounds of Garamond."

"It's forty pounds we cannot lift onto a gangway while also lifting Mama's case and Tam's marbles."

"The marbles stay."

"They do not!" said Tam.

"Gentlemen," Mama said, meaning neither of them, meaning me.

I took a breath and did the thing I had been circling all night, the thing that would start the quarrel properly, because some part of me—some stubborn, frightened part—wanted it started. Better a quarrel than the silence we'd been living in since the notices went up, that polite, careful quiet of a family walking on eggshells made of each other.

"Papa, if the choice comes down to it—if it ever actually comes down to it—the type stays and we go. All of us. I need you to say it."

"I need you to stop talking like a customs officer."

"I need you to promise."

"And I need you," he said, turning, and his voice cracked upward into a register I had heard maybe five times in my life, usually directed at suppliers who shorted him on kerns, "to understand that if we leave this type, we leave *ourselves*. You think they'll let us set type downriver? Refugees don't get presses, Hazel. Refugees get ration cards and barracks and gratitude for their silence. This—" he put his hand flat on the case of Garamond, tenderly, obscenely, like a man touching a wife, "—this is the only thing we own that makes us who we are."

"We are *people*, Papa!"

"Yes! People who are nothing without their work!"

"That is the saddest sentence I have ever heard you say."

"Hazel—"

"Four generations of Vanes and the thing you choose to save is the alphabet. Do you hear yourself? Mama crossed half a continent with less than you're planning to spend on ampersands. Tam is twelve years old and his whole life fits in a shoebox with a dead frog in it. And you—you'd trade a berth's worth of weight for punctuation."

"Fleurons are not punctuation, they are *ornaments*—"

"*That is worse!*"

Mama laughed. It burst out of her unexpectedly, sharp and bright, and for a moment the three of us stared at her, and she pressed her hand to her mouth and shook her head and said, "God forgive me, but listen to us. Listen to us, fighting over commas while the government decides whether we exist. Aldous. Beloved. Put down the Garamond."

He did not put down the Garamond.

* * *

Here is what I did not say, that night, because I was not yet brave enough or dishonest enough to say it: I already knew the type wouldn't go. I already knew, in the way you know a storm is coming before the wind arrives, that before this was over there would be bargains, and papers, and choices made alone in lamplight, and that the question of what fits in a case would turn out to be the smallest question in the house.

What I said instead was quieter, and crueler for being true:

"You taught me every letter in that rack. You put my hands on the graver when I was nine. If the Vane legacy is the type, then the type is already safe—because it's in me, and I'm getting on that barge, and so are you, and so are they. Leave the lead, Papa. Take the printer."

The lamp flickered. Rain worked at the window. And my father looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read then and can read perfectly now—it was the look of a man realizing his apprentice has surpassed him, and grieving it, and being proud of it, all in the same breath, none of which he could afford to show.

"The printer stays with the press," he said finally. "That's how it works. That's how it has always worked."

"Then we'll find another press."

"There is no other press."

"There is always another press," I said, "somewhere. That's rather the whole nature of presses."

He almost smiled. I saw it happen and then watched him strangle it, because fathers must not smile when daughters win arguments; it sets precedents. He closed the case of Garamond with exaggerated care, as though closing a casket, and said, "We'll decide tomorrow. Tonight we sleep on it."

"We've slept on it for six weeks."

"Then we'll sleep on it *decisively*."

From the type cabinet, Tam raised his pencil. "For the record," he said, "I vote we take the type and hide in the sewers."

"Noted and rejected," said all three of us at once, and for a moment—the last such moment for a long time—we sounded like a family again, four voices in a lamplit room, disagreeing about everything except each other.

* * *

They went up to bed. I stayed.

I stayed because the workroom at night is the only church I have ever believed in, and because I wanted to look at the type honestly, without my father's hands hovering over it, and decide what I actually thought.

I ran my finger along the cases. Caslon, sturdy and forgiving, the typeface of parish notices and pancake recipes. The Didot, all hairline elegance, good for wedding invitations and lies told beautifully. The heavy poster types we used for playbills, letters tall enough to shout. Every voice a city might need, sorted into drawers, waiting.

Three thousand pounds of language. It would not fit in a case. It would not fit on a barge. It barely fit in the building.

And yet.

I thought about what Papa had said—that refugees get silence, not presses—and I thought about the notices nailed to the bridge, printed on government stock, the government's alphabet telling us who could stay and who must go, and I felt something shift in my chest, some small heavy sort dropping into a new line, forming a word I didn't yet have the courage to read aloud.

If we left the type behind and it fell into strangers' hands, it would print whatever strangers wanted. But if it fell into *my* hands—

Well. I was my father's daughter. I knew what type was for.

I blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark among all those sleeping letters, and upstairs I could hear my parents' voices, low and continuous, still negotiating, still deciding what could be carried and what must be left, and I thought: tomorrow. Tomorrow we'll fight about it again, and it will look like a quarrel about luggage, and it will really be a quarrel about souls, and we will lose sight of the fact that somewhere out there a man named Kettle is writing our names in a ledger with a very fine pen.

I was right about all of it. I wish I had been wrong about the pen.
