## 7. The Price of One Page

There is a particular pleasure in being owed something by a desperate woman, and I will not pretend I did not savor it. Ambition, like type metal, is mostly lead — heavy, dull, worthless until someone applies heat. Hazel Vane had applied the heat herself when she sat across from me in the records office and asked what it would take to make a page disappear.

I had told her to come back in two days. This was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. A price named too quickly is a price that can be haggled down; a price left sitting in silence grows fat on its own mystery. I spent those two days doing what any careful man does with leverage: I measured it.

The Vane file was thin but promising. Aldous Vane, proprietor of the foundry on Cordwainer's Lane, forty-one years in Vellamark by his own account — though his account had shifted between the trade registry of 'sixty-two and the militia rolls of 'seventy-four, which was the sort of drift that happens when a man builds a life out of paper and assumes no one will count the pages. Ilse Vane, born — according to nothing at all, because she existed nowhere except her husband's declarations. Twenty years married to a city that had never once written her name down. And the boy, Tam, twelve, born in Vellamark itself, which meant he was legally a child of the river even if his mother was a ghost.

It was the ghost who interested me. Ghosts are useful. You cannot hang a ghost, but you can charge rent for the haunting.

* * *

I received the summons from Director Graveling on the morning of the second day, which I took as an omen, though omens in the census bureau are indistinguishable from paperwork.

"Ambrose." He did not look up from the ledger he was ruining with red ink. "You flagged the Vane household. Discrepancy in the marital record."

"I noted it, sir."

"Note it again. Louder." He turned a page. "The Commissioner wants examples before the barge season closes. Households handled cleanly, publicly, with the proper documentation trail. It reflects well on the office." He finally looked at me, and there was that expression he wore the way other men wore medals — the look of a man explaining that your work has become his. "Deliver the Vane case complete by month's end and I can put your name forward for berth assignment."

Berth assignment. I kept my face in the modest register — pleased, humbled, faintly stupid — while my pulse did its vulgar dance behind my collar. Berth assignment was not clerical work. Berth assignment was the most powerful pen in Vellamark. Every family in the lower quarters would spend the winter calculating their worth against your signature. You would become, in effect, weather.

"Complete, sir," I said. "Meaning?"

"Meaning the discrepancy resolved. Either the woman exists or she doesn't, Kettle, and either answer requires a page that says so. The bureau does not traffic in ambiguity. Ambiguity is for poets." He returned to his red ink. "And do it quietly. The Commissioner dislikes mess. He prefers his examples tidy."

Tidy. I walked back through the records room thinking about tidiness, and about how the difference between a bureaucrat and a criminal is largely one of stationery.

* * *

She came at dusk, as I'd instructed, when the office emptied and the lamps along Cordwainer's Lane were just being lit. She wore a coat too thin for the season and carried herself with the straight-backed economy of someone who had spent years lifting trays of lead. Her hands were ink-stained at the cuticles. I have always noticed hands. Hands confess faster than faces.

"You've had two days," she said, without preamble, without sitting. She stood in front of my desk like a witness rather than a supplicant, and I confess the distinction irritated me even as it impressed me.

"And you've had two days to consider whether you'd rather bargain than beg," I said. "Sit down, Miss Vane. Standing negotiations go badly. People say things they mean."

She sat. Good. I let the silence run long enough to be felt, then opened the Vane file and laid three sheets on the desk between us, squared and neat, the way a card player shows his hand only after the money is committed.

"Your mother," I said, "does not exist."

"She exists."

"Legally, Miss Vane, existence is a document. Your mother has none. No parish record, no arrival manifest, no marriage registration under any name I can find in four dioceses. Which means one of two things. Either your father never registered the marriage — a misdemeanor, fineable, forgettable — or the marriage cannot be registered, because the woman standing in your kitchen entered this country under circumstances the bureau would find *interesting*." I tapped the middle sheet. "Your father's story has drifted twice over thirty years. Drift is innocent nine times in ten. But the tenth time pays for the other nine."

Her face did very little. That was the thing about Hazel Vane that would keep me off-balance for months afterward: she rationed her expressions the way poor people rationed coal. What little escaped was deliberate.

"So name it," she said. "What you want."

* * *

Here is where I should perhaps explain myself, since history — if it bothers to record me at all, which I doubt; history prefers its villains loud — will likely get it wrong. I did not hate the Vanes. I did not hate anyone in particular. Hatred is a luxury of men with secure positions. What I had was a window, closing fast, and the Vane family happened to be standing in the frame. If it hadn't been them it would have been the Ostranders, or the Pell widow, or half the tannery quarter. Someone always stands in the frame. The trick is not to apologize for the glass.

"The client ledger," I said.

Her brow moved a fraction. "The foundry's ledger?"

"Every job your father has taken in the last fifteen years. Every commission, every client, every private order. Dates, names, amounts." I folded my hands. "Ledgers are honest documents, Miss Vane. That's what makes them dangerous. A man will lie to the census, to the church, to his wife. He will not lie in his own ledger, because a ledger is how a man knows himself."

"What could possibly—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, watched the calculation move behind her eyes like a rat crossing a floor. "Who's in it?"

"Magistrate Corven," I said, "for one. Fourteen commissions over eleven years. Personal stationery, certificates, proclamations — and three orders, in the winters of 'ninety-one, 'ninety-three, and 'ninety-six, marked in your father's own hand as *private plates, no counterfoil*. Do you know what a plate without a counterfoil is, Miss Vane? It is a plate that prints things no one is meant to trace. Seals, most likely. Magistrates are not permitted to commission seals privately. It suggests the magistrate was minting his own authority, and selling it."

"That's speculation."

"It's a ledger entry. Speculation is what the tribunal will do with it." I leaned back. "I don't need Corven convicted. I need the possibility of Corven. The Director wants a tidy example. A foundry owner who forged state-adjacent documents for a sitting magistrate — that is not merely tidy. That is *festive*."

"You'd destroy my father to decorate your report."

"I'd *cite* your father," I said, "to furnish my career. Let's not dress it in your vocabulary. Destruction is what tribunals do. I am merely the man who brings the lamp."

Something flickered across her face then — not fear, which I expected, and not pleading, which I'd have enjoyed managing, but a cold, quick appraisal, as if I were a piece of machinery she was pricing for scrap. It unsettled me more than tears would have. Tears tell you that you've won.

"And in exchange?" she said.

"In exchange, page eleven of the Vane file — the page noting your mother's absence from every record in this city — develops an ink blot. A bad one. Unreadable. The clerk's error, regrettable, uncorrectable before the deadline. Your mother becomes a smudge, and smudges are not deported. They're simply never counted." I let that sit. "One page, one ledger. Symmetry. Even you must admire the bookkeeping."

* * *

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the lamplighter made his rounds along Cordwainer's Lane, and his shadow crossed the frosted window twice. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, stripped of the shop-girl courtesy she'd worn like an apron.

"You understand what you're asking. That ledger is the foundry. Half our clients are in it. If it goes to your tribunal, every name in it gets investigated, and every investigation takes weeks, and every week is a week closer to the last barge. You're not trading one page for one page. You're trading my mother's life for my father's reputation, and mine, and Tam's, because a family under tribunal doesn't board anything. They wait. They wait in a hall somewhere while men like you decide."

"Yes," I said.

She blinked. I think she had prepared arguments against denial, against deflection, against some plausible fiction about bureau procedure. She had not prepared for agreement.

"You admit it."

"I prefer it admitted early. It saves us both the theater." I stood and went to the window, because a man delivering ugly terms should deliver them standing; it spreads the indignity around. "Miss Vane, here is what I know that you also know but haven't said aloud. The barge holds four hundred souls. Vellamark holds forty thousand who want to be on it. The lottery is theater — I helped draft the weighting tables, so believe me. Favor decides berths, and favor is purchased, and the currency is never coin. It is *usefulness*. Somewhere in this city tonight, a hundred families are deciding what they can offer, and most of them will offer money, and money is boring, and boring men stay clerks." I turned back to her. "I am offering to be bought with the single most interesting object in your house. You may hate me for it. I recommend you do it efficiently, because we haven't much time."

"Corven," she said slowly. "If you burn him, he'll burn you. Magistrates keep their own ledgers, Mr. Kettle. Of favors."

Ah. There it was — the thing I had been waiting for, the moment she stopped bargaining for her family and started bargaining like me. Revulsion and arithmetic, braided together in one girl's voice. I could almost hear the loom working.

"Corven will be too busy surviving to audit me," I said. "And if he isn't — well. A man holding a magistrate's indiscretions is not a man without friends. Disgraced officials make excellent patrons. They have nothing left to lose and excellent memories." I smiled, and I am told my smile is my least trustworthy feature. "But that's my risk, isn't it? Yours is smaller. Bring me the ledger, and your mother becomes ink."

"My mother becomes ink," she repeated. She said it the way a printer might — testing the weight of the phrase, feeling for the kerning, checking whether the words would hold up under pressure. Then: "I'll need time. The ledger isn't kept at the house. My father keeps it locked at the works, and he counts the pages."

"Naturally. He's a careful man. Careful men built this city and careful men will drown in it." I pulled a blank sheet toward me and wrote out the terms in my best clerk's hand — date, exchange, no signatures, because signatures are for amateurs; a document unsigned is deniable, and deniability is the true gold standard. I slid it across. "Eight days. After that, page eleven goes into the monthly compilation, and compilations cannot be amended. Not even by me."

She read the sheet once, folded it into her coat, and rose. At the door she paused — and I want to record accurately what she said, because I have turned it over many times since, usually at night, usually after the third glass.

"You think you're climbing," she said. "But ladders made of people have short rungs, Mr. Kettle. You'll run out of family before you run out of ambition."

"Then I'll hope the view is good from wherever I stop," I said.

She left. The door clicked shut with the small, decent sound of ordinary wood, and I sat alone in the lamplight with the Vane file open in front of me, feeling the peculiar double sensation that I suspect all successful extortionists know: satisfaction in one hand, and in the other, held at arm's length so it won't stain, the knowledge of exactly what kind of man the evening had made me.

I told myself the usual things. That Corven deserved whatever came. That the bureau would sort these people anyway, with or without my lamp. That a rising tide lifts all boats, and I had merely agreed to describe the water. The usual things. They worked the way weak tea works — technically liquid, spiritually insufficient.

Then I turned to page eleven and looked at it for a long time. Ilse Vane. Born nowhere, recorded nowhere, twenty years a wife and mother in a city that had never once consented to see her. One ink blot and she would be free. One client ledger and her husband would be ruined. Such clean symmetry. Even I admired the bookkeeping.

Eight days, I had given her. I wondered — watching the lamplight shiver in the draught from the shut door — whether she would bring me the ledger, or something better. She had looked at me, near the end, the way a compositor looks at a line of type that isn't sitting right. As though I were movable.

That thought kept me at the office past midnight. Not the ledger. The look.
