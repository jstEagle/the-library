## 8. Tam's Map

The thing nobody tells you about the end of the world is that it smells like coal dust and cabbage water and your sister burning the porridge again. I had expected something grander. Trumpets, maybe. Or at least a decent thunderstorm.

Instead there was Hazel at the foundry bench every night, cutting type by lamplight like the world wasn't ending, and Father counting cases of lead like a miser counting coins he couldn't eat, and Mother folding the same three shirts over and over as if repetition were a kind of prayer. And me, Tam Vane, twelve years old and the only person in this entire drowning city who had a plan.

Well. The beginning of a plan. A plan is mostly just knowing things other people don't know, and I knew Vellamark better than anyone alive who wasn't employed in the business of knowing Vellamark.

I'd been mapping since summer. Not on paper, at first—paper could be taken from you, paper was evidence—but in my head, which nobody had invented a way to search yet. Then in October I found an old school copybook behind the type racks, half its pages spoiled by ink blots, and I decided that spoiled pages were exactly right for what I needed. Nobody confiscates a child's ruined arithmetic book.

I drew the city the way I understood it: not as streets and squares, because streets were for people going somewhere they were allowed to go, but as the underneath. The sewers first. Old Marrow Lane's main ran under the tannery and came up behind the fish market; I'd walked it twice with a candle stub held high and my sleeve over my nose, and come out the other side smelling so bad that Mother made me sleep in the kitchen for a week. Worth it. I knew where the grates were loose, where the brick had crumbled enough to squeeze a thin boy through, where the water ran shallow enough to wade without losing a boot.

Then the coal chutes. Every cellar in the old quarter had one—a little iron door flush to the pavement where the coalmen tipped their sacks—and half of them opened into passages between buildings, or onto the backs of courtyards, or in the case of the chandlery on Rope Walk, directly into the next cellar over, which belonged to a widow who kept forty cats and would never notice one more boy passing through.

And the rooftops. And the gaps between warehouses where carters stacked barrels and forgot them until spring. And the drainage culvert behind the gasworks that everyone said was barred but hadn't been since the flood of '93, when the bars were cut away and never replaced because nobody remembered whose job replacing them was. That was the secret of Vellamark, if you wanted to know it: half the city's defenses existed only because somebody once had a job, and the job was forgotten, and the iron rusted, and nobody checked.

That was my theory of everything, really. Grown-ups built systems and then stopped looking at them. If you were small and patient, you could live in all the places they'd stopped looking.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," I told Hazel on Thursday evening, spreading the copybook across the kitchen table while she tried to mend her boot by candlelight. "Look. Here's us." I tapped the foundry, marked with a little X. "And here—" my finger traveled down the page, through the sewers, past the culvert, out along the river road, "—is everywhere we can go without ever setting foot on a street where a soldier could see us."

Hazel looked at the map the way she looked at badly set type. Like something that needed correcting.

"That's a sewer," she said.

"Several sewers. And a culvert."

"And you propose we live in them."

"Not live. *Travel.* There's a difference." I'd thought about this a lot, so I laid it out properly, the way Father laid out a case of type before a big job. "The census men count houses. They chalk doors, they write down names, they come back when you're not expecting them. But they can't count what's underneath. We hide in the old quarter—the cellars under the rope walk are connected, I've been through six of them—and when the counting's done, when they've written *gone, gone, gone* next to our name, we come back out and open the foundry again and they can't say anything because officially we left years ago."

I sat back, pleased with myself. It was a good speech. I'd practiced it against the wall of the privy, which has excellent acoustics.

Hazel didn't laugh. She didn't even smile, which was unusual, because Hazel laughed at most things I said, usually in the way that means *you're ridiculous but I love you*. She put down her boot and her awl and looked at me for a long moment, and the candlelight did something strange to her face, made her look older and tired in a way I didn't have a word for yet.

"Tam," she said. "How many days have you spent underground this autumn?"

"I don't— some."

"And how many meals have you missed coming home?"

"One. Maybe two. The cats get fed anyway, the widow feeds anything that moves—"

"The widow on Rope Walk."

"She doesn't mind."

"Tam." She said my name the second time more gently, and somehow that was worse. "You smell like the inside of the world, and you've got coal in the creases of your hands that won't wash out, and I haven't said anything because I thought—I don't know what I thought." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, smearing ink across her eyebrow, though she'd have died before admitting the ink was there. "It's a good map. It's honestly a good map. But maps aren't the same as doors, and doors aren't the same as permission, and permission is the thing they're selling now."

"Then we don't buy," I said. "We go around."

She almost smiled then. Almost. "You sound like Father."

"He sounds like *me*, he's just older about it."

That earned a real half-laugh, quick and quiet, and I counted it a victory and pressed on. "Show Father. He'll see the sense of it. The sewers run within fifty yards of the river road—if the barge comes, we could be on it without a soul marking us, no lottery, no papers—"

"No papers." Hazel went very still, in a way I noticed but didn't understand. Not then. "Yes. Well. Let me think about it."

Which, in Hazel language, meant *no*, but I was twelve and optimistic, and optimism is just stubbornness wearing its good coat. I took my copybook to bed that night and added three new chutes I'd scouted after supper, and drew a little star over the gasworks culvert, and slept the sleep of a general who believes his war will be short.

* * *

On Sunday I went out to finish the survey of the east quarter, and that was the day the childhood ended. Though I want it noted that it didn't end dramatically, with trumpets or thunderstorms, but with a piece of chalk.

I'd come up out of the Marrow Lane sewer around noon—it was the fastest route east and I'd gotten fast, four minutes corner to corner if the water was low—and I was brushing the worst of the muck off my knees behind the cooperage when I heard boots. Not carters' boots. Soldiers' boots have a particular rhythm to them, all together, like one enormous person walking, and by December I should have known to freeze at that sound instead of peeking around the barrel stack. But I was twelve and optimistic, and I peeked.

Four of them, marching up Tanner's Row. And behind them, a clerk on a stool carried out of the registrar's office and set down at intervals, holding a ledger and a stick of chalk, white as a bone.

They stopped at the first house. The cooper's house. One soldier knocked, spoke briefly to whoever answered—I saw old Mrs. Cooper's face appear, gray above the door—and then the clerk stood, wetted his chalk, and wrote on the door itself. Big letters. Numbers. A slash between them, like a wound.

I knew what it meant. Everyone by then knew what the chalk meant, the way you know what a black wreath on a door means, without anyone having to explain. The numbers were a count. How many souls inside. And the slash was the mark that said *counted, recorded, awaiting disposition*, which is a phrase I'd read on a notice posted by the bridge and copied out in my best hand into the back of the copybook, because I collected official phrases the way other boys collected marbles. *Disposition.* Such a tidy word. It sounds like arranging flowers.

They went to the next house. Chalk. The next. Chalk. Mrs. Cooper stood in her doorway the whole time with her arms folded tight, watching them write on her home like a man writes on a crate before shipping, and she didn't say one word, and neither did anyone else on the row, and the silence was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

I crouched behind my barrels and did the thing I do, the useful thing, the thing I'd been training myself for all autumn without knowing why: I memorized. Four soldiers, blue-gray coats, one corporal with a scarred cheek. Clerk young, left-handed, chewed his chalk. They worked west to east, odd numbers first. They'd reach our street by evening, if they kept that pace. Our street. Our door. Our father's name, in chalk, over the lintel where the lantern hung.

My legs wanted to run and I made them stay, because running makes noise and noise makes soldiers look, and a boy seen near a sewer grate is a boy who gets questions asked of him that don't stop being asked. So I stayed crouched in the coal-smell behind the cooperage and watched the whole row get numbered, seven doors, eight, nine, and at the ninth house a woman came out crying and begged the clerk something I couldn't hear, and the corporal stepped between them—not rough, not gentle either, just *there*, a wall with a rifle—and the woman went back inside and the clerk finished his number and they moved on.

Nobody screamed. That was the terrible part. I'd always imagined the end of things would involve screaming, fires, something you could point to later and say *that was the moment*. But it was chalk on wood, in daylight, on a cold Sunday, and the sky was the color of dishwater, and a dog trotted past the soldiers' boots like nothing was happening, because to the dog nothing was.

When they'd turned the corner I waited the length of twenty breaths, then another twenty, then slipped out and ran—not along the streets, I wasn't a fool, but down and in, through the chandlery chute, past the widow's forty cats, through two cellars and a passage that smelled of onions, all the way home the underneath way, my map working perfectly, every door opening the way I'd drawn it.

And here's the thing I couldn't explain to anybody, not Hazel, not Father, not God if He was taking calls that afternoon: the map *worked*. That was the horror of it. Everything I'd learned, every chute and culvert and forgotten gap, functioned exactly as designed, and it changed nothing, because the soldiers weren't hunting us through the streets. They were writing on the doors. You can't hide from a man who already knows where you live. My beautiful underworld was a maze for mice, and the cat had learned to count.

* * *

I came up through the foundry cellar and into the workshop still smelling of the river-dark, and Hazel was there at the bench, and Father was there among his cases, and Mother upstairs, and for a second I just stood in the doorway breathing foundry air—lead and oil and lamp-black, the smell of my whole life—and nobody noticed me, because I was small and quiet, which had always been my advantage and suddenly felt like my whole biography.

"Hazel," I said.

She looked up. Whatever was on my face made her set down her type stick at once.

"They're chalking doors," I said. "Tanner's Row, nine houses, heading west. Odd numbers first. They'll reach us tonight." My voice held steady, which I was proud of. "Corporal with a scarred cheek. Left-handed clerk. I've got it all."

I said *I've got it all* like it was treasure, like information was the same as rescue, and Hazel crossed the room and knelt down in front of me, which she hadn't done since I was eight, and took my face in her ink-stained hands.

"Tam. What did it look like? The chalk."

"Big letters. Numbers." I swallowed. "A slash."

"A slash." Something moved behind her eyes, some calculation turning over fast, and I understood dimly that she was thinking about papers, about forms, about whatever it was she'd been doing late at night that smelled faintly of practice ink when I crept past her door—though that thread, that particular secret, I didn't pull yet. Twelve-year-olds know less than they think, but we know more than adults believe. I filed it. Filing things was becoming my trade.

"We're hiding them," I said. "Right? We've got my map. Cellars, sewers, the culvert—we disappear tonight and come back when—"

"Tam."

"—because they can't count what's underneath, I *proved* it, I walked the whole—"

"*Tam.*" She shook me once, gently, the way you wake someone from a nightmare. "Come sit down. Both of you—Father, put the type down for five minutes. We need to talk."

Father grumbled that the type had been put down quite enough lately, but he came, and Mother came down the stairs with her hands still smelling of bread, and we gathered around the stove in the back room like a family in a painting, except the painting was of the last ordinary hour, and I was the only one who didn't know it.

I spread my copybook on the table one last time—all those chutes, all those stars, my summer and autumn of candles and stink and patient crawling—and I pushed it toward the middle of the family like a boy laying down his cards.

"There," I said. "Tell me it's worth something."

And Hazel looked at the map, and then at Mother, and then out the window toward the bridge where the notices fluttered on their posts like pale moths, and she said, quietly, in the voice I would learn to hate and admire in equal measure for the rest of my life:

"It might be. Not for hiding." She turned the page with one finger, to the drawing of the river road, the little boat I'd sketched beside it, the barge I'd imagined carrying us all away free. "But ask me again tomorrow. By tomorrow I may have a use for it you haven't thought of."

Outside, far off, faint as a heartbeat, I heard the knock-knock-knock of boots on our own street, and a voice asking a question of somebody's door.

The chalk was coming.
