## 9. The Last Barge Announced

The proclamation went up on the bridge on a Tuesday morning, pasted to the stone pillar where the census notices had been nailed all autumn, and I will tell you what struck me first: the type was beautiful. Whoever had set it knew his business—tight leading, clean justification, not a river of white space running through a single paragraph. Somebody in the Ministry's print office was very good at his work, and that somebody had spent the last of October setting the words that would make half this city go mad.

*By order of the Provisional Registry,* it read, *one vessel, the Meridian Star, shall depart the quay at Vellamark on the thirty-first day of December in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred ninety-nine. This shall be the final sanctioned passage downriver. Berths shall be assigned by public lottery and by consideration of registry standing. All persons remaining after said date do so at their own risk and without claim upon the state.*

I stood in the cold with my hands jammed in my apron pockets and read it three times, because reading it three times made it no truer but gave me something to do with my eyes while the truth caught up with me.

One barge. One. For a city of forty thousand souls, give or take the ones the census had already decided didn't count.

Beside me, an old woman in a fishmonger's shawl read it aloud, slowly, moving her lips, and when she reached *without claim upon the state* she laughed—a short, dry sound like a stick breaking—and said to nobody in particular, "As if we'd ever had a claim."

The crowd around the pillar thickened as I watched. Nobody shouted. That was the strange thing, the thing I kept returning to later: there was no shouting, no weeping, none of the theater you'd expect from a city being told it had one boat. Instead there was a kind of terrible quiet arithmetic happening everywhere at once, faces going still as people did the sums. Families counting heads against berths. Tradesmen calculating whether their registry standing—their *consideration*, that soft bureaucratic word—was worth anything now. Mothers looking at their children the way you look at a bill you cannot pay.

I did my own arithmetic standing right there. Four Vanes. One barge. A lottery whose odds I could feel in my teeth.

Then I walked home through streets that were already changing shape around me.

* * *

You can tell a lot about a city by its shop windows, and by Wednesday the windows of Vellamark had become sermons. The jeweler on Corvess Lane had emptied his cases except for one ring marked SOLD, displayed like a trophy. The bookseller had hung a hand-lettered sign: EVERYTHING MUST GO, WHICH IS TO SAY, EVERYTHING WILL GO, WITH US OR WITHOUT US. Even the undertaker had gotten into the spirit, advertising a discount on burials "for those who intend to remain"—a sentence so cheerfully sinister that Tam read it out twice and laughed until he coughed.

At home, Father had taken down the proclamation's wording from memory and set it himself, just to see how he'd have done it better. He stood over the galley critiquing the Ministry's compositor the way other men criticized generals.

"Loose here," he muttered, tapping the proof sheet. "And this widow—*state* alone at the end of a line, dangling like a loose tooth. If they're going to end a century for people, they might at least set it decently."

"Papa." I took the proof from him before he could adjust anything else. "They're ending our lives and you're grading their kerning."

"Somebody has to," he said, and there was nothing wry in it at all. "If the world must be told bad news, let it be told well. It's the only dignity left in it."

That was Aldous Vane for you. Other men raged at heaven; mine corrected its typography.

Mother said nothing about the proclamation. She had been saying nothing about most things since Kettle's visit, folding herself smaller week by week like a letter being prepared for its envelope. But I saw her that evening standing at the window, watching the bridge lantern come on across the water, her hand flat against her sternum as though holding something in place. I almost spoke. I didn't. There is a particular cowardice in families under sentence—we are tender with each other precisely where we ought to be honest, handling the truth like type fresh from the mold, too hot to touch.

Tam, meanwhile, had gone practical, which for Tam meant cartographic. He spread his papers on the kitchen table after supper: the sewers, the coal chutes, the culvert behind the tannery, drawn in his fierce little pencil strokes.

"If the lottery goes wrong," he said, "we don't need the barge. Look—here, and here, and the old ice house connects to—"

"Tamsen." Mother's voice, quiet.

"I'm just saying there are ways. People have hidden worse than us."

"Have they?" Mother asked, and it wasn't really a question, and Tam looked at his maps and then folded them away, and the table stayed clear and empty the rest of the night, which somehow felt worse than if he'd left them out.

* * *

By the end of the week, the city had sorted itself into two congregations, and you could identify them on sight.

There were the Waiters. They queued. That was the whole of their religion: the queue outside the Registry Hall began forming at four in the morning, a patient gray river of people clutching their papers, believing—or pretending to believe—that if they stood long enough, filled out enough forms, answered enough questions, the state would notice their virtue and reward it with a berth. I watched them from the foundry doorway some mornings, hunched against the cold, and what broke my heart was not their desperation but their *faith*. They believed the lottery would be fair. They believed consideration of registry standing meant what it said. They believed the word *lottery* the way children believe in the tooth fairy: because the alternative was to believe in the dark.

And there were the Schemers.

The Schemers did not queue. The Schemers wrote letters. I know because some of them wrote letters to us. Within days of the proclamation, the foundry's front bell—which had rung only for trade these two years, and precious little of that—began ringing for strangers. A cloth merchant offered Father three months' rent for "a small printing favor." A woman I'd never seen arrived with a sealed note asking, delicately, whether the Vanes' press might produce "a document of transit, vintage quality." A man who claimed to be a shipping agent left a card and a number, and the number was large enough that Father burned the card rather than leave temptation lying in the scrap bin.

"We are not counterfeiters," Father announced to the empty room, which is a sentence that means different things depending on who else is in it. I happened to be in it. He looked at me a beat too long when he said it, and I looked back a beat too innocently, and neither of us mentioned the practice sheets I'd been burning in the stove, or the fact that my father had taught me, two nights prior, how to shave the shoulder of a sort so the impression sat lighter, like age.

"Of course not," I said.

"Because forgery is—" he began.

"A hanging offense. I know."

"It's also," he said, turning back to his case, "the only growth industry left in this town. Which tells you everything about what we've become."

He didn't ask me to stop. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head: he never once asked me to stop.

* * *

The lottery itself was scheduled for the second week of December, to be held publicly on the quay, berths drawn "under supervision of the Registry Office." In the meantime, the *consideration of registry standing* clause worked its quiet poison. Favor was the other currency now, and everyone suddenly discovered they knew someone who knew someone. The magistrate's wife was seen taking tea with the Registrar's clerk. The harbor master's brother-in-law, a man I had personally watched fall drunk into the canal twice last summer, acquired a registry classification of Essential Services. Essential. Services. The man could not find his own feet in daylight.

And Ambrose Kettle—Kettle, whom I had not seen since he sat in our parlor with his ledger and his careful questions and his conscience flickering behind his face like a bad lamp—Kettle was promoted. Everyone knew within a day; Vellamark is a city that processes news faster than it processes grain. Assistant Registrar, berth assignment division. The announcement appeared in the *Vellamark Gazette*, and I read it at the corner shop with a sensation I couldn't immediately name. Not surprise. Something colder. Recognition, maybe. I had bargained with that man. I had shaken his damp hand and agreed to bring him the foundry's client ledger, and now the man I'd fed held the spoon that would portion out the rest of my family's lives.

*Berth assignment division.* Five words that meant he would decide, or help decide, or stand close enough to the deciding to sell proximity to it. And I remembered the look on his face when he'd named his price—not greedy, exactly. Hungry in a way that had been starving a long time and had finally smelled meat.

"He'll be fair," Mother said, when the Gazette lay open between us at breakfast. She said it the way you say a prayer you've stopped believing in, just to keep your mouth in practice.

"Yes," I said, because arguing would require explaining, and explaining would require confessing, and there are confessions a daughter does not put on her mother at breakfast. "He seemed a fair man."

He was not a fair man. Neither, by then, was I. Perhaps that's why the recognition ran both directions.

* * *

It was Tam who brought home the detail that changed everything, because Tam collected details the way other boys collected marbles. He'd been down at the quay—he went everywhere, my brother, slipping through the city like water through a grate—counting bollards or measuring gangplanks or whatever his twelve-year-old reconnaissance required, and he came back breathless with the news.

"There's a name painted on her already. The barge. Under the old name—they just painted over it. You can still see the ghost of the letters." He drew it in the frost on the window with his fingernail: *MERIDIAN STAR*. "But underneath, if the light hits right, you can read what she used to be. She was a *coal barge*, Hazel. She carried coal. Forty years of coal, and now they're going to scrub her and pack people into her hold."

"How many berths?" I asked.

"That's the thing." His finger stopped on the glass. "I counted the life rings. Twelve."

Twelve. I did the arithmetic again and got the same answer as before, and the answer was a hole shaped like everything.

Twelve berths, drawn from a lottery drum, plus whatever the *consideration* clause sold off to the well-connected on the side. Against forty thousand people. The Waiters would queue and pray and draw their paper slips, and nearly every slip would be blank, and the Schemers would buy and bribe and forge, and nearly every scheme would fail, and between them the two congregations would tear at each other like gulls over one fish while the state watched from the warm offices above the quay, having designed, with beautiful typographic precision, a machine that required the desperate to destroy one another so that no hand need ever be laid on them directly.

I stood at the frosted window a long time after Tam went up to bed, looking at the ghost letters under the ghost letters. A coal barge. We would cross out of the century in the hull that had hauled fuel for our fires, and someone in the Ministry print office would set the proclamation beautifully, tight leading, clean justification, not a widow in the whole broadsheet, and call it mercy.

Here is what I resolved that night, alone at the window with my breath foging the glass and the bridge lantern burning steady across the black water: we would not draw lots. Lots are for people who accept the odds, and I had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was an insult. Somewhere between the proclamation pillar and the frost on the glass, the fear in me finished hardening into something else—something with edges.

One berth. I had promised Kettle one berth's worth of cooperation already, and Kettle had been promoted, and Kettle would be sitting at the assignment table when the Vanes' file crossed it. Whatever the price turned out to be—and I was no longer naive enough to think I'd seen the bottom of that ledger—I would pay it, and I would pay it cleverly, and when the moment came I would put my own name in my father's hand onto whatever page needed signing, because I had discovered something about myself that winter that I did not entirely like:

I lie well. And increasingly, I did not mind.

The next morning, the client ledger sat in the drawer beneath the composing stone where it had sat for thirty years, fat with names, and among them, in Father's careful entries, a magistrate's name appeared more often than any other.

I took it out, and I wrapped it in brown paper, and I told myself it was only the beginning of the payment.

I did not yet know how right I was.
